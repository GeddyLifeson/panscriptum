# Sentences

This chapter describes how Ròdais builds sentences: the clause and its two verbs "to be", aspect and
the verbal noun, the particles, the passive and the impersonal, the experiencer constructions with
prepositions, relative and subordinate clauses, the noun phrase, and the conventions of speech and
writing. It closes with three sample texts with interlinear glosses: a dialogue, entries from the
annals, and a proclamation.

The chapter cites a form and says how it is used. The full paradigms are in *Word forms*: the
mutations (§1), the article (§2), noun cases and plurals (§3), adjectives and comparison (§4),
pronouns and prepositional pronouns (§5), the verb with its impersonal and relative forms (§6), the
copula (§7), and the numerals and dates (§8). Spelling, the mutations and a short account of word
order are in *The grammar in brief*. Every example is numbered, written in Ròdais spelling, glossed word by
word and translated (§0.2). Many are taken from the conversations and tales of the *Texts* chapter,
and these are marked (T).

## 0. Conventions

### 0.1 Register: the Ròdais norm is the conservative one

Ròdais is the tongue of an old island that has long kept to itself, and its norm is the
conservative one. For centuries the island's scribes and the library at Muileann chaol have held
good speech close to the written language. Where the language has an older, literary construction
beside a newer spoken one, the norm is the older, in speech as well as in writing (*Word forms*
§0.1). The newer constructions are heard in casual talk, and this chapter marks them
*colloquial*. Five things follow from this:

1. **Synthetic verb forms are preferred.** The old present-future carries habitual and general
   statements (*Òlaidh e uisce gach madainn*), the conditional carries the past habitual (*Sheasadh
   i aig an doras*), and the synthetic first persons of the conditional and imperative are kept
   (*sheasainn*, *sheasamaid*, *seasam*, *seasamaid*). The periphrastic *bidh e ag òl* is correct
   but plainer.
2. **Case is used in full.** Nouns take the genitive after a noun, after a verbal noun and after
   compound prepositions; adjectives agree with a genitive noun (*taigh an duine mhòir*); feminine
   nouns take their dative after simple prepositions (*air a' chloich*); the dual follows *dà*
   (*dà làimh*); the vocative is marked.
3. **The older relative and conditional forms are the norm.** A prepositional relative puts its
   preposition before the relative particle (*an taigh anns an robh mi*, not *an taigh a bha mi
   ann*). The relative future in *-(e)as* is used after *a*, *nuair a*, *ma* and *cho fad 's a*.
   Conditional clauses keep the full sequence of tenses.
4. **Examples use old native vocabulary.** When new things came to the island, it named most of
   them from its own old roots (*The grammar in brief* §13): a teacher is an *oide*, a doctor a
   *lighiche*, a telephone a *guth-sreinge*. The loans that came in with the humans at the Crossing
   and after, and are still heard (*tidsear*, *dotair*, *oifis*), are shunned in formal Ròdais.
5. **Old registers are represented.** Proverbs, the formulas of the tale-teller, the quotative
   *arsa*, the jussive of proclamations and the terse verb-noun headings of the annals all appear
   in the examples, because a Ròdach meets them daily.

Two kinds of note stand beside the rules. A note headed **Older usage** sets a construction beside
its shape in the older language: the tongue of the bardic schools, whose verse the Library keeps,
and the older speech behind it, known from the oldest leaves. Such notes
explain, and add no rule. A note headed **Usage** marks a point where good Ròdais holds to a form
that careless speech lets go.

## 1. The clause

### 1.1 Verb, subject, object

The Ròdais clause begins with the verb. The subject follows the verb, the object follows the
subject, and everything else (prepositional phrases, adverbs of place, then adverbs of time) comes
after that:

```
(1)   Chunnaic  an   t-iascair  an   ròn   air  a'   chladach   an-dè.
      see.PST   the  fisherman  the  seal  on   the  shore.DAT  yesterday
      ‘The fisherman saw the seal on the shore yesterday.’

(2)   Thug      am   bàrd  am   bradan  do  Fhionn.
      give.PST  the  poet  the  salmon  to  Fionn
      ‘The poet gave the salmon to Fionn. (T)’
```

The verb stands first in statements, questions and commands alike. A question or negative adds a
particle before the verb and changes nothing else (§3):

```
(3)   Am  faca         an   t-iascair  an   ròn?
      Q   see.PST.DEP  the  fisherman  the  seal
      ‘Did the fisherman see the seal?’

(4)   Chan  fhaca        an   t-iascair  an   ròn.
      NEG   see.PST.DEP  the  fisherman  the  seal
      ‘The fisherman did not see the seal.’
```

Only three things can stand before the verb: a particle (negative, interrogative, conjunction,
relative); the copula, which itself is a verb and brings its own fronted phrase with it (§1.6);
and, in narrative, a scene-setting adverbial phrase, followed by a comma:

```
(5)   Aon  oidhche  ghealaich,  chaidh  e      sìos  chun  a'   chladaich.
      one  night    moon.GEN    go.PST  3SG.M  down  to    the  shore.GEN
      ‘One moonlit night, he went down to the shore. (T)’
```

A pronoun object tends to move to the end of its clause, after a short prepositional phrase, while
a heavy noun object stays next to the verb or moves to the end in its place:

```
(6)   Dh'fhalaich  e      fo     a    chòta  e.
      hide.PST     3SG.M  under  his  coat   3SG.M
      ‘He hid it under his coat. (T)’

(7)   Chuir    i      uimpe        an   craiceann.
      put.PST  3SG.F  about.3SG.F  the  skin
      ‘She put on the skin. (T)’
```

The verb is followed by its subject even when the subject is long; Ròdais does not move a heavy
subject forward. What English does with a heavy subject Ròdais does with a relative clause after
the subject, or with a cleft (§1.6).

### 1.2 The two verbs "to be"

Ròdais has two verbs that English translates as "to be":

- the **substantive verb** *bi* (*tha, bha, bidh, bhiodh…*), which says that something exists, is
  somewhere, or is in some state;
- the **copula** *is* (*is, bu*), which links a subject to what it *is*: a class it belongs to, an
  identity, or a property treated as essential. The copula also fronts a phrase for emphasis.

The choice is never free. It depends on what kind of thing follows.

| After "be" in English | Ròdais | Example |
|---|---|---|
| an adjective (a state) | *bi* | *Tha an cala beag.* |
| a place, a prepositional phrase | *bi* | *Tha e air a' chladach.* |
| a verbal noun with *ag, air, ri, gu* | *bi* | *Tha i a' scrìobhadh.* |
| existence ("there is") | *bi* + *ann* | *Tha stoirm ann.* |
| an indefinite noun (class) | copula, or *bi* + *ann* + possessive | *Is iascair e. / Tha e na iascair.* |
| a definite noun or name (identity) | copula | *Is e Tormod an t-iascair.* |
| emphasis on one part | copula + fronted part | *Is ann an-dè a thàinig e.* |

### 1.3 The substantive verb

*Bi* takes an adjective, a prepositional phrase or an adverb as its predicate:

```
(8)   Tha     an   t-eilean  mòr.
      be.PRS  the  island    big
      ‘The island is big.’

(9)   Bha     a'   mhuir  ciùin  fad     na   h-oidhche.
      be.PST  the  sea    calm   length  the  night.GEN
      ‘The sea was calm all night.’

(10)  Bidh    na   longan  anns  a'   chala    a-màireach.
      be.FUT  the  ships   in    the  harbour  tomorrow
      ‘The ships will be in the harbour tomorrow.’
```

**Existence** is *bi* + subject + *ann* "in it", literally "X is in it":

```
(11)  Tha     stoirm  ann.
      be.PRS  storm   in.3SG.M
      ‘There is a storm.’

(12)  Bha     siud  ann       uair  iascair.
      be.PST  yon   in.3SG.M  once  fisherman
      ‘There was once a fisherman. (tale opening, T)’

(13)  Chan  eil         duilgheadas  ann.
      NEG   be.PRS.DEP  difficulty   in.3SG.M
      ‘There is no difficulty. (No problem.)’
```

Weather is expressed as existence or as a state of the day, with the feminine pronoun *i* for the
weather itself: *Tha an t-uisce ann* "it's raining" (the rain is in it), *Tha i fuar an-diugh*
"it's cold today".

A **non-verbal predicate with *bi* + *ann* + possessive** classifies the subject: literally "he is
in his fisherman". The possessive agrees with the subject, and *ann* + possessive fuses into *nam,
nad, na, na, nar, nur, nan* (see the table in §2.7):

```
(14)  Tha     e      na             iascair.
      be.PRS  3SG.M  in.3SG.M.POSS  fisherman
      ‘He is a fisherman.’

(15)  Bha     i      na             bean-phòsta  chòir.
      be.PST  3SG.F  in.3SG.F.POSS  wife         kind
      ‘She was a kind wife. (T)’

(16)  Nuair  a    bha     mi   nam          bhalach,  bha     mi   a'    fuireach  aig
      when   REL  be.PST  1SG  in.1SG.POSS  boy       be.PST  1SG  PROG  live.VN   at
      Ceann  Dubh.
      Ceann  Dubh
      ‘When I was a boy, I lived at Ceann Dubh.’
```

This construction suits a role or a stage of life, something one *is at the moment* or *became*
(*Tha e na bhodach a-nis* "he's an old man now"). The copula (§1.4) states what a thing is.

### 1.4 The copula: classification and identification

The present copula is *is* ('*s* unstressed, and written '*S* at the start of a sentence in speech
and dialogue). Its past and conditional form is *bu* (*b'* before a vowel or *fh*), which lenites.
Negative *chan*, *cha bu / cha b'*; interrogative *an*, *am bu*; negative interrogative *nach*,
*nach bu*. The copula has no future of its own; the present covers it. The full table is in
*The grammar in brief* §7.4 and *Word forms* §7.

**Classification** ("X is a Y", Y indefinite). The oldest and most literary pattern is copula +
predicate noun + subject. It is the Ròdais norm in formal prose, proverbs and verse:

```
(17)  Is   iascair    e.
      COP  fisherman  3SG.M
      ‘He is a fisherman.’

(18)  Is   Ròdach  mi,  agus  is   Tuathach    i.
      COP  Ròdach  1SG  and   COP  northerner  3SG.F
      ‘I am a Ròdach, and she is a Tuathach.’

(19)  Bu       ghobha  a    athair.
      COP.PST  smith   his  father
      ‘His father was a smith.’
```

The everyday pattern puts a pronoun after the copula and turns the rest into a relative clause:
"it is a fisherman that is in him":

```
(20)  Is   e      iascair    a    th'     ann.
      COP  3SG.M  fisherman  REL  be.PRS  in.3SG.M
      ‘He is a fisherman.’

(21)  'S   e      oileanach  a    th'     annam.
      COP  3SG.M  student    REL  be.PRS  in.1SG
      ‘I am a student.’

(22)  Is   e      seòladair  a    th'     ann.
      COP  3SG.M  sailor     REL  be.PRS  in.3SG.M
      ‘He's a sailor. (T)’

(23)  Chan     e      bàrd  a    bh'     innte,    ach  scrìobhaiche.
      NEG.COP  3SG.M  poet  REL  be.PST  in.3SG.F  but  scribe
      ‘She was not a poet but a scribe.’
```

The *a th' ann* pattern is obligatory when the predicate noun has an adjective or a long modifier
(*Is e seòladair math a th' ann*), and in questions with *dè*: *Dè a th' ann?* "What is it?"

**Identification** ("X is the Y", both definite). The copula is followed by a pronoun that stands
for the subject, then the subject, then the predicate:

```
(24)  Is   e      Tormod  an   t-iascair.
      COP  3SG.M  Tormod  the  fisherman
      ‘Tormod is the fisherman.’

(25)  Is   i      Eilidh  a'   bhana-bhàrd.
      COP  3SG.F  Eilidh  the  poetess
      ‘Eilidh is the poetess.’

(26)  Is   iad  na   Ròdaich  sluagh  an   eilein.
      COP  3PL  the  Ròdaich  people  the  island.GEN
      ‘The Ròdaich are the people of the island.’

(27)  B'       e      Fionn  an   t-ainm  a    bh'     air.
      COP.PST  3SG.M  Fionn  the  name    REL  be.PST  on.3SG.M
      ‘Fionn was his name. (T)’
```

**Usage.** The pronoun after the copula agrees with the subject: *e* for a man or a masculine
noun, *i* for a woman or a feminine noun, *iad* for a plural. Careless speech often lets *'s e*
stand for all three; good writing never does.

With a demonstrative or a personal pronoun as subject, the pronoun follows the copula directly;
the emphatic pronouns *mise, thusa…* are used, and no *e* is added:

```
(28)  Is   mise      Eilidh.
      COP  1SG.EMPH  Eilidh
      ‘I am Eilidh. (T)’

(29)  Is   e      seo   an   cala.
      COP  3SG.M  this  the  harbour
      ‘This is the harbour.’

(30)  An     e      seo   a'   chiad  turas    agaibh  an-seo?
      Q.COP  3SG.M  this  the  first  journey  at.2PL  here
      ‘Is this your first time here? (T)’
```

**Copula + adjective.** The copula with an adjective predicate states a lasting quality, a
judgement, or a general truth. It is common in proverbs and formal speech, and in fixed
expressions of opinion:

```
(31)  Is   mòr  an   t-eilean  seo.
      COP  big  the  island    this
      ‘Great is this island.’

(32)  Is   domhainn  na   h-uisceachan  ciùin.
      COP  deep      the  waters        calm
      ‘Still waters run deep.’

(33)  Is   fìor  sin.
      COP  true  that
      ‘That is true.’

(34)  Bu        mhath  sin.
      COP.COND  good   that
      ‘That would be good.’
```

*Tha an t-eilean mòr* reports the island's size; *Is mòr an t-eilean* exclaims or asserts it.
Many fixed predicates are copula + adjective or noun + preposition: *is toil le*, *is fheàrr le*,
*is urrainn do*, *is aithne do*, *is èiginn do*, *is fhiach* (§5).

**Older usage.** In the oldest verse the copula is already *is*, with the negative *nì* and the past
*ba*; *bu* is the old past and conditional *budh*. The "X is in his Y" construction (*tha e na
iascair*) is younger. It first appears in the middle period of the language, and it spread as the
copula's classifying use grew formal.

### 1.5 Answers with the copula

The copula is answered with the copula and its first element: *An e seo an cala? — 'S e. / Chan
e.* "Is this the harbour? — It is. / It isn't." *An toil leat scadan? — 'S toil. / Cha toil.* See
§3.5.

### 1.6 Fronting and clefts: *is e* and *is ann*

Any part of a clause except the verb can be put in focus by fronting it with the copula and
turning the rest of the clause into a relative clause. Which pronoun the copula takes depends on
what is fronted:

- a **noun, a noun phrase or a pronoun** is fronted with *is e* (*is i*, *is iad* by agreement for
  a definite noun; plain *is* before an emphatic pronoun);
- **anything else** (a prepositional phrase, an adverb, an adjective, a verbal-noun phrase) is
  fronted with *is ann*.

```
(35)  Chunnaic  Calum  an   long  an-dè.
      see.PST   Calum  the  ship  yesterday
      ‘Calum saw the ship yesterday. (neutral)’

(36)  Is   e      Calum  a    chunnaic  an   long  an-dè.
      COP  3SG.M  Calum  REL  see.PST   the  ship  yesterday
      ‘It was Calum who saw the ship yesterday.’

(37)  Is   i      an   long  a    chunnaic  Calum  an-dè.
      COP  3SG.F  the  ship  REL  see.PST   Calum  yesterday
      ‘It was the ship that Calum saw yesterday.’

(38)  Is   ann       an-dè      a    chunnaic  Calum  an   long.
      COP  in.3SG.M  yesterday  REL  see.PST   Calum  the  ship
      ‘It was yesterday that Calum saw the ship.’

(39)  Is   mise      a    rinn    e.
      COP  1SG.EMPH  REL  do.PST  3SG.M
      ‘It was I who did it.’

(40)  Is   ann       à     Ròdos  a    tha     mi.
      COP  in.3SG.M  from  Ròdos  REL  be.PRS  1SG
      ‘I am from Rodos.’

(41)  Is   ann       scìth  a    tha     mi,  chan     ann       tinn.
      COP  in.3SG.M  tired  REL  be.PRS  1SG  NEG.COP  in.3SG.M  ill
      ‘I'm tired, not ill.’

(42)  Is   ann       a'    tilleadh   a    bha     iad.
      COP  in.3SG.M  PROG  return.VN  REL  be.PST  3PL
      ‘They were coming back (not going).’
```

The copula keeps present form in a present or neutral frame, even for past events (*Is e Calum a
chunnaic*); in a narrative in the past it may itself go into the past: *B' e Calum a chunnaic an
long*. Negative *chan e / chan ann*, question *an e / an ann*, negative question *nach e / nach
ann*:

```
(43)  An     ann       dhutsa       a    bha     am   bradan  an  dàn?
      Q.COP  in.3SG.M  to.2SG.EMPH  REL  be.PST  the  salmon  in  fate
      ‘Was it for you that the salmon was destined?’

(44)  Cha  b'       e      ainm  a    thagh       iad  fhèin  a    bh'     ann.
      NEG  COP.PST  3SG.M  name  REL  choose.PST  3PL  self   REL  be.PST  in.3SG.M
      ‘It was not a name they chose themselves. (T)’
```

A fronted adjective with *is ann* is common in exclamation and contrast; the older pattern puts the
adjective directly after the copula (*Is fuar an oidhche!*). A cleft with *is ann* + *mar sin*
ends many tales: *Agus is ann mar sin a tha iad chun an latha an-diugh* "and that is how they are
to this day" (T).

**Older usage.** Clefting with the copula is old; the oldest verse already fronts with *is è* and
with the copula + a prepositional phrase. The *ann* of *is ann* is the prepositional pronoun
"in it", used as a dummy predicate.

## 2. Aspect and the verbal noun

Ròdais tense is carried by the inflected verb; aspect is carried by *bi* + a preposition + the
verbal noun (VN). The verbal noun is a noun: it takes the article, governs a genitive, and takes a
possessive for a pronoun object. The preposition says where the subject stands in relation to the
action: *ag* "at" it (in progress), *air* "on, after" it (done), *ri* "at, engaged in" it, *gu*
"towards" it (about to happen).

### 2.1 The progressive: *ag* / *a'*

*Ag* before a vowel, *a'* before a consonant. The progressive describes an action in progress. It
is also the ordinary present of all verbs except *bi*, since the old simple present survives only
in the present-future (§2.9):

```
(45)  Tha     mi   a'    leughadh.
      be.PRS  1SG  PROG  read.VN
      ‘I am reading.’

(46)  Tha     i      ag    òl        uisce.
      be.PRS  3SG.F  PROG  drink.VN  water.GEN
      ‘She is drinking water.’

(47)  Bha     na   mnathan  a'    dannsadh  air  a'   ghainmhich.
      be.PST  the  women    PROG  dance.VN  on   the  sand.DAT
      ‘The women were dancing on the sand.’

(48)  Bidh    sinn  a'    feitheamh  riut.
      be.FUT  1PL   PROG  wait.VN    for.2SG
      ‘We will be waiting for you.’
```

A few verbs of perception and state take the progressive where English uses a simple tense: *Tha
mi a' tuigsinn* "I understand", *Tha mi a' smaoineachadh gu bheil* "I think so", *Tha e a'
fuireach aig Ceann Dubh* "he lives at Ceann Dubh". Verbs of position do not (they use the state
construction, §2.7).

### 2.2 The perfect: *air*

*Bi* + *air* + VN is a perfect: the action is complete and its result holds. With *tha* it is a
present perfect, with *bha* a pluperfect, with *bidh* a future perfect, with *bhiodh* a
conditional perfect:

```
(49)  Tha     mi   air  tighinn  à     Cathair  dhearg.
      be.PRS  1SG  PRF  come.VN  from  Cathair  dhearg
      ‘I have come from Cathair dhearg. (T)’

(50)  Tha     sinn  air  ithe.
      be.PRS  1PL   PRF  eat.VN
      ‘We have eaten.’

(51)  Bha     i      air  tilleadh   mus     do   dhùisc    e.
      be.PST  3SG.F  PRF  return.VN  before  PST  wake.PST  3SG.M
      ‘She had returned before he woke.’

(52)  Bidh    sinn  air  ruigsinn  ron         oidhche.
      be.FUT  1PL   PRF  reach.VN  before.the  night
      ‘We will have arrived before night.’

(53)  Bhiodh   iad  air  falbh.
      be.COND  3PL  PRF  go.VN
      ‘They would have gone.’
```

Because *air falbh* also means "away", *Tha e air falbh* is both "he has gone" and "he is away";
the two senses are one.

**An dèidh** "after" is used like *air*, for an action just completed:

```
(54)  Tha     e      an dèidh  tighinn  a-steach.
      be.PRS  3SG.M  after     come.VN  in
      ‘He has just come in.’
```

### 2.3 The prospective: *gu*, *a' dol a*, *an impis*

*Gu* + VN (often *gu bhith*) is "about to", "due to":

```
(55)  Tha     mi   gu       bhith  deiseil.
      be.PRS  1SG  towards  be.VN  ready
      ‘I'm about to be ready.’

(56)  Bha     an   long  gu       falbh  nuair  a    thàinig   an   stoirm.
      be.PST  the  ship  towards  go.VN  when   REL  come.PST  the  storm
      ‘The ship was about to leave when the storm came.’
```

*A' dol a* + lenited VN is "going to", an intended or expected future:

```
(57)  Tha     mi   a'    dol    a   dh'fhalbh  a-màireach.
      be.PRS  1SG  PROG  go.VN  to  go.VN      tomorrow
      ‘I'm going to leave tomorrow.’
```

*An impis* + VN is "on the point of":

```
(58)  Bha     a'   chraobh  an impis         tuiteam.
      be.PST  the  tree     on.the.point.of  fall.VN
      ‘The tree was on the point of falling.’
```

### 2.4 *Ri*: engaged in, and "to be done"

*Bi ri* + noun or VN says what someone is busy with or given to:

```
(59)  Dè    a    tha     thu  ris?
      what  REL  be.PRS  2SG  at.3SG.M
      ‘What are you up to?’

(60)  Tha     i      ri  bàrdachd.
      be.PRS  3SG.F  at  poetry
      ‘She makes poetry. / She is at her poetry.’

(61)  A  bheil       thu  ri  fealla-dhà?
      Q  be.PRS.DEP  2SG  at  joking
      ‘Are you joking?’
```

With a thing as subject, *ri* + VN means "to be done", a passive of obligation (see also §4.4):

```
(62)  Tha     mòran  ri  dhèanamh.
      be.PRS  much   to  do.VN
      ‘There is a lot to be done.’
```

### 2.5 The object of the verbal noun: noun objects

In the progressive, the verbal noun is followed by its object **in the genitive**, because the
object is grammatically the possessor of the verbal noun ("at the reading of the book"):

```
(63)  Tha     mi   a'    leughadh  an   leabhair.
      be.PRS  1SG  PROG  read.VN   the  book.GEN
      ‘I am reading the book.’

(64)  Tha     mi   a'    sireadh  arain      agus  èisc.
      be.PRS  1SG  PROG  seek.VN  bread.GEN  and   fish.GEN
      ‘I'm looking for bread and fish. (T)’

(65)  Bha     i      a'    scrìobhadh  litreach    gu  a    màthair.
      be.PST  3SG.F  PROG  write.VN    letter.GEN  to  her  mother
      ‘She was writing a letter to her mother.’

(66)  Bidh    iad  a'    loscadh  mònach    an àite     guail.
      be.FUT  3PL  PROG  burn.VN  peat.GEN  instead.of  coal.GEN
      ‘They burn peat instead of coal. (T)’
```

**Usage.** Speech often leaves an indefinite object in the nominative (*a' leughadh leabhar*).
Writing always uses the genitive, as the bards did.

In the **perfect**, and after every other preposition that governs a VN (*gus, airson, mus, gun,
an dèidh*) and after verbs that take a VN complement (*feumaidh, is urrainn do, is toil le*), the
object goes **before** the verbal noun, joined by *a* "to", which lenites; *a* is dropped before a
vowel or *fh*:

```
(67)  Tha     mi   air  an   leabhar  a   leughadh.
      be.PRS  1SG  PRF  the  book     to  read.VN
      ‘I have read the book.’

(68)  Bha     am   balach  air  an   t-each  fhaicinn  roimhe.
      be.PST  the  boy     PRF  the  horse   see.VN    before.3SG.M
      ‘The boy had seen the horse before. (T)’

(69)  Feumaidh  mi   an   doras  a   dhùnadh.
      must.FUT  1SG  the  door   to  close.VN
      ‘I must close the door.’

(70)  Bu        toil      leam      an   t-eilean  fhaicinn.
      COP.COND  pleasing  with.1SG  the  island    see.VN
      ‘I would like to see the island.’

(71)  Thàinig   i      airson  an   leabhar  a   leughadh.
      come.PST  3SG.F  for     the  book     to  read.VN
      ‘She came to read the book.’
```

In this infinitive construction the object stays in the nominative. An intransitive verbal noun
stands alone, with no *a*: *Feumaidh mi falbh* "I must go", *Tha mi airson fuireach* "I want to
stay".

If the infinitive has its own **subject**, the subject is introduced by *do* "to, for":

```
(72)  An dèidh  dha       tilleadh,  chaidh  e      a   chadal.
      after     to.3SG.M  return.VN  go.PST  3SG.M  to  sleep.VN
      ‘After he returned, he went to sleep.’

(73)  An dèidh  don     long  seòladh,  thill       iad  dhachaigh.
      after     to.the  ship  sail.VN   return.PST  3PL  home
      ‘After the ship sailed, they went home.’
```

### 2.6 The object of the verbal noun: pronoun objects

A pronoun object of a verbal noun becomes a **possessive** before it. In the progressive, *ag* +
possessive fuses into *gam, gad, ga, ga, gar, gur, gan*:

| object | progressive | example | perfect / infinitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| me | *gam* + len. | *Tha e gam fhaicinn.* "He sees me." | *air m' fhaicinn*, *mo chuideachadh* |
| you (sg.) | *gad* + len. | *Tha i gad fhaicinn.* | *air d' fhaicinn*, *do chuideachadh* |
| him, it (m.) | *ga* + len. | *Tha mi ga fhaicinn.* | *air fhaicinn*, *a chuideachadh* |
| her, it (f.) | *ga* (*ga h-* before a vowel) | *Tha mi ga faicinn. / ga h-ithe* | *air a faicinn*, *a cuideachadh* |
| us | *gar* | *Tha iad gar cuideachadh.* | *ar cuideachadh* |
| you (pl.) | *gur* | *Tha sinn gur faicinn.* | *ur faicinn* |
| them | *gan*, *gam* before b f m p | *Tha mi gam faicinn.* | *am faicinn*, *an cuideachadh* |

```
(74)  Tha     mi   ga          fhaicinn.
      be.PRS  1SG  PROG.3SG.M  see.VN
      ‘I see him / I see it.’

(75)  An     urrainn  dhuibh  mo  chuideachadh?
      Q.COP  able     to.2PL  my  help.VN
      ‘Can you help me? (T)’

(76)  Cha  robh        duine   eile   ga          iarraidh.
      NEG  be.PST.DEP  person  other  PROG.3SG.M  want.VN
      ‘No one else wanted it. (T)’

(77)  Feumaidh  mi   a    dùnadh.
      must.FUT  1SG  her  close.VN
      ‘I must close it (the window, f.).’
```

The perfect *air a fhaicinn* contracts to *air fhaicinn* "(has) seen him"; *air a faicinn* "seen
her" does not contract.

### 2.7 States: *na shuidhe*, *nan seasamh*

Posture and similar states are not progressive actions. They are expressed with *ann* +
possessive + VN, "in his sitting":

| | *ann* + possessive | example |
|---|---|---|
| 1SG | *nam* (+ len.) | *Tha mi nam sheasamh.* "I am standing." |
| 2SG | *nad* (+ len.) | *Tha thu nad shuidhe.* |
| 3SG.M | *na* (+ len.) | *Tha e na shuidhe.* "He is sitting." |
| 3SG.F | *na* (*na h-* before a vowel) | *Tha i na laighe.* "She is lying down." |
| 1PL | *nar* | *Tha sinn nar dùisc.* "We are awake." |
| 2PL | *nur* | *Tha sibh nur seasamh.* |
| 3PL | *nan*, *nam* before b f m p | *Tha iad nan cadal.* "They are asleep." |

```
(78)  Bha     ballachan  cloiche    fhathast  nan          seasamh.
      be.PST  walls      stone.GEN  still     in.3PL.POSS  stand.VN
      ‘Stone walls were still standing. (T)’

(79)  Na   dùisc     an   cù   a    tha     na             chadal.
      NEG  wake.IMP  the  dog  REL  be.PRS  in.3SG.M.POSS  sleep
      ‘Let sleeping dogs lie. (proverb)’
```

The progressive of the same verb describes the movement: *Tha e a' suidhe* "he is sitting down".
The same *ann* + possessive with a noun gives the classification of §1.3 (*Tha e na iascair*) and
many idioms: *na dhragh* "a nuisance", *nam bheachd-sa* "in my opinion", *na theine* "on fire".

### 2.8 Aspect in the other tenses

Aspect combines with every tense of *bi*:

| | progressive | perfect |
|---|---|---|
| present | *tha mi a' seasamh* | *tha mi air seasamh* |
| past | *bha mi a' seasamh* | *bha mi air seasamh* |
| future | *bidh mi a' seasamh* | *bidh mi air seasamh* |
| conditional | *bhithinn a' seasamh* | *bhithinn air seasamh* |
| infinitive | *a bhith a' seasamh* | *a bhith air seasamh* |

```
(80)  Is   toil      leam      a   bhith  a'    leughadh.
      COP  pleasing  with.1SG  to  be.VN  PROG  read.VN
      ‘I like reading.’
```

### 2.9 The synthetic tenses and aspect

The simple (synthetic) tenses carry aspect of their own:

- The **past** (*sheas, chunnaic*) is perfective: a completed event in a narrative.
- The **present-future** (*seasaidh, chì*) is a future, and also a **habitual or timeless present**:
  what always happens, what one does as a rule. This is the Ròdais norm for habits:

```
(81)  Òlaidh     e      uisce  gach   madainn.
      drink.FUT  3SG.M  water  every  morning
      ‘He drinks water every morning.’

(82)  Gheibh   an   t-eun  moch   a'   chnuimh.
      get.FUT  the  bird   early  the  worm
      ‘The early bird catches the worm. (proverb)’
```

- The **conditional** (*sheasadh, chitheadh*) is also a **past habitual**, "used to, would":

```
(83)  Gach   feascar  sheasadh    i      aig  an   doras.
      every  evening  stand.COND  3SG.F  at   the  door
      ‘Every evening she would stand at the door. (T)’

(84)  Thigeadh   e      às      an   loch  air  an   oidhche.
      come.COND  3SG.M  out.of  the  loch  on   the  night
      ‘At night it would come out of the loch. (T)’
```

The periphrastic forms say the same thing more plainly: *Bidh e ag òl uisce gach madainn*, *Bhiodh
i a' seasamh aig an doras*. They are correct, but the synthetic forms are the Ròdais norm and the
periphrasis is felt as colloquial.

**Older usage.** The tongue of the bards still had a synthetic present (*do-chì* "sees", *molaidh*
"praises"). Afterwards the language lost it everywhere but in *bi*, and the old present became the
present-future; so *òlaidh e* is both "he will drink" and "he drinks". The verbal-noun periphrasis
with *ag* spread in the middle period. Ròdais still divides the work as the bards did: synthetic
forms for fact and habit, the periphrasis for action in progress.


## 3. Tense, mood and the particles

### 3.1 Independent and dependent forms

Every tense of a Ròdais verb except the imperative has two forms (*The grammar in brief* §7.1;
paradigms in *Word forms* §6). The **independent** form stands at the head of a main clause and after
the relative particle *a*, *nuair a*, *ma*, *ged a* and the other conjunctions built on *a*. The
**dependent** form follows the particles that end in a nasal or come from one: *cha(n)*, *an/am*,
*nach*, *gun/gum*, *mura*, *nan/nam*, *mus*, *gus an*, *far an*, *càite an*, and the prepositional
relative (*anns an*, *ris an*…).

| | independent | dependent |
|---|---|---|
| *bi*, present | *tha* | *(a) bheil*, *eil* |
| *bi*, past | *bha* | *robh* |
| regular past | *sheas, dh'òl* | *do sheas, do dh'òl* |
| regular future | *seasaidh, òlaidh* | *seas, òl* |
| *faic*, past / future | *chunnaic / chì* | *faca / faic* |
| *rach*, past / future | *chaidh / thèid* | *deach / tèid* |
| *dèan*, future | *nì* | *dèan* |

```
(85)  Thèid   mi   ann.
      go.FUT  1SG  in.3SG.M
      ‘I will go there.’

(86)  Cha  tèid        mi   ann.
      NEG  go.FUT.DEP  1SG  in.3SG.M
      ‘I will not go there.’

(87)  Nì      mi   sin.
      do.FUT  1SG  that
      ‘I'll do that.’

(88)  Cha  dèan        mi   sin.
      NEG  do.FUT.DEP  1SG  that
      ‘I won't do that.’
```

**Older usage.** The split is as old as the oldest verse. There a verb had one form when it stood
alone (the absolute or deuterotonic form) and another when a particle stood before it (the
conjunct or prototonic): *do-beir* "gives" but *nì tabair* "does not give", the older shapes of
*bheir* and *cha toir*. The *do* of the dependent past (*cha do sheas*) is the old perfective
preverb *do/ro*.

### 3.2 The tenses in use

- **Past**: a completed event, and the tense of narrative. English perfects with "ever, never"
  are pasts: *Chan fhaca mi a-riamh cala cho brèagha* "I have never seen such a beautiful harbour"
  (T).
- **Present**: only *bi* has one (*tha*). Other verbs use *tha* + progressive (§2.1).
- **Present-future**: the future, and the habitual or timeless present (§2.9). It also gives an
  offer or decision made now: *Gabhaidh mi dà lof* "I'll take two loaves" (T).
- **Conditional**: the hypothetical "would", the past habitual "used to" (§2.9), and the future in
  the past in reported speech (§7.8).
- **Relative future** (*a sheasas, a bhios, a thig*): the future and the habitual in relative
  clauses and after *nuair a*, *ma*, *cho fad 's a* (§6.2, §7).

### 3.3 Negation: *cha*, *chan*, *nach*, *na*, *nar*

**Cha / chan** negates a main clause. *Cha* lenites except *d* and *t*; *chan* stands before a
vowel and before a lenited *f* that falls silent (*chan fhaca*). It takes the dependent form:

```
(89)  Cha  do   thill       i      tuilleadh.
      NEG  PST  return.PST  3SG.F  more
      ‘She never came back. (T)’

(90)  Chan  fhaca        mi   e      o      chionn  fhada.
      NEG   see.PST.DEP  1SG  3SG.M  since  time    long
      ‘I haven't seen him for a long time. (T)’

(91)  Cha  b'       urrainn  dhi       tilleadh   gun      a    craiceann.
      NEG  COP.PST  able     to.3SG.F  return.VN  without  her  skin
      ‘She could not go back without her skin. (T)’

(92)  Cha  sheas          poca  falamh.
      NEG  stand.FUT.DEP  sack  empty
      ‘An empty sack won't stand. (proverb)’
```

**Nach** is the negative of the question particle, of the conjunction "that" and of the relative
particle (§3.4, §6.2, §7.1): *Nach eil thu scìth?* "Aren't you tired?", *Thuirt e nach tigeadh e*
"He said that he wouldn't come", *am fear nach tàinig* "the one who didn't come".

**Na** negates the imperative, and **nar** the optative (§3.6, §3.7).

Negative words need *cha* with them. Ròdais has no word for "nobody, nothing, never" that negates
by itself:

```
(93)  Cha  tàinig        duine.
      NEG  come.PST.DEP  person
      ‘Nobody came.’

(94)  Chan  fhaca        mi   nì.
      NEG   see.PST.DEP  1SG  thing
      ‘I saw nothing.’

(95)  Chan  eil         agam    ach  aon  long.
      NEG   be.PRS.DEP  at.1SG  but  one  ship
      ‘I have only one ship.’

(96)  Cha  tèid        mi   ann       gu  bràth.
      NEG  go.FUT.DEP  1SG  in.3SG.M  to  judgement
      ‘I will never go there.’
```

*A-riamh* is "ever, never" of the past, *gu bràth* and *a-chaoidh* "ever, never" of the future,
*idir* "at all", *tuilleadh* "any more", *ach* "only" (with *cha*: "not… but").

### 3.4 Questions

**Yes-no questions** put *an* (*am* before *b, f, m, p*) before the dependent form; with the
present of *bi* it is *a bheil*. The negative question uses *nach*:

```
(97)  An  robh        sibh  ann?
      Q   be.PST.DEP  2PL   in.3SG.M
      ‘Were you there?’

(98)  Am  faca         tu   an   long?
      Q   see.PST.DEP  2SG  the  ship
      ‘Did you see the ship?’

(99)  An  do   dh'òl      thu  an   t-uisce?
      Q   PST  drink.PST  2SG  the  water
      ‘Did you drink the water?’

(100) Nach   tàinig        e      fhathast?
      NEG.Q  come.PST.DEP  3SG.M  yet
      ‘Hasn't he come yet?’
```

A **tag question** repeats the verb with *nach* after a positive statement, or with *an* after a
negative one: *Tha e fuar, nach eil?* "It's cold, isn't it?"; *Chan eil e fuar, a bheil?*

**Question words** are not particles. Most of them are fronted predicates of a hidden copula, and
the rest of the question is a relative clause after them. So most take the relative particle *a*
and the independent form, as a relative clause does (§6); so do the question words built with a
preposition (*cò ris, cò leis*). The exception is *càite* "where", which takes *an* (*a* before
*bheil*) and the dependent form:

| word | meaning | construction | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *cò* | who | + *a* + indep. | *Cò a thàinig?* "Who came?" |
| *dè* | what | + *a* + indep. | *Dè a rinn thu?* "What did you do?" |
| *ciamar* | how | + *a* + indep. | *Ciamar a tha sibh?* "How are you?" |
| *cuin* | when | + *a* + indep. | *Cuin a thig an long?* |
| *carson* | why | + *a* + indep.; *carson nach* | *Carson nach tàinig thu?* |
| *càite* | where | + *an* + dep. (*a bheil*) | *Càite an robh thu?* |
| *cia mheud* | how many | + singular noun + *a* | *Cia mheud long a sheòl?* |
| *cò às* | where from | + *a* + indep. | *Cò às a tha thu?* |
| *cò leis* | whose | + *a* + indep. | *Cò leis a tha an cù?* |
| *dè cho* | how (+ adj.) | + adj. + *'s a* | *Dè cho fada 's a tha e?* |
| *cò ris, cò air…* | to whom, on what… | prep. + *a* + indep. | *Cò ris a bha thu a' bruidhinn?* |

```
(101) Cò   a    rinn    e?
      who  REL  do.PST  3SG.M
      ‘Who did it?’

(102) Dè    an   t-ainm  a    th'     oirbh?
      what  the  name    REL  be.PRS  on.2PL
      ‘What is your name? (T)’

(103) Càite  a  bheil       sibh  a'    fuireach?
      where  Q  be.PRS.DEP  2PL   PROG  stay.VN
      ‘Where are you staying? (T)’

(104) Ciamar  a    ruigeas        mi   an   cala?
      how     REL  reach.REL.FUT  1SG  the  harbour
      ‘How do I get to the harbour?’

(105) Cia mheud  long  a    sheòl     à     Seann  Skell?
      how.many   ship  REL  sail.PST  from  Seann  Skell
      ‘How many ships sailed from Seann Skell?’

(106) Cò   ris   a    bha     thu  a'    bruidhinn?
      who  with  REL  be.PST  2SG  PROG  speak.VN
      ‘Whom were you talking to?’
```

*Ciamar a ruigeas mi* has the relative future: the question is general ("how does one get"), and
the relative clause takes the relative form (§6.2). *Cia mheud* takes the singular, like *fichead*
(§8.8).

**Indirect questions** keep the particle of the direct question after a verb of asking or knowing:
*Chan eil fhios agam an e an fhìrinn a th' ann* "I don't know whether it is the truth" (T);
*Dh'fhaighnich e càite an robh an cala* "He asked where the harbour was" (§7.8).

### 3.5 Answers: echo, not "yes" and "no"

Ròdais has no words for "yes" and "no". An answer repeats the verb of the question, positive or
negative, in the tense of the question and without its subject; a copula question is answered by
the copula and the word after it.

| question | yes | no |
|---|---|---|
| *A bheil thu scìth?* "Are you tired?" | *Tha.* | *Chan eil.* |
| *An robh i aig a' bhanais?* | *Bha.* | *Cha robh.* |
| *Am bi thu ann a-màireach?* | *Bidh.* | *Cha bhi.* |
| *An cuala tu an naidheachd?* | *Chuala.* | *Cha chuala.* |
| *An tèid thu leinn?* | *Thèid.* | *Cha tèid.* |
| *An dèan thu sin?* | *Nì.* | *Cha dèan.* |
| *An do bhlais thu den bhradan?* | *Bhlais.* | *Cha do bhlais.* |
| *Am feum mi a dhol thairis air an drochaid?* | *Feumaidh.* | *Chan fheum.* |
| *An e seo an cala?* | *'S e.* | *Chan e.* |
| *An toil leat scadan?* | *'S toil.* | *Cha toil.* |
| *An urrainn dhuibh mo chuideachadh?* | *'S urrainn.* | *Chan urrainn.* |
| *An aithne dhut Tormod?* | *'S aithne.* | *Chan aithne.* |

A question in the progressive or perfect is answered with *bi* alone: *A bheil thu air ithe? —
Tha.* A negative question is answered as if it were positive: *Nach eil thu scìth? — Tha* "(Yes,)
I am". The answer may add a word of force: *Chuala, gu dearbh* "I did indeed"; *'S urrainn, gu
dearbh* (T).

**Usage.** For a question with no verb to echo (a bare "yes?" or a word in a list), Ròdais says
*Seadh* "it is so" for "yes" and *Chan eil* for "no" (*The grammar in brief* §7.2). *Seadh* is also
the listener's "I see, go on".

### 3.6 The imperative and the jussive

The imperative has a full set of persons. The second persons are the everyday forms; the first and
third persons ("let me, let us, let him") are the old synthetic jussive. Ròdais keeps it in full
use: it is the only form in proclamations, prayers and oaths, and ordinary in speech:

| | *seas* "stand" | *bi* "be" | *rach* "go" |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1SG "let me" | *seasam* | *bitheam* | *racham* |
| 2SG | *seas* | *bi* | *rach* |
| 3SG "let him, her" | *seasadh e/i* | *bitheadh e*, *biodh e* | *rachadh e* |
| 1PL "let us" | *seasamaid* | *bitheamaid* | *rachamaid* |
| 2PL / polite | *seasaibh* | *bithibh* | *rachaibh* |
| 3PL "let them" | *seasadh iad* | *bitheadh iad* | *rachadh iad* |

The negative is *na* + the same form, without lenition: *na seas*, *na bithibh*, *na biodh*.

```
(107) Thigibh       còmhla    rium.
      come.IMP.2PL  together  with.1SG
      ‘Come with me. (T)’

(108) Na   gabh      dragh.
      NEG  take.IMP  worry
      ‘Don't worry.’

(109) Scuireamaid    an-diugh.
      stop.JUSS.1PL  today
      ‘Let's stop for today.’

(110) Biodh    fios       aig  gach   neach.
      be.JUSS  knowledge  at   every  person
      ‘Let every person know.’

(111) Na   biodh    eagal  oirbh.
      NEG  be.JUSS  fear   on.2PL
      ‘Do not be afraid.’
```

The impersonal imperative (*gleidhtear* "let it be kept", §4.2) completes the set. **Colloquial.**
Casual speech often uses *leig* + *le* "let" for the first and third persons (§3.7), and
*Thugainn!* "come along, let's go" for *rachamaid*.

### 3.7 The optative and "let" constructions

**The optative** is a wish: *gun* (*gum*) + the dependent future, or + *robh* for *bi*; negative
*nar*. It survives in blessings, curses and thanks:

```
(112) Gu   robh        math  agad.
      OPT  be.PST.DEP  good  at.2SG
      ‘Thank you. (lit. may good be at you)’

(113) Gun  èirich        dhut!
      OPT  rise.FUT.DEP  to.2SG
      ‘Good luck! (lit. may it rise for you)’

(114) Gum  beannaich      Dia  thu.
      OPT  bless.FUT.DEP  God  2SG
      ‘God bless you.’

(115) Gum  bu       fada  beò    thu!
      OPT  COP.PST  long  alive  2SG
      ‘Long may you live!’

(116) Nar      leigeadh  Dia!
      OPT.NEG  let.COND  God
      ‘God forbid!’
```

**Usage.** Thanks are given with the optative, *gu robh math agad / agaibh*, at every door and
every table, high and low. The reply is *'S e do bheatha / 'S e ur beatha* "you're welcome".

**Older usage.** *Robh* in *gu robh* is the old subjunctive of *bi* (in the bards' spelling *go
raibh* "may there be"). The subjunctive has since been lost as a tense, but its forms live on in
the dependent past of *bi* and in these formulas.

**"Let"** in the sense of permission is *leig le* + VN, and it gives the everyday first- and
third-person commands:

```
(117) Leig     leam      smaoineachadh.
      let.IMP  with.1SG  think.VN
      ‘Let me think.’

(118) Leigibh      leis        falbh.
      let.IMP.2PL  with.3SG.M  go.VN
      ‘Let him go.’

(119) Cha  leig         na   màthraichean  leis  a'   chloinn       each   a   shlìobadh.
      NEG  let.FUT.DEP  the  mothers       with  the  children.DAT  horse  to  stroke.VN
      ‘The mothers will not let the children stroke a horse. (after T)’
```

## 4. The passive and the impersonal

Ròdais has three ways of leaving out or demoting the agent: *rach* + VN, the impersonal forms of
the verb, and the resultant state with *air* + possessive + VN.

### 4.1 The passive with *rach*

*Rach* "go" + the object + *a* + lenited VN is a passive, literally "the house went to building".
It is used in every tense that *rach* has:

```
(120) Chaidh  an   taigh  a   thogail   an-uiridh.
      go.PST  the  house  to  build.VN  last.year
      ‘The house was built last year.’

(121) Chaidh  a    dhèanamh  sa      mhadainn.
      go.PST  its  make.VN   in.the  morning
      ‘It was made this morning. (T)’

(122) Thèid   an   doras  a   dhùnadh   aig  meadhan-latha.
      go.FUT  the  door   to  close.VN  at   midday
      ‘The door will be closed at midday.’

(123) An  deach       na   fiachan  a   phàigheadh  a-riamh?
      Q   go.PST.DEP  the  debts    to  pay.VN      ever
      ‘Were the debts ever paid? (T)’

(124) Chaidh  mo  bhualadh.
      go.PST  my  strike.VN
      ‘I was struck.’
```

A pronoun subject of the passive is a possessive before the VN: *Chaidh mo bhualadh* "I was
struck", *Chaidh a marbhadh* "she was killed", *Chaidh ar faicinn* "we were seen". An agent, when
it is named, is introduced by *le*:

```
(125) Chaidh  an   leabhar  a   scrìobhadh  le    scrìobhaiche  à     Muileann  dhearg.
      go.PST  the  book     to  write.VN    with  scribe        from  Muileann  dhearg
      ‘The book was written by a scribe from Muileann dhearg.’
```

*Chaidh innse dhomh* "I was told" has no subject: *innse* is intransitive here, and the person told
is in *do* (T).

### 4.2 The impersonal forms

Every Ròdais verb has impersonal (or "autonomous") forms: finite forms with no subject, meaning
"someone does, one does, it is done". The object, if any, stays an object. The main forms:

| | past | future | conditional |
|---|---|---|---|
| regular (*tog*) | *thogadh* (dep. *do thogadh*) | *togar* | *thogtadh* (dep. *togtadh*) |
| regular (*bris*) | *bhriseadh* | *brisear* | *bhristeadh* |
| *faic* | *chunnacas* (dep. *facas*) | *chithear* (dep. *faicear*) | *chiteadh* (dep. *faicteadh*, *faicte*) |
| *dèan* | *rinneadh* | *nithear* (dep. *dèanar*) | *dhèantadh* |
| *faigh* | *fhuaireadh* (dep. *d' fhuaireadh*) | *gheibhear* (dep. *faighear*) | *gheibhteadh* |
| *abair* | *thuirteadh* | *theirear* (dep. *abrar*) | *theirteadh* |
| *beir* | *rugadh* | *beirear* | *bheirteadh* |
| *bi* | *bhathar* (dep. *robhar*) | *bithear* | *bhite* (dep. *bite*) |
| *bi*, present | *thathar* (dep. *eilear*) | | |

The regular past impersonal has the same shape as the conditional third person (*thogadh*); the
impersonal has no subject after it, and context decides. There is also an impersonal imperative
in *-tar/-tear*, "let it be done" (*togtar*, *gleidhtear*), the voice of rules and proclamations.
The full set, with the short conditional *faicte* of the tales, is in *Word forms* §6.4 and §6.9.

```
(126) Chunnacas     an   cabhlach  bhon      chidhe.
      see.PST.IMPS  the  fleet     from.the  quay
      ‘The fleet was seen from the quay.’

(127) Chan  fhacas            iad  a-riamh  tuilleadh.
      NEG   see.PST.IMPS.DEP  3PL  ever     more
      ‘They were never seen again. (T)’

(128) Rugadh         mi   ann an  Seann  Dunn.
      bear.PST.IMPS  1SG  in      Seann  Dunn
      ‘I was born in Seann Dunn.’

(129) Cha  d'   fhuaireadh    sceul  orra    tuilleadh.
      NEG  PST  get.PST.IMPS  trace  on.3PL  more
      ‘No trace of them was ever found. (T)’

(130) Theirear      Seann  Skell  ris  a'   bhaile  sin.
      say.FUT.IMPS  Seann  Skell  to   the  town    that
      ‘That town is called Seann Skell.’

(131) Thathar      ag    ràdh    gu    bheil       an   cnoc  a'    smocadh.
      be.PRS.IMPS  PROG  say.VN  that  be.PRS.DEP  the  hill  PROG  smoke.VN
      ‘It is said that the hill is smoking.’

(132) Cha  loiscear       leabhar  anns  an   taigh  seo.
      NEG  burn.FUT.IMPS  book     in    the  house  this
      ‘No book shall be burned in this house.’
```

Both constructions are used in every register. The impersonal is the norm for general statements
("one says", "it is known"), for laws and rules, and for events whose agent does not matter;
*rugadh* "was born" has no other form. The *rach* passive is the norm when a particular event
happens to a particular subject, especially one named by a pronoun (*chaidh mo bhualadh*).

**Older usage.** The impersonal is the old passive of the language. The oldest verse has a present
in *-thar/-ther*, a preterite built on the past participle (*rugad* "was born"), and a conditional
in *-tae/-tì*. The bards kept all three as their passive (*do-chìthear*, *do chonncas*,
*do-chìfidhe*), and Ròdais has all three in full use still.

### 4.3 The resultant state: participles and *air* + possessive + VN

A finished state is expressed by the participle in *-te/-ta* after *bi*, or by *air* + possessive
+ VN, where the possessive agrees with the subject:

```
(133) Tha     e      scrìobhte   a-nis.
      be.PRS  3SG.M  write.PTCP  now
      ‘It is written now. (the minute of the Moot)’

(134) Tha     an   doras  air  a    dhùnadh.
      be.PRS  the  door   PRF  its  close.VN
      ‘The door is closed.’

(135) Tha     an   uinneag  air  a    dùnadh.
      be.PRS  the  window   PRF  its  close.VN
      ‘The window is closed.’

(136) Tha     na   longan  air  an     togail.
      be.PRS  the  ships   PRF  their  build.VN
      ‘The ships are built.’

(137) Fhuair   i      craiceann  air  a    phascadh  fo     sheann  lìon.
      get.PST  3SG.F  skin       PRF  its  fold.VN   under  old     net
      ‘She found a skin folded under an old net. (T)’
```

The same form after a verb of speaking gives "it is said": *Bha e air a ràdh gum faigheadh…* "It
was said that… would get" (T). Participles are also plain adjectives: *an cnoc loiscte* "the burnt
hill", *uinneag bhriste* "a broken window".

### 4.4 "To be done": *ri* + VN

*Bi* + *ri* + VN, with a thing as subject, gives the passive of obligation or possibility: *Tha an
obair ri dhèanamh* "the work is to be done", *Chan eil nì ri fhaicinn* "there is nothing to be
seen", *Tha e ri fhaighinn* "it is to be had".


## 5. Possession, modality and the experiencer

Ròdais has no verb "to have", and few verbs of feeling, liking, needing or knowing. It expresses
all of them with *bi* or the copula and a preposition that marks the person concerned. The
prepositions carry fixed meanings: *aig* "at" marks the possessor, *le* "with" the owner and the
one who likes, *do* "to" the one who can or must, *air* "on" the one a feeling or state has come
upon, *ri* "against, towards" the thing one is engaged with. The prepositional pronouns are in
*The grammar in brief* §6.

### 5.1 Having: *bi* + *aig*

```
(138) Tha     cù   agam.
      be.PRS  dog  at.1SG
      ‘I have a dog.’

(139) Tha     rùm   agam    anns  an   taigh-òsta.
      be.PRS  room  at.1SG  in    the  inn
      ‘I have a room in the inn. (T)’

(140) Chan  eil         airgead  aig  an   iascair.
      NEG   be.PRS.DEP  money    at   the  fisherman
      ‘The fisherman has no money.’

(141) Tha     Ròdais  aice.
      be.PRS  Ròdais  at.3SG.F
      ‘She speaks Ròdais. (lit. Ròdais is at her)’
```

Possessive pronouns are for the body, kin and what is part of oneself (*mo cheann, mo mhàthair*);
for ordinary possessions Ròdais prefers the article + noun + *aig*: *an taigh agam* "my house", *an
long aca* "their ship". This *aig* phrase follows any adjective: *an taigh mòr agam*.

### 5.2 Belonging: the copula + *le*

Ownership as a fact about the thing (whose it is) uses the copula with *le*:

```
(142) Is   le    Calum  an   cù.
      COP  with  Calum  the  dog
      ‘The dog is Calum's.’

(143) Cò   leis        a    tha     an   long  seo?
      who  with.3SG.M  REL  be.PRS  the  ship  this
      ‘Whose ship is this?’

(144) Is   ann       leamsa         a    tha     i.
      COP  in.3SG.M  with.1SG.EMPH  REL  be.PRS  3SG.F
      ‘It's mine.’

(145) Buinidh     mi   don     mhuir.
      belong.FUT  1SG  to.the  sea
      ‘I belong to the sea. (T)’
```

### 5.3 Obligation

| construction | force | example |
|---|---|---|
| *feumaidh* + subject + VN | must, need (general) | *Feumaidh mi falbh.* |
| *is èiginn do* + VN | must, cannot avoid (formal, strong) | *Is èiginn dhomh falbh.* |
| *tha aig* + person + *ri* + VN | have to (an obligation owed) | *Tha agam ri falbh.* |
| *bu chòir do* + VN | ought to, should | *Bu chòir dhut èisteachd.* |
| *cha leig* + person + *a leas* + VN | need not | *Cha leig thu a leas tighinn.* |

```
(146) Feumaidh  sinn  an   long  a   chàradh.
      must.FUT  1PL   the  ship  to  mend.VN
      ‘We must mend the ship.’

(147) B'       èiginn     dhaibh  teicheadh.
      COP.PST  necessity  to.3PL  flee.VN
      ‘They had to flee.’

(148) Tha     agam    ri  tilleadh   dhan    chala.
      be.PRS  at.1SG  to  return.VN  to.the  harbour
      ‘I have to go back to the harbour.’

(149) Tha     mo  mhàthair  ag    ràdh    gum   bu        chòir  dha       fuireach  aig
      be.PRS  my  mother    PROG  say.VN  that  COP.COND  right  to.3SG.M  stay.VN   at
      an   taigh.
      the  house
      ‘My mother says he ought to stay at home. (T)’
```

*Feum* is a regular verb in its future and conditional (*feumaidh, dh'fheumadh*; question *am
feum?*, negative *chan fheum*). Need for a thing is *tha feum aig* + person + *air* + thing: *Tha
feum agam air lighiche* "I need a doctor".

### 5.4 Ability and permission

*Is urrainn do* "can, be able" is the copula + noun + *do*; its past and conditional is *b' urrainn
do*. *Faod* "may" is a verb (*faodaidh, dh'fhaodadh*):

```
(150) Is   urrainn  dhomh   snàmh.
      COP  able     to.1SG  swim.VN
      ‘I can swim.’

(151) B'       urrainn  dhaibh  na   ròin   a   ghairm   le    òran.
      COP.PST  able     to.3PL  the  seals  to  call.VN  with  song
      ‘They could call the seals with a song. (T)’

(152) Am  faod         mi   suidhe  an-seo?
      Q   may.FUT.DEP  1SG  sit.VN  here
      ‘May I sit here?’

(153) Chan  fhaod        thu  an   leabhar  a   thoirt   a-mach.
      NEG   may.FUT.DEP  2SG  the  book     to  take.VN  out
      ‘You may not take the book out.’
```

*Dh'fhaodadh* (or impersonal *dh'fhaodte*) also means "perhaps, it could be": *Dh'fhaodte a ràdh*
"it could be said, arguably".

### 5.5 Liking and preference

*Is toil le* "like", *is fheàrr le* "prefer", *bu toil le* "would like", *b' fheàrr le* "would
rather". The thing liked is the subject of the copula; the one who likes is in *le*:

```
(154) Is   toil      leam      scadan.
      COP  pleasing  with.1SG  herring
      ‘I like herring. (T)’

(155) An     toil      leat      a   bhith  a'    seòladh?
      Q.COP  pleasing  with.2SG  to  be.VN  PROG  sail.VN
      ‘Do you like sailing?’

(156) Is   fheàrr  leinn     a   bhith  fuar  agus  saor.
      COP  better  with.1PL  to  be.VN  cold  and   free
      ‘We would rather be cold and free. (T)’

(157) Bu        toil      leam      deoch  uisce.
      COP.COND  pleasing  with.1SG  drink  water.GEN
      ‘I would like a drink of water.’

(158) Is   e      ceòl   as       fheàrr  leam.
      COP  3SG.M  music  REL.COP  better  with.1SG
      ‘Music is what I like best.’
```

Fondness with *bi* is *Tha mi dèidheil air* "I am fond of it"; love and hate are nouns at the
lover: *Tha gaol agam ort* "I love you" (love is at me on you), *Tha fuath aige air* "he hates it".
Worth is *is fhiach*: *Is fhiach e* "it's worth it", *Is fhiach feuchainn* "it's worth a try".

### 5.6 Feelings and states: *bi* + *air*

A feeling, hunger, illness or state that comes upon a person is the subject of *bi*, and the person
is in *air* "on". Most such nouns take the article:

```
(159) Tha     an   t-acras  orm.
      be.PRS  the  hunger   on.1SG
      ‘I am hungry.’

(160) Tha     am   pathadh  oirre.
      be.PRS  the  thirst   on.3SG.F
      ‘She is thirsty.’

(161) Bha     an   t-eagal  orra    ron         each-uisce.
      be.PST  the  fear     on.3PL  before.the  water-horse
      ‘They were afraid of the water horse.’

(162) Tha     an   cnatan  air.
      be.PRS  the  cold    on.3SG.M
      ‘He has a cold.’

(163) Dè    tha     a dhìth  oirbh?
      what  be.PRS  lacking  on.2PL
      ‘What do you need?’

(164) Tha     ceann  goirt  orm.
      be.PRS  head   sore   on.1SG
      ‘I have a headache.’

(165) Dè    an   t-ainm  a    th'     ort?
      what  the  name    REL  be.PRS  on.2SG
      ‘What is your name?’
```

Others: *tha fearg air* "he is angry", *tha nàire orm* "I am ashamed", *tha cabhag orm* "I'm in a
hurry", *tha faochadh orm* "I'm relieved", *tha iongnadh orm* "I'm surprised", *tha an cadal orm*
"I'm sleepy". A name is "on" a person, a price "on" a thing (*Dè a' phrìs a th' air seo?*), and a
debt "on" a debtor (*bha fiachan orra* "they were in debt").

With the verbal noun *cur air* "put on" the state is caused: *Tha sin a' cur eagal orm* "that
frightens me". *Thoir air* + VN is "make someone do": *Na toir orm tòiseachadh* "don't get me
started".

### 5.7 Knowing: *is aithne do* and *tha fios aig*

Ròdais distinguishes knowing a person or place (acquaintance) from knowing a fact:

```
(166) Is   aithne        dhomh   an   t-iascair  sin.
      COP  acquaintance  to.1SG  the  fisherman  that
      ‘I know that fisherman.’

(167) An     aithne        dhut    Tormod?
      Q.COP  acquaintance  to.2SG  Tormod
      ‘Do you know Tormod?’

(168) Tha     fios       agam    gu    bheil       e      fuar.
      be.PRS  knowledge  at.1SG  that  be.PRS.DEP  3SG.M  cold
      ‘I know that it is cold.’

(169) Chan  eil         fhios      agam.
      NEG   be.PRS.DEP  knowledge  at.1SG
      ‘I don't know.’

(170) Bha     fios       aig  a h-uile  duine   gun   robh        an   t-each-uisce  fo
      be.PST  knowledge  at   every     person  that  be.PST.DEP  the  water-horse   under
      a    uisce.
      its  water
      ‘Everyone knew that the water horse was under its water. (T)’
```

*Fios* is lenited to *fhios* after *chan eil* and *a bheil* in the everyday phrase. Skill and
familiarity is *tha mi eòlach air*: *Bha iad eòlach air a' mhuir* "they knew the sea well" (T).
Memory is *tha cuimhne agam air* "I remember it"; expectation *tha dùil agam ri* "I expect".

## 6. Relative clauses

### 6.1 Direct relatives

A relative clause follows its noun and is introduced by the particle *a*, which lenites and takes
the independent form of the verb. The same *a* serves for subject and object, so a direct relative
can be ambiguous; word order and sense decide:

```
(171) an   duine  a    chunnaic  mi
      the  man    REL  see.PST   1SG
      ‘the man I saw / the man who saw me’

(172) iascair    a    bha     a'    fuireach  leis        fhèin
      fisherman  REL  be.PST  PROG  live.VN   with.3SG.M  self
      ‘a fisherman who lived alone (T)’

(173) na   teaghlaichean  a    thuirt   "Chan  eil         sinn  airson  a   bhith  fo
      the  families       REL  say.PST  NEG    be.PRS.DEP  1PL   for     to  be.VN  under
      fhiachan"
      debts
      ‘the families who said "We don't want to be in debt" (T)’
```

Before a vowel and before *f* the particle is *a dh'*: *an duine a dh'òl an t-uisce* "the man who
drank the water", *an long a dh'fhàg an cala* "the ship that left the harbour". In speech the *a*
of *a bha, a tha* is often swallowed (*a th' ann, a bh' ann*); it is always written.

The negative relative is **nach** + dependent form:

```
(174) am   fear  nach     tàinig
      the  man   NEG.REL  come.PST.DEP
      ‘the one who didn't come’

(175) bliadhna  nuair  nach     do   dh'fhàs   an   t-arbhar
      year      when   NEG.REL  PST  grow.PST  the  corn
      ‘a year when the corn did not grow (T)’
```

### 6.2 The relative future

In a relative clause the future has its own form, the **relative future** in *-(e)as*, lenited:
*a sheasas*, *a dh'òlas*, *a bhios*, and for the irregular verbs *a nì*, *a chì*, *a thig*, *a
thèid*, *a gheibh*, *a bheir*, *a their*, with *dh'* before a vowel
and before *fh* + vowel, as in the past. It covers future and habitual meaning, like the
main-clause present-future:

```
(176) an   duine  a    bhios       an-seo  a-màireach
      the  man    REL  be.REL.FUT  here    tomorrow
      ‘the man who will be here tomorrow’

(177) an   long  a    sheòlas       a-màireach
      the  ship  REL  sail.REL.FUT  tomorrow
      ‘the ship that sails tomorrow’

(178) Rud    a    chuireas     dragh    orm
      thing  REL  put.REL.FUT  trouble  on.1SG
      ‘a thing that bothers me (a pet peeve)’
```

The relative future is also used after *nuair a*, *ma*, *cho fad 's a* and *mar a* (§7), because
these are relative in origin: *nuair a thig an làn* "when the tide comes in", *ma dh'èisteas tu*
"if you listen" (T), *cho fad 's a bhios gual fon talamh* "as long as there is coal under the
ground" (T).

**Older usage.** The *-as* ending is what is left of the special relative forms of the old verb
(*beires* "who carries"), which the bards kept in the third person (*an tì do-bheir*, *mar
a-deir*). Of all these the language has kept one, the future.

### 6.3 The relative copula

In a relative clause the copula is *as* (present) and *a bu* / *a b'* (past): *an rud as toil
leam* "the thing I like", *an duine as sine* "the eldest person" (the superlative, §8.6), *na bha
aca de ghoireasan* "whatever goods they had" (T). Negative *nach*: *an rud nach toil leam*.

### 6.4 Indirect and prepositional relatives

When the noun is the object of a preposition in the relative clause, the Ròdais norm puts the
preposition first, followed by the relative particle *an* (*am* before *b, f, m, p*) and the
dependent form. The preposition takes the article's forms before *an*: *anns an, aig an, air an,
ris an, leis an, don, bhon, fon*:

```
(179) an   taigh  anns  an   robh        mi
      the  house  in    REL  be.PST.DEP  1SG
      ‘the house I was in’

(180) far    an   robh        an   teine  aig  Fionn
      where  REL  be.PST.DEP  the  fire   at   Fionn
      ‘where Fionn's fire was (T)’

(181) an   duine  ris  an   do   bhruidhinn  mi
      the  man    to   REL  PST  speak.PST   1SG
      ‘the man I spoke to’

(182) loch  dorcha  ris  an   canadh        daoine  Loch  Dubh
      loch  dark    to   REL  say.COND.DEP  people  Loch  Dubh
      ‘a dark loch that people called Loch Dubh (T)’

(183) na   daoine  aig  an   robh        an   fhuil-ghuail
      the  people  at   REL  be.PST.DEP  the  coal-blood
      ‘the people who had the coal-blood (T)’
```

**Colloquial.** Speech leaves the preposition at the end, as a prepositional pronoun, after a
direct relative: *an taigh a bha mi ann*, *an duine a bhruidhinn mi ris*. This is correct and
common, but the fronted form is the Ròdais norm.

**Older usage.** The fronted preposition + *an* is the old preposition + the nasalizing relative
(*i n-a* "in which"), which the bards wrote *ina*, *dà* ("to which"), *lè* ("with which").

### 6.5 Genitive relatives ("whose")

Ròdais has no relative pronoun "whose". It uses a relative clause with a possessive that refers
back to the noun, or turns the possession into *aig*:

```
(184) am   fear  a    bheil       a    mhac  anns  a'   chabhlach
      the  man   REL  be.PRS.DEP  his  son   in    the  fleet
      ‘the man whose son is in the fleet (literary)’

(185) am   fear  aig  a    bheil       mac  anns  a'   chabhlach
      the  man   at   REL  be.PRS.DEP  son  in    the  fleet
      ‘the man who has a son in the fleet (general)’

(186) am   bàrd  a    chuala    mi   a    dhàn
      the  poet  REL  hear.PST  1SG  his  poem
      ‘the poet whose poem I heard’
```

The first is the older, literary form: the relative particle takes the dependent form (*a bheil*),
as after a preposition. The spoken form *am fear a tha a mhac…* uses the independent form.

### 6.6 Headless relatives: *na*, *an rud a*, *cò*, *ge b' e*

*Na* (lenites; *na dh'* before a vowel) is "all that, whatever": *na chunnaic mi* "all that I saw",
*gach nì a bha ann agus a bhiodh ann* "everything that was and that would be" (T). *Ge b' e cò* is
"whoever", *ge b' e dè* "whatever":

```
(187) Ge      b'       e      cò   a    rachadh  air  a    mhuin,  leanadh     a
      though  COP.PST  3SG.M  who  REL  go.COND  on   its  back    stick.COND  his
      chraiceann  ris  an   each.
      skin        to   the  horse
      ‘Whoever got on its back, his skin would stick to the horse. (T)’
```


## 7. Subordinate clauses

Ròdais conjunctions fall into two groups by what they take. Those built on the relative particle
*a* take the **independent** form (and the relative future): *nuair a, ma, ged a, mar a, cho luath
's a, fhad 's a, on a*. Those that end in a nasal take the **dependent** form: *gun/gum, nach,
mura, nan/nam, mus, gus an, far an, a chionn 's gun*. The clause keeps verb-first order after all
of them.

### 7.1 "That": *gun*, *gum*, *nach*, *gur*

*Gun* (*gum* before *b, f, m, p*; *gu* before *bheil*: *gu bheil*, but *gun robh*) + the dependent
form introduces a statement; *nach* is its negative. With the copula,
"that it is" is *gur* (present) and *gum b'* / *gum bu* (past), negative *nach*:

```
(188) Tha     mi   a'    smaoineachadh  gu    bheil       e      fuar.
      be.PRS  1SG  PROG  think.VN       that  be.PRS.DEP  3SG.M  cold
      ‘I think that it is cold.’

(189) Thuirt   na   h-iascairean  gum   bi          stoirm  ann       a-nochd.
      say.PST  the  fishermen     that  be.FUT.DEP  storm   in.3SG.M  tonight
      ‘The fishermen said there will be a storm tonight. (T)’

(190) Tha     eagal  orm     nach      tig           e.
      be.PRS  fear   on.1SG  that.NEG  come.FUT.DEP  3SG.M
      ‘I'm afraid he won't come.’

(191) Tha     fios       agam    gur       e      iascair    a    th'     ann.
      be.PRS  knowledge  at.1SG  that.COP  3SG.M  fisherman  REL  be.PRS  in.3SG.M
      ‘I know that he is a fisherman.’
```

The *gun* clause cannot be left out as English "that" can.

### 7.2 Conditionals: *ma*, *nan*, *mura*

Ròdais has three conjunctions for "if": **ma** for an open condition, **nan** (*nam* before *b, f,
m, p*) for an unreal one, **mura** (*mur* before a vowel) for "if not, unless" of either kind.
*Ma* takes the independent form; *nan* and *mura* the dependent. Future and habitual *ma* clauses
take the relative future.

| type | *if* clause | main clause |
|---|---|---|
| 1. open, present | *ma tha* / *mur eil* | present, future, imperative |
| 1. open, future or habitual | *ma* + relative future (*ma bhios, ma thig, ma dh'èisteas*) / *mura* + dep. future | future, imperative |
| 1. open, past | *ma bha, ma chaidh* / *mura robh, mura do* + past | any tense |
| 2. unreal, present or future | *nam biodh, nan robh* / *nan* + dep. conditional; *mura biodh* | conditional |
| 3. unreal, past | *nam biodh… air* + VN / *nan robh… air* + VN | conditional + *air* + VN (*bhiodh… air*) |

**Type 1**, open:

```
(192) Ma  tha     thu  scìth,  fuirich   an-seo.
      if  be.PRS  2SG  tired   stay.IMP  here
      ‘If you're tired, stay here.’

(193) Ma  bhios       an   t-uisce  ann,      cha  tèid        sinn  a-mach.
      if  be.REL.FUT  the  rain     in.3SG.M  NEG  go.FUT.DEP  1PL   out
      ‘If it rains, we won't go out.’

(194) Ma  dh'èisteas      tu   gu   cùramach,  cluinnidh  tu   òran.
      if  listen.REL.FUT  2SG  ADV  careful    hear.FUT   2SG  song
      ‘If you listen carefully, you will hear a song. (T)’

(195) Mura    tig           e      a-nochd,  falbhaidh  sinn  às aonais.
      if.not  come.FUT.DEP  3SG.M  tonight   leave.FUT  1PL   without.him
      ‘If he doesn't come tonight, we'll leave without him.’

(196) Ma  chaidh,  cò   a    phàigh   iad?
      if  go.PST   who  REL  pay.PST  3PL
      ‘And if they were, who paid them? (T)’
```

**Type 2**, unreal present or future. The conditional follows *nan* and is used in the main clause
too; with *bi*, *nan robh* (the old subjunctive) is as common as *nam biodh*:

```
(197) Nam  biodh        long  agam,   sheòlainn      gu  Inis  bhàn.
      if   be.COND.DEP  ship  at.1SG  sail.COND.1SG  to  Inis  bhàn
      ‘If I had a ship, I would sail to Inis bhàn.’

(198) Nan  tigeadh        e,     bhitheamaid  toilichte.
      if   come.COND.DEP  3SG.M  be.COND.1PL  glad
      ‘If he came, we would be glad.’

(199) Mura    biodh        an   stoirm,  bhiodh   iad  air  tilleadh.
      if.not  be.COND.DEP  the  storm    be.COND  3PL  PRF  return.VN
      ‘But for the storm, they would have come back.’
```

**Type 3**, unreal past. The perfect with *air* is put into the conditional in both clauses:

```
(200) Nam  biodh        tu   air  èisteachd,  bhiodh   tu   air  a    chluinntinn.
      if   be.COND.DEP  2SG  PRF  listen.VN   be.COND  2SG  PRF  its  hear.VN
      ‘If you had listened, you would have heard it.’

(201) Nan  robh        an   long  air  fuireach  sa      chala,   cha  bhiodh       i
      if   be.PST.DEP  the  ship  PRF  stay.VN   in.the  harbour  NEG  be.COND.DEP  3SG.F
      air  a    call.
      PRF  her  lose.VN
      ‘If the ship had stayed in the harbour, she would not have been lost.’
```

"If it were not for, but for" is *mura b' e* + noun: *Mura b' e an t-iascair, bhiodh i air a
bàthadh* "But for the fisherman, she would have drowned". "If it is" is *mas e* (*ma is e*), as in
*mas e do thoil e* "please" (if it is your will), and "if it were" *nam b' e*.

**Usage.** The synthetic first persons (*sheòlainn*, *bhitheamaid*) are the norm in the main
clause of a conditional sentence; speech often says *bhiodh sinn toilichte*. "Whether" is not *ma*
but the question particle (*an*, §3.4).

### 7.3 Concession: *ged a*

*Ged a* "although" takes the independent form; negative *ged nach*; with the copula *ged a b' e*,
*ged nach e*:

```
(202) Ged     a    tha     e      fuar,  thèid   sinn  a-mach.
      though  REL  be.PRS  3SG.M  cold   go.FUT  1PL   out
      ‘Although it is cold, we will go out.’

(203) Ged     nach     robh        e      beartach,  bha     e      còir.
      though  NEG.REL  be.PST.DEP  3SG.M  rich       be.PST  3SG.M  kind
      ‘Though he was not rich, he was kind.’
```

### 7.4 Purpose and "until"

Purpose with the same subject is *gus* or *airson* + VN (with the object before *a* + VN, §2.5).
After a verb of motion, *a* + lenited VN alone is enough:

```
(204) Chaidh  e      don     chala    gus  na   longan  fhaicinn.
      go.PST  3SG.M  to.the  harbour  to   the  ships   see.VN
      ‘He went to the harbour to see the ships.’

(205) Chaidh  iad  a   dh'iascach.
      go.PST  3PL  to  fish.VN
      ‘They went fishing.’
```

Purpose with a new subject is *gus an* (*gus nach*) + the dependent form, in the conditional after
a past main verb:

```
(206) Bhrùth     e      sìos  e      le    a    òrdag  gus      nach     loisceadh
      press.PST  3SG.M  down  3SG.M  with  his  thumb  so.that  NEG.REL  burn.COND.DEP
      am   bradan.
      the  salmon
      ‘He pressed it down with his thumb so that the salmon would not burn. (T)’
```

*Gus an* also means "until", with the same forms: *Fuirich gus an till mi* "Wait until I come back";
*chuir iad comhairle ri chèile gus an do shoilleirich an latha* "they took counsel together until the
day dawned" (T). Result is *cho… 's gun* "so… that" and *air chor 's gun* "so that, with the
result that": *bha e cho ciùin 's gun deach sianar dhiubh air a mhuin* "it was so gentle that six of
them got on its back" (T).

### 7.5 Time: *nuair a*, *mus*, *an dèidh do*, *fhad 's a*

*Nuair a* "when" takes the independent form, and the relative future for a future or habitual
event; negative *nuair nach*:

```
(207) Nuair  a    thàinig   na   Ròdaich  gu  na   cladaichean  sin,   fhuair   iad
      when   REL  come.PST  the  Ròdaich  to  the  shores       those  get.PST  3PL
      bailtean  falamh.
      towns     empty
      ‘When the Ròdaich came to those shores, they found empty towns. (T)’

(208) Nuair  a    thig          an   làn,  falbhaidh  an   long.
      when   REL  come.REL.FUT  the  tide  leave.FUT  the  ship
      ‘When the tide comes in, the ship will leave.’
```

*Mus* "before" takes the dependent form (*mus do* in the past); *an dèidh do* + VN is "after" (§2.5);
*cho luath 's a* "as soon as" and *fhad 's a* "while, as long as" take the independent form:

```
(209) Thalla  dhachaigh  mus     tig           an   stoirm!
      go.IMP  home       before  come.FUT.DEP  the  storm
      ‘Off home before the storm comes! (T)’

(210) Cho  luath  's   a    chunnaic  e      an   craiceann,  dh'aithnich    e      e.
      as   soon   and  REL  see.PST   3SG.M  the  skin        recognise.PST  3SG.M  3SG.M
      ‘As soon as he saw the skin, he knew it.’
```

### 7.6 Manner and place: *mar a*, *far an*

*Mar a* "as, the way that" takes the independent form; *mar gun* "as if" the dependent conditional:

```
(211) Seo   mar  a    dh'innis  na   seann  daoine  e.
      this  how  REL  tell.PST  the  old    people  3SG.M
      ‘This is how the old people told it. (T)’

(212) Bhruidhinn  e      mar  gum   biodh        e      na             rìgh.
      speak.PST   3SG.M  as   that  be.COND.DEP  3SG.M  in.3SG.M.POSS  king
      ‘He spoke as if he were a king.’
```

*Far an* (*far am* before *b, f, m, p*; *far a* before *bheil*) "where" takes the dependent form,
negative *far nach*: *far an robh an abhainn a' dòrtadh dhan mhuir* "where the river poured into
the sea" (T); *Far a bheil toil, tha dòigh* "Where there's a will there's a way".

### 7.7 Reason: *oir*, *a chionn 's gun*, *on a*

*Oir* "for" joins two main clauses and changes nothing after it. *A chionn 's gun / nach* and *air
scàth 's gun* "because" take the dependent form; *on a* "since, seeing that" the independent:

```
(213) Ghabh     iad  ris  an   ainm  le    uaill,  oir  cha  robh        fiachan  orra
      take.PST  3PL  to   the  name  with  pride   for  NEG  be.PST.DEP  debts    on.3PL
      do  dhuine.
      to  person
      ‘They took the name with pride, for they owed no debt to anyone. (T)’

(214) Canaidh   sinn  na   Seann-Dhaoine  riutha,  a chionn 's  nach  eil
      call.FUT  1PL   the  Old-People     to.3PL   because      NEG   be.PRS.DEP
      fios       aig  duine   beò.
      knowledge  at   person  living
      ‘We call them the Old Ones, because no living person knows. (T)’
```

### 7.8 Reported speech

Reported statements use *gun / nach*, reported questions the question particle or question word,
reported commands the verbal noun. After a verb of saying in the past, the tenses shift back:

| direct | reported after *thuirt e* |
|---|---|
| *Tha mi scìth.* | *gun robh e scìth* |
| *Thig mi.* | *gun tigeadh e* |
| *Chunnaic mi an long.* | *gum faca e an long* |
| *Is iascair mi.* | *gum b' iascair e* |
| *An tig thu?* | *an tigeadh i* (*dh'fhaighnich e*) |
| *Fuirich!* | *(thuirt e rium) fuireach*; negative *gun fhuireach* |

```
(215) Thuirt   e      gun   robh        e      scìth.
      say.PST  3SG.M  that  be.PST.DEP  3SG.M  tired
      ‘He said that he was tired.’

(216) Dh'fhaighnich  i      an  tigeadh        e.
      ask.PST        3SG.F  Q   come.COND.DEP  3SG.M
      ‘She asked whether he would come.’

(217) Dh'iarr  e      orm     fuireach.
      ask.PST  3SG.M  on.1SG  stay.VN
      ‘He asked me to stay.’

(218) Thuirt   e      rium    gun      a   dhol   ann.
      say.PST  3SG.M  to.1SG  without  to  go.VN  in.3SG.M
      ‘He told me not to go there.’
```

The shift is the Ròdais norm in narrative. A statement still true when it is reported may keep its
tense, as in *Thuirt na h-iascairean gum bi stoirm ann a-nochd* (T).

Direct speech is introduced by *thuirt* or, in narrative and verse, by the old quotative *arsa*
(*ars* before a vowel and the article), which stands after the quotation or inside it and needs no
tense: *"Bheir mi sin dhut," ars an guth* "'I will give you that,' said the voice" (T); *"Gabhaidh
mi ris a' chùmhnant," ars esan* (T).


## 8. The noun phrase, comparison, coordination and emphasis

### 8.1 Order in the noun phrase

The order is fixed:

    (article) (prenominal adjective) NOUN (adjective…) (demonstrative) (genitive) (aig-phrase / PP)

```
(219) an   long  bheag  dhubh  sin
      the  ship  small  black  that
      ‘that small black ship’

(220) seann  bhàrd  an   eilein
      old    poet   the  island.GEN
      ‘the old poet of the island’

(221) an   taigh  mòr  agam
      the  house  big  at.1SG
      ‘my big house’
```

Adjectives follow the noun; after a feminine singular noun they are lenited (*long bheag*), after a
masculine one they stay plain in the nominative (*bàta beag*). Only a few adjectives stand before
the noun and lenite it: *seann* "old", *deagh* "good", *droch* "bad", *fìor* "true", *prìomh*
"chief" (*seann* and the article block *d, t, s*: *seann taigh*). The demonstratives *seo* "this",
*sin* "that", *ud* "yon" follow the adjectives, and the noun keeps its article: *an cnoc ud* "yon
hill". Declension of nouns and adjectives is in *Word forms* §3–4.

### 8.2 The genitive and its article

A noun that qualifies another follows it in the genitive. The article in the genitive:

| | before a consonant | before *f* | before *s* + vowel, *sl, sn, sr* | before a vowel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| masc. sg. | *an* + len. (*a'* before *b c g m p*): *ceann a' bhàta* | *an fh-*: *deireadh an fhoghair* | *an t-*: *taigh an t-sagairt* | *an*: *mac an iascair* |
| fem. sg. | *na*: *ceann na sràide* | *na*: *doras na fàrdaich* | *na*: *solas na sùla* | *na h-*: *uisce na h-aibhne* |
| plural | *nan*, *nam* before *b f m p*: *bàtaichean nan iascairean* | *nam*: *obair nam fear* | *nan* | *nan*: *clann nan eilean* |

```
(222) mullach  a'   chnuic
      top      the  hill.GEN
      ‘the top of the hill’

(223) doras  na   h-eaglaise
      door   the  church.GEN
      ‘the door of the church’

(224) clann     nan  Tuathach
      children  the  northerners.GEN
      ‘the children of the Tuathaich (T)’
```

An indefinite masculine genitive is lenited: *pìos arain* "a piece of bread", *làn dhaoine* "full of
people" (T), *glinn àrda Ròdais* "the high glens of Rodos" (a name after a noun is genitive).

An adjective agrees with its noun in the genitive: masculine singular lenited and slenderised,
feminine singular with *-e*:

```
(225) taigh  an   duine    mhòir
      house  the  man.GEN  big.GEN
      ‘the big man's house’

(226) airson  a'   mhargaidh   mhòir
      for     the  market.GEN  big.GEN
      ‘for the big market (T)’

(227) bruaichean  na   h-Aibhne   mòire
      banks       the  river.GEN  big.GEN
      ‘the banks of the Abhainn mhòr (T)’
```

**Usage.** Settlement names do not decline: *Leabharlann Muileann chaol* "the Library of Muileann
chaol", *ann an Cathair dhearg*, *mac Thormoid à Seann Skell*. Names of rivers and natural features
built on a common noun decline like that noun (*na h-Aibhne mòire*, *air an Abhainn mhòir*).
Personal names in the genitive are lenited, and masculine ones slenderised: *Donnchadh mac
Thormoid*, *taigh Chaluim*, *Beathag nic Thòmais*.

### 8.3 One article per phrase

"The X of the Y" has one article, on the last noun. The first noun is definite because its genitive
is, and it takes no article of its own:

```
(228) doras  an   taighe
      door   the  house.GEN
      ‘the door of the house (not *an doras an taighe)’

(229) ceann  na   sràide
      end    the  street.GEN
      ‘the end of the street (T)’
```

In a chain of genitives only the last noun takes the article and the genitive form; the nouns
between keep their plain form:

```
(230) taigh  bràthair  m'  athar
      house  brother   my  father.GEN
      ‘my father's brother's house’

(231) clachan  teallaichean  nan  Seann-Dhaoine
      stones   hearths       the  Old-People.GEN
      ‘the stones of the hearths of the Old Ones’
```

A possessive counts as the article: *doras mo thaighe* "the door of my house". To say "this door of
the house", or to keep the first noun indefinite ("a door of the house"), use a preposition: *an
doras seo aig an taigh*, *doras de dhorsan an taighe*.

### 8.4 The dative after prepositions

Simple prepositions govern the dative. With the article, masculine and feminine nouns are lenited
after most prepositions (*air a' bhòrd*, *bhon a' bhaile*); feminine nouns take their slender
dative where they have one (*The grammar in brief* §3):

```
(232) air  a'   chloich
      on   the  stone.DAT
      ‘on the stone’

(233) air  an   làimh     chlì
      on   the  hand.DAT  left.DAT
      ‘on the left hand (T)’
```

*Ann an* (*ann am* before *b, f, m, p*) is "in" without the article; with it *anns an, anns a'*,
contracted *san, sa*. *Do* and *de* with the article are *don, dhan*; *bho* and *ro* are *bhon, ron*.
A feminine noun after *dà* takes the dual, which has the shape of the dative: *dà làimh*, *dà
chloich* (*Word forms* §3.6). The old dative plural in *-(a)ibh* survives only in fixed expressions and
verse: *air beulaibh* "in front of", *air cùlaibh* "behind", *fo chasaibh* "underfoot", *anns na
beanntaibh* (*Word forms* §3.8).

### 8.5 Emphasis: emphatic pronouns, suffixes and *fhèin*

Every personal pronoun has an emphatic form (*mise, thusa, esan, ise, sinne, sibhse, iadsan*), and
every possessive and prepositional pronoun takes an emphatic suffix (*-sa, -se, -san, -ne*):

| | pronoun | at | possessive + noun |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1SG | *mise* | *agamsa* | *mo thaigh-sa* |
| 2SG | *thusa* | *agadsa* | *do thaigh-sa* |
| 3SG.M | *esan* | *aigesan* | *a thaigh-san* |
| 3SG.F | *ise* | *aicese* | *a taigh-se* |
| 1PL | *sinne* | *againne* | *ar taigh-ne* |
| 2PL | *sibhse* | *agaibhse* | *ur taigh-se* |
| 3PL | *iadsan* | *acasan* | *an taigh-san* |

```
(234) Chunnaic  mise      e,     ach  chan  fhaca        thusa     e.
      see.PST   1SG.EMPH  3SG.M  but  NEG   see.PST.DEP  2SG.EMPH  3SG.M
      ‘I saw it, but you didn't.’

(235) Canaidh   sinne     na   Seann-Dhaoine  riutha.
      call.FUT  1PL.EMPH  the  Old-People     to.3PL
      ‘We call them the Old Ones. (T)’

(236) Chan     ann       dhòmhsa      a    bha     e      an  dàn.
      NEG.COP  in.3SG.M  to.1SG.EMPH  REL  be.PST  3SG.M  in  fate
      ‘It was not for me that it was destined.’
```

*Fhèin* "self, own" follows a pronoun, a noun or a possessed noun: *e fhèin* "himself", *an rìgh
fhèin* "the king himself", *a mheur fhèin* "his own finger" (T), *leis fhèin* "alone" (with
himself). After *thu* it is *thu fhèin*, the polite *sibh fhèin* "yourself" (*Ciamar a tha sibh
fhèin?*). Colloquial *fhìn* is the same word.

### 8.6 Comparison

| | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| comparative, present/future | *nas* + comparative | *nas motha* "bigger" |
| comparative, past/conditional | *na bu* (*na b'*) + len. comparative | *na bu mhotha, na b' fhaide* |
| superlative, present | *as* + comparative (relative copula) | *an taigh as motha* |
| superlative, past | *a bu* (*a b'*) + len. comparative | *an rubha a b' fhaide an iar* (T) |
| "than" | *na* | *nas motha na an cnoc ud* |
| equative | *cho* + adj. + *ri* (noun) / *'s a* (clause) | *cho socair ri caora* (T) |

```
(237) Tha     an   cnoc  seo   nas   àirde   na    an   cnoc  ud.
      be.PRS  the  hill  this  COMP  higher  than  the  hill  yon
      ‘This hill is higher than that hill.’

(238) Bha     a'   mhuir  na bu     chiùine  an-dè.
      be.PST  the  sea    COMP.PST  calmer   yesterday
      ‘The sea was calmer yesterday.’

(239) Is   e      seo   an   long  as   luaithe  air  an   eilean.
      COP  3SG.M  this  the  ship  SUP  fastest  on   the  island
      ‘This is the fastest ship on the island.’

(240) Ruith    e      dhachaigh  cho  luath  's   a    b'       urrainn  dha.
      run.PST  3SG.M  home       as   fast   and  REL  COP.PST  able     to.3SG.M
      ‘He ran home as fast as he could. (T)’
```

The **copula comparative** is the older and more forceful pattern, and the pattern of proverbs:
the copula + comparative + subject + *na*:

```
(241) Is   tighe    fuil   na    uisce.
      COP  thicker  blood  than  water
      ‘Blood is thicker than water.’

(242) Is   fheàrr  aon  ghnìomh  na    ceud     facal.
      COP  better  one  deed     than  hundred  word
      ‘One deed is better than a hundred words.’
```

"More" of a quantity is *barrachd* + genitive (*barrachd èisc* "more fish"); "less, fewer" is *nas
lugha*. *Nas motha* after a negative is "either, neither": *Chan eil mise nas motha* "Nor am I".

### 8.7 Coordination

*Agus* "and" (unstressed *is*, *'s*), *ach* "but", *no* "or"; *an dà chuid… agus* "both… and"; *an
dara cuid… no* "either… or"; *gun… no* "neither… nor" after a negative (*cha robh rìgh no
rìoghachd* "there was neither king nor kingdom", T).

*Agus* / *is* + a subject + a predicate with no verb makes an **absolute clause** of circumstance,
"(while) X was Y":

```
(243) Tha     cuid  ann       fhathast,  's   iad  a'    coimhead  air  a'   chnoc.
      be.PRS  some  in.3SG.M  still      and  3PL  PROG  look.VN   on   the  hill
      ‘There are still some, watching the hill. (T)’

(244) Chaidh  i      a-mach,  is   an   t-uisce  ann.
      go.PST  3SG.F  out      and  the  rain     in.3SG.M
      ‘She went out, though it was raining.’

(245) Thàinig   e      dhan    eilean  agus  e      na             bhalach.
      come.PST  3SG.M  to.the  island  and   3SG.M  in.3SG.M.POSS  boy
```

### 8.8 Numerals with nouns

The numeral stands before the noun (*The grammar in brief* §10):

- *aon* "one" and *dà* "two" lenite a **singular** noun (*aon* not *d, t, s*): *aon long*, *aon
  taigh*, *dà long*, *dà bhàta*. After *dà* a feminine noun takes its dative form where it has one:
  *dà làimh* "two hands", *dà chloich*.
- *trì* to *deich* take the plural: *trì coin*, *seachd longan*. Nouns of measure keep the singular:
  *trì bliadhna*, *seachd bliadhna* (T).
- *deug* follows the noun in the teens: *aon long deug* "eleven ships", *dà long dheug* "twelve".
- *fichead*, *ceud*, *mìle* and the decimal tens take the **singular**: *fichead long*, *ceud
  bliadhna*, *mìle bliadhna*. A noun counted by a decimal compound follows the whole numeral:
  *ceathrad còig long* "forty-five ships".
- In the vigesimal count, used for ages and in old texts, the noun comes before *air fhichead*:
  *aon long air fhichead* "twenty-one ships", *dà fhichead bliadhna* "forty years".

**Usage.** Ròdais counts in tens (*trithead, ceathrad, caogad…*), and joins tens and units with
nothing between them: *ceathrad còig*, not *ceathrad 's a còig*.

People are counted with the **personal numerals**, followed by the genitive plural or *de*:
*dithis* 2, *triùir* 3, *ceathrar* 4, *còignear* 5, *sianar* 6, *seachdnar* 7, *ochdnar* 8,
*naoinear* 9, *deichnear* 10:

```
(246) bha     seachdnar      chloinne      a'    cluich
      be.PST  seven.persons  children.GEN  PROG  play.VN
      ‘seven children were playing (T)’

(247) chaidh  sianar       dhiubh  air  a    mhuin
      go.PST  six.persons  of.3PL  on   its  back
      ‘six of them got on its back (T)’
```

The ordinals are *a' chiad* (lenites, but not *d, t, s*), *an dàrna*, *an treas*, *an ceathramh*,
*an còigeamh*… *an deicheamh*; *an t-aonamh… deug*; *am ficheadamh*: *a' chiad latha*, *an seachdamh
leanabh* (T), *an naoidheamh latha deug*.

### 9.1 Greetings and leave-taking

| *Madainn mhath* / *Feascar math* / *Oidhche mhath* | good morning / evening / night | greeting; *oidhche mhath* also at parting |
| *Ciamar a tha thu / sibh?* | how are you? | reply *Tha gu math, gu robh math agad* |
| *Fàilte!* / *Ceud mìle fàilte!* | welcome / a hundred thousand welcomes | to a guest, *fàilte gu Seann Dunn* (T) |
| *Mas e do thoil e* / *mas e ur toil e* | if it is your will | please |
| *Gu robh math agad / agaibh* | may good be at you | thank you (§3.7) |
| *'S e do bheatha* / *'S e ur beatha* | it is your life | you're welcome |
| *Gabh mo leisceul* / *Gabhaibh mo leisceul* | take my excuse | excuse me |
| *Beannachd leat / leibh* | a blessing with you | goodbye |
| *Mar sin leat / leibh* | the same with you | goodbye (in reply) |

| *Slàinte mhath!* | good health | a toast |
| *Meal do naidheachd!* | enjoy your news | congratulations |

**Usage.** *Dia dhut* is the only everyday "hello"; the humans' *halò* never took root on the
island.

### 9.2 *Thu* and *sibh*

*Sibh* is the plural "you" and the polite singular: to anyone older, anyone of rank, and any
stranger. *Thu* is for family, friends, children and animals, and for prayers and spirits (the
smith says *Thoir dhaibh do theas* "give them your heat" to the voice in the fire, T). Polite
*sibh* takes everything plural that agrees with the person: the verb (*thigibh*, *bithibh*), the
possessive (*ur*: *mas e ur toil e*), the prepositional pronouns (*agaibh, dhuibh, leibh*) and the
emphatic *sibh fhèin*. A predicate adjective stays singular: *A bheil sibh scìth?* "Are you
(sir) tired?". Moving from *sibh* to *thu* is an offer of friendship, and the elder makes it.

### 9.3 The vocative

A name or noun of address is preceded by the vocative particle *a*, which lenites. A masculine
noun in the singular also slenderises (like its genitive); a feminine noun is only lenited;
Ròdais writes *a* before a vowel too (*The grammar in brief* §9):

```
(248) A    Chaluim,   thig      an-seo!
      VOC  Calum.VOC  come.IMP  here
      ‘Calum, come here!’

(249) Ith      e,     a    Fhinn.
      eat.IMP  3SG.M  VOC  Fionn.VOC
      ‘Eat it, Fionn. (T)’

(250) A    mhàthair,  dè    a    tha     seo?
      VOC  mother     what  REL  be.PRS  this
      ‘Mother, what is this? (T)’

(251) Carson  a    thàinig   thu,  a    ghobha?
      why     REL  come.PST  2SG   VOC  smith
      ‘Why have you come, smith? (T)’

(252) A    chàirdean,  èistibh         rium.
      VOC  friends     listen.IMP.2PL  to.1SG
      ‘Friends, listen to me.’
```

Other vocatives: *a Mhàiri*, *a Sheonag*, *a Eilidh*; *a bhalaich* "boy", *a charaid* "friend";
plural *a dhaoine* "people", *a fheara* "men" (the old vocative plural), *a dhaoine uaisle*
"gentlefolk". An adjective after a masculine vocative is lenited and slenderised: *a charaid
ghràdhaich* "dear friend".

### 9.4 Interjections

*Obh obh!* dismay; *Mo chreach!* "my ruin!", *Mo thruaighe!* "woe is me", *Ochòin!* "alas" (old);
*Ist!* "hush!"; *Seall!* "look!"; *Abair…!* "what a…!" (*Abair naidheachd!* "What news!", T);
*Dha-rìribh?* "really?"; *Seadh* "I see, go on"; *Thugainn!* "come on"; *Trobhad!* "come here";
*Siuthad!* "go on, go ahead"; *Mo nàire!* "for shame!". Good speech does not use the fillers
that some have taken from the humans' tongue (*uill, okay*); it says *ma-thà* "then" or *seadh*.

### 9.5 Sentence adverbs and discourse words

| adverb | meaning | adverb | meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *gu dearbh* | indeed, certainly | *gun teagamh* | without doubt |
| *is dòcha*, *dh'fhaodte* | perhaps | *co-dhiù* | anyway, at any rate |
| *ma-thà* | then, in that case | *mar sin* | so, therefore |
| *uime sin* | therefore (formal) | *a thaobh sin* | as to that |
| *fhathast* | still, yet | *a-cheana* | already |
| *a-riamh* | ever (past) | *gu bràth*, *a-chaoidh* | ever (future) |
| *idir* | at all | *dìreach* | just, exactly |
| *gu h-àraidh* | especially | *cuideachd* | also |

Sentence adverbs stand at the end of the clause or, set off by a comma, at its head: *Mu dheireadh,
ghabh am bradan biathadh a' bhàird* "At last the salmon took the poet's bait" (T).

### 9.7 Time and the calendar

**Adverbs of time**: *an-diugh* today, *an-dè* yesterday, *a-màireach* tomorrow, *a-nochd*
tonight, *a-raoir* last night, *an-uiridh* last year, *an ath-bhliadhna* next year, *a-nis* now,
*an-dràsta* just now, *a dh'aithghearr* soon, *an-còmhnaidh* always, *uaireannan* sometimes. *O
chionn* is "ago, for": *o chionn trì bliadhna* "three years ago". *Fad* + genitive is "all through":
*fad na h-oidhche*. Time adverbs close the clause (§1.1).

**The day**: *sa mhadainn* in the morning, *aig meadhan-latha* at midday, *feascar* evening, *air
an oidhche* at night, *aig meadhan-oidhche* at midnight. Hours: *Dè an uair a tha e?* "What time is
it?" — *Tha e trì uairean* "three o'clock"; *aig uair*, *aig dà uair*, *aig leth-uair an dèidh
ceithir*.

**The seasons** keep an old article: *as t-earrach* in spring, *as t-samhradh* in summer, *as
t-fhoghar* in autumn, *sa gheamhradh* in winter.

**The week** (*The grammar in brief* §11; *The Reckoning of Years* §VIII). The week came to the island
with the Mission, and its day names with it. It begins with *Didòmhnaich*, the Lord's day, the
Mission's day of rest. A day name used as an adverb needs no preposition: *Thill i Diluain* "she
came back on Monday" (T).

| | day | the name |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Didòmhnaich* | the Lord's day, from the Mission's church-tongue |
| 2 | *Diluain* | the moon's day, from the church-tongue |
| 3 | *Dimàirt* | the day of the humans' old war-god, from the church-tongue |
| 4 | *Diciadain* | the day of the first fast |
| 5 | *Diardaoin* | the day between the fasts |
| 6 | *Dihaoine* | the day of the fast |
| 7 | *Disathairne* | the day of another old god of the humans, from the church-tongue |

**The months** (*The Reckoning of Years* §VII). The civil year runs from *am Faoilleach*. Before the
Crossing the Ròdaich year began at *Samhain* and was reckoned by the moon, and the Old Faith still
reckons it so. A month takes the article; "in" is *anns an* (*san, sa* + lenition), and a day "of"
a month is *den* + lenition:

| | month | "in" | "the Nth of" |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *am Faoilleach* | *san Fhaoilleach* | *den Fhaoilleach* |
| 2 | *an Gearran* | *sa Ghearran* | *den Ghearran* |
| 3 | *am Màrt* | *sa Mhàrt* | *den Mhàrt* |
| 4 | *an Giblean* | *sa Ghiblean* | *den Ghiblean* |
| 5 | *an Cèitean* | *sa Chèitean* | *den Chèitean* |
| 6 | *an t-Ògmhios* | *san Ògmhios* | *den Ògmhios* |
| 7 | *an t-Iuchar* | *san Iuchar* | *den Iuchar* |
| 8 | *an Lùnastal* | *san Lùnastal* | *den Lùnastal* |
| 9 | *an t-Sultain* | *san t-Sultain* | *den t-Sultain* |
| 10 | *an Dàmhair* | *san Dàmhair* | *den Dàmhair* |
| 11 | *an t-Samhain* | *san t-Samhain* | *den t-Samhain* |
| 12 | *an Dùbhlachd* | *san Dùbhlachd* | *den Dùbhlachd* |

The old moon-months (*The Reckoning of Years* §IV) are *Samhnas, Dubhlas, Faolach, Gearras,
Sìolach, Bearnach, Bealltach, Ògach, Teasach, Lùghnas, Pailteach* and *Damhrach*, with the
between-moon *Eadrach*. They are names and take no article: *ann an Samhnas* "in Samhnas", *a'
chiad oidhche de Ghearras* "the first night of Gearras". The old reckoning counts nights, not days,
and halves the moon: *an treas oidhche den leth gheal* "the third night of the bright half".

The quarter-days are feasts on the first of their civil month: *Là Fhèill Brìghde* (1 an Gearran),
*Bealltainn* (1 an Cèitean), *Lùnastal* (1 an Lùnastal), *Samhain* (1 an t-Samhain). The Old Faith
keeps them on the first night of their moons. "At Samhain" is *aig an t-Samhain* or *air Samhain*.

**Dates and years.** Years are counted by eras, *linntean*, one to each of the six ages, each from
its age's first event (*The Reckoning of Years* §I). The Library writes a date as day, month, era
and year: *19 an t-Ògmhios, DE 1*. It is read with an ordinal, *den*, *bliadhna* and the era's
name in the genitive:

```
(273) air  an   naoidheamh  latha  deug  den     Ògmhios,  sa      chiad  bhliadhna
      on   the  nineteenth  day    ten   of.the  Ògmhios   in.the  first  year
      de  Linn  an   Dubhain
      of  era   the  Dubhan.GEN
      ‘on 19 an t-Ògmhios, DE 1’

(274) anns  a'   bhliadhna  fichead  seachd  de  Linn  an   Dubhain
      in    the  year       twenty   seven   of  era   the  Dubhan.GEN
      ‘in the year DE 27’

(275) trì    mìle      bliadhna  ron         Diosal
      three  thousand  year      before.the  Diosal
      ‘three thousand years before the Diosal’
```

The last is the old count, the years of the Diosal, *bliadhnaichean an Diosail* (*The Reckoning of
Years* §III), which ran from the sunwise turn at Seann Skell and counted everything older backwards.
It was the island's count until the eras were adopted in the eighteenth year of the Severance Era,
and older writings are dated by it: *anns a' bhliadhna mìle naoi ceud ceathrad còig den Diosal* "in
the year 1945 of the Diosal", which was SE 16 (§10.3).
