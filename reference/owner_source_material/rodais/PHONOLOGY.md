# Dia-thìris — phonology

This chapter describes the sounds of Dia-thìris, the tongue of Dia-thìr, and the rules that bind them to
the spelling. *The grammar in brief* §1 has a summary on one page; here is the full account.

## 0. Conventions, and the age of the sounds

**Dia-thìris and its record.** Dia-thìris is the tongue of the Dia-thìrich, spoken on Dia-thìr since the Ancient
Age. Its history is the island's own: the oldest Dia-thìris words in stone are the lines cut
on the standing stones, the first letters came from the stag-teachers of Muileann chiar, and its
books were kept by scribes and, in the kingdom, by the Library at Muileann chaol. The spelling was
settled early and has changed little since, while the sounds beneath it have moved a little, as
sounds will. So the letters often remember an older sound than the one now spoken, and this chapter
says where they do. The spelling has two marks that set it apart at a glance: *sc* for the cluster
/sk/, and the grave accent as its only accent mark (§11).

**An old-sounding tongue.** Dia-thìris is conservative in its sounds. Where a sound might have fallen
together with another, or worn away, Dia-thìris nearly always keeps the older: it holds apart what a
looser speech would merge, and it keeps consonants that a quicker speech would slur. These points are
noted where they arise; §12 gathers them at a glance, and §13 tells what the spelling shows of the
older sounds of the tongue.

**Transcription.** Transcriptions are broad and phonemic, in the phonetic letters used throughout
this book, between square brackets: [ˈpaːʰt̪ə] for *bàta*. The symbols are:

- `ˈ` stress, written before each stressed word. Unstressed proclitics (*an, a', mo, gu, cha*)
  carry no mark.
- `ː` length. `ʰ` after a stop is aspiration, and before a stop it is pre-aspiration: [pʰ], [ʰp].
- `ʲ` palatalised (slender). `ˠ` velarised (broad), written only on the tense sonorants [l̪ˠ n̪ˠ r̪ˠ],
  where it tells them apart from the lax ones.
- `̪` dental: [t̪ d̪ n̪ˠ l̪ˠ]. `̃` nasal vowel. `.` a syllable break inside a word (hiatus).
- `|` minor intonation break (comma, colon), `‖` sentence end.

Lenis stops are written [p t̪ k]. They are voiceless and unaspirated, and a reader of English will
hear them as *b d g*.

## 1. The two contrasts that organise everything

Two contrasts run through the whole consonant system. They explain most of what is unfamiliar in
the spelling.

1. **Broad and slender.** Almost every consonant comes in two forms. A *broad* consonant is
   velarised: the back of the tongue rises towards the soft palate, which gives an "a/o/u"
   colouring. A *slender* consonant is palatalised: the front of the tongue rises towards the hard
   palate, which gives an "i/e" colouring. The spelling marks the difference with the vowels
   beside the consonant (*caol le caol, leathan le leathan*, §10.1). So *e, i* next to a consonant
   are often not pronounced as vowels at all. They are markers of consonant quality.
2. **Fortis and lenis.** Stops come in a strong (*fortis*) and a weak (*lenis*) series. Fortis
   *p t c* are aspirated at the start of a word and pre-aspirated after a vowel. Lenis *b d g* are
   plain, voiceless and unaspirated. Sonorants have a parallel contrast between *tense* and *lax*:
   *nn, ll, rr* and word-initial *n l r* are tense, and single *n l r* elsewhere are lax.

Both contrasts go back to one old opposition in the tongue, between "strong" consonants (doubled,
or at the start of a word) and "weak" ones (lenited, single, or between vowels), and they still fall
where that opposition put them (§13).

vowel, *b* in *beag* [ˈpek] and in *bàta* is the same sound.

### 2.3 Stops: fortis and lenis

In the older tongue *b d g* were voiced and *p t c* voiceless, and the letters were fixed in that
day. Since then the difference has become one of breath rather than voice:

- **Fortis *p t c*.** Aspirated at the start of a word: *pòg* [ˈpʰɔːk], *tì* [ˈtʃʰiː], *cù*
  [ˈkʰuː]. Pre-aspirated after a vowel (§2.4). Plain after a consonant: *slàinte* [ˈsl̪ˠaːɲtʃə],
  and plain after *s*: *sc, sp, st* are [sk, sp, st̪].
- **Lenis *b d g*.** Voiceless and unaspirated in all positions: *beag* [ˈpek], *bradan*
  [ˈpɾat̪an], *gorm* [ˈkɔɾɔm]. After a nasal they are voiced (§9.3).
- **Slender *t, d*** are affricates, [tʃʰ] and [tʃ]: *teine* [ˈtʃʰenʲə], *Dia* [ˈtʃiə]. Slender
  *c, g* are palatal stops [kʲʰ kʲ]: *ceud* [ˈkʲʰiət̪], *gille* [ˈkʲiʎə].
- **Broad *t, d*** are dental, with the tongue against the teeth: [t̪ʰ t̪].

### 2.4 Pre-aspiration

After a vowel, fortis *p t c* are preceded by a short puff of breath, [ʰ]: *bàta* [ˈpaːʰt̪ə],
*cat* [ˈkʰaʰt̪], *mac* [ˈmaʰk], *cnoc* [ˈkʰn̪ˠɔʰk], *boc* [ˈpɔʰk]. The *c* of *sc* is
never pre-aspirated (*èisc* [ˈeːʃkʲ]). It is this breath that keeps *bàta* "boat" apart from a
*bàda*, if there were such a word. It is strongest after a stressed vowel, but is heard after
unstressed ones too (*chunnaic* [ˈxun̪ˠɪʰkʲ]). After *l n r* the sonorant is made voiceless instead
(*olc*, *cearc*). The transcription leaves this unmarked, since it follows of itself.

**The weight of the breath.** Dia-thìris has a light [ʰ] before all three stops. It is never thickened
into a full rasp at the back of the mouth, so *mac* is [ˈmaʰk], not [ˈmaxk], and the difference of
fortis and lenis is always plain to hear.

### 2.5 Fricatives and h

- **[f]** is written *f* and *ph*. **[v]** is written *bh* and *mh* (§9.1). *fh* is silent.
- **[s] / [ʃ]** is written *s*, broad or slender: *sàl* [ˈsaːl̪ˠ], *sìth* [ˈʃiː]. Word-initial *s*
  before *c, p, m* is always [s], even when slender: *scian* [ˈskʲiən], *sceul* [ˈskʲiəl̪ˠ].
- **[x] / [ç]** is written *ch*: *loch* [ˈl̪ˠɔx], *deich* [ˈtʃeç], *chì* [ˈçiː].
- **[ɣ] / [j]** is written *dh, gh* at the start of a word: *dhà* [ˈɣaː], *ghabh* [ˈɣav],
  *dhèan* [ˈjiən]. Elsewhere these letters are usually silent (§10.4).
- **[h]** is written *th* and *sh* (lenited *t, s*), and *h-* before vowels: *tha* [ˈha],
  *sheas* [ˈhɛs], *na h-eileanan* [nə ˈhelʲanan].

### 2.6 Sonorants: tense and lax

The older tongue had four of each kind, four nasals, four laterals and four rhotics: tense and lax,
each broad and slender. Dia-thìris has kept all four nasals, and three each of the laterals and the
rhotics.

| | broad tense | broad lax | slender tense | slender lax |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **nasals** | [n̪ˠ] *nochd, ceann* | [n] *bean, òran* | [ɲ] *nighean, sinn* | [nʲ] *duine, sin* |
| **laterals** | [l̪ˠ] *loch, balla* | [l̪ˠ] *cala, balach* | [ʎ] *leabhar, gille* | [lʲ] *baile, scoil* |
| **rhotics** | [r̪ˠ] *ruadh, barr* | [ɾ] *mòr, fear* | [r̪ˠ] *rìgh* | [ɾʲ] *muir, athair* |

- A sonorant is **tense** when it is double (*nn, ll, rr*), at the start of a word (unless
  lenited), or, for *n*, before a homorganic stop (*slàinte* [ˈsl̪ˠaːɲtʃə], *cainnt*). Otherwise
  it is **lax**.
- **Lenition of *l n r*** is not written, but it is heard: after *mo, do, glè, ro*, the tense
  initial becomes lax. So *leabaidh* [ˈʎɛpi] becomes *mo leabaidh* [mə ˈlʲɛpi], and *nead*
  [ˈɲɛt̪] becomes *mo nead* [mə ˈnʲɛt̪].
- The tense sonorants lengthen or diphthongise a short vowel before them (§4.2).

**The four nasals kept.** Dia-thìris holds lax slender [nʲ] apart from tense slender [ɲ] in every
place: inside a word, where a careless mouth would say [ˈt̪ɯɲə] for *duine*, and at the end, where
it would say a plain [n]. So *duine* is [ˈt̪ɯnʲə], *sin* [ˈʃinʲ], *Diluain* [tʃiˈl̪ˠuənʲ]; the old
system of four nasals stands whole. The laterals are three, [l̪ˠ ʎ lʲ], for broad lax *l* has
fallen in with broad tense. Lax slender *l* is [lʲ], a clearly palatalised *l* (*baile*
[ˈpalʲə]), never a plain [l]. Slender tense *r* has fallen in with broad [r̪ˠ], and so three
rhotics are left of four.

### 2.7 Velar nasal

[ŋ] occurs in *ng* (*long* [ˈl̪ˠɔuŋk], *teanga* [ˈtʃʰɛŋə]) and before *c/g* after the article
(*an cù* [əŋ ˈɡuː]). Final *ng* after a stressed vowel has a stop release, [ŋk]. It is not an
independent phoneme.

### 3.3 Nasal vowels

Nasal vowels are phonemic only where a lost *mh* has left its nasality on the vowel: *samhradh*
[ˈsãũɾəɣ], *dhomh* [ˈɣɔ̃ː], *fhathast* [ˈhã.əst̪], *Didòmhnaich* [tʃiˈt̪ɔ̃ːnɪç]. Vowels next to
*m, n* and after *mh* are lightly nasalised as well (*mhàthair* is [ṽãːhɪɾʲ] phonetically), but
this is automatic and is not written in the phonemic transcription.

### 3.4 Unstressed vowels

- Final *-a, -e* are [ə]: *bàta* [ˈpaːʰt̪ə], *duine* [ˈt̪ɯnʲə].
- *ai, i, ei, ui* in unstressed syllables are [ɪ]: *athair* [ˈahɪɾʲ], *Dia-thìris* [ˈtʃiəˈhiːɾʲɪʃ],
  *madainn* [ˈmat̪ɪɲ].
- *a, ea* before *n*, and in final *-ag/-eag*, keep [a]: *òran* [ˈɔːɾan], *eilean* [ˈelʲan],
  *caileag* [ˈkʰalʲak].
- Final *-aidh, -idh* are [i]: *òlaidh* [ˈɔːl̪ˠi], *Eilidh* [ˈelʲi].
- Everything else is [ə]: *balach* [ˈpal̪ˠəx], *rathad* [ˈr̪ˠa.ət̪], *fichead* [ˈfiçət̪].

### 4.2 Vowels before tense sonorants

A short stressed vowel lengthens or diphthongises when it stands before a tense sonorant (*ll,
nn, ng*, final *m*, or *rr*) that closes the syllable, that is, when no vowel follows in the same
word:

| vowel | before *ll nn ng m* | before *rr* | examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| a, ea | au | aː | *ceann* [ˈkʲʰaun̪ˠ], *gleann*, *barr* [ˈpaːr̪ˠ] |
| o | ɔu | ɔː | *long* [ˈl̪ˠɔuŋk], *donn*, *trom* |
| i | iː | iː | *sinn* [ˈʃiːɲ], *rinn* [ˈr̪ˠiːɲ], *cill* |
| io | uː | | *fionn* [ˈfʲuːn̪ˠ] |
| u | uː | uː | *Dunn* [ˈt̪uːn̪ˠ] |
| ai | ai | | *cainnt* [ˈkʰaiɲtʃ] |

When a vowel follows, the vowel stays short: *bainne* [ˈpaɲə], *gille* [ˈkʲiʎə], *balla*
[ˈpal̪ˠə].

### 4.3 Other vowel allophony

- *ea* is [e] before *c, g* (*beag* [ˈpek], *eaglais* [ˈekl̪ˠɪʃ]), [a] before *l* (*geal*
  [ˈkʲal̪ˠ]), and [ɛ] elsewhere (*fear, bean, dearg, each*).
- *ui* is [ɯ] before *n, s, g, c* (*duine, uisce, tuig*) and [u] elsewhere (*fuil* [ˈfulʲ], *muir*
  [ˈmuɾʲ], *cuir*).
- Stressed *a* before *dh/gh* is raised to [ɤ]: *taigh* [ˈt̪ʰɤj], *saighdear*.

## 5. Syllable structure and phonotactics

The maximal syllable is **CCCVVCCC**, but most syllables are CV, CVC or CVV.

- **Onsets.** A single consonant, or a cluster of up to three: *s* + stop (*sc, sp, st*), *s* +
  stop + *r/l* (*scr-, spr-, str-*), stop or *f* + *r/l* (*br, cr, dr, fr, gr, tr, bl, cl, fl,
  gl*), stop + *n* (*cn, gn, tn*), *mn*, *sl, sn, sr*. In a cluster before *r*, the first
  consonant is broad even when the cluster is slender: *trì* [ˈt̪ʰɾʲiː], *cridhe* [ˈkʰɾʲi.ə].
- **Codas.** A single consonant, a sonorant plus obstruent (*rd, rt, lt, nt, ns, rs, ls*), *sc,
  st*, *chd*. A sonorant followed by a labial or velar sets off the helping vowel (§6), so
  **lb, rg, nm** and the like never close a syllable in speech.
- **Quality harmony.** A consonant cluster is broad or slender as a whole: *uisce* [ˈɯʃkʲə] has
  slender [ʃ] and slender [kʲ]. Word-initial *s* before *c, p, m* is the one exception: it is
  always [s].
- **Vowel sequences.** Two full vowels never stand side by side inside a syllable. Where the
  spelling puts a silent consonant between two vowels, they belong to two syllables (hiatus, §7).
- **No [h] before a consonant**, except the pre-aspiration of fortis stops.
- **Word-final** lax sonorants are common (*fear, sin, baile*). Final *dh, gh* are silent after
  a front vowel and [ɣ] only after a broad one (*ruadh*, *-adh*).

## 6. Epenthesis (the helping vowel)

Between a lax sonorant (*l, n, r*) and a following *b, bh, g, ch, m, mh*, Dia-thìris puts in a vowel
that the spelling does not show, the *helping vowel*. It is an echo of the stressed vowel before
it:

| written | spoken | pattern |
|---|---|---|
| *albam* | [ˈal̪ˠapəm] | l + b |
| *falbh* | [ˈfal̪ˠav] | l + bh |
| *tilg* | [ˈtʃʰilʲikʲ] | l + g |
| *dearg* | [ˈtʃɛɾɛk] | r + g |
| *gorm* | [ˈkɔɾɔm] | r + m |
| *marbh* | [ˈmaɾav] | r + bh |
| *dorcha* | [ˈt̪ɔɾɔxə] | r + ch |
| *oirbh* | [ˈɔɾʲɔv] | r + bh |
| *ainm* | [ˈanʲam] | n + m |
| *Tormod* | [ˈt̪ʰɔɾɔmət̪] | r + m |

Rules:

1. The vowel before the cluster must be **stressed and short**. A long vowel or a diphthong
   blocks it.
2. The sonorant must be **lax**. *ll, nn, rr* never take it.
3. Coronal stops and *s* do not trigger it: *àrd* [ˈaːɾt̪], *ceart* [ˈkʲʰɛɾt̪], *olc*, *falt*.
4. The helping vowel **does not count as a syllable** for stress or verse. *Dearg* is a
   monosyllable with a long, broken vowel, and in the bards' metre it was counted as one syllable
   (§13). The stress stays on the first vowel, and the helping vowel carries the same pitch.

## 7. Hiatus

When a consonant between two vowels is silent (*dh, gh*, some *th* and *bh*), the two vowels are
pronounced in separate syllables. There is a slight break, which the transcription marks with a
dot, and no glide:

| written | spoken |
|---|---|
| *latha* | [ˈl̪ˠa.ə] |
| *rathad* | [ˈr̪ˠa.ət̪] |
| *piuthar* | [ˈpʲʰu.əɾ] |
| *nighean* | [ˈɲi.an] |
| *cridhe* | [ˈkʰɾʲi.ə] |
| *tighinn* | [ˈtʃʰi.ɪɲ] |
| *abhainn* | [ˈa.ɪɲ] |
| *leabhar* | [ˈʎɔ.əɾ] |

A disyllable in hiatus behaves as one stressed unit: *latha* has a falling pitch across both
vowels, much like a long vowel. *Th* between vowels is [h] before a slender vowel and before
*-an* (*athair* [ˈahɪɾʲ], *bothan* [ˈpɔhan]), and silent otherwise.

Across word boundaries, a final vowel before an initial vowel is often elided in speech and
sometimes in writing: *m' athair*, *d' athair*, *dh'òl*.

## 8. Stress and intonation

**Stress** falls on the first syllable of the word, the first vowel group in the spelling. Long
vowels occur freely in unstressed syllables of compounds, but short vowels there reduce (§3.4).

Exceptions:

- **Adverbs formed with a particle** stress the second element: *a-màireach* [əˈmaːɾʲəx],
  *an-diugh* [ən̪ˠˈtʃu], *an-dè* [ən̪ˠˈtʃeː], *a-nochd* [əˈn̪ˠɔxk], *a-mach* [əˈmax], *ma-thà*
  [məˈhaː].
- **Weekday names** stress the name, not *Di-*: *Diluain* [tʃiˈl̪ˠuənʲ], *Dimàirt*.
- **A few loans and names** from other tongues keep their own stress. Dia-thìris has almost none.

**Proclitics** have no stress: the article (*an, am, a', na, nan, nam*), the possessives (*mo, do,
a, ar, ur*), the verbal particles (*cha, chan, an, nach, gu, gun, do*), the copula *is*, and the
simple prepositions *ri, le, de, fo, mu, ro*.

**Hyphenated compounds** stress both parts, the second more strongly in a noun + genitive
compound: *taigh-òsta* [ˈt̪ʰɤjˈɔːst̪ə], *each-uisce* [ˈɛxˈɯʃkʲə].

**Phrase stress** falls on the last stressed word of a phrase. In a noun + adjective phrase that
is the adjective: *Cathair dhearg* [ˈkʰahɪɾʲ ˈjɛɾɛk].

**Intonation.** The tunes of Dia-thìris are these:

- Statements fall on the last stressed syllable: *Tha e fuar.*
- Questions introduced by a particle (*an, am, nach*) also fall. The particle marks the question,
  so the tune does not have to.
- Questions with a question word (*cò, dè, càite, ciamar*) fall from a high start on the question
  word.
- Rising or level endings mark continuation (a list, a clause before *ach, agus*) and a polite,
  tentative question.
- Dia-thìris has no tone belonging to the word itself. Hiatus words and helping-vowel words are given
  no special contour of their own: they keep a single rise-fall, as a long syllable does.

## 9. The sounds of the mutations

### 9.1 Lenition

Lenition turns a stop into the corresponding fricative, or weakens a fricative to [h] or to
nothing. Written *h* after the consonant marks it:

| plain | broad | slender | lenited | broad | slender | example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| p | pʰ | pʲʰ | ph | f | fʲ | *do phiuthar* [t̪ə ˈfʲu.əɾ] |
| b | p | pʲ | bh | v | vʲ | *a' bhean* [ə ˈvɛn] |
| m | m | mʲ | mh | v (nasalising) | vʲ | *mo mhàthair* [mə ˈvaːhɪɾʲ] |
| f | f | fʲ | fh | ∅ | ∅ | *an fhìrinn* [ən̪ˠ ˈiːɾʲɪɲ] |
| t | t̪ʰ | tʃʰ | th | h | h | *thuirt* [ˈhuɾʲtʃ] |
| d | t̪ | tʃ | dh | ɣ | j | *dhà* [ˈɣaː], *dhearg* [ˈjɛɾɛk] |
| c | kʰ | kʲʰ | ch | x | ç | *mo cheann* [mə ˈçaun̪ˠ] |
| g | k | kʲ | gh | ɣ | j | *ghabh* [ˈɣav] |
| s | s | ʃ | sh | h | h | *sheas* [ˈhɛs] |
| l | l̪ˠ | ʎ | (l) | l̪ˠ | lʲ | *mo leabaidh* [mə ˈlʲɛpi] |
| n | n̪ˠ | ɲ | (n) | n | nʲ | *mo nead* [mə ˈnʲɛt̪] |
| r | r̪ˠ | r̪ˠ | (r) | ɾ | ɾʲ | *mo rùn* [mə ˈɾuːn] |

Lenited *mh* and *bh* are the same [v], but *mh* nasalises the vowels next to it. That is audible
in *mhàthair* and decisive where *mh* is lost (§3.3).

Lenited *fh* is silent, so *fh*-words behave as vowel-initial: *chan fhaca* (*chan* as before a
vowel), *dh'fhàg* [ˈɣaːk]. Three very common words have an irregular [h] instead: *fhuair*
[ˈhuəɾʲ], *fhèin* [ˈheːnʲ], *fhathast* [ˈhã.əst̪].

*sc, sp, st, sm* do not lenite, in writing or in speech. The *s* keeps its [s].

### 9.2 The prefixed t-, h-, n-

- **t-** (after the article *an*): before a vowel it is fortis [t̪ʰ]/[tʃʰ], taking its quality
  from the vowel: *an t-uisce* [ən̪ˠ ˈt̪ʰɯʃkʲə], *an t-iascair* [ən̪ˠ ˈtʃʰiəskɪɾʲ]. Before *s*
  (*sl, sn, sr*, *s* + vowel) the *s* goes silent and the *t* is lenis: *an t-sràid*
  [ən̪ˠ ˈt̪ɾaːtʃ], *an t-sagart* [ən̪ˠ ˈt̪akəɾt̪].
- **h-** is [h] before the vowel: *na h-eileanan* [nə ˈhelʲanan], *a h-athair* [ə ˈhahɪɾʲ].
- **n-** (after *ar, ur*) is the tense nasal of the vowel's quality: *ar n-athair*
  [əɾ ˈn̪ˠahɪɾʲ], *ur n-eilean* [əɾ ˈɲelʲan].

### 9.3 Nasalisation (eclipsis) after the nasal-final article

Dia-thìris spelling does not write the old nasal mutation, called eclipsis, except by the final *-n* or
*-m* of the word that causes it; but it is still heard. It works as follows.

**After *an, am* (singular article, the question particle, the possessive "their"):**

- A fortis stop becomes a voiced unaspirated stop, and the *n* of the article takes its place of
  articulation: *an cù* [əŋ ˈɡuː], *an taigh* [ən̪ˠ ˈd̪ɤj], *am poca* [əm ˈbɔʰkə].
- A lenis stop stays voiceless, and the *n* assimilates: *an gille* [əŋ ˈkʲiʎə], *am bàta*
  [əm ˈpaːʰt̪ə].

**After *nan, nam* (genitive plural), the full old eclipsis:**

- A fortis stop is voiced, as above: *nan coin* [nəŋ ˈɡɔnʲ], *nan cat* [nəŋ ˈɡaʰt̪].
- A lenis stop becomes the nasal itself, and the article loses its final nasal:
  *nam bàtaichean* [nə ˈmaːʰt̪ɪçan], *nan daoine* [nə ˈn̪ˠɯːnʲə], *nan gillean*
  [nə ˈɲiʎan].
- *f* becomes [v]: *nam fear* [nə ˈvɛɾ].

**Where the mutation is strongest.** Dia-thìris neither voices the stop everywhere and keeps it a stop
(so that *nam bàta* would be [nəm ˈbaːʰt̪ə]), nor puts the nasal in its place everywhere. It has the
full nasal replacement in the genitive plural, and the lighter voicing after the singular article.
The genitive plural article is the oldest home of eclipsis, where the old final *-n* stood from the
beginning; after the singular article the mutation came later, by likeness. So the nasal mutation
in Dia-thìris is strongest exactly where it is oldest.

### 9.4 Lenition blocks in sound

The "dental block" (*The grammar in brief* §2) has a phonetic reason. After the *n* of *an,
seann, aon*, a dental *d, t, s* would lenite to [ɣ, h, h] right after a dental nasal. The language keeps the
stop instead: *seann duine* [ˈʃaun̪ˠ ˈt̪ɯnʲə], *an taigh* [ən̪ˠ ˈd̪ɤj].

## 10. From spelling to sound

### 10.1 Reading the quality of a consonant

1. Look at the vowel letter **after** the consonant (its first letter): *e, i, è, ì* make it
   slender, *a, o, u, à, ò, ù* make it broad.
2. At the end of a word, look at the vowel letter **before** it (its last letter): the *i* of
   *-ai-, -oi-, -ui-* makes it slender.
3. A whole consonant cluster shares one quality.
4. Letters used only as markers are not pronounced as vowels. The *e* of *ea, eo, eu* after a
   consonant shows that the consonant is slender. The *i* of *ai, oi, ui* before a consonant shows
   that the following consonant is slender. The *a* of *ea, ia* before a consonant shows that it is
   broad.

## 11. What is particular to Dia-thìris

1. **sc is [sk], always unaspirated.** Dia-thìris writes the cluster *sc*, the letter the scribes have
   used for it from the first, and says it with a plain, unbreathed stop: *uisce* [ˈɯʃkʲə], *scoil*
   [ˈskɔlʲ], *iasc* [ˈiəsk], *èisc* [ˈeːʃkʲ]. The *c* after *s* is never aspirated or
   pre-aspirated. A reader must not take *sc* for a fortis *c*.
2. **The grave accent marks length, and only length.** *à è ì ò ù* are the long vowels. There is
   no acute accent: *mòr, sceul*. The grave accent does not show the quality of the vowel, so *ò*
   covers both [ɔː] and [oː] and *è* both [eː] and the [iə] of *dèan*. (For *è*, [iə] occurs only
   in *èa*.)
3. **Old letters, present values.** The spelling keeps letters made for older sounds (the *sc*
   above; see also *Names*), but every letter is read with the value it has in speech now: *th* is
   [h], *dh/gh* are [ɣ]/[j], pre-aspiration is present, *ao* is [ɯː].
4. **The names of the Old Ones** (*Skell, Dunn, Chwen, Bral*), which are older than Dia-thìris and
   come from the tongue of the Seann-Dhaoine, are read with Dia-thìris values where they can be:
   *Seann Skell* [ˈʃaun̪ˠ ˈskʲeʎ], *Seann Dunn* [ˈʃaun̪ˠ ˈt̪uːn̪ˠ], *Seann Chwen*
   [ˈʃaun̪ˠ ˈçwenʲ]. *k* and *w* keep their foreign values [k] and [w], and by these the hearer
   knows the names for strangers.

## 13. What the spelling remembers

The spelling of Dia-thìris was settled long ago, and it has kept the shape of sounds that speech has
since changed. Read with care, it tells something of what the tongue was like in its older days.

**Lenition.** In the oldest Dia-thìris the lenited consonants were true fricatives, and the letters were
made for them: *th* [θ], *dh* [ð], *gh* [ɣ], *ch* [x], *bh* [β], *mh* [β̃], *fh* [ɸ] or nothing,
*sh* [h]. In time *th* became [h], *dh* and *gh* fell together in [ɣ]/[j], and *bh, mh* became
[v, ṽ]; and these are the values Dia-thìris has now. The spelling *dh* for a sound that is the same as
*gh* is the memory of the old [ð]. So is final *-adh*, whose [əɣ] was once [əð].

**Voice and aspiration.** In the older tongue *b d g* were voiced and *p t c* voiceless. The voiced
stops lost their voice, and the voiceless ones gained breath, which after a vowel moved in front of
the stop as pre-aspiration. The contrast is as old as the tongue; only its sound has changed. The
pre-aspiration of Dia-thìris is light, near to a plain aspirated stop.

**Tense and lax sonorants.** The older tongue set doubled *ll, nn, rr* (and initial *l, n, r*)
against single ones, and with broad and slender this gave four of each, twelve in all, on which the
bards' metre depended. Dia-thìris has four nasals, three laterals and three rhotics. The diphthongs
before tense sonorants (*ceann* [ˈkʲʰaun̪ˠ], *long*) are a later growth. They are the one place
where the tense sonorants have changed the vowel before them instead of being lost.

**Eclipsis.** The older tongue nasalised the first sound of a word after words that had once ended
in *-n*, and the genitive plural article first among them. The spelling ceased to write it, but the
sound lived on. Dia-thìris has the full nasal replacement in the oldest home of eclipsis, the genitive
plural (*nam bàtaichean* [nə ˈmaːʰt̪ɪçan]).

**Long vowels.** *à, ò, ù, ì* are the old long vowels, with their old sounds. The old long *e* has
become the diphthong [iə] (*ceud, sceul*), and the old *ae* has become [ɯː] (*gaol, craobh*). The
diphthongs *ia, ua* are old. The grave accent marks the same long vowels it has always marked.

**The helping vowel.** The bards did not count the helping vowel as a syllable. A line with *dearg*
in it scanned as if the word were one syllable shorter, and the teachers of verse held it so from
early days. Dia-thìris treats the helping vowel in the same way (§6, rule 4).

**sc.** The cluster has been written *sc* from the earliest writing (*sceul, uisce, scoil*). In
speech the stop has since become lenis; Dia-thìris keeps the old letter and says the newer sound.

**cn.** *Cnoc, gnè, mnathan* had [kn, gn, mn] of old, and have them still. Dia-thìris has not let the
*n* go.

## 15. Worked transcriptions

The transcriptions are broad and phonemic, as everywhere in this chapter. Some things vary from
word to word in Dia-thìris, as in any living tongue, and are not predicted by the rules of §2–§10: the exact
vowel of *ea* in some words, silent versus sounded *bh* and *th* between vowels in old words,
secondary stress in long compounds, and elision in connected speech beyond the written
apostrophes. The [oː] words of §3.1 are a closed list, and so are the frequent words whose sound
the spelling does not show (*math, fhuair, fhèin, mòr, còig, thu, robh, abhainn, leabhar,
Dòmhnall, dhomh, aig, ciamar*, the weekdays).

### 15.2 Sentences from *Texts*

**1.** *Dia dhuibh! Ciamar a tha sibh?* "Hello! How are you?"

    ˈtʃiə ˈɣuj ‖ ˈkʲʰimɛɾ ə ˈha ˈʃiv

*Ciamar* is irregular ([ˈkʲʰimɛɾ], from *cia mar*), and *dhuibh* is [ɣuj] with its *bh* silent.
The particle *a* is unstressed.

**2.** *Tha gu math, gu robh math agaibh.* "Well, thank you."

    ˈha kə ˈma | kə ˈr̪ˠɔ ˈma ˈakɪv

*Math* and *robh* lose their final consonant. The adverbial *gu* is a proclitic [kə].

**3.** *Tha mi air tighinn à Cathair dhearg air a' bhàta.* "I have come from Cathair dhearg on
the boat."

    ˈha ˈmi ˈɛɾʲ ˈtʃʰi.ɪɲ ˈaː ˈkʰahɪɾʲ ˈjɛɾɛk ˈɛɾʲ ə ˈvaːʰt̪ə

This sentence shows hiatus in *tighinn*, slender *dh* = [j] and the helping vowel in *dhearg*,
lenition after the feminine-pattern dative article (*a' bhàta*), and pre-aspiration in *bhàta*.

**4.** *Madainn mhath. Tha mi a' sireadh arain agus èisc.* "Good morning. I'm looking for bread
and fish."

    ˈmat̪ɪɲ ˈva ‖ ˈha ˈmi ə ˈʃiɾʲəɣ ˈaɾɪnʲ ˈakəs ˈeːʃkʲ

*Mhath* is [va]. The verbal noun ending *-eadh* keeps its [ɣ]. *Èisc*, the genitive of *iasc*, has
slender, unaspirated *sc* [ʃkʲ].

**5.** *Cha tèid m' athair a-mach air a' mhuir a-nochd, ma-thà.* "My father won't go out on the
sea tonight, then."

    xa ˈtʃʰeːtʃ ˈmahɪɾʲ əˈmax ˈɛɾʲ ə ˈvuɾʲ əˈn̪ˠɔxk | məˈhaː

*Cha* does not lenite *t* (the dental block), so *tèid* keeps its aspirated [tʃʰ]. *M' athair*
is one phonological word. The adverbs *a-mach, a-nochd, ma-thà* are stressed on their second
element.

**6.** *Tha gaol agam oirbh, ach buinidh mi don mhuir.* "I love you, but I belong to the sea."

    ˈha ˈkɯːl̪ˠ ˈakəm ˈɔɾʲɔv | ˈax ˈpɯnʲi ˈmi t̪ən̪ˠ ˈvuɾʲ

*Oirbh* has the helping vowel [ɔɾʲɔv]. *Buinidh* shows *ui* = [ɯ] before *n* and final *-idh* =
[i].
