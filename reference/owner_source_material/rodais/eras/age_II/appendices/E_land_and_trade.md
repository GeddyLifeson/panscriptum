# Land and Trade: the Ford, the Roads and the Weather

Dia-thìr in An Aois Ailein was an island of households, not of towns. At the grove there was one hill with a bank, one hill with a palisade by the lake, Seann-Dhaoine' empty harbours on every coast and the hearths of Na Glinn Àrda; by the close of the age there were banked places in the east, the north and the midlands, harbours on every coast, and a gathering at the ford that drew households from all of them. This appendix sets out the ford and its grounds, the roads that ran to it, the goods that went along them, the fires kept for the boats, and the weather, water and ground the age had to live with.

## Cruinneachadh an Àtha

The ford lies fourteen miles below An Cnoc Ruadh, where the lake-folk's salt and the hill's wool first met. In the thousand one hundred and seventy-fifth year Raghnall Ceannfhionn found his grandfather's salt-kists near empty, rode west with nine companions so that the company would not be taken for a raid, and waded into the ford to meet the holder of An Cnoc Ruadh in the water. They agreed salt for wool. Both hills were the king's, and neither was the other's; the king would not do their talking for them. The bargain held on nothing written, only on each side's wish to be trusted the year after: when An Cnoc Ruadh refused a thin, unwashed clip, Raghnall sent a better one the next spring with fleece besides, and neither hill pressed harder than a fair measure again.

Traders with no part in either household began to bring flint, dried fish and pots on the appointed days and set their wares apart, and no one afterward remembered deciding to let them stay. In the twelve hundred and eighth year the meeting became a yearly gathering, and the first marriage of the ford was made on the trodden ground with both banks' households standing for it. No meeting-place on the island was used longer without a break. By the twelve hundred and sixtieth year the children who ran between the stalls made no distinction between the hill's side of the water and An Cnoc Ruadh's.

The gathering had three grounds: the salt people downstream, the hill people upstream, and the flint people on the rise beyond the river. By the three thousand four hundred and fiftieth year it drew households from every coast. It was at the ford that the lines were first recited before a marriage, that Flòraidh laid her amber bead in the old ground among the ribbons and worked bones other travellers had left there, that Anndra Breac was shouted down, that the saying was first spoken at the opening, that An Dà Chlàr were heard whole in one night, and that Catrìona gave the households of Dùn dhearg ground of their own on the far bank.

In the three thousandth year since the grove the gathering was kept greater than any before it. More households came in than the three grounds were ever laid out to hold, until by the second morning no one could say which bank belonged to whom. The king came down the hill road dressed as any herdsman and the crowd opened a way for him without a word called; five holders waded out to put their hands between his; the rings of his blood went into the river, Somhairle at his left hand, Murchadh a head taller than most, Eithne laughing in the outer ring; and the reciters counted some four hundred of his blood standing in the water. No household from east of the vein came. When the gathering had gone back to its fires the king stood alone in the emptied ford at dusk, and a girl of An Cnoc Ruadh found the water warm as bath-water round his legs and saw it smoke downstream of him.

## The roads

**Rathad an t-Salainn** ran from the pans of Baile ghorm by the ford to An Cnoc Ruadh and on to the north-west. Murchadh Gearr walked it every autumn to be seen upon it, and every household on it had seen An Cnoc Ruadh's holder pass in the flesh. In the two thousand four hundred and sixty-fourth year carriers cut through its last unbroken thicket, and Muileann àrsaidh stood a day nearer the pans; at the far end the salt cost three times its price at the pans and was paid for in flint and seal-skins. Eilidh Ghlas's younger son walked it west at six years old, fifty miles and more from Dùn ìseal to An Cnoc Ruadh, past a low green mound his guide would go no nearer to, and walked it east again twenty-four years later with a train of laden pack-beasts.

**Rathad na Spor** ran from the pit at Cnoc àrsaidh in the north-west south and east toward Cruinneachadh an Àtha, worn by generations of carriers with yokes on their shoulders until, by the eighteen hundred and seventeenth year, it sank a full yard below the ground on either side and a stranger took it for a dry ditch dug on purpose. Carriers caught by storm dug into its banks and roofed themselves with their yokes. Cattle were walked up it to Cnoc ghorm in the north-west. Murchadh walked it for a hundred and thirty years and asked his father for the country it ran through, and was refused.

**The drovers' roads** of Na Glinn Àrda ran by fords like Àth gharbh, where the beasts were rested and the full hand began, and by Àth shean, the old crossing below Dùn ìseal. The coast road ran round the east and south, and carried grain to Cuan ruadh after A' Ghaoth Dhubh.

## The goods

**Salt** from An Cnoc Ruadh's pans on the lake shore and from the clay basins at Baile ghorm, raked from the crust without breaking it, went to every hearth that could pay in wool, fish or flint. **Wool** came down from the hill and from the sheep-households of Baile gharbh. **Flint** from the one good pit on the island went to hearths from one end of it to the other in grey flakes, and the first flake of every new working was given free to whoever asked. **Pots** of coiled clay, fired in open bonfires, were first made at Doire shean in the seven hundred and fifth year, and within a few lifetimes the whole southern shore used them. **Amber** came out of the western wrack and was drilled with flint points by a few hands on the bay of hawks. **Cloth** was woven on standing frames with fired-clay weights at Muileann leathan from the sixteen hundred and twenty-eighth year, and Beitris taught the standing weights to the hill's women. **Light** came first from seal-oil lamps of hollowed stone at Caol leathan. **Salmon** came from the wattle fence at Caol fhiadhaich, **seal-skins** from the rocks of Inis bhàn and the east, **feathers** from the cliffs of Inis mhin, **boar** from Baile fhiadhaich, and **green axes** from the quarry at Àth àrsaidh, too fine for work, until the quarry was forsaken. The one thing no one traded was the vein's coal, which went by the handful to every hearth and was sold by none.

## The boats

The oldest boat on Dia-thìr known in its whole shape is a dug-out hollowed from a single oak trunk, long enough for six, sunk at a landing on the Abhainn uaine in the seven hundred and sixteenth year. The river-folk who came down to Seann Skell built small hide boats and trusted them to open tide for the first time in the six hundred and fortieth year. The first households of the west coast fished from the shore and not from boats. Stitched planks from hulls built for open water, like nothing built on any coast of the island, washed up south of Seann Skell in the two thousand four hundred and twenty-first year, and no household claimed them. Peigi Dhubh laid a landing of laid stone below An Cnoc Ruadh so a boat could come alongside at any state of the water, and the fishing households of Caol bheag named their boats for the winds.

## The five fires

After A' Ghaoth Dhubh a young woman of Cuan ruadh, Lìleas, kept a fire on the cape east of the village for boats coming home in the dark, in a stone-walled pit, driving flame and smoke seaward. Word of it travelled the coast roads faster than its light, and within two years there were fires on the northern point, on the north-eastern cape where Seathan mac Pheadair cut a stroke for every ship's light he saw pass safe, and on the southern cape on a mound of stacked stone breast-high. With the older fire on the Baile chrom headland, the coasts of Dia-thìr were lit at five points, and a reciter named them the five fires. They were lit to bring boats home, the tellings say, and not to warn them off.

## Weather, water and ground

**Storms.** The first storm Clàr nan Rìghrean named came in the hundred and forty-seventh year; its name is lost, only the memory of the naming remains. It stripped Dùn ìseal's thatch and broke Seann-Dhaoine' shell causeway at Seann Warr in three places. A' Ghaoth Dhubh of the two thousand four hundred and seventeenth year stripped every roof between Cuan ruadh and Ceann chiar, and it is the eldest storm whose name is kept. Cuan ruadh rebuilt in stone to the eaves, and Ceann chiar cut a mark for every house lost: twenty-two.

**Waves.** In the seven hundred and seventy-seventh year the sea came far inland over the village at Cnoc chaol and left a sheet of sand a hand thick on its hearths, with the shells of deep-water creatures in it; the village was not rebuilt on the same ground. When the sea came in again in the three thousand eight hundred and seventeenth year, less far, the new houses stood higher on the slope and none was lost.

**The crack.** In the eight hundred and twenty-seventh year a crack a man could fall into opened across a valley two days inland and moved its spring a hundred paces downhill. The king walked its length and forbade nothing; Seasaidh laid white stones a stride back from its edge, and no house was built within a stone's throw of it for the rest of the age. It moved again in the three thousand eight hundred and sixty-third year and broke the line in two places, and the line was laid again.

**Hunger and drought.** Two failed fishing seasons emptied Baile chrom in the nineteen hundred and sixty-sixth year, the oldest hunger on Dia-thìr tied to a place. The springs of Achadh shean ran dry forty years later and the field banks were forsaken. The rivers of the eastern plain ran low for a whole summer in the three thousand seven hundred and seventy-first year, and the cattle of Dùn ìseal and Muileann bhàn were driven to Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh to drink. Each time the store within the bank and the sharing held.

**The lake and the moor.** The marks on the Ros dhomhain rock crept upward from keeper to keeper until Loch chrom stood a hand higher than at the first cut. In the north, about Doire ghlas, heather, birch and bog-moss spread over the oak ground in the three thousand five hundred and eighty-eighth year, and the groves that gave the north its many doire-names drew back into valleys the wind could not reach.

**The fords.** A spate on the Abhainn chaol took a party of twelve crossing with salt in the three thousand six hundred and eightieth year, and none crossed there in the wet season after. Stepping-stones were laid across the Abhainn fhionn at its shallows a stone at a time until a man might cross dry-shod in every season but true flood: the first made crossing of any river on the island.
