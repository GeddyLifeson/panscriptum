# The King, Rìghrean na Cloiche and An Cnoc Ruadh

An Aois Ailein had one king and many holders. Ailean Mòr mac Shuibhne was crowned in the grove at Doire ghlas in the first year and ruled the whole island until he fell at the ford below Dùn dhearg in the last days of the age, four thousand six hundred and three years later. Under him the hill of Dùn ìseal was held in his name by a line of his own blood that the reciters called Rìghrean na Cloiche, and An Cnoc Ruadh by the lake was held by a line that was not his blood at all and swore to him and not to Dùn ìseal. An Dà Chlàr were spoken, never written, in this age. They give each holder a name and a place in the order and say almost nothing of how long each held; where the years are known they are given here from the tellings, with each ruler's age at the taking of the holding and at death.

## The king

**Ailean Mòr mac Shuibhne**, Rìgh Chlann nan Dè. He was twenty-four when he kept the seven nights in the grove and was crowned on the eighth, and four thousand six hundred and twenty-six when he fell. He did not age after the grove: his hair went grey in a single winter in about his seventeen-hundredth year of kingship and changed no further. The king married several times; the tellings name Muireall, the mother of Gormshuil, who died in the twenty-fourth year of the hill; Sorcha, a woman of the valleys, the mother of Somhairle and Iain Dubh; and Beitris of Muileann leathan, the weaver, the mother of Murchadh. He named every wife before Beitris to her in order when she asked whether he would forget her. He heard judgements in person in the first years, gave the north to Dòmhnall Clachach, walked south alone to Tràigh an t-Salainn after Iain Dubh was killed there, and in the last age of his reign took back Dùn ìseal into his own hand when Catrìona Mhòr died, giving it to none of her kin.

## Rìghrean na Cloiche

Rìghrean na Cloiche, counts the king himself first and Catrìona Mhòr twelfth and last. Each holder after the king held Dùn ìseal under him, heard its judgements, called its gatherings and kept its bank. The list passes from one name to the next without saying how long each held or what came between.

Years are counted from the grove.

**Gormshuil nic Ailein**, the king's eldest daughter by Muireall, was born before the grove and had none of na Bliadhnachan Fada. She kept the gate of the hill from the ninth year and held it from her thirty-second year, and died at eighty-one beside a father who looked her grandson's age. It was at her grave that the hill understood na Bliadhnachan Fada were the grove's alone.

**Dòmhnall Clachach**, the first child born to the king after the grove, took the hill at seventy-four and set the boundary stones of the valleys in the hundred and seventh year. At two hundred and twenty-five he asked his father for unsettled ground and was given the north, and he grew old there among people who called him only the stone-cutter's son. He died at three thousand three hundred and eight.

**Fearchar Bacach**, lamed in Cogaidhean nan Gleann, took the hill at two hundred and seventeen when his brother rode north, and was carried to the gatherings in a chair of woven birch: the first ruler on Dia-thìr remembered for a hurt. He carried his lameness near three thousand years and died at three thousand one hundred and thirty.

**Mòrag Chruaidh**, Dòmhnall's daughter, the first of the king's grandchildren to carry na Bliadhnachan Fada, raided Tràigh an t-Salainn at Baile ghorm in her three hundred and eighty-second year and lost seven, whose names the hill kept at its fire. She rode to An Cnoc Ruadh, weighed Seonag's pebble bag against her own spears, and raised no raid again. She gave the hill to Iain Dubh at four hundred and twenty-nine and died at one thousand three hundred and twenty.

**Iain Dubh**, a son of the king by Sorcha, took the hill at six hundred and seventy-four and held it eight years. He went down to the salt-pans at dawn with more spears than Mòrag had taken and was killed on the clay by Tadhg Garbh, at six hundred and eighty-two. His son Tormod stood at his cairn.

**Raghnall Ceannfhionn**, Fearchar's son, found the hill's salt-kists near empty in his four hundred and fifteenth year and waded into the ford to meet the holder of An Cnoc Ruadh, and the two hills agreed salt for wool. He died at one thousand four hundred.

**Sìleas Bhàn**, of the king's blood, held the hill through the hungry years, opened the store within the bank by the count of mouths, and was told by the king that four hundred years of not failing was itself the deed the list would keep. She gave the hill to Tormod Liath at five hundred and ten and died at eight hundred and eighty among her kin in the valley.

**Tormod Liath**, Iain Dubh's son, took the hill at one thousand three hundred and seventy, raised the second bank, outlived three sons, went grey through, and died in his own hall in the three hundred and eightieth year of his holding, at one thousand seven hundred and fifty.

**Eilidh Ghlas**, of the king's blood, took the hill at three hundred and forty. She sent her younger son to be fostered at An Cnoc Ruadh, the first fostering in either list, and knew him at the door when he came home. She died at seven hundred and thirty.

**Calum Ciar**, Eilidh's elder son, asked in his hundred and sixty-second year that the keeper's lot be drawn at Dùn ìseal in his sight, and was refused; the list sets the refusal down. He died at seven hundred and ten.

**Catrìona Mhòr**, of the king's blood, gave the households of Dùn dhearg ground at the ford at two hundred and twenty-one, took the hill formally at two hundred and forty-seven, knew every household of the hills by its line seven names back, and had the households counted on a cord of nine hundred knots. She answered the nine keepers "not this year; ask me again", and died at two hundred and ninety-one. With her the spoken list ends.

## The list of An Cnoc Ruadh

An Cnoc Ruadh kept its own holders and its own list, An Cnoc Ruadh's roll, and swore to the king from the two hundred and ninetieth year and never to Dùn ìseal. None of its holders had na Bliadhnachan Fada. The list sets every holder of Uisdean Mòr's line "after her father" or "after his father", whatever lay between.

**Beathag Ruadh** held the hill by the lake when the valleys came in to the king, and stayed away. She was fifty-one when the tellings first name her and died at seventy-three. **Uisdean Mòr** took the hill at twenty-five, cut the ditch across the neck, saw the palisade burn twice, and knelt to the king in the ford at sixty-five; he died at eighty-seven. **Seonag nic Uisdein** counted her people by pebbles in a bag and was twenty-four when Mòrag Chruaidh came to her; she died at seventy-one. **Murchadh Gearr** took the hill at twenty-five and walked Rathad an t-Salainn every autumn to be seen upon it; he died at sixty-five. **Lachlann Ruadh** took Eilidh Ghlas's younger son to foster at forty-one and was called Lachlann an Altraim for it; he died at sixty-five, asking that the fostering be ended. **Peigi Dhubh** took the hill at forty, sent the fosterling home with salt, and laid the stone landing; she died at seventy-five. **Seumas Òg** took the hill at twenty-seven and died at seventy; his is the last name An Cnoc Ruadh's spoken roll carries. Between the holders the list names, others held whose names it does not keep, among them the unnamed holder who waded to meet Raghnall Ceannfhionn and the new holder who stood in the river at An Cruinneachadh Mòr of the three thousandth year.

Murchadh Mòr had eleven sons, born over the better part of three centuries, each given a corner of the northern plain; the four hundred of the king's blood reckoned standing in the river at An Cruinneachadh Mòr are not named one by one in any telling that survives.

## Sloinneadh a' Chlachair and Somhairle's line

Sloinneadh a' Chlachair, runs from Neachdan the mason to Ailean, and was given whole by Coinneach mac Shuibhne, the king's mortal brother and the first teller of his deeds, to Gormshuil before he died in the fifty-fifth year. Somhairle carried it after, among Clann na Ceiste in Na Glinn Àrda, and gave it whole to his son Beathan in the eleven hundred and fortieth year and to Diorbhail, a child of the watch at Tobar dhìreach, in the three thousand and tenth; Diorbhail kept it by mouth and took no share for it. Somhairle's own line held no share of the hill. It runs from Somhairle to Beathan, and from Beathan to the son who cleared the path for Somhairle's stick and kept the whole watch at the slab after him; the tellings name his descendants only as they come to Gille-Brìde, whose father died in his three hundred and eighth year, and to Gille-Brìde's son Ceallach, born in the four thousand five hundred and forty-third year, in whom the years were already coming down faster than in his father.

## Luchd-faire na Lice

The families near the crack sat the slab in turn, a household at a time, from the eleven hundred and twentieth year. From the two thousand four hundred and third year they drew one keeper by lot. **Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill**, a herder's daughter of three-and-thirty, drew the white stone first and kept the slab until she died in the winter after Coinneachadh nan Cupan. **Gilleasbuig Beag**, a roof-mender, drew after her and was rowed to Eilean chrom for a basket of coal. **Giorsal Bhàn** drew after him and laid the coal back under the slab. The tellings do not name the keepers after Giorsal one by one. In Catrìona Mhòr's years the lot was drawn from every part of the hills, and the keepers were made nine, one for each of nine districts no household ever marked on the ground.

## The holders of Dùn dhearg as the east names them

Rìghrean na Cloiche' list does not name the holders of Dùn dhearg; it says only that Dùn dhearg came not to the gatherings. The households east of the vein name two. **Fearghal Donn**, who held the double bank when the king sent Niall Òg in the four thousand five hundred and twentieth year, sent the messenger back with nothing and died an old man in his own bed in the four thousand five hundred and seventieth. **Artair mac Fhearghail**, his son, took up the holding with no more ceremony than his father had used and led Sluagh an Ear down to the ford below the fort in the last days of an Lùnastal of the four thousand six hundred and third year.
