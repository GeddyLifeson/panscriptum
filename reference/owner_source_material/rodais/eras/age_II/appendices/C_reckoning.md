# Reckoning: Years from the Grove, na Bliadhnachan Fada and the Turning Year

An Aois Ailein counted its years from the grove. Every telling of the age that gives a year gives it as a count from the night Ailean was crowned at Doire ghlas: the ninth year of the hill, the thousand and sixty-sixth year, the three thousandth year since the grove. The count needed no other beginning, for no one alive in the age remembered one, and the king, who did, never offered another. This appendix sets out how the age counted its years, its lists and its lives; how it divided the turning year; and the marks it cut to keep time on the coasts.

## The count from the grove

The reckoning was the king's own. In the first years the hill counted by the years of the hill, which were the grove's years under another name, for the king came to Dùn ìseal in the summer of his crowning; within a generation the reciters spoke only of the grove. The reciters of Dùn ìseal kept the count by mouth, and the households learned it from them at the gatherings. It was an unbroken count, never begun again at the taking of a holding: Sìleas Bhàn held the hill in the nineteen hundred and twenty-seventh year, not in her first. A thousandth year was marked as a thing worth travelling for. In the three thousandth year since the grove, word of the count was carried out along every road that summer, and it drew to the ford households who had never before troubled to send anyone to an ordinary gathering.

Ordinary households kept their own years less exactly. They counted by holders, by storms and by marriages: the winter A' Ghaoth Dhubh came, the year the second bank was dug, the summer the foster-son came home. Most tellings of the age give a year only for what touched the hill, and leave the rest to the order of events.

## Lists without lengths

The lists of the age are orders of names, not counts of years. Rìghrean na Cloiche gives twelve names from Ailean to Catrìona Mhòr; the list of An Cnoc Ruadh gives its own holders from Beathag Ruadh to Seumas Òg, and sets every holder of Uisdean Mòr's line "after his father" or "after her father" though Seonag was born more than three hundred years after Uisdean died. Neither list says how long any holder held, or what lay between one name and the next. The reciters held that the list keeps who and in what order, and that the length of a holding is the business of those who lived under it. When An Dà Chlàr were spoken one after the other at the ford in Catrìona's time, from dusk to the grey before morning, it was the first time either was heard whole, and the tellings count the hours of the night and not the years of the holders.

The households' own lines were orders of the same kind. At the ford, before any marriage was made, each household gave its line aloud seven names back on either side, so that a forbidden blood might be named before the salt was carried. Catrìona Mhòr could give any household's line back seven names without pausing to think.

## Na Bliadhnachan Fada

The power of the coals was Ailean's alone, and he did not age after the grove. His children born after it had na Bliadhnachan Fada, and every generation after them had fewer: the longest life in a generation was never longer than the longest in the one before, and no child of the kin outlived its long-lived parent's span. Those born before the grove, like Gormshuil, and those not of the king's blood, like every holder of An Cnoc Ruadh, had ordinary lives. The long-lived aged slowly and then, near the end, all at once: Tormod Liath went grey through in his hundred and seventy-fifth year of holding, and Somhairle found the first lines in his face in his two thousand four hundred and thirty-fifth year, and came in his last years to look, a woman of the watch-households said, like a withered apple left too long on the sill.

The kin reckoned their own ages in the ordinary way until the counting grew too long to be worth saying aloud. Ìomhar Dearg, near five hundred at his nephew's birth, had stopped troubling to count them; Somhairle had stopped counting in the ordinary way long before, as Clann na Ceiste counted, by Samhains sat and not by years. Ceallach, born to Gille-Brìde in the four thousand five hundred and forty-third year, was slower to walk and to run than the children before him, the first sign, told back afterward, that the years were coming down faster in him than in his father.

## The turning year

The year of the age was a year of two halves and Na Ceithir Fèilltean. The dark half began at Samhain, when the herds came down and the slab's watch began; the light half began at Bealltainn, when they went up. Between them lay the feast of the lambing in the cold and Lùnastal at the harvest. Samhain was the night the hill kept for its dead, when Mòrag's seven were named at the fire, and in Catrìona's years it was the night on which every household burned its handful of an gual dathach together, watching for the first low flame on the nearest hill. The gatherings of the valleys were held at the feasts, and Cruinneachadh an Àtha in the autumn after the shearing and the salt-raking. The months of the reckoning, from am Faoilleach to an Dùbhlachd, were used by the reciters of the hill for the days of the great deeds, and by few others.

The panthers kept a season of their own. Twice a year the great cats crossed the passes by the same road, and every household near the road knew the crossing season and kept its herds out of the passes while it lasted. At Baile dhomhain the households cut a sign on their gateposts at the start of the crossing season and scraped it away at its end, the first mark on the island cut for a season. When three cats turned back at the highest pass in the two thousand four hundred and twenty-eighth year, the households read it for a hard winter and stored double, and the winter was hard.

## Na h-Ochd Gaothan

In the two thousand and forty-seventh year Na h-Ochd Gaothan of the year were given names all at once, by no one the coasts could afterward name, and every coast that told them used the same eight names in the same order from that year on. Their meaning was dark even to those who spoke them, and they were never changed. The brothers of Ceann leathan raised a ring of eight stones for them on the point; the people of Eilean fhada cut the names as notches into the rock of their highest ground, one facing each way, so a man lost in thick weather might find his bearing by touch; a cutter of Seann Chwen set one of the names beside Seann-Dhaoine' own marks on the great boulder there, because the name wanted a stone already old; and the fishers of Caol bheag named each boat for the wind it was built to fish in.

## The tide-rocks

In the sixteen hundred and seventy-first year an unnamed hand on an unnamed shore began to cut a mark on a fixed rock at each high tide's furthest reach, at intervals more even than any fisherman's need would ask. A young keeper at Ros dhomhain began a rock of his own in the seventeen hundred and eighth year at the spacing he judged by memory of the telling, and the households of Inis thais cut a third in the two thousand four hundred and twenty-fourth, at the Ros dhomhain interval, though none of them could say why that width and no other. Seònaid Mhòr of Ros dhomhain cut her marks in pairs, one for high water and one for low; the keepers after her went back to single marks. On the Ros dhomhain rock the marks crept upward from keeper to keeper, so slowly that no one year's mark could be told from the next, until the lake stood a hand higher on the stone than at the first cut. The keepers went on cutting. The king told Beitris, standing before the rock, that a thing can be worth doing before anyone has worked out why.

## Tallies and counts

The age counted things more than it counted time. Seann-Dhaoine cut single strokes on flat stones at the edges of their middens, then counted their dead in fives, until their tally stones thickened together on three coasts and stopped. Seonag of An Cnoc Ruadh counted her people by pebbles in a bag, one to a household. The seal-rock households of Inis bhàn kept their turns on notched sticks. The keeper of the tide-rock at Ros dhomhain cut a stroke for every tide, and Seathan mac Pheadair a stroke for every ship's light he saw pass safe by his fire. The greatest count of the age was Catrìona Mhòr's cord, a knot to a hearth, which grew to some nine hundred knots; Sìne nan Snaidhm kept its like for the whole valley of tents below Dùn ìseal in the last years, and the king's own kin were never upon it.
