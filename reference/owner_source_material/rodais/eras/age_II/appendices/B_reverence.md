# The Reverence of the Hearths

An Aois Ailein had no priests, no temple and no god at the hearth. What it had was a set of customs about fire, coal, the dead and certain places, kept by households who could seldom say where a custom had begun and never troubled to write one down. This appendix gathers them as the tellings of the age give them: Aithris a' Chruthachaidh kept among Clann na Ceiste; the slab and those who watched it; the handful, the basket, the cup and Oidhche na Glaice; the sharing before the storms; the saying spoken at the ford; the unlit and the covered hearths; the holy woods; and the keeping of the dead.

## No god at the hearth

The hill-folk of this age made no offering at the hearth and named no one who lived in the fire. Coimhdeach na Fine, belonged to the grove and to Sloinneadh a' Chlachair, and the tellings put him in only two places: before Diarmad of that line in the oldest days, and in the grove on the eighth night, when he named Ailean king. No household of the age claimed to have seen him, and none set a place for him. No one prayed to the king himself. He was knelt to at gates and in fords, as a holder is knelt to, and he raised those who knelt with his own hand; the hill talked of his warmth and his unageing face in the way a valley talks of weather, and let the matter rest.

## The king's warmth

The one wonder the age took for granted was the king himself. A girl of Dùn ìseal stood near his untended fire in the first hard winter and was warmed through though no flame burned close enough to account for it; Coinneach found him warm at dawn after the vigil with the frost standing off him in a bare ring; the water of the ford ran warm round his legs at An Cruinneachadh Mòr and smoked downstream of him in the dusk. At Achadh dhìreach he laid his open hand across a hearthstone running with colour and it greyed under his palm. At An Cnoc Ruadh, in the summer Uisdean knelt, a frost came down out of a clear sky in an t-Ògmhios and put a white rime on the grass along the whole lake shore; the tellings call it Reothadh an Rìgh, and Uisdean's people could not say afterward whether it was his doing or the sky's. The hill spoke of these things in the way a valley speaks of weather, and made no rite of any of them. No one asked him for rain or a good calving. The hill asked him for judgements, and for grain in a hungry year, and he gave both.

## Aithris a' Chruthachaidh among Clann na Ceiste

The households of Na Glinn Àrda, Clann na Ceiste, kept Sloinneadh a' Chlachair and the tellings that ring it. Coinneach mac Shuibhne gave the Line whole to Gormshuil; Somhairle, who asked his father for no land and was counted a single mouth at a single hearth, carried it among Na Glinn Àrda for near three thousand years, gave it to Beathan and then to Diorbhail, and set in it what he saw on the three shores of Seann-Dhaoine. The keepers of the Line took no share from any hall for the keeping, and set none of it down on leaf or hide. It was said twice through without a name out of place when it was given, and after that each Samhain to any of the watch-households who would hear it, with the year's names added at its foot. Clann na Ceiste held that a thing given too soon to too young a head is given badly, and that a question the ground does not mean to answer is best let lie.

## The slab and its keepers

At Tobar dhìreach a slab lay over the mason's crack, stained where Teine a' Chlachair was said to have come up out of the vein. In the thousand and seventy-first year a crack opened in the turf below it and the households laid white stones along it. From the eleven hundred and twentieth year the families nearest the crack sat by the slab in turn, a household at a time, through the dark half of every year. What they watched for none ever said; Gilleonan, after forty years of sitting it, asked Beathan, and Beathan answered that he did not know and never had. The king sat one night among them and said nothing of why he came.

In the two thousand four hundred and third year the turns wore too thin for one memory to hold and the families agreed to choose one keeper by lot, from a bag of pebbles with one marked white. The first keeper had a house raised beside the slab with its door facing the stone, the first building on Dia-thìr made only to be near the vein. When a keeper was found with a basket of the vein's coal, the families judged him by a silence held past any voice rising to answer it and rowed him to Eilean chrom without a boat; the basket was set inside Taigh na Lice under its cloth and was neither burned nor kept, and the keeper after him laid the coal back under the slab piece by piece. The coal went home, the valleys said. Calum Ciar asked that the lot be drawn at Dùn ìseal in his sight and was refused, and the king would not overrule a thing older than the grove of his own crowning. From then the holders of the hill ruled the grazing and the gatherings, and the keepers ruled the vein, and the line between them was sworn to in no words either side wrote down. In Catrìona Mhòr's years the lot was drawn from every part of the hills, the keepers were made nine, and the nine asked to have their charge declared before the whole gathering. She told them to ask again.

## The handful, the cup and Oidhche na Glaice

An gual dathach of the vein was burned by the handful. The custom that no fire should have more of it than one cupped hand could hold took hold in all the hill settlements about the thousand and eleventh year, nowhere written and everywhere kept: làn na glaice, 's gun tuilleadh, a full hand and no more. The king named it a good custom at Achadh dhìreach after Eanraig Ruadh burned a whole basket in one night and his house went up in a flame of no colour anyone had a word for, and he made no law of it. Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill had a cup ground from grey stone to the size of a cupped hand so that no household's handful ran bigger than another's by the accident of whose palm measured it; its copies were tried against it with water at Coinneachadh nan Cupan, and from then the hills measured the coal with one measure. In Catrìona's years every household came to burn its handful on the same dark night, each watching for the first low flame on the nearest hill before lighting its own. One household only, Torcall Tochlair's at the black hill, refused to burn at all.

## The sharing before the storms

Any household with food to spare shared it before the storm season: this too was unwritten. At Dùn ìseal it was done in the open, every household bringing its surplus within the bank to be divided by the count of mouths and not by the size of a herd, as Sìleas Bhàn had opened the store in the hungry years. The first punishment under it that is remembered fell on a household of Àth shean that hid its grain in a pit under the byre floor; it was put out with what it could carry. After A' Ghaoth Dhubh the valleys sent grain to the east coast unasked, and the coast called the custom only the bringing-in, or the help.

## The saying

The hill-folk did not hold that they had come to Dia-thìr from anywhere. The saying cut on Calbh Cnoc ghorm, we did not come, we were here, was held at every hearth long before it was spoken aloud. When Anndra Breac told a telling at the ford in which the people crossed the sea, the crowd shouted him down and every hearth closed to him; from the next year the gathering opened with the saying spoken together before any business began. The words on Calbh Cnoc ghorm, worn shallow, were recut in their old grooves by a hand from Catrìona's hill.

## The unlit hearth and the covered hearth

At Achadh dhìreach a hearth was laid true, with a slip of an gual dathach beside it, and never lit; the valley left it under peat and ploughed round the strip it lay in. In the hills where Marbh-bheò was told of, no open fire was lit after dark. A herding family new to that country lit one and was never found; two herders who had lit none saw Marbh-bheò walk the ridge with no breath showing in the cold and lived. The covered hearth, pit-dug and lidded with stone, spread from those hills toward the settlements nearer Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, and hardened there into a rule no council set. The platforms on Loch chrom burned to the waterline in one night, and no household built on the lake again in the age.

## Holy woods, holy ground and the places of Seann-Dhaoine

Some woods were not entered. At the edge of the forest at Cnoc bheag the settlers took their timber only from the river's far side and marked the line with unhewn stones; when a child went past the stones and was not found, the searchers stopped at them, and the telling did not blame them. At the edge of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar people came singly and in pairs to sit one night by a small fire that burned out before morning, and went home quieter. Cnoc naomh was already called holy when households came to it for its spring, and they kept its water and its holiness together without asking more of either. Seann-Dhaoine' work was let stand wherever it stood: the tower at Seann Skell, Làrach an Dùin ruin above Ceann mhin, Làrach an Teampaill in the eastern hills with a path worn round it at a distance, the old landings used unmended, the marked stones of the middens laid aside, the tally stones set back as they lay.

## The dead

The hill buried its dead in the ground and raised cairns over those it meant to remember: Iain Dubh's on the hill, the salt-folk's own at Baile ghorm with seven stones for the seven of Mòrag's raid, and the slope of small cairns above the fold at Muileann chaol, one stone laid at a time by whoever had known the dead. Mòrag named her seven at the hill's fire on the night kept for the dead, and the naming went on at the same fire after her. At the old burial ground a family who buried none of their own there cleared its thorn each spring, and when diggers broke into an older grave lined with Seann-Dhaoine' slabs they closed it and moved the row a spear's length off. The households of the rough strait laid paddles beside their dead. At Tobar fhiadhaich wolves were buried whole, laid on their sides as people were. In the last centuries the households of the south-east coast began to row their dead out to Eilean ìseal, after the old manner of Seann-Dhaoine or of themselves; the tellings let the question stand.
