# Peoples: Seann-Dhaoine, the Hill-Folk and the East

Three peoples, or four, shared Dia-thìr in An Aois Ailein, and only one of them lasted the age. Seann-Dhaoine, whom the hill-folk called the Seann-Dhaoine, held the coasts when the king was crowned and were silent within six hundred years. The hill-folk, who called themselves Clann nan Dè and then Dia-thìrich, spread from the valleys under Dùn ìseal to every coast. Among them the salt-folk of the south and the lake-folk of An Cnoc Ruadh kept ways of their own long after they knelt to the king; and in the last centuries the households east of the vein grew into a people who owed the hill nothing and said so.

## The last centuries of Seann-Dhaoine

Seann-Dhaoine built harbours, towers, moles, a shell causeway at Seann Warr, fortresses whose ruins stood above Ceann mhin and Cathair mhòr, and a temple in the eastern hills. They had a script, cut on stone, that no hill-folk could read. The hill-folk traded with them at the shore by gesture, in silence kept on both sides: Dùghall Ceannaiche walked the road down to their shore for sixty-two years and never learned a word of their tongue, and his father, who had asked once, was met with a courtesy that would not be moved.

In the three hundred and eighth year Seann-Dhaoine began to cut single strokes on flat stones at the edges of their middens, one stone to a household, a mark and then another, simpler than their script. Forty years later they began to keep count of their dead. At the eastern Seann Warr the counting stones were built into a wall, some three thousand strokes in all, and the king rode east with Somhairle to see it and stood before it a long while without speaking. At Seann Tarr the stones were cut in fives and set in the floor of a house that was lived in over its own dead. About the four hundred and seventieth year the stones thickened together on three coasts at once, more strokes to each stone and more stones to each course; death came on three coasts together, and what it was the strokes do not tell. At Seann Chwen the last tally stone was left half-cut in the five hundred and tenth year, two strokes finished and a third begun and forsaken, the chisel marks stopping in mid-groove. After it no work of Seann-Dhaoine can be dated. Their middens stopped growing, their harbours silted, and their script was cut no more. Somhairle rode the three shores in one circuit and set the silence in the Line.

The hill-folk kept Seann-Dhaoine' place-names and set before them only their own word for old: Seann Dunn, Seann Chwen, Seann Skell, Seann Warr, Seann Tarr. They turned nothing into their own tongue, and the roots of those names were not understood. For the rest of the age Seann-Dhaoine' work was let stand: the hill-folk took up their empty harbours and used the moles and landings unmended, built on their middens but laid the marked stones aside, set their tally stones back as they lay, took no stone from their ruins, closed their graves when they broke into them, and walked round the temple at a distance.

## The hill-folk and the name

The people of the grove called themselves Clann nan Dè, the children of the gods, and the king Rìgh Chlann nan Dè. Most of the age they were simply the hill-folk, the households of the valleys under Dùn ìseal and of Na Glinn Àrda, who spread down every river to every coast. In the two thousand two hundred and first year the children of Dùn ìseal began, unbidden by any elder, to call themselves Dia-thìrich and their island Dia-thìr, and within a generation the old words fell out of use among the young. A boy of seven told Tormod Liath that he was Dia-thìreach and that everyone was; Tormod answered that it was a good enough name for a good enough island. With the names went the belief of the saying on Calbh Cnoc ghorm: they did not come; they were here.

Clann na Ceiste, the households of Na Glinn Àrda, kept Sloinneadh a' Chlachair and the slab, took no share of any hall, and asked their questions of the ground and not of the king. Somhairle lived among them nearly three thousand years, counted a single mouth at a single hearth, and his line held no share of the hill after him.

## Lines and the forbidden marriage

The hill-folk ordered themselves by blood long before the coal gave them cause. In the two thousand two hundred and twenty-third year the match of Ealag and Torcall Tochlair, which two households who shared a slope and a spring had treated as settled, was refused, and every elder of Ealag's household gave the same three words, that it could not be, and no reason. Ealag was married to a fisher of the ford town and lived her whole life without the reason; Torcall went east to dig for a second vein. Twenty-four years later, at Cruinneachadh an Àtha, households began to give their lines aloud before any match was spoken, seven names back on either side, so that a forbidden blood might be named before the salt was carried: the first genealogies of the common households. Only Sloinneadh a' Chlachair is older. Of the king's own kin, in the last centuries every marriage among the blood came to be reckoned for the shares it brought nearer the front of a reckoning the king never made; the hill argued without settling whether a claim through daughters weighed more or less than one through sons.

## The king's kin

By the last centuries of the age the king's blood was a people within the people. It was reckoned at some four hundred at An Cruinneachadh Mòr of the three thousandth year, and it went on growing. The kin held grazing in every part of the island by the king's giving, and the king took shares back as freely as he gave them: Ìomhar Dearg's river-grazing, Raonaid's river-meadow within the month of her burying, a household's western slopes after eleven generations. When Catrìona Mhòr died and the king held the hill in his own hand, every soul of the blood within three days' walk came to press a claim, each able to say the two names of a line whose second name was always Ailean's. From then the kin married for shares. After the four thousand six hundredth year they came down out of their holdings, household by household and line by line, and camped in the valley below Dùn ìseal, each line with its own tents, its own fire and its own steward, until the valley looked less like a gathering than a town that never raised walls. Gille-Brìde of Somhairle's line, who asked for no ground and no share, was the one soul in the valley whose name was on no cord.

## The salt-folk

The salt-folk of Baile ghorm scraped the flats of the south coast and dried the crust on the shingle, and in the six hundred and eighty-third year made shallow clay basins behind the shingle bar, the first salt-works of the island. They had no king and a hill without salt had none of theirs. Mòrag Chruaidh raided them and lost seven; Iain Dubh came down on them with more spears and was killed by Tadhg Garbh; and the king came down alone, and they raised spears and lowered them, and old Mòr knelt on the wet shingle and gave him a double handful of salt. The southern shore was his from that day, with no blood shed and none paid. The salt-folk raised a cairn of their own for Iain Dubh with seven stones in it for the seven of Mòrag's raid, having come to think of both raids as one account, and sang at the raking of the pans the four-line refrain the fosterling later brought them from An Cnoc Ruadh's lake shore, the oldest song on Dia-thìr whose words are said to survive.

## The lake-folk of An Cnoc Ruadh

An Cnoc Ruadh's people lived by salt and fish at the western end of Loch chrom, behind a palisade on the neck of their hill. They were not the king's blood and had none of na Bliadhnachan Fada. They swore to the king in the two hundred and ninetieth year when Uisdean Mòr knelt in the ford, but kept their own holders and their own list and never swore to Dùn ìseal. Salt went from their pans for the hill's wool at the ford from Raghnall Ceannfhionn's wading on, and married across the water at the ford within a generation. They settled Cala nan Taighean Cruinne, the landing of laid stone below the hill, the boat-sheds at Baile ghlas, Cnoc bheag at the forest's edge and the old landing at Ceann àrsaidh; their children, fostered or born, learned the salt-rake before the spear.

## The coasts

The households who took up the empty coasts after Seann-Dhaoine became peoples of their own by the middle of the age. The river-folk who came down the Abhainn naomh to Seann Skell had never dwelt by the water before and learned from Marsaili how a household newly of the sea buries one the sea has taken. The west-coast households of the bay of hawks fished from the shore and not from boats and drilled amber from the wrack. The east-coast households lived through A' Ghaoth Dhubh on the sharing of the valleys and kept five fires for the boats. The south-coast harbours became the busiest quays of the island. Each coast kept the eight wind-names, the handful and the sharing as the valleys did, and each thought itself the valleys' equal.

## The east

East of the vein lay country the king's blood held no hall in and claimed no grazing on. Households went out into it a generation at a time for their own reasons, among them Beasag's household from Àth shean after the hidden pit was found, and they dug no pits under their new floors. In the four thousand two hundred and seventy-fourth year the eastern households raised a double bank at Dùn dhearg, owing nothing to Dùn ìseal and asking leave of no one, and shared out the labour and afterward the ground within it by households and not by standing. They came to the ford for the first time in Catrìona's day with seal-skins, and she gave them ground on the far bank asking no oath. Ìomhar Dearg called them the short-lived; Ailis of Dùn dhearg answered that she had five days' walking behind her and no wish to spend what time she had left arguing with a man who would have five centuries to be sorry in. When the king sent Niall Òg to bid them come in, Fearghal Donn answered that his people had raised their own bank and buried their own dead without any man's leave, and would not be counted among a kin who married for shares and waited a thousand years for a hill never given. From that year the spears of the east were gathered at Dùn dhearg.
