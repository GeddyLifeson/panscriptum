# Of Dùn dhearg

*GE 4,000 – 4,324*

*This is kept at Dùn dhearg's own hearths, east of the vein, as a plain account of how the double bank came to stand, and how, once only, its people carried salt home from a fire that was not their own.*

## I. Of the Fields East of the Vein

In those centuries the eastern country filled the way water fills a shallow pan set out in rain, from a hundred small runnings that no one troubled to trace to their source. At Achadh mhòr, under the warm springs, a household cleared and banked the widest field any of that country had yet cut from the rough, and set the bank on the bones of an older wall whose builders none of them could name and none thought to ask after; the field grew wheat enough, in a good year, to feed three households where before there had been land for none. Down at the lake shore the Red Hill's own people drew their boats up at Baile ghlas in sheds of stone and turf built low against the wind, and it became, before the eldest of that first crew were dead, the harbour the Red Hill fished from oftener than its own strand. In the north, at Achadh naomh, the settlers led water round a spring in channels of their own digging rather than over it, and fenced the spring before they had raised a single house, so that a stranger coming on the place for the first time might have thought the water, and not the people, was what had been settled there.

Further east and north the coast was harder and the land behind it thinner, and the households that took it took to the sea instead. At Caol gharbh, on the rough strait, they went out in boats of skin stretched over bent wood, and buried their paddles beside their dead as other households buried a spindle or a hoe, so that a man might go into the ground with the one tool his hands had known best. Under the dark oaks of Doire chiar, a morning's walk inland, pigs ran loose in the wood all summer and were driven out to the fallen acorns each autumn by boys too young yet to be trusted with anything that could bite back, and the smoke of that place hung low and blue among the trunks from one year's end to the next. And where the Abhainn ìseal ran down past the black hill of the old trench toward the sea, a landing grew up at Caol shean that took in salt and fish and seal-skins from the coast and sent them on inland, into the keepers' country and beyond it, so that a thing caught in a net one morning off the northern skerries might lie, within the month, on a hearth three days' walk from any smell of salt water.

None of these households owed anything to the hill at Dùn ìseal, and few of them had ever seen the man who held it, though all of them had heard of him, the way a man far inland hears of the sea: as a fact rather than a sight. They were of Clann nan Dè, the godkin, as every household on the island was, sprung of the same first fires as the king's own kin, and no less sure of it for having no drop of his blood in them. They kept the handful at Samhain as every hearth on Dia-thìr had come to keep it, low and dark and burned all at once by agreement rather than by any law, and they shared before the storm season as every coast did, mouth for mouth, without a written word behind either custom. What they did not do, east of the vein, was send anyone to be counted, and no one from Dùn ìseal came east to count them.

At Caol shean, in the years before the double bank went up, two households fell out over a stretch of the river landing that each claimed to have cleared first, and there being no lot to settle it and no keeper nearer than the slab in the high valleys, three days' walk and more, the households of the landing sat down together and heard both sides out at one fire until the sky went grey, and gave the disputed ground at last to neither family outright but to the landing, to be used by whoever had a boat drawn up that day and no other claim on it. It held so ever after, and the two households that had quarrelled over it were, within two generations, so thoroughly mixed by marriage that no one at Caol shean could any longer have said where one family ended and the other began, which some of the older tellers held had been the landing's own quiet way of finishing what the quarrel had begun.

It was said among them, at fires from Caol shean to Doire chiar, that the coast beyond the vein had had rulers enough of its own, and that a ruler who did not grow old was no bargain merely for being strong. Travellers brought word west to east the way water finds its own low ground: of a hill in the north held eight hundred years by one grey woman who had outlived three heirs of her own house and would outlive a fourth before she went into the ground herself; of a hill-holder in the middle country who had wanted, before she died, some deed to set beside the holding, some paper or stone that said the land was hers past her own lifetime, and had been told, gently and finally, that the holding was the deed and there was no other; of grandmothers who governed their own great-grandmothers' grandmothers, looking, the whole while, no older than a woman of thirty summers. None of it was told as complaint exactly, for none of those tales touched Dùn dhearg's own people, who had never been ruled by any of them. It was told, rather, as a reason already half made before the reasoning began: that a bank raised by one's own hands, and held by whoever's hands raised it, answered to no list and needed none.

So it was that in the year four thousand two hundred and seventy-four of the Grove, the households east of the vein put up, without asking leave of anyone, a fort with a double bank at Dùn dhearg, on rising ground above the river, and dug its ditch deep enough that a man standing in it could not see over the rim, and set the second bank a spear's throw beyond the first so that an enemy who won the outer ditch had another to take before he had won anything at all. The Stone Kings' own list, recited at the ford, gave the fort no holder's name, for it had none that any list would keep; it said only, of that whole country, that Dùn dhearg did not come to the gatherings.

## II. Of Ailis

Ailis was born at Dùn dhearg in the year four thousand two hundred and forty, thirty-four years before the double bank went up, into a household that had already made its choice about the hill at Dùn ìseal without troubling to say so aloud. Her father cut turf for the first bank with his own hands when she was still too small to carry water to the diggers, and she grew up knowing the ditch before she knew the sea, playing along its lip with the other children of that country until an elder cuffed them back from the edge for the dozenth time and told them, not unkindly, that a fort dug to keep other men out was no fit place to break a child's neck in.

She had heard the old tales as every child east of the vein heard them, at hearths where the telling passed from a grandmother's mouth to a granddaughter's before either of them had thought to ask who had told it first: of a king who did not grow old, west beyond the vein, who had buried a wife in every hundred years of his life and never once forgotten her name; of a gathering held once, so long past that it was already a thing out of an old song rather than a thing anyone living had seen, where the king's whole kin had stood in rings about him at a river ford and the reciters had gone from dusk to sunrise naming them. Nobody at Dùn dhearg could say how many years had gone over that gathering by the time Ailis was grown, only that it was a great many, and that no one now living, east of the vein or west of it, had stood at that ford as anything but a teller's word.

Once, when she was no more than ten, a trader out of the west came through Caol shean's landing with cloth to sell that no one east of the vein had woven, and sat by the household fire that night answering the children's questions until the older folk grew tired of listening and went to their beds. He had stood, he told them, at the very gate of the hill at Dùn ìseal, and had seen with his own eyes a man come in at it who looked no older than the trader's own son, and been told by everyone about the gate that this was the same man who had held that hill since before any wall now standing on it was built. Ailis asked him, before her mother could hush her, whether it did not frighten the man's own children to grow old beside a father who did not, and the trader said he supposed it must, though he had never troubled to ask any of them outright, not wanting, as he put it, to spoil a fair trade in cloth by putting a bad question to it. She turned the thought over for years after, that a household might be built and outlived by its own maker the way a house of turf is built by hands that will crumble before the turf does.

She married a net-mender of Caol shean when she was two-and-twenty, and went to live a season on the river landing before she and her husband came back inland to Dùn dhearg, where the fort's ditch by then had a bank on either side of it and houses growing up thick along the inner slope. Three of her children lived, and one did not; that one she buried, as her own mother had taught her, with a small cup of salt at its feet, for the road, though no one in that country could have said any longer where the custom had first been learned or from whom.

Her husband was a quiet man, better with a net than with words, who took some years to grow used to a wife more curious about a river she had never seen than about the one that ran past their own door, and who told her once, not unkindly, that a woman might spend a whole life wanting a thing and be none the worse for never getting it. She answered that she had noticed as much of other people's wanting, and saw no reason it should hold true of her own.

In her middle years she was known along that stretch of the eastern coast less for any one deed than for a kind of restlessness that her neighbours took, by turns, for wisdom and for a nuisance: she asked, of every trader who came through Caol shean's landing, what the ford was like now, and how far it lay, and whether the king still sat among his own kin at it as the old tellings said, and she went on asking whether anyone troubled to answer her or not.

## III. Of the Double Bank

The digging of the double bank was the largest work Dùn dhearg's people had ever done together, and it settled, without any law behind it, the shape their custom would take from then on. Every household that meant to live inside the banks sent hands to the digging in proportion to the mouths it meant to feed there, an old man of that country keeping the count on a knotted cord much as the salt-folk kept their counts at the ford, though no one at Dùn dhearg had ever seen a salt-folk's cord to copy it from. When the ditch was done and the inner bank raised and turfed, the ground within it was shared out the same way, by households and not by favour, and a household that had sent three grown hands to the digging took no more ground than one that had sent three, whatever its standing had been before the digging began.

Not every household came to the digging willingly. One family on the northern edge of that country, holding good grazing already and wanting no ditch across the middle of it, sent word that they would keep to their own ground and let the rest dig as they pleased. No one from Dùn dhearg rode out to make them, there being no one with the standing to ride out on any such errand; instead the households already committed to the digging simply left that family's ground outside the second bank when the time came to draw it, so that within a generation that family found itself living, by its own choice and no one else's law, outside the shelter its neighbours had raised, and quietly asked, at last, to be let in on the same terms as everyone else, hands sent late being reckoned, by the same rough justice, at somewhat less than hands sent early.

Ailis's own household took a strip along the eastern slope, above the spring that fed the fort's own well, and it was there her husband died at last, an old man by any island's reckoning though not by the reckoning of the hill west of the vein, and was carried out and laid on the slope above the houses among the others of that place, with his own net-needle set in the ground at his feet.

The fort held its handful at Samhain as every hearth in that country did, each household burning its own measure in the dark of the year, and Ailis, growing old herself now, was one of the elders who walked the ring of houses on that night to see that no fire was lit before its neighbour's, the way the custom asked, though no one east of the vein could have named the keeper who had first drawn that lot at the slab, so far off and so long ago had that custom been made. It struck her once, walking that ring with a torch in the cold, that Dùn dhearg kept every custom the hill folk kept and owed the hill nothing at all for keeping them, and she said as much to the elder walking beside her, who only grunted that a custom belonged to whoever kept it and not to whoever had thought of it first, and went on with the walking.

## IV. Of the Seal-Skins and the Ford

In her eighty-first year, in the year four thousand three hundred and twenty-one, Ailis told her eldest son that she meant to see the ford before she died, and would go herself if no one else would carry her.

She had said it before, at intervals, for the better part of sixty years, and her son had learned to let it pass him like weather. This time she did not let it pass. She had put by, over three winters, a store of seal-skins taken off the rocks below Caol gharbh, more than her own household needed and more than any one trader passing through Caol shean's landing had offered a fair price for, and she said now that she would carry them west herself, to the ford, and trade them there for salt, since the salt-folk's pans lay nearer the ford than any coast of her own, and since, she said, she had asked after that river long enough that she meant to stand in it once before her legs gave out under her for good.

Six went with her in the end: her son, two of his own grown children, and three others of that country curious enough, or fond enough of the old woman, to make the walk. They went the road west from Dùn dhearg past Caol shean and Doire chiar, over ground none of them had ever crossed before, sleeping at other households' hearths some nights and in the open on others, until on the fifth day they came down out of hill country into a valley crowded with more people and more fires than any of them had seen gathered in one place in their lives.

The road surprised them before the ford did. Twice in the first two days they were taken in by households who had never heard of Dùn dhearg and had to be told, at some length, that a country lay east of the vein at all, and that it kept its own bank and its own hearths and owed nothing to any hill; and once, on the third night, they slept at a house whose grandfather remembered, or said he remembered, a telling of the great gathering held in the three-thousandth year of the Grove, though he could not himself have been born within a thousand years of it, and had it, he admitted freely, from his own grandmother, who had had it from hers. Ailis's son found it a strange thing, that a tale should be handed down mouth to mouth for so many lifetimes among people with no long-lived blood in them at all, and said so to his mother; she told him only that a good tale outlasted whoever had first the wit to tell it, which was, she said, no different east of the vein than west of it.

By the fourth day the road had thickened with other travellers going the same way, salt-carriers and wool-carriers and once a whole household driving cattle before it, and Ailis's small party fell in among them without any of the strangers troubling to ask where they had come from, so that by the time they came down into the valley of the ford they were only one more thread in a great deal of foot traffic, unremarked, which suited the old woman, she said afterward, better than being remarked upon would have.

The ford itself was nothing like the ford of the old tellings, or it was exactly like it and larger than any telling had prepared them for; Ailis, standing at the edge of that crowd with her bundle of skins on her back, could not afterward have said which. The salt people held the ground downstream and the hill people the ground upstream, as the tellings said, and beyond the river the flint people had their own crowded rise, and among them all, moving without any mark to set him apart from another herdsman, was a big grey-haired man that half the crowd would not look at directly and the other half seemed not to notice at all. Ailis looked at him a long while from where she stood and said nothing of it to her son, who had not yet seen him and would not, for the whole of that gathering, quite believe which man among the crowd he had been.

They were taken, in the end, to a woman not much past two hundred years of age, young by the reckoning of that blood though old by any other, who was named to them at the fire as Catrìona, of the king's own line, though she did not yet hold the hill herself and would not for some years after. She heard Ailis out with more patience than the old woman had expected of anyone born to that blood, asking where Dùn dhearg lay and how the double bank had been raised and whether the fort answered to anyone at all, and Ailis told her, plainly, that it answered to no one, and had been raised for that reason among others.

It was there, at that same fire, that one of the king's own blood, a red-haired man some centuries old who held river-grazing not far from the ford, looked over the seal-skins Ailis had carried the length of the island on her own back and asked Catrìona, in a voice not troubling to be quiet, whether she meant to trouble over ground for people who would all be dead, every one of them, inside a single lifetime of his own. He did not say it unkindly so much as carelessly, the way a man remarks on weather he has stopped noticing, and called them, in that same breath, the short-lived, a name that went round the fires of that gathering for some years after among those of the blood, though never once, so far as the tellings east of the vein remember it, in Catrìona's own mouth.

Ailis's son, hearing it, opened his mouth to answer the red-haired man in kind, but his mother put a hand on his arm before he could, and it was she who spoke instead, telling the red-haired man, plainly and without heat, that she had walked five days to stand at that river and did not mean to spend any part of the little time she had left in it arguing with a man who would have five hundred years to be sorry in, should sorry ever occur to him. Some at the fire laughed at that. The red-haired man said nothing further to her, and turned his attention back to his own grazing and his own grievances, which, it was plain even to a stranger from the east, ran a good deal deeper than any quarrel over seal-skins.

Catrìona did not answer him directly. She looked at Ailis a moment longer, at the seal-skins and the old woman's hands and the six who had come with her, and said only that the far bank of the river had room enough on it for one more fire, and that Dùn dhearg's people might have ground there, this year and every year after, if they cared to come to it. No oath was asked of them in return, and none was offered.

## V. Of the Salt Carried Home

They traded the seal-skins for salt on the far bank through that same evening, more salt, by weight, than any household of Dùn dhearg had carried home in living memory, and slept that night on ground that was, for the first time, theirs by nothing but a woman's word and their own presence on it. Ailis walked down to the river before she slept, and stood in the shallows a while with her skirts held up out of the water, an old woman alone at the edge of a crowded ford in the dark, and told no one afterward what she had thought while she stood there, only that the water had been colder than she expected and that she had stood in it longer than she meant to all the same.

They went home the way they had come, six going out and six coming back, with salt enough on their backs to see three households through a hard winter and word enough to keep every hearth from Dùn dhearg to Caol shean talking through it. No oath had been asked of them at the ford and none had been given, and the elders of Dùn dhearg, hearing it told, agreed among themselves, without needing to say so twice, that this suited them exactly as it stood: ground held and no word owed for the holding of it, which was, when it came down to it, no more nor less than the double bank had been built to be.

Ailis lived three years more after that journey, long enough to tell it herself at her own hearth a good many times, adding, each time she told it, some small thing she had forgotten the time before: the weight of the seal-skins going west and the lightness of them coming back; the grey-haired man in the crowd whom half the gathering would not look at; the red-haired one's word for her own people, which she came, in the telling, to say without any bitterness in it at all, as if a name given in scorn by a stranger who would outlive her ten times over was no great matter to carry home, set beside a river stood in once and salt enough for the winter.

She died in her own house on the eastern slope of the double bank in the year four thousand three hundred and twenty-five, eighty-five years old, with her net-mender husband's needle already long in the ground above her and her own place made ready beside it. Her grandchildren, when they were old themselves, still told of the seal-skins and the ford and the woman of the king's blood who had given them ground without asking anything back for it, and some of them told, too, in a lower voice and less often, of the word the red-haired man had used, the short-lived, which stayed at Dùn dhearg's hearths long after the man himself and everyone who had heard him say it were gone into the ground the same way Ailis had gone: at their own pace, in their own time, owing nothing to anyone for the manner of it.

The double bank stood after her as it had stood before her, and the ground on the far side of the river stayed Dùn dhearg's, without oath, without law, and without any name of a holder for the Stone Kings' own list to keep.

In the years after, when a trader passing through Caol shean's landing asked, as travellers will, whether the fort's people ever meant to send word to the hill and be reckoned among the king's own households at last, the answer given him, by more than one household and always in near enough the same words, was that Dùn dhearg had gone to the ford once, on its own feet and by an old woman's own wanting, and had come home again with salt and no master, and saw no cause to make the journey a second time on any other errand.
