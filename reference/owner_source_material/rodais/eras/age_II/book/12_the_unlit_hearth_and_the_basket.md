# Of the Unlit Hearth and the Basket

*GE 902 – 1,070*

*At Achadh dhìreach they tell this at the laying of every new hearth, before the first flame is set to it, and no one there lights a fire without hearing the end of it.*

## I. Of the Hearth Laid and Left

The peat-cutters of Achadh dhìreach struck the wall of it with a spade in the dry summer of the nine hundred and second year, a household out early on the high ground above the settlement to get in a season's fuel before the weather turned. Frost and water lay stones in the ground the way anyone who had ever cut peat knew stones to lie, tumbled and without pattern; what the spade struck that morning was stone laid by a hand, four low walls no higher than a man's knee, roofed over long since by the peat above them, and within them a hearth built as any hearth is built, a ring of flat stones set to hold a fire, with dry wood laid across it in the manner anyone alive then still laid a fire, kindling under and split branches over.

It had never been lit. That was the whole of the wonder, and it was wonder enough. Beside the wood, set apart a little as a thing is set apart that a hand meant to come back to, lay a sliver of the coloured coal, no bigger than a thumb's first joint, dark and quiet as the coal always was until fire woke the colours in it. The peat-cutters stood over the find a long while before any of them touched it, and the one who did touch it, an old woman named for nothing the tellers kept, lifted the sliver out with two fingers and held it to the light and put it down again exactly where it had lain, and said, of all the things she might have said, that whoever laid that hearth had known precisely what they held and had chosen, all the same, not to burn it.

No one then living could say who had built the house or laid the hearth or how long the peat had been growing over it, only that the growing took time the households of the high valleys did not reckon in single lifetimes; peat does not close over four low walls and a hearth of dry wood in a season, or in ten seasons, and the reckoners of Achadh dhìreach, turning the matter over at their own hearths that winter, judged the house older than any grandmother's grandmother could account for, older, some said, than the walk south itself, though that was said more to give the thing a shape than because anyone could prove it. They left the hearth as they had found it. They roofed the walls over again with turves cut to fit, so that the place could be found a second time by anyone who knew where to look and passed over by anyone who did not, and they called it, from that summer, An Teallach gun Lasadh, the hearth that was never lit, and no household built within a spear's throw of it, the same instinct that would, in later years and a different valley, set a line of white stones along the crack at Cill ghlas and keep every house the far side of it.

The peat went on growing. By the nine hundred and fifty-sixth year the turves the peat-cutters had laid back over the walls had themselves gone under a hand's depth of new bog, and the place that had been, for a generation, a known hollow a person could step down into and touch the cold hearthstones, became instead a low soft rise in the ground with nothing above it to say what lay beneath, marked only by the word passed down at the hearths of Achadh dhìreach and by a single flat stone one of the peat-cutters' grandsons had dragged up and set on the rise's crown, unmarked, because there was no mark that would have said what needed saying better than the stone's own silence. Children were told to keep off the rise, the way children everywhere are told to keep off ground that has swallowed something once and might, for all anyone can prove otherwise, be minded to swallow again. Most of them kept away. The few who dug there anyway found only turf under the turf, and came away disappointed and a little ashamed of the disappointment, as children generally do when a forbidden place gives up nothing worth the forbidding.

What the peat-cutters had found, though none of them said it in as many words and the tellers who kept the finding afterward said it less, was a household that had held the coloured coal in its own two hands, laid it by, and gone away without lighting it. It was the oldest sign the settlement had of a thing its own people would come, in time, to know a harder way: that the coal could be refused, and that a hand that knew when to close over it might be worth more than any store of it laid up open.

## II. Of the Handful Nowhere Written

Anna nic Coinnich was born at Achadh dhìreach in the thousandth year of the age, into a household that kept the telling of the unlit hearth the way every household on that rise kept it, as a thing said once a season and believed without much thought given to believing it, the way a child believes that fire burns before ever once being burned. She grew up within sight of the low soft mound on the high ground, and was old enough by the time she first thought to ask her grandmother why no one built there to be given an answer that satisfied her less than the question had pleased her, which is the ordinary fate of children's questions about the doings of the dead.

It was in her own childhood, so far as she or anyone else of that generation could afterward fix it, that the handful took hold. No mouth with any right to make a law made this one, and no one Anna ever asked remembered a day it was decided; it was simply, by the time she was eleven or twelve years old, the custom of every hearth she had ever sat beside, as fixed a habit as banking a fire for the night or salting meat against the winter, and no better explained than either. A cupped hand's worth of the coloured coal, and no more, went onto any fire at one laying; that was the whole of it, and it was kept at Achadh dhìreach and at Àth gharbh and at Cnoc chaol and along every hearth-row of the high valleys where the coal was gathered from the broken scree above the settlements and carried home in baskets to be doled out sparingly, the way salt was doled out, or seed corn, a thing valuable enough to want and dangerous enough, by some understanding no one troubled to explain, to want carefully.

Anna's grandmother taught her the measure the same evening she taught her to bank a peat fire for the night and to tell a lamb's cough that would pass from one that would not, three ordinary pieces of a girl's schooling given together because they belonged together in the grandmother's own mind, without any of the three seeming, to either of them, more solemn than the others. "A hand's full," the old woman said, cupping her own palm and closing the fingers a little to show the shape of the measure, "and no more, however cold the night is, and however much you have by you." When Anna asked why, her grandmother said what most grandmothers along that row said when the same question came to them, which was that she did not rightly know, only that her own mother had taught her the same measure the same way, cupping the same hand, and that a custom kept by everyone for reasons no one living could give was not, on that account, a custom to be tried against.

It held. Anna grew to a woman inside that holding, married a herdsman of a neighbouring household and raised three children of her own by the same measure her grandmother had shown her, and if she ever wondered, in the years her own hair began to grey, whether a habit so widely kept and so little questioned might one day be tried by someone who had not been given the measure young, or had been given it and judged it a fool's caution, she did not trouble to say so aloud, any more than anyone else on that rise troubled to say it. It was, after all, only a habit. Nothing had ever come of breaking it, because no one, in the memory of anyone then living at Achadh dhìreach, had ever broken it.

## III. Of the Basket

The household two hearths down from Anna's own had come late to the high valleys, in her grandmother's day, from grazing country further south where the coloured coal was a thing traded for rather than gathered, brought up the valley road in small worked pieces by carriers who took payment in wool and said little else about it; and though that household had kept the measure as faithfully as any other for three generations, keeping a custom taught to them being no different, in the doing, from keeping one worked out for themselves, they had never had cause to learn what lay behind it, any more than Anna had, and in the sixth year of the eleven hundredth decade of the age, in a winter that came in hard and early and stripped the last warmth from the ground before the household's usual store of dried gorse and driftwood was properly laid in, the old man of that house judged that a colder night than most called, reasonably enough to his own mind, for more of the coal than most nights called for, and that a measure kept out of caution rather than out of any known cause could be set aside, once, by a household that knew its own fire and its own need better than a custom that explained itself to no one.

Anna was at her own hearth that evening, mending a net her husband had brought in torn, when she first smelled it. Woodsmoke she had smelled every evening of her life and would not have looked up from her mending to notice. This smell came from under the woodsmoke, sharper, with a taste to it at the back of the throat like a struck flint magnified a hundredfold, and it came with a light through the gap of her own doorway that was the wrong colour for firelight, too many colours at once, running one into another the way oil runs on standing water. She was at the door before she had rightly decided to go to it, and so were most of the hearths along that row, drawn out by the same smell and the same wrongness of light, in time to see the old man's house lit from within like a lantern of coloured glass, every chink in its walls throwing out a different colour than the chink beside it, and to hear, from within, a sound Anna never afterward tried very hard to describe to anyone who had not heard it, a sound she said only, when pressed, was not a sound wood makes.

They could not get near it. Four men of the row tried, going in low with wet hides held before their faces the way a household goes in against an ordinary house-fire, and came back out again within a few breaths each, one of them with the skin of his forearm blistered through the wet hide as though the hide had not been there at all, and none of them ever afterward willing to say exactly what he had seen inside, beyond that the fire did not behave as a fire behaves, throwing its heat outward in waves rather than upward in a column, so that a man standing at what should have been a safe distance from an ordinary blaze found the wave reaching him all the same. The roof went first, then the walls fell inward rather than outward, which struck more than one of the watching households as wrong in a way none of them could have explained wrongness in a burning house, and by the time what was left of it had burned low enough to let anyone near, there was nothing left to save. Three of that household died in it: the old man, his son, and his son's wife, who had gone in after her husband when he did not come out and had not come out either. A grandchild, sent to a neighbour's hearth that evening on some ordinary errand that had kept her from her own supper, lived, and was raised after by the row between them, no one household claiming her over the others, in the way a settlement absorbs a child it has no better claim to divide.

What the fire left, when it was cold enough at last to be gone into, was the household's own hearthstone, cracked clean through from one edge to the other as no ordinary heat cracks stone, and every hairline of the crack running with colour, the same colours the walls had thrown out in the burning, only fixed now, sunk into the stone itself the way a stain sinks into wool, so that the crack looked, in the grey morning light, less like a break than like a vein of something laid open. Anna went to look at it herself, once the row judged it safe enough to be looked at, and stood over it a long while, the way the old peat-cutting woman was said to have stood over the sliver found under the turf two centuries before, and thought, though she did not say it to the neighbours standing with her, that she was looking at the same colour the tellers described running through the crack at Cill ghlas the winter the ground first opened there, and wondered whether the two things, the crack in a hillside and the crack in a hearthstone, were nearer kin than anyone had ever thought to say.

## IV. Of the King's Hand

Word went down the valley road that same night, carried by a boy on the fastest pony the row could spare, and the king came up in the first light of the second morning, alone, on a horse that had plainly been ridden hard through the dark, which was itself a thing worth remarking among households that had grown used to seeing him arrive at his own pace and no other's. He had come, Anna understood afterward, no faster for three deaths than he would have come for one, or for none at all, only that word of a fire that burned the wrong colour was the kind of word that reached him quickly however it travelled, by whatever roads carried such word to wherever he happened to be.

He looked at the burned house a while without speaking, standing at what was left of its threshold with his hands loose at his sides, and the row stood back from him the way a row of households generally did, near enough to be called forward if he called them and far enough not to crowd a man they had none of them ever grown entirely used to having among them, however many times he came. Then he crossed to the cracked hearthstone, knelt by it in the ash, and laid his open hand flat across the crack, over the running colour, the way a person lays a hand on a beast to quiet it or on a child's forehead to feel for fever, and held it there.

Anna was near enough to see what happened, though she was never afterward sure she had the words to say it rightly to anyone who had not seen it themselves. The colour did not vanish under his hand, not all at once and not as though wiped away; it drew inward, the way frost draws back from a fire brought near it, sinking from the crack's outer edges toward the line his palm covered, until what had run the length of the stone was gathered under his hand alone, and then, in the space of a breath more, was gone, and the stone under his palm was grey stone and nothing else, cracked still, but cracked as any stone might crack, holding no more colour than the ash around it.

He stood, wiped his hand on his own leg without seeming to notice he had done it, and looked at the row a while before he spoke, the plain, short, dry way the household had come, by then, to expect of him whenever a thing needed saying and no more than the saying of it. "You keep a measure here," he said. It was not quite a question. Someone, Anna thought it might have been her own husband, said that they did, a hand's full and no more, though none of them could say where the measure had come from or who had first kept it. The king looked at the burned house a while longer.

"It is a good custom," he said at last. "Keep it. I will not tell you why, because I do not think the why would serve you as well as the keeping does, and a thing kept for a reason understood is no more surely kept than a thing kept because it has always been kept. But it is a good custom, and you have paid, this once, to be shown it is." He looked round the row of watching faces, the old and the young and the children too young to understand what had been lost in the house behind him, and said nothing further about the three who had died, then or, so far as Anna ever heard, at any later time; only asked, before he left them that morning, whether the child who had lived had somewhere to go, and was told she had, and rode back down the valley the way he had come, no faster and no slower than a man riding home from an ordinary errand.

It was the only law the age ever had that Anna knew the making of, if it could rightly be called a making at all: no word set down, no gathering called to agree it, only a habit already kept by every hearth for reasons no one could give, spoken over once by a king who gave it no reason either, and kept the harder afterward for having been paid for. The household's cracked hearthstone was carried down, in the spring, and set into the low wall the row built round its shared well, where it stayed, grey and ordinary to look at, marked to no passer-by who did not already know its history, for as long as anyone thought to look for it.

## V. Of the Cupped Hand

Anna held the measure the rest of her own life, and taught it to her children the way her grandmother had taught it to her, cupping her palm and closing the fingers a little to show the shape, and by the time she was old, past sixty and then past seventy, with grandchildren of her own old enough to be given their own first lesson at a hearth, she had come to say, over the cupped hand, the words her own grandmother had never troubled to put into a saying at all, because there had been, in her grandmother's day, no burned house and no cracked stone and no king's hand laid over a running crack to make the saying necessary.

"Làn na glaice," she told them, closing her fingers over the little heap of coal she measured out before them, "'s gun tuilleadh." A hand's full, and no more. She told them, if they asked why, as her own grandmother had been asked and had not rightly answered, that there had once been a household two hearths down from her own that had not kept the measure, once, on a hard winter's night, and had not lived to keep it again; and she told them, if they pressed her further, of the king kneeling in the ash with his hand over the cracked stone, and the colour drawing in under his palm like frost drawing back from a fire, and his plain few words that it was a good custom and that they should keep it. She did not tell them why he had said it was good, because she did not know, any more than her own grandmother had known why the measure was kept at all; she told them only that it had been paid for once, in front of her own eyes, and that a thing paid for at that price did not want trying twice.

The rise on the high ground above the settlement stood as it had always stood, unbuilt on, the flat unmarked stone still on its crown where the peat-cutter's grandson had set it, and the households of Achadh dhìreach went on calling it An Teallach gun Lasadh and keeping their own distance from it without any longer, most of them, remembering clearly why; and the cracked hearthstone in the well-wall stood the same way, grey and quiet, known to a fewer number each generation for what it had once been. But the cupped hand went on being taught at every hearth of the high valleys the length of Anna's own long life and past it, closed to the one measure and no further, and if none of the children given it ever afterward asked why with quite the persistence Anna herself had once asked her own grandmother, it may only have been that a custom paid for once, within living memory, needs asking after a good deal less than a custom whose paying no one now living had seen.
