# Of the Seven Nights

*GE 1*

*Part One: The Grove and the Hill*

> *Chaidh e dhan doire na ghille, is thill e na rìgh.*
> He went into the grove a lad, and he came back a king.
>
> — said of Ailean Mòr, as the reciters of Dùn ìseal keep it

## I. Of the Fires under the Oaks

*Coinneach mac Shuibhne tells this, in the years before he grew grey, and Clann na Ceiste have kept his words whole, changing nothing, for they say a true thing need not be improved.*

Coinneach was three years younger than his brother, a lad of one-and-twenty that summer, and he had followed him north with a sack of oatcake and a skin of buttermilk that his brother would not eat. He did not know, when he set out, that he would not be let nearer than the edge of the trees.

Ailean had been gone the better part of a year, carrying his six pots on a pole across his shoulders, each with its lid of turf, feeding six fires that had never before burned in one place. Their father asked no questions of him before he went, and none of the household did either, for it was known in Clann na Ceiste, as it had been known since the mason's day, that the coals were not a thing to be asked about. Coinneach had watched him go with the same silence the others kept, had held it two seasons, and then could hold it no longer. He told no one where he was walking, and simply went, north and further north, by the roads his brother must have taken, until on an evening in high summer he came out of birch scrub onto a rise above a stand of oaks and saw smoke going up through the leaves in six colours, and knew he had found him.

He came down through the trees meaning to walk straight in, and got no further than the first oaks.

It was not a hand that stopped him, nor a word, though a word came soon enough. It was the ground itself, or the air over it, which changed the way water changes when it goes suddenly from a stream's cold to a spring's warmth, so that a man's skin knows it before his eyes have found any reason. Coinneach stood with one foot already lifted to the next step and could not set it down inside the ring of oaks. He looked and there was Ailean, sitting cross-legged in a clearing with six low fires around him each burning in its own colour, gold and white and a red so dark it looked nearly black, and copper-red, and a fire like beaten silver, and a sixth with no more colour to it than any hearth-flame has, though it stood straighter than any hearth-flame ought to stand. In the middle of the six, on the bare earth, lay a small stone burning with a low light of no colour he could name, for it was every colour at once and none of them would hold still.

His brother sat with his head unturned, and it was the shape beside him that spoke, tall as a standing man and made of the same grey as the smoke that went up past it without disturbing it at all.

"That is close enough," it said, not loudly, and yet Coinneach heard it as clearly as if it had been at his shoulder. "Go back to the trees. Camp there if you will. Come no nearer while the fires burn."

"I am his brother," said Coinneach, because it seemed a thing that ought to be said to whatever this was.

"I know whose brother you are," said the shape, and there was no unkindness in it, only the flat certainty of a being that had watched a very great deal and found little of it needed explaining twice. "That is why you are let stand where you stand, and not sent further off than that. Go back to the trees."

Ailean turned his head then, once, and looked at him across the fires, and Coinneach saw that his brother's face in that light was the same face he had grown up beside, and yet lit from some place behind the eyes as well as in front of them, the way a horn lantern is lit from within, and not merely warmed on the outside by a fire someone else has made. He did not speak. He lifted one hand a little way from his knee, which Coinneach took, rightly, for all the greeting he was going to get, and then his brother's eyes went back to the fires and did not come to him again that night.

Coinneach went back to the trees. He was one-and-twenty years old and he had walked the length of the island on a road with no end that he had been told of, and he sat down under a birch with his sack of oatcake between his knees and did not eat any of it, because eating in front of a fast felt to him like a kind of theft.

He had grown up three years behind his brother in the same house, under the same low roof, and had thought, until that summer, that there was little enough about Ailean he did not already know: which shoulder he favoured carrying a lamb, which songs he sang badly and on purpose to make the younger children laugh, how long he could hold his breath under the mill-pool. Their father had said nothing when Ailean took up the coals; he had only watched him go with the flat, unsurprised look of a man watching a debt come due that he had long known was owed, and had said nothing either when Coinneach, two seasons on, packed a sack of his own and went after him without leave asked or given. What sent Coinneach north, in truth, was simpler than disobedience: a year is a long time for a house to sit waiting on news of one son, and Coinneach had never in his life been good at waiting. He did not know, sitting there, that this was the first of seven nights, or that he would keep every one of them awake, or that the deeds he watched from outside a ring of trees that summer would be the only true account any teller after him would ever have of what was done inside it. He knew only that his brother's face had changed, and that he meant to be there when it changed back, if it ever did.

## II. Of the Seven Nights

*The vigil within the ring belongs to Sloinneadh a' Chlachair to tell, for Coinneach was never let see it; they keep it word for word, as it has come down from the grove itself, and it is set here as they set it, against what Coinneach watched from the trees.*

Ailean sat among the seven fires (the seventh, the stone in the ring's centre, had taken light at dusk when the shape called Coimhdeach na Fine stooped over it, and burned after that with no colour that could be named, being every colour, and none of them still), and Coimhdeach na Fine stood over him and told him how the watch must be kept.

"Stay with them until they are cold," it said. "Do not leave them, do not sleep far from them, and eat nothing while they burn."

"Why?" said Ailean.

"So that the spirits, being merged, may know one keeper, and the fire go into no hand but the hand that did not leave it."

"And after me?" said Ailean. "Will my children have it?"

Coimhdeach na Fine was a long while answering. The six fires and the seventh burned on between them without a sound, and the smoke of them stood up straight through windless air.

"It will be yours," it said at last, "and no one's after you. Your children will have a little of it, and their children less, as a hearthstone keeps the heat a while when the fire is out; and then your line will not have it, and will forget that it ever had, and the island will call you a tale. But hear this, and keep it. When strangers hold this island, the fire will wake again in your line."

"What strangers?"

"That is not given to me to say."

Then Coimhdeach na Fine went from him, or ceased to be seen, which Sloinneadh a' Chlachair does not tell apart, and Ailean was left alone among the fires to keep his watch.

He kept it as he had been told. He did not leave the ring, and did not sleep far from it, and ate nothing while the seven fires burned, though he drank from the grove's own spring on the nights the fires stood steady enough to be left a while. The first two nights the hunger in him was a plain, animal thing, no different from any hunger he had carried on any long ride before; by the third it had gone quiet, the way pain goes quiet in a wound that will either mend or kill and has stopped asking a man's opinion on which. On the fifth night he thought he heard voices in the fires, low under the sound the flames made, saying the same words over that he could never afterward recall, only as a rhythm, the way a chant is remembered by its beat after its language is lost; and on the seventh he could not have said, waking or not waking, whether he had slept at all, for the fires gave the same light and the same warmth at the black heart of that night as they had at the first evening's dusk, unspent, unlessened, as if the passing of six nights meant no more to them than the passing of six breaths.

Outside the ring, under his birch, Coinneach kept a watch of his own, with no gods to answer his questions and no fires to warm him and no lid of turf to hold his hunger down. Through the first night he lay on his back and watched the light of the seven fires stand and shift between the trunks, colours he had no names for laid over colours he did, gold on birch-bark, the black-red flickering against a stone he could not see round, and told himself he would sleep an hour and did not sleep at all. By the second night the hunger had settled into a duller ache than hunger, a low ache he had stopped arguing with, and he found he could watch the coloured light for longer stretches without his mind wandering off after food or home or his father's face when he learned where his younger son had gone. By the third and fourth he had stopped counting the nights as nights and begun counting them as fires, one fire more burned down toward whatever it was burning down toward, though from where he lay none of the seven ever seemed to burn down at all.

It was on the fifth night that he first heard the voices.

He was not sure, after, that he had heard anything, and he never claimed more than that he had. They came from the direction of the ring, under the light and the smoke-smell and the small settling sounds any fire makes, two voices or three, pitched too low for words, one of them — he would say this only once, near the end of his own life, to the woman who wrote it down — sounding almost as if it were his brother's, though shaped into a rhythm Ailean had never used in speech, more like a man saying the same word over and over to hold a thing steady in his mind. Coinneach sat up under the birch and listened until the muscles of his neck ached with listening, and heard nothing more he could swear to, and lay back down with the hunger and the cold and the sense, new to him that night, that a thing was happening within the ring of oaks that had no plain edges to it, that could not be watched the way a fight or a birth or a burial could be watched, only felt at, the way a man feels at a wall in the dark.

He did not go nearer. He had been told to keep to the trees, and he kept to them, and that is the whole of what Coinneach mac Shuibhne did for seven nights: he kept to the trees, and did not eat, though no one had forbidden him to, and did not sleep, though no one had asked that of him either, and watched colours he could not name go up through leaves he could, until he no longer knew which watch was his brother's and which was his own.

## III. Of the Eighth Night

*Here the two tellings run together, for on the eighth night there was a thing that both saw, though neither saw the same thing, and Clann na Ceiste keep both accounts and do not choose between them.*

On the seventh night Ailean could not afterward say whether he had slept. The fires had not burned low in seven nights, had shown him no more fuel spent than on the first evening, and had not needed his hand at any of them; and on the eighth night, without warning and without sound, they went out. All seven together. He came to himself, or woke, Sloinneadh a' Chlachair will not say which, and found the ring of oaks entirely dark, the pots and the pale stone lying grey and cold on the trodden leaves, and himself colder than he had been in any of the nights before, as if the fires had been lending him their own warmth all along and had just then called the loan due.

Outside the trees, at the same hour by whatever reckoning either of them kept afterward, Coinneach saw the dark come down over the grove and felt some weight go out of the night with it, some pressure he had not known was on him until it lifted. He got to his feet. More than once in seven nights he had meant to walk in regardless of what stood there and see for himself what had become of his brother, and each time some pull in the air of the place had turned him back before he had gone three steps. Now, with the fires dead and the grove black as any wood at midnight, he found his feet would carry him no better than before. Standing at the edge of the oaks with his hand on a trunk, he felt the bark shiver, very faintly, the way a held breath shivers a man's chest, and then the grove filled with light.

It did not come up from the ground as the seven fires had, and it was not the colour of any fire he had seen in his life. It came in among the trees the way people come into a hall, many at once, talking low, and it moved. Coinneach saw shapes crossing between the trunks that his mind, afterward, could give no better name than shapes: a big one that laughed without a sound reaching him, one small and quick that he took, for no reason he could give, to be carrying fire in her own two hands, one bent low as though listening at the ground. He never named them, all his life, though others named them for him later out of Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, and he would not say they were wrong, only that he had not known, standing at the edge of the trees with his hand shaking on the bark, which name went with which shape.

He tried to go in, setting his foot forward the way he had tried every night before it, and his foot would not carry him; this time it was not only the ground that refused him, for his whole body stood at the edge of that light the way a man stands at the edge of a cliff in the dark, knowing the ground is there and not trusting his legs to find it. Once he called his brother's name. Nothing in the light answered him, and after a while the light began to thin, the shapes going from many to few to none, until only the grey stillness of an ordinary wood before dawn was left among the oaks, and Coinneach sat down where he stood, because his legs had at last stopped holding him up, and waited for the sky to go from black to grey in its own time, the way skies do whether gods have walked a wood that night or not.

## IV. Of the Warm Arm and the Road South

*What was done to Ailean between the fires going out and the light coming in, no one now living was let see; what was said to him, and by whom, and what he answered, Clann na Ceiste keep as it was given them, and it is not this telling's place to add to it or take from it.*

Dawn found Coinneach still sitting at the edge of the trees with his back against an oak, and the sun perhaps a hand's width up when a figure came out of the grove toward him, walking easily, as a man walks who has slept well and eaten and has nowhere urgent to be. It was a little while before Coinneach's eyes, sore with a week of watching, made the figure into his brother, and longer still before he trusted the making.

He got up. His legs had gone stiff with a night's sitting and he stumbled the first step, and Ailean put out an arm and caught him, the ordinary way an older brother catches a younger one who has stumbled, and it was that arm, closing on his own, that told Coinneach the truth of the night before any word did. It was warm. Not warm as a living arm is warm, blood-warm, the warmth a man carries without noticing it in himself and only feels in another when the morning is cold; this was warm the way a hearthstone is warm long after the fire has gone out and been swept, a warmth with weight to it, that seemed to come up out of the bone rather than lie on the skin. Coinneach had held his brother's arm a thousand times in one-and-twenty years and he had never felt anything like it, and he did not let go at once, though he knew, holding it, that he ought to.

"You're cold," Ailean said, as if that were the only strange thing about the morning.

Coinneach found he could not, for a space, make any words come. When they came they were not the ones he had spent seven nights preparing. "What happened to you," he said, "in there," and even as he said it he heard how small a question it was for what he had watched from outside the ring.

Ailean looked at him a while before he answered, and something crossed his face that Coinneach, in all the years he told this tale afterward, could never settle on a single word for: not joy, though there was joy in it; not fear, though there was fear in it too; nearer, he came to think in his old age, to the look of a man newly returned from a country whose language he has learned well enough to think in, and cannot just then remember how to render into the one his own people speak.

"They named me king," he said.

That was all. Who had named him, he did not say, though Coinneach, standing there with his brother's warm arm still half in his grip, thought he knew well enough without being told, having watched shapes cross a lit grove all night from the wrong side of its trees. What a king was king of, on an island that had never had one, went unsaid too, and what had been put into him, or taken from him; nor did he ask his brother to swear any silence about it, for he seemed to know already, in some way Coinneach could not follow, that there was no danger of Coinneach's telling more than he had seen, since Coinneach had seen almost nothing at all.

They went back into the ring together, Ailean walking as if the ground of it held no more weight for him than any other ground now, and Coinneach following a half-step behind, still not wholly easy in himself about crossing that line of oaks even with the fires long out and his brother's hand on his shoulder. The six pots lay where they had stood for seven nights, empty and cold, their turf lids scattered where the wind or something less than wind had thrown them; the pale stone that had held the seventh fire lay among them, small and grey and giving off no more warmth to the eye than any stone off any hillside, though when Coinneach at last made himself touch it, near midday, it was warm too, in the same deep hearthstone way his brother's arm had been. Ailean gathered the six pots onto his shoulder-pole without a word, as though he meant to carry them on somewhere, though he never lit any of the six fires in them again in all his life, and left the seventh stone lying where it lay, uncovered, among the leaves.

They did not go home. Coinneach asked once, that first morning, whether they were for their father's hearth, and Ailean said only that they were going south, and began walking, and Coinneach, having followed him a year's worth of roads already, followed him down this one too.

He did not stop at Na Glinn Àrda, where the households of Clann na Ceiste kept their herds about the crack in the ground that no one living could remember the naming of, though he had kin enough there to have stopped a season among them and been fed and questioned and made much of; and some of those kin, seeing him go by without turning aside, left their own hearths and their own herds and walked out onto the road after him without being asked, the way men will follow a fire seen at night across open country before they have decided in their own minds to go anywhere at all. By the time the grove was three days behind them there were eleven of them walking south with Ailean and his brother, kin near and kin distant, none of them able afterward to say clearly why he had left byre or field to do it, only that the sight of Ailean walking past his own people's gates without stopping had seemed a thing that could not be let go by unfollowed.

## V. Of the Kin Who Followed

*This part of the road Coinneach told plainly, having been on it himself and needing no fires and no Coimhdeach na Fine to tell it by.*

They were an odd company to look at from any rise above the road: a king who had been a king nine days, walking at no great pace and carrying six empty pots on a pole across his shoulder as though they still held cargo worth the carrying; his brother a half-step behind him, hollow-eyed from a week without food or sleep and saying little; and behind the two of them, by the time Na Glinn Àrda were a day's walk gone, eleven more, none of whom had set out that morning meaning to leave home at all.

A woman with a baby on her hip had been driving geese in from a field when Ailean passed her gate; she left the geese to find their own way back and simply fell in on the road, the baby asleep against her shoulder before the first mile was out and no more roused by the change in her life than by any other jolt of the walking. An old man who had buried two wives and outlived a third said, when his daughter asked him where he thought he was going at his age, only that he had a feeling in his chest he had not had since he was a boy and did not intend to argue with it. There was a boy of nine who came because his older cousin came, and a stooped-shouldered woman with a limp who kept pace with the rest of them without complaint or explanation the whole way south, and would not be persuaded home again even when the going grew hard. None of the eleven, asked afterward, could give a better account of why he or she had come than the old man's: a feeling in the chest, not to be argued with.

Ailean spoke little to any of them on the road, and to Coinneach hardly more, though he let his brother walk always at his shoulder and never once told him to turn back, which Coinneach, in the years he had left to think on it, came to count for more than any number of words would have been worth. Twice they slept in the open under no roof at all, and both nights it was noticed, though not spoken of loudly, that the ground near where Ailean lay was warmer by morning than the ground a little further off, warm enough that the heavy dew that greyed the rest of the field left a bare, dark ring around him the size of a sleeping man, as though the ground itself remembered him from the night before it had ever been asked to.

They did not hurry. Ailean walked as a man walks who knows the road will still be there whether he covers it in ten days or twenty, and stopped where there was water and shelter and went on again when he chose and not before, and slowly, day by day, the sack Coinneach had carried north with such care went from full to half-full to empty, shared out among a company that had grown too large for one man's forethought to feed, and nobody starved, and nobody afterward could quite say how that had been managed either.

Coinneach walked among them the whole way south, and told none of them what he had watched from outside the ring of oaks. He had sworn no oath of silence; he had simply found, trying the words over to himself on the road, that they came out smaller than the thing they were meant to carry, the way a bucket of well-water looks like less once it is out of the well than it did going down for it. He kept the telling instead, turning it over through the long days of walking, fitting it to itself the way a man fits a stone to a gap in a dry wall, testing each piece against what he had truly seen and setting aside what he had only half seen or wholly imagined in the dark under the birch; and it was in this way, walking south behind his brother down a road that had no name yet because there had been no one before them to walk it and give it one, that the first account of the seven nights and the eighth was made, by a man of one-and-twenty who had been let see none of it, and who therefore, of all who ever told it after, was the most careful never to say more than he knew.
