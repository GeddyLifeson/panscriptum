# Of the Rope's End

*GE 1,441 – 1,540*

*At the moor of the eastern Toll-dubh the covering stone is still laid the same way it was laid in her father's father's day, and the household that lays it will tell a stranger only that it was Iseabail's way before it was theirs.*

## I. Of the Household at the Shaft's Mouth

The household that kept the eastern Toll-dubh had by then held it four generations without ever once finishing the work their own great-grandfathers had begun, for the shaft the old diggers had sunk into that level moor, straight and smooth-walled and reaching down past any depth a stone dropped from the lip would answer for, had long since stopped being a work in anyone's hands and become instead a thing only kept, the way a grave is kept, or a hearth that has not held a fire in a generation is kept swept all the same. The old plaited cords the first diggers had worn through hung yet inside the house's own door, two lengths of dark hide gone stiff with age, and a stranger coming to that threshold, seeing them hung where another household would have hung a net or a snare, learned soon enough not to ask after them twice, for the first asking got a plain enough answer and the second got only a look that closed the matter.

Iseabail was born into that household in the fourteen hundred and thirtieth year of the hill, the elder of two children, a dark, quiet, watchful girl who from her earliest years had a way of standing at the edge of any gathering with her arms folded and her eyes going from face to face that made the older women of the moor say, half in fondness and half in something nearer unease, that she watched a room the way her family's shaft watched the sky, taking everything in and giving nothing back. Her brother Eachann came five years after her, born loud where she was quiet, quick to laugh and quicker to dare, the kind of boy who could not pass a height without wanting to know what stood at the top of it nor a hole without wanting to know what lay at the bottom, and who by his tenth year had already been pulled twice from trouble a more careful child would never have found: once from a sea-cave the tide had near closed on him, and once from the roof-tree of the byre, where he had climbed to see, he said afterward, whether a man standing on it could see as far as the flint road.

Their father kept the shaft the way his own father had kept it before him, laying the flat covering stone loosely over its mouth at the first hard frost of every year and lifting it again when the days had lengthened past the shortest, a custom carried on with no more ceremony than swinging a byre door was carried on, and no more explaining given to a child who asked after it than the plain fact that the stone kept a wandering beast or a careless foot from the lip through the months no one went down to trouble it. He told his children, as his own father had told him, that the digging had stopped generations back when the lamp let down on its cord showed no more of the walls than a small patch no wider than two hands held apart, the rest of the depth standing dark above and below it alike, and that a stone let fall after that had given back no sound of striking anything at all, though a household had stood listening a long while to be sure of it. He told them, too, and told it more than once, for it was the one part of the telling he seemed to want driven in past all forgetting, that no one had gone down since, and that no one, so far as the household's own memory ran, had any cause to.

Eachann heard this the way he heard every closed door in his short life, which was as an invitation rather than a warning, though he was careful enough, even as a boy, not to let his father see the shine that came into his eye at the telling of it.

## II. Of the Boy Who Would Not Let It Be

The boy grew up circling that stone the way another boy might circle a girl he meant, in time, to speak to. He was forbidden the moor after dark from an early age, on no account explained to him beyond his mother's flat word that the moor at night was no place for anyone, and he kept, on the whole, to that forbidding, being fonder of his own bed than of any mystery enough to give it up entirely; but by day, through the years the covering stone stood lifted, he found reasons to be about the shaft's mouth that his elder sister, watching from a little way off with her arms folded in her old fashion, came to know for what they were without his ever once telling her plainly. He would lie flat on the moor grass with his head and shoulders out over the lip, calling down into the dark to hear how long his own voice took to come back to him, which by his own reckoning, kept in his head across three summers of trying it, was longer each year than the year before, though Iseabail told him more than once that a boy's memory of an echo was no more to be trusted than a boy's memory of a fish he had nearly caught. Every trader who came up the moor track he asked whether any other household, anywhere the trader had walked, kept a shaft like theirs, and grew quieter, not louder, each time the answer came back that none did, or that if any did the trader had never heard tell of it, as though the shaft's very rareness were argument enough for going down into it that his sister could see building in him long before he found the words to put it to their father plainly.

He put it to their father plainly in his fifteenth year, standing at the covering stone on a spring morning with his hands laced behind his back the way he stood when he wanted to seem more grown than his years, and asked to be let down on the old cord to see for himself what four generations of the household's own telling had never once claimed to know. Their father said no, without heat and without more explaining than he had ever given the shaft's own silence, and Eachann, to his credit and his sister's mild surprise, let the matter lie unspoken between them for the better part of two years after, though Iseabail, who watched him still the way she watched most things, never once believed the wanting in him had lain down with the asking.

Their father died in the fourteen hundred and fifty-first year, of no more than an ordinary fever that took three others on the moor that same wet spring, and the covering stone passed, along with the rest of the small holding, into the keeping of Iseabail and Eachann together, she twenty-two by then and he seventeen, both of them grown enough by the moor's own reckoning to be answerable for a household's custom without any elder standing over them to enforce it. It was the first winter the stone was laid by their own two hands alone, and Iseabail, laying it with her brother that first hard frost, felt him watching the dark gap it covered a moment longer than the laying asked for, and said nothing, for there was, by then, nothing left to say that had not already been said and let lie.

## III. Of the Rope's End

He asked her in the spring, when the stone had been lifted again and the moor had begun to green, not their father, who was a year in the ground by then and past asking anything of, and not any elder of the moor, for there was none left near enough to their holding to have the right of it, but Iseabail herself, standing at the shaft's mouth on a still morning with the old cord already coiled over one shoulder, the longer of the two hanging by the door, tested that same dawn for any rot the years might have worked into the hide and found, by his own report of the testing, sound. He did not ask leave so much as tell her plainly that he meant to go down, that the household's own cord was strong enough for it and had been proved so a hundred times over in the diggers' own day, and that a household which kept a wonder at its own door and never once looked into it was keeping, by his reckoning, nothing at all worth the keeping.

She told him no, in near enough her father's own words, and watched the telling glance off him the way it had glanced off their father two years before. What she had never told him plainly before, she told him then: that she had dreamed twice that winter of the shaft with no bottom to it at all, only dark going down forever, and had woken both times with her heart going hard enough to wake beside it; and Eachann, who loved his sister and did not, in the ordinary run of things, make light of what troubled her, said only that a dream was a dream and a shaft was a shaft, and that he would rather know, and be wrong to have looked, than spend the rest of his own life circling a stone the way he had circled it as a boy.

She held the rope in the end because there was no one else on the whole of that moor she would have trusted to hold it for him, and because she had long since understood, watching him these seven years and more, that a no from her would only send him down some day with no one holding it at all, which frightened her a good deal more than the holding did. The two of them went out on a clear morning in the fourteen hundred and fifty-second year, the two of them alone, with the cord looped twice about a stake her brother had driven the evening before into ground he had tested for its firmness with his own full weight, and a second cord besides, shorter, tied about Eachann himself under his arms in the manner the old telling said the first diggers had tied it, so that a fall would catch him rather than drop him clean.

He went down slowly at her paying-out, calling up to her at first in the old way he had called down as a boy, to test how long his voice took returning, and she answered him each time to know that the answer still reached him; but the calling grew further between as the cord ran out, and then stopped altogether a good while before the cord did, not through any fright in his voice, for the last of it she heard was steady enough, but simply because the distance, she thought after, had grown too great by then for a voice to carry either way without more shouting than either of them had thought, standing at the lip that morning, they would need. She stood alone at the stake with the cord running taut and cold through her two hands, feeling for its own small movements the way a fisher feels a line, and it seemed to her, though she never afterward could put a plain shape to the feeling nor swear to anyone that it was more than her own fright working on her, that the cord went, for a time near the very end of its length, curiously light in her hands, as though whatever hung on it had ceased, for those moments, to need the holding at all.

How long she stood there she could not have said, asked closely afterward, before the cord's weight came fully back into her hands and she felt, at last, the three short tugs he had promised her beforehand would mean he was ready to be drawn up. She drew him up alone, hand over hand at the stake, in a labour that left the skin of both her palms raw for a fortnight after, and did not stop nor rest the drawing once begun, for she had promised herself, standing at that lip with the cord gone light in her hands, that she would not let go of him for want of her own strength before she had seen with her own eyes what had come up out of the dark on the end of it.

He came up alive. He came up whole, with no mark on him beyond the ordinary chafing the cord had worked at his sides, and stood on the moor grass blinking in a morning light no brighter, by any telling, than the light he had gone down under, and said nothing at all for the length of time it took her own hands to stop their shaking. When at last he spoke it was only to ask, in a voice she did not at first know for his own, whether she was hurt from the drawing, and to take the raw of her palms into his own two hands and look at them a long while before he let them go.

She asked him, that same morning and every day for a long while after, what he had found at the rope's end. He never told her. He did not refuse her plainly, nor grow angry at the asking, nor once, in all the years the asking went on, give her cause to think he punished her with the silence for any fault of hers; he simply did not answer, turning the question aside each time with some small word about the cold down there, or the dark, or nothing worth the telling, in a manner so mild and so unshaken that she came, in time, to believe he had decided the not-telling before ever he was drawn back up, the way a man decides, going into deep water, how far out he means to swim before the water has told him anything of its own mind.

They laid the covering stone that same autumn and never lifted it again with any thought of going down after it. Within the year the household's own custom, which had stood four generations as a silence about a thing merely closed, hardened into something nearer a law that Iseabail herself set, without meaning to set anything so grand as a law, simply by telling every soul who asked after the shaft, traveller and neighbour and, in time, her own children, that the household went down into Toll-dubh no more, and would go down no more, and that whoever came after her might keep the stone or let it lie as their own conscience answered for, but that no cord of that house's plaiting would go over that lip again while she herself had breath to forbid it. No one on that moor, in all the years the telling of it ran, ever went down again.

## IV. Of the Question She Carried

She married, two years after, a man of a neighbouring holding who had courted her patiently through the very years the asking of her brother went unanswered, and who never once, in all the years of their own marriage, asked her to make her brother speak where she herself had failed; and she raised three children on that moor within sound of the same stone her father and his father had laid before her, teaching them, as she had been taught, to leave a thing closed that a household's own telling said was closed, though she taught it, her own children said, with a plainer weight in the telling than her father had ever put into it, as a woman teaches a lesson she has herself paid a full price to learn rather than one merely handed down.

Eachann never married. He lived on the moor within sight of his sister's own holding the whole of his life, keeping a small flock and mending the cords and creels of anyone on that stretch who asked him, a quiet, steady, well-liked man whom the moor came in time to call by no other name than Eachann Ròpa, the name settling onto him so gradually that neither he nor his sister could afterward have said which year it first was spoken to his face rather than only behind it. He bore the name without complaint and, so far as anyone on the moor ever saw, without any particular pride in it either, the way a man bears a scar got young that has long since stopped hurting and become simply a mark he answers to.

Iseabail asked him the question, in one shape or another, at every gathering of their two households across thirty years and more, at weddings and at burials and at the ordinary suppers a brother and sister keep between them when there is nothing pressing to say, and he turned it aside each time in the same mild way, until the asking itself became, between them, less a question in earnest than a kind of greeting the two of them kept up out of habit, the way another family keeps a joke worn smooth from telling; and yet she never, in all those years, stopped meaning it plainly beneath the habit, and he, she came to believe, never once mistook her meaning for less than it was, however lightly either of them wore the asking by then.

## V. Of the Hut and the Lake

There reached her, in the years her own children were growing, two tellings from other parts of the hill's country that she heard only as a traveller's news carried up the moor track, and neither of which she ever once, in her own telling of them after, set beside her brother's silence or asked to be read as answering it, for she had learned by then, better than most, how little good came of laying one unclosed thing over another for no better reason than that both were dark.

A herding family new to the hills where the shape called the Hollow Man was oldest told of lit an open fire against the custom that country kept, being strangers to it and slow, as strangers often are, to weigh a neighbour's fear as heavily as their own comfort; and word came up the moor track, some years after, that the family's hut had been found standing with its fire still burning on the hearth and not a soul of the household anywhere about it, near or far, then or ever after, though every household within a day's walk had turned out to search the ground twice over. Iseabail heard the telling at a summer gathering, from a trader who swore he had it from a cousin who had helped in the searching, and thought of her brother's own silence not at all that evening, only of the hut standing open with its fire untended, and said, when her own children asked her plainly whether the two things were the same kind of dark, that she could not tell them so, not having stood at either mouth of it herself, and that a woman did well not to guess at a neighbour's mystery only for having one of her own.

The second telling came in the years her hair had begun to grey, of a scatter of platforms built out over the shallows of a lake far to the north-west, homes raised on stilts above the water by households that had kept them, by all report, three generations and more without trouble; and it was told that the platforms burned in a single night to the waterline, the charred piles left standing bare in the shallows after, with no household of that lake troubling, from that night forward, to build there again. This too reached Iseabail only as a tale carried by traders, and this too she kept apart in her own mind from anything of her brother's, though she noted, to herself and to no one else, that the island seemed in those years to be keeping a good many silences at once, each its own, none of them, so far as she could tell, speaking to the others.

## VI. Of Her Own Ending

She lived to see her own grandchildren grown and her brother's hair, like her own, gone entirely white, the two of them old together on the same stretch of moor they had been children on, the covering stone still lifted each spring and laid each autumn by hands that were, by then, mostly her own children's rather than hers or Eachann's. He died first, in the fourteen hundred and ninety-ninth year, in his own bed, of no more than the ordinary failing of a long life well spent, sixty-four years old and easy in his going, by every account his own household gave of it afterward; and Iseabail, sitting with him the last night, asked him once more, more for the old habit's sake than for any hope she still held of an answer, whether he meant to leave the moor with the question still hers alone to carry. He smiled at that, she told her own children after, in the way he had always smiled at the asking, and said only that some things were carried better than they were told, and that she had carried this one a long while now without it once breaking her, which was, he thought, answer enough to give her of what kind of thing it was, if not what the thing itself had been.

She never told her children, at the end, that she believed him, nor that she did not; only that she had stopped, some years before his dying, asking the question to be answered, and asked it after only to hear him turn it aside once more in the old familiar way, the way a woman keeps a worn path across a field long after the errand that first wore it has been forgotten. She died in the fifteen hundred and tenth year, eighty years old, in the same house her father had died in, with the covering stone lying undisturbed on the moor a little way off exactly as she had left it every autumn of her life, and was buried, at her own asking, within sight of it, so that whatever her own eyes had never been let see might, at the least, go on lying within her sight for as long as sight was any use to the buried.

The stone stands there yet, lifted every spring and laid every autumn by a household that keeps the custom now with no more reason given than that it was Iseabail's way before it was theirs, and no cord has gone over that lip from that day to this.
