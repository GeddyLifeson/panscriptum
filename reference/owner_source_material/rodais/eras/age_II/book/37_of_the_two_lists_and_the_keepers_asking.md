# Of the Two Lists and the Asking of the Nine

*GE 4,325 – 4,390*

*This is kept at Dùn ìseal's own hearth, by the reciters who took it from Eòin Beul-binn's own mouth before he died, as the telling of the last of the Stone Kings.*

## I. Of the Last Fields and the Last Fords

In the years after Ailis of Dùn dhearg was laid on her own slope, the hills went on filling the way a cup fills that no one is watching: a household here, a ford crossed there, and no one able afterward to say which handful of years had done it. In the south-eastern hills, where wild cattle came down to drink at a stony crossing, a household settled by the ford and took the calves born on the near bank for its own, rearing them apart from the wild herd until, within three generations, the cattle of that ford were spoken of the length of the coast as the best in those hills; the ford came to be called for the cattle, Àth fhiadhaich, the ford of the wild kind. On the Abhainn fhionn, people out of the Red Hill's own country found an old landing worn into the bank, stones set for a boat's keel by hands none living could name, and built their houses about it and called the place for what they had found, the ancient headland. In the damp north-west a household drained a hill with ditches dug by their own hands, and the ditches carry water still, though the diggers' own names are forgotten; and on the Abhainn ghorm in the north-east, a household built its houses in two rows facing each other across a single trodden way, the first street any teller remembers on Dia-thìr, though none of them thought of it as a street, only as a convenient way to borrow fire from a neighbour without crossing open ground in the rain.

Further south a house was raised with its walls limed white from the burnt shell of the old middens along that coast, and the place took its name from the lime before the household that raised it had a name of its own; and on the far eastern shore, where the coast bent back on the sea and the geese came down in their season, a household settled the last ford of the age, the easternmost hearth then burning on the main island, and went out after the geese in boats no larger than a man could carry on his back.

None of this made a chapter in any list. The Stone Kings' own roll, which named every household that came in to Dùn ìseal to be counted, gave no room to a ford where the cattle were good or a lime-white house on a southern plain; it named holders, and marriages, and deaths, and left the filling of the island to be told, if it was told at all, at the hearths that did the filling. It is only because those hearths went on telling it, generation on generation, long after the households had forgotten which of their own great-grandmothers had first driven a stake into that particular ground, that any of it is known now.

## II. Of Catrìona

Catrìona was born at Dùn ìseal in the year four thousand one hundred of the Grove, granddaughter's granddaughter of a granddaughter of the king, though by then no one at the hill troubled much to count the degrees; she was of the blood, and that was the whole of what anyone needed to know of her standing. She grew up on the hill in the long years the list does not trouble to fill, when the king himself was oftener at it than any holder, and she was already an old woman by the reckoning of any hearth outside her own blood, and still young by the reckoning inside it, when the holding of Dùn ìseal came to her.

She had a memory that the hill-folk who came to be judged by her called, half in wonder and half in complaint, a curse to any man who hoped his grandmother's dealings had been forgotten. A household's line she could give back seven names without pausing to think, the marriages and the deaths and which strip of grazing had gone with which daughter, and she used the gift the way her grandfather's grandfather had used the water at the taking of the hill: not to threaten, only to be exact. A man came to her once complaining that his neighbour had moved a boundary stone in the night, and she told him, before he had finished the complaint, that the stone in question had already been moved twice before in his own grandfather's day, and each time by his own family, and asked him whether he wished the full account read out at the ford that summer or would rather let the stone lie where it now stood. He let it lie.

She took the hill formally in the year four thousand three hundred and forty-seven, in her two hundred and forty-seventh year, the twelfth to hold it of the Stone Kings' line and, though none of them yet knew it, the last whose name would go into that roll at all. She did not think of herself as an ending; the tellings give her, in those early years, no thought beyond a woman with a great many households to keep straight in her head, and she set about doing it with the same flat patience she brought to everything else.

That same season, at the Red Hill, a young man named Seumas took up the holding his own line had kept since the palisade first burned and burned again in Uilleam Faire's day; he was seven-and-twenty, the last name the Red Hill's own spoken roll would carry, though he no more knew it than Catrìona knew hers. He and Catrìona had never met before that year. They would meet within it, across a fire at the ford, and neither of them, so far as anyone at either hill ever heard either of them say, thought anything of the meeting beyond the ordinary business of two neighbours who had never before had cause to sit down together.

## III. Of the Two Lists

It had never before been done, in all the years the ford gathering had drawn the hill people and the salt people and the flint people together, that the whole roll of the Stone Kings and the whole roll of the Red Hill should be spoken out in the one place on the one night, first name to last, each list heard entire by ears that had heard the other list entire as well. Whether it was Catrìona's own wish, in her first summer of holding, to have her name set down properly beside the men and women who had held the hill before her, or whether it was only that the two rolls had grown long enough by then that the idea of hearing them side by side had occurred to more than one mind at once, the tellings do not agree, and no one troubled, at the time, to ask.

The reciter of Dùn ìseal stood first, a man called Eòin, whom the hill-folk knew for the sweetness of his voice long before they knew him for anything else, and he gave the roll from its beginning: Ailean Mòr and the bank he raised in the snow; Gormshuil at the gate, greying while her father came in each autumn no older than her own sons; Dòmhnall Clachach and his stones that only moved the quarrels they were meant to settle; down through Mòrag the Hard and Iain Dubh brought home from the shingle at Baile ghorm and Tormod Liath who went truly grey and Eilidh Ghlas who sent her younger son west unnamed, and Calum Ciar, who had asked that the lot be drawn in his own sight and been refused, the refusal spoken as plainly as the granting of anything else would have been. When Eòin came at last to Catrìona's own name he said it, once, and sat down, and she felt, sitting where every eye at that fire could find her, every face at the ford turn toward her at once, the way a field of standing barley turns all together when the wind changes.

Then a woman called Ùna stood for the Red Hill, and gave that roll in its turn: Beathag Ruadh who had first held the red earth by the lake; Uisdean who rebuilt the palisade through a whole winter under the king's own eye; Seonag nic Uisdein, who weighed her pebble bag before the king and was set into the roll, as every holder of that line was set, "after her father"; Lachlann an Altraim, who took in a fosterling with no name and sent him home again grown; down to Seumas, who had taken the hill that same season and now sat across the fire from Catrìona listening to his own name spoken for the first time in a roll rather than merely in his own household. By the time Ùna had done, the sky over the river had gone the grey that comes before the true grey of morning, and the children of both crowds were long asleep in heaps against their mothers' knees, and no one now living could say, the tellings admit plainly, how many hours the two rolls together had taken, only that it was the whole of one night.

The king sat among the listeners the entire while, his cloak drawn round him against the river's damp, saying nothing and asked nothing, one more grey-haired man among a crowd of grey-haired men, until Eòin came to his own name in its place near the beginning of the roll and some of those nearest him, glancing sideways as a person will at a thing he is not sure he ought to be looking at, saw him smile. A man hearing his own name read out among four hundred others he had buried or begotten or both is, by any account, a small enough matter. It was told after, at more than one hearth, as though it had been a large one, and Catrìona, asked about it in her own middle years by one of her grandchildren, said only that she had seen it too, and that it was the one time in all her years of holding the hill that she had seen him smile at hearing anything said of himself.

## IV. Of the Cord and the Districts

It was in those same years that Catrìona set her own people to counting the households of the hills plainly, a knot to a hearth, on a cord kept at Dùn ìseal for that purpose and no other. She did it, she told those who asked, because she found she could no longer carry the whole tally in her own head as her grandmother's grandmother once had, the hills having grown past the reach of any one memory, however good; and having said so once, plainly, she never troubled to explain it further to anyone who asked twice. The cord grew through her years of holding until it bore, by the reckoning of those who came after and counted the knots themselves, some nine hundred of them, the largest count made on Dia-thìr in that whole age. The king's own kin were not upon it. There was no counting them any longer by then, the tellings say, and no one at Dùn ìseal thought to try.

In the same years the keepers of the slab, who for generations past had been drawn by lot from the households nearest the crack in the high valleys, began to be drawn instead from every part of the hills, a household of the far south or the eastern marches standing as good a chance of the lot as a household that had lived its whole life within a day's walk of Tobar dhìreach. Catrìona neither ordered this nor opposed it; it happened, the tellings say, the way water finds a wider bed when the narrow one has filled, and by the middle of her years the keepers had settled at nine, one drawn for each of nine districts of the hills that no one had ever formally marked out on the ground but that every household somehow knew the edges of, the way a family knows where its own dooryard ends without a stone to show it. The nine met at the slab only when one of their number died and a new lot had to be drawn, and at no other time; the rest of the year each kept the custom in their own country and answered to no one for it.

Tobar dhìreach, where the keepers' house stood over Somhairle's own grave, came in these years to be a place people walked to for its own sake and not only on the business of a lot, households arriving from country so far off that their own grandparents had never heard the crack under the slab so much as mentioned at a neighbour's hearth. What they came to see, when asked, none of them could very well say. They came, and stood a while, and went home again, and the keepers let them, asking nothing of them and offering nothing beyond the standing there.

## V. Of the Handful That All Burned Together

The measured handful of the coloured coal, kept since Anna nic Coinnich's own day at Achadh dhìreach, had by Catrìona's years spread to every hearth in the hills without ever once being written into a lot or a judgement; it was simply done, everywhere, in the dark turning of the year, each household burning its own small share, measured in a copy of the stone cup Niocol Snaidhear had shaped near two thousand years before, on the one night that every other household in reach of it also burned. No one had commanded that the nights should agree. They had come to agree the way neighbours come to rise at the same hour who have no bell between them but the same rising sun, each household watching for the first low dark flame on the nearest hill and lighting its own the moment it saw it, until across the whole breadth of the hills the fires went up within the same short hour of the same dark night, and stayed low, and went out together before the next dawn. That night had, by then, a name of its own among the hill-folk, Oidhche na Glaice, the night of the handful, and it fell at Samhain, which has been the night of the handful ever since.

Only one household, in all the country the handful had come to reach, would not burn it at all. They were of the line of Torcall Tochlair, who had dug for another vein near the black hill in the east near two thousand years before Catrìona's own birth, and whose brother had been crushed there along with another digger when the ground gave; Torcall's own household had stayed by the open trench long after the vein was given up, holding the ground where the two men died as their own, and their descendants, asked why they would not burn the handful with the rest of the hills, gave no single answer twice. Some said the coal had already taken what it meant to take from that family and would get no more of it burned or unburned. Others said only that a household that had stayed where it was told to leave saw no reason to start keeping customs it had never been asked to keep in the first place. Catrìona, hearing of it, sent no one to compel them, and no one at the slab suggested that she should; the household went its own way in this as it had gone its own way over the trench, and the hills, having no law to press against it, let it.

## VI. Of the Column and the Old Middens

In the seventeenth year of her holding a hand went out from Catrìona's own hill to Cnoc ghorm, where the ancient column stood with its marks worn shallow by the weather of an age no one could number, and recut the grooves along their old lines so that the words should not be lost to the rain. Whoever the hand belonged to left no name behind, only the work; and some who came after, comparing the cut marks against tellings of how the column had once run, held that a single stroke had changed in the recutting, though none of them could ever agree which stroke it was, or prove that any had changed at all. The column stood after that as it had stood before, telling whoever could read it only that the people who raised it had always been there, in words that every gathering at the ford still spoke aloud at its opening.

Further east, at the household places about Seann Dunn, a family digging a new house-floor into the shoulder of an old midden struck, not two spans down, a row of flat stones each cut with a single stroke, set upright in the old refuse the way a hearthstone is set, and knew them at once, from tellings that had reached even that far corner of the hills, for the tally stones of the people who had once lived along that coast and counted their own dead upon them. They did not take the stones for their own use, nor break them up for the new floor, though it would have cost them less labour to do either. They set the stones back in the order they had found them lying, as near as memory and care could manage it, and built their house-floor a little further along the shoulder of the midden instead, losing a morning's digging for the sake of ground that had, by then, no living person left to claim it.

Along the south-eastern coast, in those same years, the custom grew of rowing the newly dead out to Eilean ìseal rather than burying them on the mainland shore, a custom no one at those hearths could say for certain they had learned from anyone rather than come to on their own; some held it was taken from the graves the old coast people had left on the low island across the strait, and others held that a low island surrounded by water was simply where any sensible coast puts what it does not wish disturbed, whether the thought had first come from a stranger's grave or from a fisherman's own good sense. Both might have been true at once, or neither; the tellings, for once, let the question stand without troubling to settle it.

And out past the same coast, on Eilean chrom where Gilleasbuig Beag had been rowed and left with no boat to bring him back, no household in all those years put out a boat to fish its waters, though the fishing there was said to be as good as anywhere on that shore. His fire had been seen three nights running from the mainland, the tellings say, in the first year after he was left, and then no more; and whether that had been a true fire or only three nights of a frightened coast seeing what it feared to see, no one now living could settle either, and the island stayed unfished, empty, and left alone.

## VII. Of the Asking of the Nine

In the twenty-ninth year of her holding, in the year four thousand three hundred and seventy-six, the nine keepers of the slab came up together across the trodden ground of the ford while the gathering stood at its fullest, salt-carriers and wool-carriers and flint-carriers all about them, and stood before Catrìona where she sat among her own kin, and asked to be heard.

She knew them, all nine, before any of them had said a word; she had held their lines in her own memory since before any of the nine now standing there had been drawn by lot at all, and she named the eldest of them, who had come forward to speak for the rest, by his own line seven names back before he had finished bowing to her. He did not seem surprised by it. He had likely expected nothing less.

What they asked was this: that their charge over the slab, the lot, and the handful be declared before the whole gathering, spoken out as plainly as the two rolls had been spoken the year of her own taking, so that it should stand fixed past any doubt, and so that no king who came after her should ever again think, as Calum Ciar had thought once, that the slab was a thing to be reached for by anyone who held Dùn ìseal, or ask that the lot be drawn in his own sight as though the sight of a king could weigh upon a stone that had lain over the vein since before the grove was planted.

Catrìona looked at them a long while and said nothing at first, and the old keeper, mistaking the silence for a hearing not yet finished, opened his mouth to say more. She looked past the nine of them while she considered, out over the whole crowded ground of the gathering, the salt people on their own bank and the hill people on theirs and the flint people crowded on the rise beyond the river, every one of them, so far as she could tell from where she sat, waiting on nothing more urgent than the next trade or the next marriage to be agreed at that year's fires, none of them, she was fairly sure, aware that nine old men and women stood asking her, at that moment, to fix something that had never yet been fixed.

"Not this year," she said.

The old keeper's mouth, already open to press the matter, stopped where it was.

"Ask me again," Catrìona said.

She gave them, in the whole of it, neither a refusal nor a consent, and the nine went back across the trodden ground to their own fires by the slab's own country to wait, as keepers had waited on lots and on kings for longer than any of the nine then living could have told her, without complaint, and without any sign that they took her answer for anything other than exactly what it was: an answer given, and a door left standing as it had stood before, neither opened wider nor shut.

## VIII. Of Her Growing Years

She held the hill a long while after that night at the ford, longer than any single reign the hill-folk outside her own blood had known, though shorter, by a very great deal, than the reigns of the blood who had held it before her. Eòin, whose voice had carried the whole roll of the Stone Kings across one long night at the ford, died in his eightieth year, an old man by any measure the hills themselves would use, and was carried to his own household's ground with no more ceremony than any other hill-folk death; and Ùna, who had answered him roll for roll out of the Red Hill's own line, followed him within two years, in her seventy-third, so that within a short span both voices that had first spoken the two rolls whole were gone from the hills that had heard them, and the telling of that single night passed, from then on, at second hand.

Catrìona herself went on a good while past either of them, keeping her households in her head as she always had, judging a boundary stone here and a marriage there with the same flat exactness she had brought to it from her first year of holding. It was in her two hundred and eighty-ninth year that the sickness came on her that would not leave again, a slow failing that those about her on the hill first took for nothing more than the ordinary tiredness of a long-held office and then, watching it deepen through one whole turning of the seasons, could no longer mistake for anything but what it was. She kept her seat at Dùn ìseal through it as long as she was able, still hearing what was brought to her, still able, when a household came before her with some quarrel over a strip of grazing, to give its whole line back seven names without having to be reminded of a single one of them.

The nine keepers, hearing that she had grown ill, sent no word asking again. They had been told to ask her again, and an asking made of a woman too worn to answer it plainly would have been, in the reckoning of that country, no true asking at all; and so they waited still, by their own slab, in their own nine districts, keeping the custom she had neither fixed nor unfixed, exactly as they had kept it before she was ever asked, and exactly as they would go on keeping it after.
