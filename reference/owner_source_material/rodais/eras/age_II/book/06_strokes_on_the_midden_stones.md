# Of the Strokes on the Midden Stones

*GE 290 – 388*

*At Seann Chwen the salters still point out the flat stone under the fourth boat-shed and say a hill man once wept over it, though none of them now living saw him do it, and the telling has grown shorter with every hearth it has passed through.*

## I. Of the Road by Gesture

Dùghall was nine years old the summer his father first let him come the whole way down to the shore rather than turning him back at the last ridge with the pack-ponies to mind, and he remembered afterward, better than he remembered most of that year, the smell of the place before he saw it: salt and rot and woodsmoke, and under those a fourth smell he had no word for and never found one, which he came in time to think of simply as the smell of the Seann-Dhaoine, the way a byre has its own smell and a smokehouse its own, and no more to be explained than either.

His father carried hides down that road, cured goat and deer hide from the high pastures where Dùghall's people wintered their herds, and hill cheese pressed hard enough to travel a week without spoiling, and he had carried both for longer than Dùghall had been alive, walking a track that ran three days from the pastures to the coast and three days back, by way of a ford and a wood and a long turn round a bog that swallowed a cart to the axle if a man forgot to keep to the causeway of laid brush. He carried them to trade, and what he traded them for, over a lifetime of the road, was whatever the shore had that the pastures did not: strings of pale shell, salted fish packed in baskets lined with kelp, and blades of a black stone that took an edge no flint on the hill could match and kept it longer, though no one on the hill had ever found where the black stone came from or how it was worked, only that the Seann-Dhaoine traded it and would trade nothing else half so willingly.

There was no tongue between them. Dùghall's father had walked the road forty years and could no more speak a word of what the Seann-Dhaoine spoke among themselves than Dùghall, watching him that first summer, could speak it himself; and it seemed to Dùghall, young as he was, a strange thing that two peoples could trade across the same stretch of shingle for two generations and never once trouble to learn each other's words for the things they were trading, until he grew older and understood that his father had tried, once, as a young man, and had been met with a courtesy so complete and so unyielding that he had never tried again. The Seann-Dhaoine would take a hide from his hand and turn it over and weigh it with their eyes, and hold up so many fingers for so many strings of shell, and if the count did not please him he could shake his head and hold up fewer fingers of his own until it did; but if he pointed to his own mouth and then to theirs, meaning what word do you call this, they only looked at him, not unkindly, the way a man looks at a child asking something he has decided not to answer, and went on with the weighing.

"They are not unwilling," his father told him, the first night they camped within sound of the sea, with the ponies hobbled and the fire built low against a wind that smelled of nothing Dùghall knew. "I used to think they were unwilling. They are not. They simply do not mean to be understood, and there is a difference between a door shut against you and a door that was never built to open at all."

Dùghall asked him, because he was nine, and had not yet learned the questions a boy holds back from a man three days from home with the whole evening free for talk, whether the Seann-Dhaoine had a king of their own, the way the hill had one, and whether that king was old the way his own king was said to be old and yet not old at all. His father was quiet long enough that Dùghall thought he would not answer, and then said only that no hill man had ever seen anything among the Seann-Dhaoine that looked like a king, or like a hearing-house, or like a bank round a hill; that they lived in stone houses set low against the wind, with roofs of turf grown so thick and so old that from a distance the houses looked like the ground itself had swelled; and that whatever held them together, if anything held them together at all beyond the shore and the boats and the middens at the backs of their houses, no hill man who had traded with them for two lifetimes had ever come close enough to it to say.

They traded that first summer as they had traded every summer before it, Dùghall standing a little behind his father with the ponies' lead-ropes in his fist and his eyes going everywhere at once: at the low houses with their smoke rising thin and grey from holes in the turf rather than any chimney; at the boats drawn up on the shingle, sewn from planks in a manner no hill boat used, without a single dug timber in them that he could see; at the Seann-Dhaoine themselves, who were neither taller nor shorter than hill folk, and dressed in skins sewn close against the wet, and moved about their work with an unhurried economy that made his father's brisk hill way of doing things look, by comparison, almost wasteful. Nothing about that first summer told him anything was different from the fifty summers that had come before it, or the fifty that his father told him, without much interest, would likely come after.

## II. Of the Marks at the Midden's Edge

It was some years later, in the eighteenth summer of Dùghall's own carrying, that he first noticed the stones.

He was three-and-twenty that year, walking the road on his own for the first time with a string of ponies that was his in all but the naming of it, his father having grown too heavy in the knees for three days of bog-causeway and having handed the road to his son the way a man hands on a thing he is glad to be rid of and sorry to be rid of in the same breath. Dùghall knew the shore by then as well as he knew the high pastures, knew which household traded fairly and which wanted watching, knew the particular stretch of shingle where the black stone blades were always best worked and the particular household, three houses back from the water, whose midden ran along the whole eastern side of their turf-roofed house in a long grey ridge of shell and ash and broken bone, taller each year than it had been the year before, the way a midden grows behind any household that has lived long in one place.

What he had not noticed before, or had noticed without troubling to ask himself why it should be noticed at all, was that flat stones stood set on their edges at intervals along that ridge, driven into the ground like small grey teeth, and that each one, when the light fell right across it, showed a mark cut into its face: a single stroke, deep enough to hold shadow, and nothing else. Some stones showed one stroke. A few, further along the ridge, showed two, cut close together as though by the same hand at the same sitting. None showed more than two.

He asked his father about it that autumn, back on the hill with the season's trading told over and counted, and his father said he had seen the stones himself, years back, and had asked a Seann-Dhaoine once, by pointing and by the beginnings of a question he could not finish in gesture, what they were for, and had got nothing for his asking but the same courteous, unyielding look that met every question of that kind. "I stopped asking," his father said. "A man who trades on a shore for forty years learns which of his questions the shore means to answer and which it does not, and he does himself no good going on asking the second kind."

Dùghall went on asking, though only of himself, and the stones stayed in the back of his thought the way a half-heard word stays in the back of a man's thought until he happens on the rest of it. He began, without much deciding to, to count them on each visit, and to notice that the count grew, slowly, by no more than a stone or two in a summer, but steadily, the ridge lengthening a little further each year along the side of the low turf house, and the household living beside it going on with its fishing and its weaving and its unhurried work as though the growing ridge of stones were no more worth remarking on than the growing midden it stood beside.

It was in his seven-and-twentieth summer, the same year word reached the hill of a burning on the Red Hill's own shore far to the west, a matter that meant nothing to Dùghall beyond an evening's talk round a fire, that he stood longer than he meant to before the ridge of stones and worked out, counting on his fingers the way a boy counts, that there were now eleven of them where nine summers before there had been six, and that no one in the household beside the midden had ever, in all his visits, set a new stone while he was there to see it done. He asked himself, standing on that shingle with his ponies shifting behind him and the tide starting to turn, what manner of thing was counted five at a time in some years and not at all in others, cut once and left, never added to, never changed once cut.

He had no answer that summer, or the next, and he told himself, the way his father had told himself before him, that it was a question the shore did not mean to answer, and went on with his trading.

## III. Of the Stroke Cut After a Burial

The answer, when it came, came to him unasked, in the way such things most often do.

He was seven-and-sixty that summer, an old man by any hill reckoning, with sons of his own long grown and one of them now carrying the greater share of the packs at his shoulder, and grey enough all through, not only at the temple, that the household by the midden, who had never once in his lifetime of visiting given any sign of marking one hill trader from another, seemed to him, for the first time, to greet him as something other than a stranger, and did it wordlessly, there being still no words between them, in a kind of settled ease, an old woman of the house handing him dried fish before he had finished unpacking, as though the handing were no longer a trade freshly struck but a thing already agreed between them long before, renewed rather than made.

It was on the third morning of that visit, with the mist not yet burned off the water and the household's boats still drawn up rather than launched, that Dùghall woke in the lean-to he was given to sleep in and heard, through the turf wall, a sound he had not heard on that shore before: low voices, several together, rising and falling in a way no ordinary talk of a household's morning work ever took, something with a shape to it, a beginning and a middle he could feel coming though he understood not one word of it, the way a man who cannot read music can still feel a tune reaching for its close.

He did not go out while it lasted, for something in the sound itself told him not to, the same instinct that would have kept him from walking into a hill household's grief before he was asked. It went on the better part of the morning, and when it ended he heard, instead, a single low note, held a long while and let go, and then nothing, and then the ordinary sounds of a household returning to itself: a pot set on a fire, a child's voice, the drag of a boat's keel over shingle as the men of the house at last put out to fish, later than they had ever put out on any morning of Dùghall's eight-and-fifty summers of visiting.

He went out then, and found the old woman who had handed him the fish, sitting alone at the door of the house with her hands still in her lap, and he did not ask her anything, for he had long since learned there was nothing to ask that she would answer; but he looked, without meaning to make a study of it, toward the ridge of stones along the midden's edge, and saw that the newest of them, the twelfth, cut some years before and standing bare since, now carried, beneath its single old stroke, a second stroke, fresh enough that pale stone still showed raw in the groove of it, not yet weathered to the grey of the stroke above.

He stood a long while looking at that stone, the fresh cut and the old one side by side on the same grey face, and it came to him, all at once and entire, the way a thing comes to a man only after his mind has been carrying the pieces of it separately for twenty years without knowing they belonged together: the low voices in the turf house that morning, the single held note let go at the end of it, the boats kept ashore past their hour, and now the fresh stroke cut beneath the old. His own mother's grave-mound came to him then, on the hill, and the stone his father had set at its head with her name scratched into it by a hand that had never learned to cut letters cleanly; and he understood, standing on that shingle with the mist still not fully burned away, that he was looking at the same thing in another people's hand: a household marking, one stroke at a time, the going of its own dead.

A word the hill had begun to use of the king came to him too, though he said nothing of it that day to anyone: something quietly said among those old enough to have watched three holders of Dùn ìseal come and go while the man who crowned each of them stayed exactly as he had always been, that the king did not count his dead the way other men counted theirs, for he had never yet been given cause to stop counting and start remembering instead. Dùghall was no teller and made nothing of the thought beyond turning it once in his own mind and setting it down again, but it stayed with him longer than most of what he carried home from that shore.

He counted the whole ridge again that morning, slower than he had ever counted it before, and found that every household along that stretch of shore kept its own stones the same way, one ridge to a house, and that some stones bore a single stroke only and some bore two or three, cut close together as brothers might be cut close together, and a very few, at the old ends of the oldest ridges, bore strokes enough that he lost the count and had to begin again. He did not ask the old woman anything that day, or any day after, for he understood now, as clearly as his father had ever understood it without being told, that this was one of the things the shore did not mean to answer by gesture, because it had never been a question that gesture could hold; but he carried the understanding home with him at the season's end the way another man might carry a wound, quietly, and told his father, who was by then too old to make the road himself and glad only of the telling of it, what he had seen.

His father heard him out and said nothing for a long while, and then said only that he was glad, at the last, to have an answer to a question he had stopped asking forty years before, and that he was sorrier than he could easily say that the answer, now it had come, was that kind of answer and not some other.

## IV. Of the Grandson and the Fewer Hearths

Dùghall walked that road another six-and-twenty summers after the morning of the fresh stroke, through years in which the hill itself grew busier behind him than it had ever been in his father's time, a king's blood settling deeper into valleys that had known no king at all when Dùghall was a boy, though little enough of that reached him on a road that ran, each summer, the same three days to the same stretch of shingle and the same three days home. He carried the stones with him after that morning the way a man carries a thing he has decided not to speak of past the one telling it needs; he did not mention them again to his sons as they grew old enough to walk the road at his shoulder, for a thing seen once with such plainness needs no repeating, whatever wish a man might have to spare his sons the seeing of it, and he judged that each of them, in his own year, would see the ridges for himself, the way he had, and he judged rightly.

He was one-and-seventy, and had not carried the whole road himself in some summers, when he took his eldest son's boy down to the shore for what both of them understood, without either saying so, was likely to be the old man's last visit to it. The boy was near enough grown to carry his own share of the packs and had been down the road twice before at his father's side, but never yet with his grandfather, and Dùghall had asked for the going the way a man asks for a thing he means to have, plainly, and had been given it without argument by a household that had learned long since not to argue with him over which of his own wants were worth the arguing.

They came down to the shore on a grey morning with rain not far behind them, and Dùghall, walking the last stretch more slowly than he remembered ever walking it, past the ford and the wood and the long turn round the bog, found the shore quieter than he had ever found it in two-and-sixty years of coming to it. Two of the turf houses nearest the water stood roofless, their thatch long fallen in and not renewed, the walls softening back into the shape of the ground the way an old grave-mound softens; and where he counted, from long habit, the smoke rising from the houses further back, he found himself counting on fewer fingers than the count had ever taken before, four threads of smoke where a man his age remembered counting nine, and no boats drawn up before two houses that he remembered, in his father's day, as always having a boat.

He led his grandson along the shore to the ridge of stones by the household that had first shown him the fresh stroke, all those years before, and found the old woman gone, as he had known she must be gone, and a younger woman in her place who did not know him, or gave no sign of knowing him, though she took the hides from his hands with the same unhurried courtesy her mother, or her mother's mother, had shown his father before either of them was born. The ridge of stones had grown longer still, stone on stone further than Dùghall's eye could easily follow in one look, and near its older end, where the earliest stones stood weathered grey and soft-edged with age, he found gaps in the ground where stones must once have stood and stood no longer, fallen or taken or simply sunk, he could not tell which, into turf that had grown up round the place they had been.

"Count them," he told his grandson, not because the boy could make anything more of the count than Dùghall himself had ever made of it, but because he wanted the boy to have done it once, with his own hand and his own eye, before the old man who had first taught him to look at that ridge was no longer there to tell him to. The boy counted, moving along the stones with his lips working the numbers, and lost the count twice and began again, and came back to his grandfather at last to say that he could not be sure, past a certain point, whether he was counting stones the household still kept or stones that had simply fallen and lay half-buried among the ones still standing, and that either way the number was larger than any number he had learned to hold easily in his head at once.

Dùghall said only that he had found the same trouble himself, the first time and every time since, and that it was, he had come to think, no bad thing for a man to meet a count he could not finish, if the count itself was an honest one. He did not tell the boy, that day or after, all that he had come in two-and-sixty years to guess and never to know for certain: that the smoke he had counted thinner every visit these last ten summers, and the boats missing from before houses that had always kept a boat, and the roofless walls softening back into the ground where households he had traded with all his life no longer answered a knock, seemed to him, taken together with the lengthening ridges of stone, less like a shore that was simply quiet that year than like a shore that was, house by house and summer by summer, running out of itself; for it was not a thing he could prove by any count he knew how to keep, only a weight that had settled on him, walking that grey morning past the fallen roofs, that he had no gesture to put down and no one on that shore who would have understood the putting down if he had found one.

He traded what he had brought, that last visit, for less than he had once traded the same weight of hides for, though whether the shore itself had grown poorer or only that household had, he could not have said; and he walked home the three days with his grandson at his shoulder saying little, the boy carrying, without knowing he carried it, the whole of what his grandfather had carried down that road for two-and-sixty years and never once found a way to set down whole for anyone but himself. Dùghall died on the hill the following winter, an old man by any hill man's reckoning and older than most who walked that road before or after him, and it was his grandson, and not his son, who took up the packs the next summer and walked south alone to the shore, counting the stones at the midden's edge before he traded a single hide, the way the old man had taught him, on a morning grey enough, and quiet enough, that the boy stood a long while at the ridge before he could bring himself to begin.
