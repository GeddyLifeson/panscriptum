# Of the Forbidden Marriage and the Trench

*GE 2,211 – 2,327*

## I. Of Ealag and Torcall Tochlair

*This is told at Dùn ìseal by the hearths that still keep the shape of the old spring, though the two households that once stood there are long since gone into other names.*

Ealag was born in a household under the eastern shoulder of Dùn ìseal, the fourth of six, in a year when the hill's flocks did well and nobody troubled to remember which year that was by any other mark. Torcall was born two households over, the son of a man who gathered the coloured coal where it showed in the peat-banks and stream-cuts of the hills and was called Tochlair for it, the digger, a name that stuck to the boy before he had dug so much as a post-hole. The two families kept sheep on the same slope and drew water from the same spring, and the children of both ran together from as far back as either of them could afterward call to mind. Neither could say when it had started, only that it had always been so.

There is a telling, kept longer in that house than most of the rest, of the two of them as children no higher than the sheep-pen rail, caught out in a storm on the open hill with the flock scattered and no grown person near, and Torcall standing over Ealag with his own coat pulled off and held above her head against the rain until his father found them both wet through and laughing under it, the coat long since useless and neither of them minding. Whether it happened as the telling has it, or happened at all, no one now living can say; but it was told at both hearths for years afterward, by the mothers of the two children to each other, as proof of a thing neither household thought it needed proving.

They grew up counting each other's sheep by the ear-notch without being asked, and knowing which of the two spring paths the other would take of a morning by nothing more than habit, and by the time they were old enough to be spoken of for marrying, the whole slope had long since stopped thinking of them as two people who might or might not be joined and thought of them instead as a thing already joined, waiting only for its proper words.

By the time Ealag was grown enough to be spoken of for marrying, nobody on the hill thought to ask whether she and Torcall would be given to each other. It was taken for a settled thing, the way the path from the spring to the houses was taken for a settled thing, worn in by so many feet that no one remembered the first of them. Torcall asked her outright at the shearing in the year she turned sixteen, standing in wet wool to the elbows with the whole slope watching, and she laughed at him for asking what everyone already knew, and said yes in front of forty people, and that should have been the end of the matter and the start of another.

Word went between the two households in the old way, an elder of each sent to the other's hearth with a measure of salt, which is accepted or is not. Torcall's mother carried the salt across herself, being a woman who trusted nothing she had not done with her own hands, and she was not gone long. She came back with the salt still in its cloth and her face set in a way her son had not seen it set before, not at his father's dying, not at any hard winter.

"They will not have it," she said.

"Which of them said so?"

"All of them. The old man spoke for the rest, but I watched their faces, and none of them thought he spoke wrong."

Torcall went himself the next day, to hear it said to his own face rather than carried to him at second hand, and the elders of Ealag's household gathered in their doorway to tell him so, all of them together, as if one alone could not be trusted to hold the line against him. He asked why. He asked it plainly, the way a man asks the price of a thing before he buys it, expecting a plain answer back.

"It cannot be," said the eldest, a woman named Beathag who had held that household longer than Torcall had been alive.

"Cannot, or will not?"

"It cannot be," Beathag said again, as though the words themselves, said twice, were an answer, and turned back into the house.

He went to Ealag's father next, and her father would not meet his eye, and he went to two of her uncles, and they said the same three words between them and would say no more, and in the end he stood in the yard with the sheep moving round his legs and understood that no one on that hill was going to tell him why, because either none of them knew, or all of them had sworn not to say, and there was no way from where he stood to tell one from the other.

He tried the oldest woman of his own household too, thinking that if the refusal came of some old quarrel it might be known on his own side of it as well as the other, but his grandmother only shook her head at him and said that she had never heard the two families named together in any grievance in all her years, not over grazing, not over water, not over anything a person could point to, and that this was the strangest part of it to her: a refusal with no history behind it that she had ever been told of, standing as flat and as final as one with forty years of bad blood at its back.

Last of all he went to Ealag, at the spring, in the evening, when the water was the colour of the sky and there was nobody else to hear them.

"Do you know why?" he said.

"No," she said. "I asked my mother. She held my two hands in hers and told me it was not a thing I would ever be told, not by her, not by anyone living, and that I was to stop asking, because asking would not open it and might close other doors besides. Then she cried, which I have never seen her do, not once, not at any burying."

"That is not a reason."

"No," Ealag said. "It is not."

They stood a while at the spring without more to say, because there was nothing further to be got from either side of it, no thread that would not come away loose in the hand for the pulling. Of the true cause the households never spoke again in either of their lifetimes, and no telling that has come down from that hill has ever named it; it is set down here only as it stands, refused and unexplained, because that is how it stands in every version the reciters keep. Already, in those years, the Dia-thìrich had begun to order themselves by blood in ways that ran deeper than any one match or any one grudge, long before the vein gave them further cause to reckon who was kin to whom; and whatever lay beneath the refusal of Ealag and Torcall, it lay in that older ordering, and was not brought out into the light for either of them to see.

## II. Of the Marriage at the Ford

Ealag was given instead to a household of the ford, where salt had first been traded for wool in the days of Raghnall Ceannfhionn, and where households gathered every year for the fair in the same season the shearing had been held on the hill. Her husband was a fisher of the shallows there, a quiet man named Donnchadh who had asked for her honestly and without knowing, when he asked, that there had ever been another asking before his. She went to him in the year she turned seventeen, the two thousand two hundred and twenty-fourth year of the hill, the spring after the refusal.

She was married at the ford itself, in sight of the water, with the households of the ford standing about her on the bank in their fair-day cloaks and the salmon running so thick that season that two boys were sent to keep them from the shallows where the feet of the guests would trouble the fishing later. Donnchadh gave her a comb of horn he had carved himself through the winter evenings, clumsily, so that one tooth was shorter than the rest, and she wore it every fair-day after for the whole of her marriage, the short tooth catching on her hair in a way she never had mended. She did not weep at her own wedding, though she had wept twice in the week before it, alone, at the spring on the hill where no one from the ford could see her; and she came to her husband's house that evening dry-eyed and quiet, and was, by every account that survives of her, a good wife to him from the first night to the last, whatever else she carried that he did not ask after.

She did not tell him of Torcall Tochlair until they had been three years married, and when she told him he said only that it explained a look she sometimes had at fair-time, and never asked more, which she was grateful for the whole of her life after.

It was at that same fair, some years on, that she first heard a thing recited that had not been recited before. The ford had long drawn every kind of household to its yearly gathering, to trade salt for wool and wool for whatever the sea brought in, and it had always been a place where matches were spoken of and made, the elders of one hearth walking across to another with their measure of salt as Torcall's mother had once walked. But in that year, and it is remembered as the first year, before any match was spoken a household stood up and gave its line: the names of its dead, back seven generations, father to father and mother to mother, said aloud in the hearing of the whole fair, so that anyone who knew of a blood too close, or a blood forbidden for some reason nobody living could give, might speak before the salt was carried across. It began, so the telling goes, with a household that had been burned once already by a match no one had thought to question until it was too late to unmake, though whose household that was the tellers no longer agree.

Ealag stood in the crowd at the ford and heard the lines given, one after another, seven names back on each side, fathers answering fathers and mothers answering mothers, and she thought of Beathag in the doorway saying *it cannot be* and giving no seventh name, no sixth, no first, nothing a listener could have weighed. She thought that if such a reciting had stood between her and Torcall, the whole hill would have heard whatever it was that only the old people knew, said aloud where it could be judged, rather than kept behind four shut mouths. She said as much to Donnchadh that evening, and he said that a thing kept behind shut mouths might be kept there for good reason as easily as bad, and she said she supposed that was true, and neither of them believed it entirely.

The reciting at the ford held from that year on, and other fairs along the coast took it up after, until it was a common thing at any gathering where a match might be made: the households of the common people giving their lines aloud, seven names back, the same distance the Mason's own line was said to keep, though no common household claimed kinship with that older line, only the same caution about it. It is the oldest tally of ordinary blood that the island keeps outside the king's, and no single telling of it has come down whole; each hearth kept its own seven names, and when a hearth failed the names failed with it.

## III. Of the Trench at the Black Hill

Torcall Tochlair did not marry. He gathered the coal as his father had, a few handfuls a season from the banks where it showed, and by all accounts he did it well and without complaint, but something in him had gone restless in a way that gathering what the hills let fall could not answer. He began to ask the older diggers, on the walk home from the peat-banks of an evening, whether the vein under the mountain was the only coal the island held, and the older diggers, who had asked the same question themselves in their own youth and had it answered by their own fathers' silence, told him what they had been told: that the vein chose where it lay, and had not chosen to lie twice.

Torcall was not a man the answer satisfied. He had a brother, Lachann, younger by five years and easier in his temper, who had never much minded being told a thing and made to let it stand, but who loved his brother more than he minded being talked into things, and when Torcall began to speak of a black hill in the east where the ground had a look about it he had heard described by traders from that coast, Lachann said he would go and look with him, if only to see him satisfied and brought home again.

Lachann had a wife of his own by then and a daughter not yet walking, and he told his wife he would be gone a season, no more, and was gone the better part of four years, coming back only twice in that time to see the child grow, once when she could stand and once when she could run from him laughing across the yard and had to be caught. He never complained of the distance in any telling that has come down, and the diggers who worked beside him at the black hill said afterward that he sang more than any of them at the trench face, old songs of the hill country sung in the wrong place, which made the other three laugh at him and made the work go easier for it.

They went east together in the dry season, with two other diggers who had grown as restless as Torcall on less cause, to the black hill, a rise of empty ground in the east, named already for the darkness of its soil before ever a pick touched it. They opened a trench along its shoulder with picks and fire-cracked stone, the same way a well is sunk, working by turns through the wet months and the dry, cutting down through soil the colour of char into rock that gave off no smell of coal and no shine of it either, only a grey stone that came away in slabs.

It was in the fourth year of the digging, in the two thousand two hundred and sixty-seventh year of the hill, that the trench wall gave. There had been rain enough that season to loosen what the diggers had already undercut more than they knew, and the fall came without any of the small warnings that a careful digger learns to read, no trickle of loose stone beforehand, no settling underfoot. Lachann was at the face with one of the other two men when the wall came down; Torcall was above, hauling a basket up on the rope, and heard it rather than saw it, a sound he said afterward he had no word for and never tried twice to find one. He dug with his hands until the others came running with tools, and dug after that too, long past when digging could do any good, and had to be pulled bodily away from the fall by three men before he would stop.

Lachann's wife came out to the black hill herself when word reached her, walking the whole distance with the child on her hip, and stood at the trench a long while without speaking to anyone, and went home again three days later with a handful of the char-black soil tied in a cloth, which she kept at her own hearth for the rest of her life and would let no one touch but herself. She did not blame Torcall to his face, then or ever, though it is told that she never again asked after him by name, and spoke of him only, when she spoke of him at all, as her daughter's uncle at the black hill.

Lachann was buried on the black hill, at the mouth of the trench he had died beside, because Torcall would not have him carried elsewhere, and the other man's people took him home to be buried among his own. Torcall did not go home. He built himself a turf shelter at the trench's edge and stayed there through the winter that followed, and when the diggers who had come with him packed their tools and went back west to the hills, he did not go with them. Word of it reached Ealag at the ford by way of a trader who had stopped at the black hill and seen him there, a man sitting alone by an open trench with his dead brother in the ground a few feet off, still saying, when anyone asked him why he stayed, that the coal was there to be found and he meant to find it.

## IV. Of the Stone That Would Not Break

He was not, in the end, the only one who tried. Word of the black hill's trench had gone about the island in the years it was being dug, as word of any strangeness does, and it woke in other places the same restlessness it had woken in Torcall: if coal could be looked for in the east, it could be looked for anywhere, and more than one household elsewhere on the island took up picks in those years with the same hope Torcall had carried east with him.

The largest of these second attempts was made some distance from the black hill, on rising ground the diggers judged as like to the mountain's own shoulder as any ground on the island could be, by folk who had heard of Torcall's trench but not of Lachann's death, or had heard of it and judged the risk worth taking regardless. They shored their trench with timber where Torcall's had gone unshored, having heard enough of the black hill by then to take that much warning at least, and they set watchers on the lip of the cutting in turns through every shift, and for all their care the ground gave them nothing to watch for. They dug two full seasons, deeper by the end than Torcall's own trench had gone, through soil and then through a bed of common grey stone that gave way at last to more of the same stone beneath it, unbroken, ordinary rock going down further than any of them had tools or patience to follow.

The leader of that digging, a man whose name has not come down with the rest of the telling, was said to have gone down into the cutting himself on the last day and struck the grey stone three times with his own pick before he would call the work done, as if the stone might yet answer a fourth household where it had refused three years of others; and when it did not, he climbed out, and told the rest to bank their fires, and the digging at that hill ended there. They struck at it a while longer out of stubbornness more than hope, and then they stopped, because there is only so long a household can feed itself on the hope of what a hillside has not shown it, and this hillside, in the end, showed them nothing at all.

It was from that failure, more than from Torcall's trench, which after all had found nothing but had not yet been called finished, that the saying began to travel the island: that the vein under the mountain was alone of its kind, that no second coal lay waiting anywhere else to be found by whoever dug hardest for it, and that the stone the second diggers had struck was proof of it, a stone that would not break because there was nothing under it to break for. Some said the black coal itself had chosen its one place in the world the way the seven coals of the old tellings were said to have chosen theirs, and that a stone unwilling to give up anything was simply the island's way of saying the choice had already been made and was not to be argued with by picks.

No one tried a third opening in that age. No elder of any hearth is remembered forbidding it outright; the will for it went out of the island unasked, the way the will for a bad match goes out of two families once the salt has been carried and refused, each hearth coming to the same mind alone and none of them saying so aloud.

## V. Of the Vein That Chooses

Torcall Tochlair stayed at the black hill for the rest of his years, and never took a wife, and never went back to the hills, though he sent word west now and then for tools he could not make himself. He kept the trench open well past the point any sensible digger would have let it fill with rain and go back to grass. Closing it, he told the few who still came to ask him, would mean agreeing that Lachann had died for nothing, and that he would not do. A small household grew up around him at the black hill's foot in his old age, folk who came for one reason or another and stayed because the ground was as good as any and the man at the trench asked nothing of them; by the time he was old it was Torcall's hill to those who lived near it, or Cnoc na Claise, the hill of the trench, which is the name the households about it keep.

Ealag lived out her years at the ford, and raised three children with Donnchadh the fisher, and grew old in a house within sound of the ford where the lines were recited every year at the fair, seven names back on either side, a custom that had not existed when she was a girl and that she had, in her own quiet way, watched into being. Her own children were given without trouble, in their turn, to households of the ford and the coast about it, their lines recited plainly and answered by no objection from anyone, and she stood in the crowd for each of their weddings with the same short-toothed comb in her hair that Donnchadh had carved her, worn nearly smooth by then with handling.

In her last years she would sometimes walk down to the ford at fair-time and stand at the edge of the crowd to hear the reciting, and those who knew her story, which by then was most of the ford's households, said she listened harder than anyone else there, though whether she was listening for Torcall's line, or for her own, or only for whatever answer the world had never given her, none of them could have said, and she never told them. She died at the ford in the two thousand three hundredth year of the hill, in her own bed, with her children about her, an old woman who had asked one honest question in her youth and had gone the whole of her life afterward without an answer to it.

Of Torcall it is told that he died at the black hill some years after her, in the two thousand three hundred and eighteenth year, and was buried beside his brother at the trench's mouth, and that the small household he had gathered kept the ground and did not fill the trench in even then, though nothing further was ever dug from it. What the two of them, Ealag and Torcall, might have made of each other's later lives, had the salt been carried and accepted instead of refused, is not a thing any telling of this age presumes to say. The reciters who keep the story end it always the same way, with the same words the elders of the two households were said to give and no others: that the vein chooses where it lies, and that the choice was never anyone's to make but its own.
