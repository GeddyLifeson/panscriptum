# Of the Rings under the Oaks

*GE 2,461 – 2,600*

> *Sheas an darach mus do sheas sinne; seasaidh e às ar dèidh.*
> The oak stood before we stood; it will stand after us.
> — said of the grove at Doire ghlas, as the households that ring it keep it still

## I. Of the Middens and the Strait

*This is told among Clann na Ceiste, who kept the years of the settling the way they kept everything: by mouth, in Somhairle's own words, passed down through his line long after his face had begun to change.*

Somhairle was four hundred and twenty-three years old in the spring the covered hearth reached the last households of the near valleys, and he had by then stopped counting his years in the ordinary way, the way Clann na Ceiste counted a herdsman's or a raker's, because a century for him had come to feel no longer than a season felt for them. He kept the Line whole in his own head, every name his father had given him and every name added since, and he kept, besides the Line, a habit that was his alone among the king's blood: he walked. Where Dòmhnall had ridden north to hold what was won, and Iain Dubh had ridden south to lose his life on a shingle beach, and the rest of his brothers and sisters had taken hills or given them up, Somhairle went on foot, slowly, from one new roof to the next, and looked, and said little, and was, by the time this telling begins, known at every threshold of the island as the king's son who came and sat by the fire and asked nothing and took no share.

It was on such a walk that he came first to Caol mhòr, on the narrow water where the Old Ones had once heaped their middens high along the strand, shell on shell for longer than any teller could reckon. The households that settled there in his four hundred and twenty-third year built their walls, when they built in stone at all, from the midden, prising loose what the tide had not already carried off; but they would not turn a stone that still showed a mark, whether cut by a hand or worn by nothing but weather, and set those aside in a low unmortared line along the shore instead, so that a stranger walking the strand a hundred years on would find two kinds of wall standing side by side, one raised by the living and one left standing by people who had chosen not to disturb what they did not understand. Somhairle asked an old woman there, raking limpets from among the shells, why they troubled to sort the marked stones from the plain ones when no one now living could read either. She said only that a mark was a mark, whoever cut it, and that a wall built careless of what it stood on had no business standing long; and Somhairle, who had heard the Old Ones' silence answered a hundred ways by a hundred different households in his four centuries of walking, thought hers as good an answer as any and better than most.

He went from there along the coast road to Baile chrom, where households out of Cnoc ghorm had come down some years before to work ground the older settlement had let lie fallow since a bad season none now remembered clearly, and found the resettling further along than the last word of it had told him: byres roofed, a well dug and lined, children old enough to be sent for water without a grown hand beside them. The headman there, a broad quiet man called Donnchadh Ruadh, walked him round the bounds himself and showed him where the old field-marks still stood under the new grass, half sunk, half grown over, and said that his own grandmother had told him whose hands first set those stones though she had never once said the name aloud, only that the ground remembered its first ploughing whether or not the people who ploughed it were remembered with it. Somhairle wrote nothing of that down, for Clann na Ceiste kept the Line before every small saying that crossed a walker's path; he carried this one with him all the same, turning it over on the road the way a man turns a stone he means to look at again by better light.

## II. Of the Salt Road and the Rebuilt Wall

The salt road that ran from the pans at Baile ghorm to the north-west had, by Somhairle's four hundred and twenty-eighth year, been walked so long and by so many feet that its last unbroken stand of scrub, a thicket of thorn and low birch that had turned every laden cart a half-day out of its way for as long as the road had run, was cut through at last by carriers weary of the going round it, and Muileann àrsaidh stood at the road's end after that a full day nearer the salt than it had ever been before. The millers there kept no count of who had swung the first axe into that thicket, for a dozen hands had reason to want it gone and none troubled to claim the doing of it alone; what they kept, and what Somhairle heard told at their own hearth, was that the thicket had stood since before the eldest living miller's grandfather's day, and that its going felt to some of the older households less like a road cleared than like a boundary taken down, though none of them could say clearly what had stood on either side of it worth calling a boundary.

Further east, on ground no household of the king's blood had ever held a share of, the settlers pushing toward the rising country raised the first turned bank east of the old workings, at Dùn thais, in the same year the millers cleared their thicket, digging a ditch and setting the spoil up behind it the way the Stone Kings' own hill had long since taught every settling household to do, without any word sent from Dùn ìseal telling them to do so and without any word sent back once it was done. Somhairle stood on that bank himself, some years after, and found it a smaller, rougher thing than his father's hill, no more than a shoulder-high ring of earth round a scatter of low houses, but built, he thought, by people who had watched the king's hill from a distance long enough to know a bank kept a herd in and a raider out better than any number of dogs, and had built one of their own the first year they had hands enough to spare for it.

Fire did the clearing at Achadh chaol, where the ground lay heavy with scrub too old and too green to be cut by axe alone; the households there burned it back a field's width at a time across three dry summers, and ploughed the black ground while the ash was still warm in it, and got, the first year, a crop the older fields further west had not equalled in a generation. Cuan ruadh, on the eastern coast the Black Wind had stripped bare not many years before, set the last course of its rebuilt wall the same season, stone laid to the eaves the whole circuit of the settlement, the first roofs on that coast built to stand against wind rather than merely to be raised again after it; and Cathair fhada, further south, opened a well within its own bank the same year, so that a siege of weather, if it came again as the Black Wind had come, need not send the whole household down to the shore for water while the wind still had teeth in it. At Caol ruadh, on the narrow strait where the current ran strong enough to carry a badly set net clean out to open water, the fishers took to marking the deep channels with woven buoys tied to stones, a small thing, Somhairle thought when he heard of it, but the kind of small thing that spread from one strait to the next the way the Red Hill's paired tide-marks had spread, carried in a carrier's mouth and taken up by people who never asked who had thought of it first.

He asked the folk of Caol ruadh, once, sitting with the fishers over their evening catch, who had first thought to tie the woven buoys over the deep water, and got the same answer he had grown used to hearing everywhere he asked such a question: no one could rightly say, only that a boy had tied the first one two or three summers back after losing a net to the current, and that the next household to lose a net had tied one too, and after that it was simply how a net was set on that strait, the way a boat was rowed with the current or against it, and not a thing anyone thought to credit to a single pair of hands. It struck Somhairle, hearing it, as much the same shape of story he had heard told of the paired marks on the tide-rock at Ros dhomhain, a custom that had spread the same way, from one keeper's trouble to every household's taking-up of it, and he began, walking on from Caol ruadh, to keep a private reckoning of his own of how many customs on the island had grown that way, out of one household's trouble and every household's willingness to take up what worked without asking who had thought of it first: more, he judged, before he had gone very much further, than his father had ever once had to command.

He walked among all of these in the years his brothers and sisters held or lost their hills, and he came, in time, to think of the island less as his father's kingdom, which the reciters at every gathering still called it, and more as a great many small kingdoms of one custom each, none of them written anywhere, all of them somehow agreed to by people who had never sat in council to agree to anything.

## III. Of the Word from the North

Word reached him at the slab, where he still kept his own turn among the households that sat out the dark half of the year with Beathan, that families out of the near valleys had begun that autumn to come down to Doire ghlas and build there, in the grove, under the very oaks his father had set the six pots beneath on the night of the seven fires. It was told to him plainly, by a herdsman's wife who had no notion what the grove meant to the man she was telling, that the new households liked the ground for its shelter and its old cleared paths, and that they had already begun marking timber for the first of the new byres.

Somhairle said nothing to her of what she had told him, and sat out the rest of his turn at the slab as he always did, but he left the high valleys within the week, alone, on foot, walking north by the same road his father had walked south on with eleven of his kin behind him four and a half centuries before, though the road had long since worn wider and straighter than the track Ailean had first taken. It was sixty-five miles from the hill of the Stone Kings to the grove, a walk of four days at the pace Somhairle kept, and he spent much of it turning over, as he had turned over Donnchadh Ruadh's word about the ground remembering its ploughing, whether he had any right at all to want the grove kept as it had stood on the one night that made it matter to him, when every field and every wall he had walked past for four centuries had been let become whatever the people living on it needed it to become.

## IV. Of the Road North

The road ran through country Somhairle had walked so many times that he no longer needed to look at it to know what lay round the next turning, and yet he found, walking it that autumn with a purpose he had not carried on any of his earlier crossings, that he saw it as if new: the boundary stones Dòmhnall had once set with his own hands, still standing where quarrels had been settled a century before Somhairle's own birth; the shell of a burned palisade at a hill whose name he had never learned to say without a small catch of anger, for a fire that low and dark had not been lit by any hand he wanted to think of as kin; the wide trodden ground where the salt-carriers rested their beasts each evening, worn bare of grass by feet that had crossed it for longer than any single generation could account for.

He slept the first night in a herder's shelter kept swept, as every shelter on that road was kept swept, by whoever had passed it last, and shared bread with a family driving cattle down from the high pastures before the weather turned, who took him, from his plain dress and his slow speech, for nothing more than an old walker of no particular house, and told him freely what they would not have told a man they knew for the king's own son: that they thought the settling gone further and faster than any of them liked, fields opened where their grandparents' grandparents had grazed without a fence between one household's beasts and the next, and no one, king or otherwise, troubling to say whether that was a good thing or a bad one. Somhairle said only that he had walked the same roads a long while and thought most changes looked worse from close by than they read afterward from a little distance, which was as near to an answer as he ever gave anyone who did not know who was giving it.

He came to Doire ghlas on the evening of the fourth day and found the households further along than the herdsman's wife's telling had prepared him for: a dozen houses already roofed, more staked out in a rough ring, and among the oaks, cut low into the bark of three of the oldest trunks in the grove, the fresh white marks that meant a tree was chosen for the axe.

## V. Of the Marks on the Old Bark

He stood a long while before the nearest of the three marked oaks, a tree with a girth no four men could have spanned with linked arms, its bark grey and split with age in a way that put him, though he did not say so to anyone that evening, in mind of nothing so much as his own father's hands, which had held their warmth through four hundred and twenty-eight years without once showing a single line of age. The households working nearby knew him for the king's son who walked and asked nothing, and one of the settlers, a lean, quick-spoken man called Fearchar mac Ailig who had come down from the near valleys with the first families that autumn, walked over to see what held him so still and told him, when he asked, that the oldest trees gave the best timber for roof-beams, being straight-grained where the younger growth still twisted, and that the households meant no disrespect to the grove in cutting them, only good sense in building.

"It is a fair grove for building in," Fearchar said, "sheltered from three winds and with good water below it, and we are grateful for the finding of it, whoever cleared the paths through it before us." He did not know, and Somhairle did not tell him, whose hands those paths had first been trodden by, or what had stood in the ring of oaks he was measuring for beams on the one night that ring had ever held its full seven fires at once.

Somhairle asked him, then, plainly, whether the household would spare the three oldest trees and take their timber elsewhere, and gave, when Fearchar asked him why, the only reason that was wholly his own to give: that his father had set six pots under those particular trunks once, long before Fearchar's grandmother's grandmother was born, and that Somhairle, who asked nothing of any household on the whole island and took no share of anything the king held, asked this one thing of this one household, for this one grove, and would ask it of no other. He did not say the word grove meant to Clann na Ceiste, or name the seven fires, or tell Fearchar what the Line said had moved between the trunks on the eighth night; he gave the household the plainest telling he could make of a thing that was not, at its root, plain at all, and left the rest to whatever weight his own asking carried.

## VI. Of the Oaks Left Standing

Fearchar carried the asking to the rest of the household that same evening, and it is told at Doire ghlas still, in the plain way the settlement kept most of its own small history, that there was little argument over it: the three trunks were old enough that felling them would have cost more labour than younger growth close at hand, and a request from the king's own son, however oddly reasoned, was not a thing a settling household weighed lightly against a morning's easier cutting. The white marks were cut away from the bark within the week, and the beams for the first byres came instead from a stand of younger oak a short walk to the east, straighter in the grain and easier to the axe than Fearchar had first thought the older trees would be, so that the household lost, in the end, less by the sparing than it had feared to.

The three oaks stood after that at the centre of the new settlement rather than at its edge, for the houses went up in a rough ring around them the way the households had first laid out their stakes, and within a generation the ring had a name among the people who lived inside it, though not one that named the grove's older meaning: they called it, simply, the ring under the oaks, for the three trunks that stood taller than any roof raised near them and gave a shade in summer no roof could match. No teller at Doire ghlas, in all the years after, ever set down why those three trees and no others had been spared when every household around them cut freely; the reason lived only in Somhairle's own telling, kept by Clann na Ceiste the way they kept everything, by mouth, apart from any written record the settlement would ever make of its own founding.

Somhairle stayed at Doire ghlas the better part of that autumn, sitting some evenings at Fearchar's own hearth and some at others, watching the ring of new roofs rise round the old ring of oaks, and it was there, more than at any slab in the high valleys or any bank raised against a storm, that he came to think the settling of the island had worked out, without any law behind it, the very thing his father never troubled to write down for anyone: a thing worth keeping got kept, in the end, because enough people, asked plainly and given a reason that cost them little, found they had no strong wish to refuse it, though no king had ever told them they must.

## VII. Of the Lines in His Face

He was walking south again, back toward the high valleys, in his four hundred and thirty-fifth year, when a woman selling cheese at a roadside stall in the near valleys looked at him a moment longer than a stranger usually troubled to and asked, not unkindly, whether he had been unwell that season, for there was grey coming into his beard that had not been there, she thought, the last time he passed her stall. Somhairle had heard such things said before, in jest, by people who took his slow way of speaking for an old man's caution and forgot, a breath later, that they had ever thought it; but he stopped that day at a still pool a little further on and looked at his own reflection for longer than he had troubled to look at it in decades, and found, when he looked closely, that she had told him true. There were lines at the corners of his eyes that had not been there when Beathan was born, and grey enough in his beard now that a stranger might, for the first time in four hundred and thirty-five years, have taken him for what he actually was: a man growing old, slowly, the way his father never would.

He told no one of it at first, not even Beathan, who still sat out his own turns by the slab in the high valleys and had not, so far as Somhairle could tell, begun to age at all; but Beathan noticed it himself before the year was out, coming upon his father unexpectedly at the slab one evening and stopping in the doorway a moment before he crossed it, the way a man stops when a familiar face shows him something he was not looking for. Neither of them spoke of it that night, or for some while after, for Clann na Ceiste were not a people who troubled a thing with words before the thing had finished happening to them; but it is told, among the few who kept that particular winter's telling whole, that Somhairle sat later that season at the fire with a young cousin of Beathan's wife, newly come into the household, who did not know him and took him, from his grey beard and his slow way of sitting, for an old man of no particular importance among the king's blood, and that Somhairle let the mistake stand a full evening without correcting it, listening to her talk of the settling further north as though he had walked none of that ground, and finding in the listening a quiet he had not expected the ageing to bring him.

It was Beathan who finally asked him of it, the following spring, sitting together at the slab through a night neither of them was properly owed by the custom, the young households having gone home early with a storm coming on. "You are changing," Beathan said, plainly, the way his father had taught him to say most things. "I have watched every face in the high valleys change, mother among them, and I never once thought to watch yours." Somhairle answered that he had not thought to watch it either, and that he did not know why it should begin now, in his four hundred and thirty-sixth year, when it had not begun in his first hundred, and that he supposed the not-knowing was itself a kind of answer, of the sort Clann na Ceiste had always been given and had always kept without asking for better. Beathan sat with that a long while, turning it the way his father turned most things, and said at last only that he was glad, whatever it meant, that his father would grow old among them rather than remain forever the one face at every hearth that time did not touch; and Somhairle, hearing it, thought it the truest thing either of them had said to the other in some decades.

He kept the Line whole in his own head still, every name his father had given him and every name added since Doire ghlas took its own new name, and he went on walking, in the years that followed, from settlement to settlement across the widening island, though a little more slowly each year, and a little more often now stopping, on a road he had walked a hundred times before, simply to sit a while and watch new roofs going up round trees his father's own hands had once passed under, unremembered by anyone building beneath them but the one man on the whole island whose own years were, at last, beginning to run out the ordinary way.
