# Of the Palisade that Burned Twice

*GE 227 – 312*

*On An Cnoc Ruadh they still tell this against no one, for it is the telling that gave them their own list, and a list is a thing worth the trouble of a fire.*

## I. Of the Hill by the Lake

Uilleam Faire was born on An Cnoc Ruadh in the twelfth year of Uisdean Mòr's holding, and grew up believing, as every child on that hill believed, that the water below it had always belonged to his own people and to no one else's. It was an old line, older than any telling the hill-folk of Dùn ìseal kept of themselves, and it did not reckon its years from a grove or a crowning or any king's coming, but from a woman named Beathag Ruadh who had held the hill before any of them were born and had never been anybody's but her own. The tellers of the western lake shore spoke of Dùn ìseal, when they spoke of it at all, the way a man speaks of weather coming up from a quarter he does not much like the look of: something that would arrive whether he watched it or not, and was not yet his trouble.

Uisdean Mòr had held the hill since he was five-and-twenty, a broad, slow-spoken man with a temper that took a long while to rouse and longer to cool, and he was the second name on An Cnoc Ruadh's own list, which the hill kept apart from any other and recited apart from any other. He had grown into the holding the way a man grows into a coat cut a little large for him, filling it out year by year until, by the time Uilleam was old enough to notice such things, no one on the lake shore could any longer picture the hill without him standing on it. It was in the seventeenth year of his holding, when Uilleam was not yet five, that Uisdean set his mind to the ditch.

The neck of the hill was its one weakness, a low saddle of ground where the rise from the lake met the rise from the inland side and a man on foot could walk up either slope without troubling to draw breath. Uisdean had watched that saddle for years the way another man watches a loose tooth, and in the spring of his own son's birth he set every household on the hill to digging it out, a ditch as deep as a tall man and as wide as three men lying end to end, and when the ditch was done he set a palisade of oak along the spoil bank behind it, trunk after trunk driven upright and lashed and packed with clay, until the neck of the hill stood higher and harder than any part of it the lake or the land had made on their own.

Uilleam remembered the finishing of it only as a child remembers a thing too large for him to hold whole: the smell of the sap where the axes had been, the noise the households made when the last trunk went into its socket, his own father lifting him onto a shoulder to see over the heads of the crowd. He did not remember understanding, that day, what the wall was for. He came to understand it slowly, the way a boy comes to understand most of what his elders already know and have stopped explaining: that An Cnoc Ruadh answered to no one, and that a wall was the shape a people gave to that answer when they meant it to hold.

## II. Of Growing to the Watch

The years after the wall went up were quiet ones on the lake, quiet enough that Uilleam, growing through them, came to think of quiet as the hill's natural weather and trouble as the thing that came from elsewhere, the way his grandmother had once told him trouble always did. He learned to fish the reed shallows below the western slope, learned the lake's moods from a boat before he learned them from the shore, and learned, from his father, who had helped dig the ditch and never tired of saying so, the two things a man of An Cnoc Ruadh was born owing: a debt to the water, which fed them, and a debt to the wall, which kept them theirs.

He came to the watch at sixteen, as most of the hill's young men did, standing his turns along the palisade in the grey hour before dawn and the grey hour after dusk, when the light off the lake made every shape along the far shore worth a second look before a man let it go. It was dull work more often than not, and Uilleam, in those first years, sometimes let his mind wander from the wall to the water and back without ever quite deciding which bored him less. He was not a clever man, by his own account, given to him late in life and never once disputed by anyone who had known him; but he was a careful one, and carefulness, on a watch, outlasts cleverness more often than either is given credit for.

It was on the watch, in his twentieth year, that he first heard the full telling of why the wall stood where it stood and swore to no one but itself, and he heard it from Uisdean's own mouth, for the holder of An Cnoc Ruadh still walked the wall himself some nights, unable, it seemed, to trust entirely to younger legs a thing he had dug with his own hands. Word had come up from the east, Uisdean said, standing beside him in the cold with his breath showing white, that the king of the hill-folk, the one the reciters called Ailean Mòr, had brought in valley after valley to the south and east of Dùn ìseal, not always by asking, and that his blood held na Bliadhnachan Fada and did not age as other men's did, and that no one who had sworn to him seemed to find a way afterward of unswearing.

"And are we to swear to him," Uilleam had asked, young enough still to ask a question so plainly, "when he comes?"

"He has not come," said Uisdean, and would say no more of it that night, though Uilleam came, turning the night over as an old man, to think his holder had not answered the question so much as set it aside, the way a man sets aside a debt he means to pay but has not yet decided how.

## III. Of the Two Hundred Spears

The king came in the thirty-ninth year of Uisdean's holding, at the end of a summer, and he came the way a change in weather comes: seen a long way off, and unhurried about arriving.

Word ran up the lake shore three days ahead of him, carried by a trader who had passed his company on the inland road and could not say, when pressed, how many spears he had counted, only that he had stopped counting. He came at last along the shore itself in the low light of an evening, some two hundred men on foot behind him, no more armed, Uilleam thought, watching from the wall with the rest of the watch crowded along it, than any company that size would carry as a matter of course; but two hundred men are two hundred men, and the hill had never in Uilleam's lifetime seen so many strangers standing on its own shingle at once.

They camped below the hill, between the water and the rise, and lit their fires, and did what any company of two hundred does at the end of a day's march: ate, and settled their gear, and set their own watch, which Uilleam noted with a watchman's interest was neither larger nor smaller than seemed reasonable for a company expecting no trouble that night. What they did not do was send up any word to the hill at all. No herald came to the ditch to ask leave, or make demand, or say so much as a name. The king's fires burned below them the whole evening, and the hill's own fires burned above, and between the two the neck of the ditch lay dark and empty and, Uilleam thought, watching it, strangely loud with the things neither side was saying.

Uisdean stood on the wall a long while that evening, further along from where Uilleam kept his own post, and said little to anyone who came near him. Uilleam heard afterward, more than once and always secondhand, that his holder had been asked by one of the elder men whether they should send down and ask the strangers' business, and had answered that a man who wanted to be asked his business generally opened with it himself, and that he would not go chasing after an answer nobody below had troubled to offer.

The night held fair and still until well past midnight, and Uilleam, on the last watch before dawn, was among those who saw the fire start.

He said afterward, and said it the same way every time he was asked, for the rest of his life, that he had been looking straight at the palisade when it took, and had seen no torch, no spark carried on the wind, nothing thrown from below or dropped from above. The wood simply began to burn, along its whole length at once rather than from any one place outward, and the flame it made was low and did not leap the way a fire leaps when it is fed, and it was the wrong colour, a dark shining thing more like the last coal of a hearth banked for the night than like any blaze Uilleam had ever put a name to. He shouted the alarm before he had finished looking at it, and the hill came awake around him in the space it takes a dog to bark twice.

They fought the fire with what they had, lake water carried up in skins and pots and whatever else came to hand, and green hides thrown over the worst of it to smother rather than douse, and by the grey edge of morning the palisade stood black and smoking and mostly whole, for the low strange fire, whatever it had been, had burned slow enough to be beaten, this first time, before it took the timber through. No one came up from the camp below while they fought it, and no one from the hill went down to ask why. When full light came the two hundred spears were still on the shore exactly where they had camped, breaking their fast over their own fires as though nothing along the hill's rim had happened in the night at all.

## IV. Of the Long Waiting

Uisdean rebuilt through what remained of that autumn and on into the winter, and he rebuilt, against the plain word of every carpenter on the hill, with green oak.

Uilleam had asked him once, hauling timber up the inland slope with a dozen others in the wet cold of that season, why a man who had just watched seasoned wood burn to the ground chose to set up wood that was worse for burning in its place, and Uisdean had given him an answer that satisfied him at the time and satisfied him less each time he turned it over afterward: that seasoned oak took years to grow seasoned and he did not have years to spare before the frost, and that green oak, whatever its faults, was oak that stood on his own hill and had not been begged, borrowed, or bought from anyone camped on his shingle. It was not, Uilleam came to think, an answer about timber at all.

Below the hill the king's company neither struck camp nor pressed any nearer. They fished the shallows, and mended their gear, and by the second month of that winter had begun trading, in a small and wary way, with households of the hill who found it easier to sell a little salted fish downhill than to carry the same fish a day's walk round the lake to anyone else who might buy it. Uilleam himself sold two baskets of eels that winter to a spearman who paid him fairly and asked no questions he did not want answered, and came away from the transaction unsure, as he said afterward, whether he had done a small treason or nothing at all.

He saw the king twice that winter, at a distance both times, and remembered afterward that there had been nothing about the man, seen from the wall on a grey afternoon, that marked him out from any of the two hundred around him, save that the others moved out of his way without seeming to notice they had done it, the way water moves round a stone in a stream without the stream troubling to remark on the stone. Once he was mending a boat drawn up on the shingle, working the caulking with his own hands like any boatwright's man. Once he was simply sitting, near the water, doing nothing that Uilleam could make out from that distance at all, for the better part of an afternoon, while his company went about their business around him and left him to it.

"He is waiting us out," Uisdean said, the one time Uilleam heard him speak of it plainly, standing on the half-finished wall with the green timber sweating sap in the cold. "He thinks a wall that burns twice is a wall that will not be rebuilt a third time, and he means to be here to see whether he is right."

"And is he?"

Uisdean did not answer that either. He set the last of the green oak into its socket himself, with his own hands, in the worst of that winter's frosts, and the hill took it, watching him do it, as an answer of a kind.

## V. Of the King's Frost

Spring came late that year and cold, and by the time the flint-pits were opened again for the season the palisade stood finished, dark green wood weeping sap along every joint, and the households of An Cnoc Ruadh had begun, cautiously, to speak of the whole matter as a thing that had passed. The king's company still camped below, for they had never once struck their tents through the winter, but two hundred men who fish and mend and ask nothing come, after enough months, to seem less like a siege and more like a second, smaller, oddly patient village that had grown up on the shingle without anyone's leave.

It was in the early days of an t-Ògmhios, when the year should by every reckoning the hill kept have been well past any frost, that the cold came down out of a clear sky in a single night and put a white rime on the grass along the whole shore, the kind of frost no one living on the lake had ever known to fall so late in the turning year. The hill's own old women called it, from that morning on, Reothadh an Rìgh, and kept the name whether or not anyone could say with certainty it was fair, for it seemed to them a thing too strange to be weather and not strange enough to be anything they had a better word for.

Uilleam was on the wall again that night, as he had been on the night of the first burning, and he said afterward that he knew, the moment the frost's cold reached him through his plaid, that it was not a night like other nights, though he could not have said, if pressed, what told him so beyond the wrongness of the season itself. He kept his eyes on the palisade the whole watch through, the way a man keeps his eyes on a wound he expects to open again, and so he was looking straight at the green oak, for the second time in his life, when it took.

It went the way Teine a' Chlachair had gone and worse, the whole wall alight at once in the same low, dark, shining flame, but the green wood, which should by every carpenter's word have resisted longer than the seasoned timber had, burned instead in what Uilleam swore, to the end of his days, was less time than it takes to say a man's name twice over. He was the first to reach it with water, running the whole length of the wall with a hide-skin held before him, and he threw what he carried into the heart of the worst of it before three other men had so much as reached the ditch; and he said afterward, when asked, and he was asked more times over the rest of his life than he ever tired of answering, that he had done it without thinking at all, the way a man's hand goes to a burn before his mind has finished deciding it is burned.

It did no good. The fire took the new wall as it had taken nothing else that year, and by the time the rest of the households reached the wall with what water and hides they could gather, there was nothing left standing worth throwing water at. Uilleam stood at the ditch's edge afterward with the empty skin still in his hand and watched the last of the low dark flame die down into ordinary embers, ordinary at last, the strangeness gone out of it the moment there was nothing left for it to burn, and he never once, from that night to the end of his life, could say for certain whose doing it had been. He had thrown the water himself, with his own two hands, at a fire he could not name and could not explain and could not, in the end, put out; and he carried that half-answered night with him the rest of his years the way another man might carry an old wound that aches before rain, without ever being wholly sure the ache and the weather were truly bound to one another at all.

## VI. Of Uisdean Going Down

In the morning Uisdean Mòr came down from An Cnoc Ruadh alone.

He gave no order that any man should follow him, and Uilleam, who stood near enough at the gap in the ditch to hear him say it, remembered the words plainly: that this was his own debt and he would carry it down himself, and that the wall, or what was left standing of it, would want every hand that was not his own for the clearing. He went down the inland path rather than the shore path, which struck Uilleam even then as a strange choice for a man walking to a reckoning, until he understood, watching from the wall with the rest of the households crowded along it in the smoking wreck of the morning, that Uisdean had wanted the longer way, the way that gave a man time to decide, step by step, what he meant to say before he arrived at having to say it.

From the wall the distance was too great for Uilleam to hear a word of what passed on the shingle below, and he was honest about that for the rest of his life, never once claiming, as certain other tellers of the tale were happy enough to claim on his behalf, to have caught so much as a syllable of it. What he saw, and swore to, was this: Uisdean coming down onto the stones and stopping twice on the way, as a man stops who is arguing with himself and losing; the king not rising from where he sat on a stone by the water, mending, by Uilleam's guess from the shape of it, some small thing held in his hands; the two of them a long while, neither moving, close enough that their shadows on the shingle nearly touched and did not; and then Uisdean going down on one knee on the wet stones, slowly, the way a heavy man goes down when his own weight has finally decided the matter for him, and putting his two hands out, and the king taking them in his own.

They sat together afterward, the two of them, a good while on the shingle with the wrecked wall smoking above them, and were seen, by those close enough on the shore to see it and not so close as to be sent away, to share out something between them that might have been food and might, from that distance, have been anything at all. Uilleam did not go down. He stood his place on the wall, as he had been told to, and watched his holder eat with the man whose two hundred spears had camped below their hill through a whole winter without once being asked to, and understood, without anyone troubling to explain it to him, that whatever An Cnoc Ruadh had been before that morning, it was going to be a different thing after it.

## VII. Of the Two Lists

Uisdean came back up the hill by evening, tired in the way a man is tired after a thing decided rather than a thing done, and called the households together at what was left of the gap in the wall to tell them, in as few words as he ever used for anything, that An Cnoc Ruadh had sworn to the king, and that the king had taken the oath as freely given and asked nothing further of it that day. He did not say, and Uilleam noticed the absence of it even then, that An Cnoc Ruadh had sworn to Dùn ìseal, or to the hill of Rìghrean na Cloiche, or to any name but the king's own; and it was some years before Uilleam came to understand that the distinction, small as it sounded on the evening it was made, was the whole of what his holder had gone down to the shingle to settle.

The palisade was rebuilt a third time, that summer and the next, with seasoned oak at last, for there was no longer any king's company camped below to hurry the choosing of the timber; and it never burned again in Uilleam's lifetime nor, so far as An Cnoc Ruadh's own list has ever had cause to record, in anyone else's. The king's company struck their tents within the month and went east, back the way they had come, and did not return; and the lake shore, which had spent a whole winter watching two hundred strangers fish its shallows and mend its boats, found the quiet afterward stranger, for a season or two, than the company itself had ever been.

An Cnoc Ruadh kept its own list after that, as it always had, and the list said what the hill wanted said and no more: that Uisdean Mòr, second of the list, had built the wall, and lost it twice, and sworn to the king on the third morning of trying. It did not name the fire, or its colour, or the frost that came too late in the year to be trusted, or the man who had thrown the water and never once, in all the tellings he was asked to give, found a way of saying more than he had truly seen. Dùn ìseal's own reciters, when word of the swearing reached them, told the tale fuller, and stranger, and An Cnoc Ruadh never once quarrelled with their telling, which struck Uilleam, hearing both versions set side by side in his own old age, as the strangest part of the whole business: that a hill so careful of its own list should let another hill's tellers keep the truer half of its own story, and never ask for it back.

Uisdean held An Cnoc Ruadh two-and-twenty years after the swearing, until his eighty-seventh year, and died on the hill in his own bed, an old man by any ordinary reckoning, having outlived the fire and the frost and the two hundred spears by a longer stretch than any of them had lasted. Uilleam stood the watch on the wall until he himself was an old man, and told the tale of the burning, when he was asked for it, exactly the same way every time, changing nothing, adding nothing, and ending it always at the same place: that he had thrown the water with his own hands, and did not know, to his dying day, whose fire it was that he had failed to put out.
