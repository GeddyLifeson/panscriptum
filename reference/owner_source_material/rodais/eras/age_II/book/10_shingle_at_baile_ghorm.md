# Of the Shingle at Baile ghorm

*At Baile ghorm the salt-rakers still tell it as Màiri nan Làn told it, and add nothing to her telling and take nothing from it.*

## I. Of the Pans

Baile ghorm in those years was a scatter of huts behind a shingle bar, and the shingle bar was the reason for everything else. Behind it lay clay basins the salt-folk had dug with their own hands out of the mud a spring tide could no longer reach, and the sun and the slow fires under the basins did the rest, so that what came out at the end of a good week was salt enough to trade the length of the southern shore and some way past it. Màiri nan Làn had raked salt from the time she was old enough to be trusted with the wooden rake and not trusted with anything sharper, and she went on raking it the whole of her life after, and it was this, more than the year she is chiefly remembered for, that filled the greater part of her days: salt, and the sun on the pans, and the particular ache across the shoulders that came from drawing a rake through drying crystal from dawn until the light went.

The salt-folk of Baile ghorm did not then call themselves the king's, and had not, in Màiri's grandmother's time, so much as heard his name spoken with any certainty of what it meant. The hill lay a long way north and inland, a good many days' walking by the tracks they knew, and the southern shore had gone on making its own salt and keeping its own dead and marrying its own children into its own households without reference to any hill at all, the way a shore does when nothing has yet come down it hard enough to make reference necessary. Word had come, in the years before Màiri's birth, of a granddaughter of the hill's line who had ridden down with spears and carried off a season's salt in one raid, and killed seven of the salt-folk doing it, and the salt-folk had kept the seven names the way they kept every name of every dead, cut nowhere, said aloud at the right times, and had gone back to their pans afterward because there was no other place to go back to. Behind the shingle bar they had raised a low bank in the years just after, a slight thing that would slow a determined raid without ever quite stopping one, and had thought, most of them, that this settled the matter as well as it was likely to be settled.

Màiri's own mother had been a girl at the pans the year Mòrag Chruaidh came, old enough to remember the shapes of the running, though too young to be given a rake she might have to drop and run from, and she had passed the telling to Màiri the way the shore passed everything, at the fire, in pieces, over years, until the child no longer remembered a time before she had known it. Seven names, her mother said, and named them each in turn, slowly, the way a thing is said that must not be said carelessly, and Màiri grew up knowing those seven names as well as she knew her own kin's, though she had never seen any of the seven living. It seemed to her, hearing it as a child, a settled story with a settled end, a raid that had happened once and would not happen twice, the bank behind the bar built and the account closed. She was thirty-one before she understood how wrong a child's sense of an ending can be.

It was Iain Dubh who unsettled it again. Màiri knew him only as a name that meant trouble was owed and had not yet been paid, a hill man's name carried south on the same paths that carried salt north, and she was thirty-seven years old, with two grown sons of her own gone to households of their own and a third child still under her roof, on the morning she first saw him with her own eyes. She did not know, raking that morning, that she was seeing the last morning he would ever have.

## II. Of the Coming of Iain Dubh

He came the way the other one had come, from the north and inland, with spears enough behind him to make the salt-folk's low bank look exactly as inadequate as it was, and he came, as the first one had, for the salt. The salt-folk had had eight years since Iain Dubh took the hill from his father, eight years of hearing that he was harder even than the woman before him, or easier, or unlike her altogether, for such word travels a long road and grows a different shape at every hearth it passes, and none of it prepared them for the morning the spears actually stood on the ridge above the pans with the light behind them.

Tadhg Garbh was thirty-two that year, a big slow man who had never in Màiri's memory raised his voice at anything, married two years to a woman named Beathag who kept geese behind the huts and was, by every account but his own, a far quicker wit than he was. He had raked salt beside Màiri since they were children too small to be trusted with the rake, and she knew him the way the shore's people knew one another, by his walk before his face, by the particular slowness with which he came to any decision and the particular stubbornness with which he held it once come to. It was Tadhg who stood nearest the gap in the bank when the spears came down off the ridge in the grey hour before the light was fully up, thirty and more of them against the dozen or so men who had reached the gap before the rest of the shore was even roused, and it was Tadhg's spear, thrown last of all among a scatter of others and almost, men said afterward, as an afterthought, that took Iain Dubh through the throat before he had closed half the distance to the pans.

Màiri was not at the gap. She had been raking the pan nearest her own hut, further along the bar, and came up out of sleep-thick confusion at the first shout to find the whole shore already running, men snatching spears from where they leaned against the hut walls, women driving the smaller children back behind the salt-sheds, and she ran with the rake still in her hand for want of anything else to carry, useless as it was for fighting, because it was the thing nearest to hand when the shouting started and she had not thought to set it down. What she saw, coming up onto the higher ground behind the bank, was the fighting break apart the way a flock breaks when a stone is thrown into the middle of it, men running one way and then another, spears thrown and missing and a few finding their mark, and in among them a tall figure that had been walking, not running, at the head of the hill men, going suddenly down onto the wet clay of the nearest pan and not getting up again.

She did not know, in the confusion of that morning, whose fall it was. It was only when the fighting broke off as suddenly as it had begun, the hill men drawing back from the bank in a huddle around the fallen man rather than pressing on for the salt they had come for, and then lifting him between four of them and carrying him away north at a pace that could not honestly be called a retreat and could not honestly be called anything else, that the shore began to understand what had happened; and it was days later, when the first traders came south with the word already travelling ahead of them the way such word always travelled, that Baile ghorm learned the man on the clay had been the king's own son, holder of the hill these eight years past, and the first of Ailean Mòr's children, in all the generations already reckoned there, to die by a spear rather than by the slow ordinary dying that took everyone else.

Seven of the salt-folk had died the first time Mòrag Chruaidh came down, and their names were kept. Only Tadhg Garbh's spear had found its mark this second time, and no one on the salt shore died that day at all, which some of the older rakers afterward called a mercy and others called only luck, arguing at the winter fires for years over which word was truer and never settling it.

Iain Dubh had come to the hill at six hundred and seventy-four years old, by the count the reciters kept, a great age by any reckoning but the hill's own, where it made him no more than a man in the middle of his strength; he died at six hundred and eighty-two, on the clay of a pan he had never so much as touched with his own hand, and the list that had until then named only the taking of hills and the giving of judgements set down, for the first time, a year of death that was not also a year of some ordinary failing of the body.

## III. Of the Cairn on the Hill

What was done on the hill afterward, Màiri did not see and only heard, the way she heard most things from the hill, thinned by distance and by however many mouths a tale had passed through before it reached the salt shore. It was told that they raised the cairn where Ailean Mòr's own eye had chosen the ground, and that Tormod, Iain Dubh's son, stood by it through the raising with a face that gave nothing away to anyone watching, which was thought remarkable in a young man not yet ninety years old whose father had just been carried home dead on a hide.

Tormod had held nothing yet in his own right; he was his father's eldest living son and no more than that, and the hill in those days gave nothing to a man for being a son, only for being chosen, so there was no certainty even at the cairn-side that Tormod would ever hold what his father had held. He was eighty-seven that year, a young man still by the reckoning of his blood, and stood the length of the raising without once being seen to weep, which the traders who carried the tale south thought worth remarking on, though Màiri, hearing it told and told again over the years, came to think it told them less about Tormod's grief than about how little the traders understood of a household that buried its dead expecting, in the ordinary run of things, centuries more of them to bury before its own turn came.

What was certain, and what the salt-folk heard clearly enough even at their distance, was that the king had not been on the hill when his son died, having been away in the north those same weeks settling some quarrel of grazing that no one on the salt shore ever troubled to learn the particulars of, and that he came home to find the cairn already raised and his son already under it.

It was told that he stood by the cairn three days and spoke to no one the whole of that time, not to Tormod, not to Sorcha's other children, not to the reciters who stood ready, as reciters always stood ready, to add whatever words he cared to give them to the list. On the fourth day he walked off the hill alone, on foot, taking none of his household and no spears at all, and it was some while before anyone understood, from the direction he had gone, that he was walking south toward the salt shore.

## IV. Of the Man on the Shingle

Màiri was raking the third pan from the bar's western end, in the heat of the day when the brine had gone thick and needed turning before it set, and she straightened once to ease her back, the way she straightened perhaps forty times in a working day without ever after remembering any of the other thirty-nine, and saw a man coming along the shingle from the west.

He was alone. That was the first thing she took in, and the strangest, for no one came to Baile ghorm alone who had any better choice, the coast track through the dunes being lonely enough that even traders went it in twos. He walked as a man walks who owns the ground he is walking on, not fast, not slow, and something in the set of his shoulders, though she had never in her life seen him before, told her plainly enough who he must be before any word had passed between them.

She dropped the rake and screamed for the men, and she was not ashamed afterward that she had screamed first and thought second, for it seemed to her the only thing a body could rightly do, seeing what she believed she was seeing come walking toward the pans where her son had died with a spear in his hand not two seasons past. The men came from the huts at a run, thirty of them and more, snatching up the same spears that had done for Iain Dubh, and made a line across the shingle between the king and the salt.

He came on. He did not slow for the line any more than he had slowed for the empty dunes behind him, and Màiri, standing back from the men with the rake still fallen where she had dropped it, noticed a thing she could never afterward explain to anyone who had not stood where she stood: that the wind coming in off the water was cold on her own face, as a sea wind is cold in that season, and yet the air seemed to sit warm around him the whole way he walked, as though he carried some part of a hearth along with him inside his clothes and none of it showed. She had heard the tale of the shingle raid told back to her often enough by then, in every version the shore's fires had made of it, and none of the tellings had thought to mention the warmth; it was hers alone, kept back the whole of her life for want of a way to say it that did not sound, even to her own ear, like a woman claiming more of the day than she had truly been given.

He wore no mail and carried no spear of his own, only a plain travelling cloak gone dusty at the hem from the dunes, and there was nothing in his dress to mark him for a king rather than any herdsman come down off the hill roads, nothing but the walk and the warmth and the thing in his face that made thirty armed men draw into a line rather than simply stand aside and let a stranger pass.

Tadhg Garbh stood near the middle of the line. Màiri knew him well enough to know the exact look that came over his face as the king closed the distance, a look she had seen on him only once before, the morning of the first raid, and she understood without being told that Tadhg had raised his own spear to his shoulder and meant, or half meant, to throw it a second time. She watched his arm come level and hold, and hold, the muscle standing out along it until it shook with the holding, and then watched him lower it slowly and stand looking at the spear in his own hand as if someone else had put it there and he could no longer think why he was holding it at all.

No one else threw. The king walked into the line of them and stopped, close enough now that Màiri could see his face plainly, an ordinary face, she thought afterward, trying to find some grander word for it and never quite finding one that fit better than ordinary, tired about the eyes the way any man's face is tired who has walked a long way and slept little. He looked along the line, face by face, the whole thirty of them, and to each face he gave the same length of looking, no longer at Tadhg Garbh's than at any other, though every soul on that shingle knew by then whose spear it had been. He said nothing at all. The silence went on long enough that Màiri heard, under it, the ordinary small sounds of the shore going on regardless, water working at the shingle, a gull somewhere behind the huts, and thought it strange that the world had not stopped to match the stillness of the men.

## V. Of the Double Handful

It was old Seonag, eldest then among the salt-folk, who broke it. She went down on her knees on the wet shingle in front of him, an old woman kneeling before a man who looked, to any eye that did not know better, a full sixty years younger than she was, and she reached into the nearest pan and took up salt in both hands, a double handful, grey with the clay still in it and wet enough to run between her fingers, and held it up to him without a word.

He took it from her hands into his own. He ate a little of it, not much, no more than a man might taste from curiosity, and put the rest of it back into her cupped palms, closing her fingers over it with his own before he let go, so that she was left kneeling there holding salt he had given back to her rather than salt she had first given him.

Then he turned and walked back the way he had come, along the shingle toward the dunes, and no one moved to stop him and no one, Màiri said afterward more times than she could count, felt afterward that there had been any moment in the whole of it when stopping him had truly been a thing within their power.

By evening word had gone the length of the shore that the salt was the king's, though no one could afterward say which of them had first put it in those words, or whether it needed saying at all after what had been seen on the shingle. No blood had been shed that day, the first time in two raids that a day at Baile ghorm had ended with no new name to be kept among the dead, and no payment was ever asked or given for the blood that had been shed before it, not Iain Dubh's, not the seven of Mòrag Chruaidh's raid a generation before. The rakers argued it at their fires for the rest of that winter and on into others, some holding that a man who ate a stranger's salt and gave the rest back with his own hand had shown them the plainest mercy a conquered shore could hope for, and others, Tadhg Garbh loudest among them at first, though he grew quieter on the subject as the years went by, holding that a man who could walk unarmed through thirty raised spears and take nothing but a taste of what he might have taken whole had shown them something a good deal less comfortable than mercy, a proof of a power that made mercy beside the point.

Màiri herself never took either side of it strongly, though she was asked often enough, being the one whose scream had first brought the men running. What she said, when pressed, in the years she had left to be asked, was only that she had stood close enough to see his face when Seonag knelt to him, and that it had not been the face of a man deciding anything in that moment. It had been, she said, the face of a man for whom the thing had already been decided a long way further back than the shingle, perhaps as far back as the cairn on the hill, perhaps further, and that whatever word the rakers wanted to put to it, mercy or the other thing, the deciding of it had happened somewhere she had no way of following him to.

## VI. Of Tadhg Garbh's Boat

Tormod came down from the hill himself the following spring, the first time any of his father's blood had set foot on the salt shore since the killing, and stood at the cairn the salt-folk raised of their own accord on the rise above the pans, a small thing beside the one on the hill but built with the same care, seven stones for the seven of Mòrag's raid worked in around its base though none of the seven had died in this second fight at all, only Iain Dubh, because the salt-folk had come by then to think of the two raids as one long account and wished the reckoning to sit together. He came with no more than four men, and asked, before he would let anyone lead him up to the cairn, to be shown the pan where his father had fallen, and stood on the clay there a while with his boots sinking into it, saying nothing that Màiri, watching from a little way off, could afterward report with any confidence. Tormod said little that anyone remembered word for word, being still careful with speech in a way his father had never troubled to be, but he swore the salt shore's peace would hold from his own line as it had held from his father's since the king's coming, asked Tadhg Garbh's name and was given it without anyone thinking to soften the telling, looked a moment at the big slow man who had thrown the spear, and said only that a man defending his own pans with his own hand owed no account to anyone for it. Then he rode north again within the day, and it was the last any of the salt shore saw of him for a great many years.

Tadhg Garbh lived a year longer, and it was not a year that sat easily on him. He raked salt as he had always raked it and spoke to his household as he had always spoken, but Màiri, working the pans beside him through that last year more often than either of them chose to remark on, saw a man turning something over that he could not set down and could not finish turning. In the spring of the year after the king's coming he took a boat out alone before first light, one of the small hide craft the shore used for the near fishing, and did not come back with the tide, nor with any tide after it. No storm was remembered that night worth the name, and no wreckage of the boat was ever found along that coast, and the salt-folk were left, as they had been left once before, without a settled word for what had happened, some saying the sea had simply taken a tired man home and others saying, more quietly, that a man who has held a spear against the king's own son and thrown it true does not always find it easy to go on living beside the shore where he did it, whatever mercy was shown him after.

Màiri outlived him more than fifty years, and raked salt at Baile ghorm the whole of that time, through summers when the pans ran thick and winters when they scarcely ran at all, and told the tale of the man on the shingle to her grandchildren and their children after her the same way each time, adding nothing and leaving nothing out, until it passed from her mouth into the shore's own keeping and needed no more telling from any one person at all. The bank behind the bar stood as it had always stood, no higher and no lower than the salt-folk had first raised it, and the pans went on giving up their grey harvest to the rake exactly as they had before any king's blood had ever bled onto the shingle beside them, salt drawn from salt water under the same sun, the shore's oldest and least remarkable labour, going on the same the year the king came as it went on every year before and every year after.
