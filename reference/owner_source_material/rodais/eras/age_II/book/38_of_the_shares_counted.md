# Of the Shares Counted

*GE 4,391 – 4,549*

*This is told at Dùn ìseal, among the households that camped nearest the hill in those years, as Niall Òg was said to have told it himself in his own age, laughing, before he had any cause to stop laughing at it.*

## I. Of the Hill Left to No One

Niall Òg was six hundred and eighty-eight years old, and looked, by the reckoning of the ordinary households camped that season at the foot of the hill, no more than a young man come newly into his strength, when Catrìona Mhòr died at Dùn ìseal in her two hundred and ninety-first year and the list of the Stone Kings ended with her.

He had neither claim on the hill nor any close standing to bring one; he went up with his mother because every soul of the king's blood within three days' walk had come, and staying away would have drawn more remark than coming did. He remembered afterward how many of them there were: cousins he had met at a dozen gatherings and cousins he had never met at all, old ones bent nearly double and young ones who did not yet know how old they would live to be, every last one of them able to say the two names of the line they stood on, and the second of the two always Ailean's. They stood in a great loose ring below the hill's own bank while the households that served the hill went in and out with water and bread for whoever asked it, and the king sat on the turf inside the bank with his back very straight, as he had sat, the old tellings said, in the first winter of the hill, and heard every claim that was brought to him.

There were more claims than Niall could ever afterward number, try as he did, more than once, to make the count come out the same way twice. Some came with a line traced back seven generations and a piece of the hill's own turf held out on an open palm, as if the ground might speak for them on its own account. Some came with nothing but their own two names said plainly, trusting the two names to be claim enough. One old woman, whom Niall's mother pointed out to him under her breath as kin of Murchadh Mòr's line and therefore, by the fashion of the day, owed more courtesy than most, came leaning on two sticks and said only that she had outlived every rival claim she had ever had, and that this ought to count for something even if nothing else about her did. A man of the salt shore's own cousinage, three lines removed but proud of every yard of the distance, brought his infant daughter up in his arms and held her out toward the king as his whole argument, saying nothing else at all. The king heard them all out with the same still face, and it was Niall's mother who named the trouble in it afterward: watching him hear any single claim, she said, no one standing near enough to see his face could tell whether it moved him, because his face gave the same account of a claim that moved him as of one that did not, and there was no reading behind it from the outside.

When the last of them had spoken and the light was going off the hill, the king stood up from the turf, and Niall's mother took his arm hard enough to hurt, though he did not understand until later that this was because she, too, did not know what was coming. The king said that he had heard every claim brought to him, and that he judged none of them better than another, because none of them was better than another; that Catrìona Mhòr had been the last of a line that had begun the day he himself first walked up that slope, and that a line was not a thing a man could hand from one stranger's palm to the next merely because the palm was open. He said he would give the hill to none of them. He said he would hold it himself, as he had held it before Gormshuil first took it up in his name, and that he would go on holding it for as long as it pleased him to.

Then he walked up into the hill's own house and did not come out again that evening, and the kin stood a while longer in the failing light not quite believing that this was the whole of what they had come to hear, and then began, in twos and threes, to go back to their own fires, and Niall's mother did not let go of his arm the whole way down.

"He has never done that before," she said, when they were far enough from the hill that she judged it safe to say anything at all. "Given nothing. Not to anyone."

"Perhaps there was no one worth giving it to," Niall said, because he was young enough still to say a true thing plainly without weighing first what it would cost him to have said it.

His mother looked at him a long moment in the dusk, the way a woman looks at a child who has said something cleverer than she expected of him and is not certain yet whether to be pleased or afraid. "Perhaps," she said. "Or perhaps he has looked at every one of us and found that giving is the one thing left he has not yet tried keeping back."

## II. Of the Marriages Made for a Share

Niall did not understand, for some years after, what his mother had meant by that, and by the time he did understand it he had come, without quite noticing the coming, to be a part of the thing.

It began, so far as anyone afterward could point to a beginning, with a marriage. In the fourth year after Catrìona Mhòr's death a young man of one of the smaller lines, whose own hold on the kingdom amounted to grazing on the northern skirts of nobody's particular attention, married a woman three lines removed from the king on her mother's side and two on her father's, a match that would have raised no eyebrow at all in an ordinary year, since the two of them had known each other since childhood and were, by every account, well suited. What raised the eyebrow was the manner of the asking: the young man's own father stood up at the betrothal fire and said, in so many words, that the marriage joined two claims that might one day be worth more joined than either was worth alone, should the king ever be brought to naming an heir after all, and that he for one meant his son's children to stand as near the front of that reckoning as five lines instead of three could put them.

No one had ever said such a thing aloud at a betrothal fire before. The thought was old; what was new was the saying of it, and the profit in the saying. There had always, before, been a king who might yet live another thousand years and give and take back shares as the fancy or the need took him, and little enough profit in marrying for what a man might never live to see given out at all. Now there was a king who had refused to give anything, and a hill held in a hand that no one could say would ever open again, and the old unspoken thought became, within that single season, a thing spoken plainly at every fire that had two unmarried children of the blood between them.

Niall watched three more such marriages made before he was two hundred years further into his own life, each one a little more openly reckoned than the last. The second joined a household holding good river-grazing to one that held none but had, on its own side, a claim traced through four generations of daughters rather than sons, which some of the elder kin said counted for less and some said counted for more, and no one on the hill that year could point to any custom, old or new, that settled the question either way. The third was broken off within the month, when the bride's own grandmother discovered a nearer claim on her groom's side that she judged would weigh the marriage's whole worth downward rather than up, and said so at the man's own hearth in words plain enough that the match did not survive the telling of them. By the time Niall was of an age to be thought of as a match himself, no one troubled any longer to pretend that a marriage among the king's own blood was made for anything but the shares it might one day gather in one house. He was asked, more than once, by aunts and great-aunts who meant it kindly, whether he had given thought to which of his cousins' lines ran nearest the front of the reckoning, and he laughed at every one of them, honestly at first and then, as the years went on, because laughing had become the answer expected of him and he had found no better one to give in its place.

It was in those same years that the first valley changed hands by the king's own word rather than by death or marriage: a household that had held good grazing on the western slopes for eleven generations was told, without explanation given beyond what the king himself troubled to give, which was none, that the grazing was wanted for another line, and that they would remove themselves and their herds to ground of equal size two valleys over before the winter closed the passes. They went. No one on the hill that year could say why that household and not another had been chosen to lose what it held, and the king, asked directly by the household's own eldest whether some fault of theirs lay behind it, said only that a share given was a share that could be taken back, and had always been so, and that nothing about the taking back was new even if the households had grown unused to seeing it done. It was the plainest word he gave on the whole matter in all the years Niall watched it unfold, and it satisfied no one, least of all the household that had lost the western slopes for reasons it never learned.

## III. Of Raonaid's Share Taken Back

Niall's own mother, Raonaid nic Ailein, held through those years a stretch of river-meadow below the hill that had come to her from the king's own giving so long before that not even she could always give the whole of its telling at one sitting. She was herself the king's daughter, though by then so many generations stood between the fact and the memory of it that most of the households camped near the hill knew her only as an old woman of the blood who kept good meadow and asked little of anyone, and it was a long while before Niall understood that his own claim, when the aunts spoke of reckonings and frontings, ran doubled through her and through his father both, a thing that made him, without his ever asking for it, one of the names spoken of at other households' own betrothal fires in the same careful voice his own father had once used of somebody else's son.

She died in her own house on the river-meadow in the year four thousand four hundred and eighty, having lived, by the count the reciters kept for her afterward, one thousand three hundred and eighty years. Not long before the end she told Niall that the span had simply come to be the shape her life had taken, the way a river takes the shape of the ground it is given and does not ask whether another ground might have made it run differently. She had stopped some centuries back, she said, asking whether it was a long life or a short one, since the question had no one left living to compare it against who had not asked her the same thing first.

The king came down to the river-meadow himself when word reached him that she was failing, and sat with her the better part of a day and a night, as the tellings said he had sat once with Gormshuil at the gate and once with Somhairle in the high valleys. What passed between them in the house no one who was not in it ever learned; she died within the week and never spoke of it, and he never spoke of any of them once they were in the ground. Niall stood outside through most of that night with his own wife beside him, and neither of them went in. He said afterward that he had wanted to, once, badly enough to put his hand on the door, and had taken it away again unopened, judging without needing to say so to his wife that the room held a conversation a good deal older than anything he himself had a part in.

His mother spoke to him alone the following morning, when the king had gone back up to the hill and the house was quiet again but for the two of them. She told him she had asked her father, in the night just past, whether he grieved the same grief each time or whether it wore differently with practice, the way a callus forms where a tool is held often enough, and that he had answered only that he did not know, because he had never yet finished grieving any one of them before the next was laid on top of it. "He did not say it unkindly," she told Niall, "only plainly, the way he says everything. I asked him because I wanted to know whether I ought to be sorry for him as well as for myself, in whatever time I have left to be sorry for anyone. I could not get an answer out of him either way, and I have stopped minding that I could not."

Within the month of her burying the king sent word to the river-meadow that the grazing his daughter had held was wanted elsewhere, and that Niall, as her nearest of that line, should remove his own household from it before the next season's herds came down. He did not say where the grazing was wanted, or by whom, and Niall, standing before him at Dùn ìseal to hear the word given in person, found that his own mouth had only the one shape left in it after a hundred aunts had asked him the same question a hundred different ways: he laughed. The loss itself did not strike him as funny. Laughing had simply become, over so many years of the same reckoning put to him in so many forms, the only answer his own mouth still knew how to make in the king's presence, whatever the question happened to be that season. The king looked at him a moment with an expression Niall could not afterward name, and said only that he was glad someone in the family still found some of it amusing.

"Someone has to," Niall said, "or we should all of us have gone mad reckoning it long before now."

"Some of us have," the king said, and did not say which of them he meant, and turned to the next matter waiting for him, which was, that season, always another claim, another marriage, another valley wanted elsewhere.

## IV. Of Niall Sent East

It was some forty years after his mother's death, in the year four thousand five hundred and twenty, that the king called Niall to him at Dùn ìseal and gave him an errand that had nothing to do with any share at all, which was, Niall said afterward more than once, the first time in a great while that anyone of the king's own house had asked something of him that was not a reckoning of some kind.

"You will go east of the vein," the king said, "to the fort they have raised there, and tell its holder that I bid him and his households come in to me at last, as every other coast of this island has come in, in its own time and its own way."

Niall asked why he, among all the king's kin nearer the front of whatever reckoning was being made that season, should be the one sent on such an errand, and the king said, plainly, that he wanted a messenger the east would not mistake for a claim: that a man sent to ask for nothing more than the coming-in, and known through every fire on the hill for asking nothing of anyone on his own account beyond a laugh, was less likely to be taken for the leading edge of a conquest than a man who held ground and wanted more of it. Niall thought this a fair enough reckoning, and said so, and went.

He went east by the long road the king bade him take, round by the households of the eastern marches so that the whole country should know a messenger was riding, and crossed the vein on the third day of it, over ground that grew stonier and less kept the further east he rode, past the last of the households that answered to Dùn ìseal in anything but name and into a country whose hearths he did not know and whose faces did not know him. He slept the first of those eastern nights at a household that fed him without asking who he was or where he rode, and the second at another that asked at once, plainly, and fed him no worse for the asking, telling him only, when he had answered, that he was welcome at their fire but that the fire owed his errand nothing on its own account. Children ran out along the road to look at his horse, which was of a taller island stock than the east bred for itself, and one boy walked alongside him the better part of a morning asking whether it was true that a man of the king's own house never grew old, and whether Niall himself was such a man, and looked plainly disappointed to be told that Niall was not, though not so disappointed that he stopped walking beside the horse until his own mother called him in for his dinner.

He came, on the fifth day, to the double bank of Dùn dhearg standing on its rise above the river, its ditch cut deep enough that a man standing in it could not see over the rim, exactly as the tellings west of the vein described it and exactly as none of those tellings had prepared him to find it: larger, and better kept, and fuller of people going about their own business without the smallest sign of having heard that a messenger of the king's own house was riding up their own road to speak with them. He counted three separate knappers' floors busy within the outer bank alone before anyone troubled to ask his business, and a granary raised on stone legs against the damp that he judged, from the width of it, held grain enough to see the whole fort through two bad harvests running, and it came to him, riding in past all of it, that he had ridden east expecting to find a grievance nursed in poverty, and had found instead a country that wanted nothing from the hill because it had, by its own hands, gone some way toward needing nothing from anyone.

They brought him, without much ceremony and less delay, before a man named Fearghal Donn, who held the fort in those years and had held it, by his own account, since before he was old enough to have chosen the holding for himself, having had it laid on him the way a family's oldest son is laid with a family's oldest debts. Fearghal was forty years old that season, by his own country's ordinary count, a broad quiet man with a scar along one forearm that he did not offer to explain and Niall did not ask after. He heard Niall out standing in the open ground within the fort's own bank, with a good part of the household gathered near enough to hear every word of it, which Niall understood, before Fearghal had finished hearing him, to be no accident at all.

## V. Of the Word Sent Back

"Tell your king," Fearghal Donn said, when Niall had given the whole of his errand plainly, word for word as the king had given it to him, "that we have raised this bank with our own hands, on ground none of his own blood ever set foot on before we did, and that we have neither asked leave of Dùn ìseal to raise it nor owed anyone west of the vein so much as a handful of salt for the raising. Tell him we have heard, from every trader who ever crossed the vein either way, what it is to be one of his own kin: to marry for a share and count another man's years against your own and wait a thousand of them, if it comes to that, for a hill that is never given after all. We have no wish to be counted among you."

Niall said that he had come to ask a coming-in, not a submission, and that the king had put no oath into the errand at all, only the asking.

"Then tell him," Fearghal said, "that the asking is answered. We will not come in. We have our own bank and our own dead in it, and we mean to keep both without any man's leave, west of the vein or anywhere else."

There was more said between them that afternoon, none of it, by Niall's own account afterward, either angry or unkind; Fearghal Donn gave him food and a bed for the night as any household on the island would have given a traveller, king's messenger or none, and asked him, before he rode out the next morning, a good many plain questions about the hill and the king and the manner of life west of the vein, listening to the answers with the same close attention he had given the errand, so that Niall came away from Dùn dhearg less certain than he had ridden in that the man who held it thought ill of the king so much as he thought the whole arrangement not worth the joining. He rode home with nothing to carry but the bare word, and gave it to the king plainly, as he had been asked to give it, standing again on the turf below the hill's own bank where he had once stood as a boy of six hundred and eighty-eight to hear Catrìona Mhòr's claimants turned away one by one.

The king heard the word and said nothing for a long while, looking east past the vein toward ground Niall knew he had never in all his years troubled to cross himself, and then said only that he had not looked for any other answer, and thanked Niall for the carrying of it, and turned to the next matter waiting for him.

It was not the last word from the east, though it was the last the king troubled to ask for. Within a season word came west, by traders and not by any messenger sent for the purpose, that the households about Dùn dhearg had begun keeping their own spears closer to hand than they had ever kept them before. No quarrel had broken out among themselves to account for it, and the traders who carried the word could not say what quarrel, whose, the spears were being kept ready against; only that they were being kept ready, and that the keeping of them had become, in the space of that one season, a plain fact of the country the way the double bank was a plain fact, needing no more explaining than the bank did. Niall heard the word at Dùn ìseal among a dozen other pieces of news that season, a bad harvest on the northern plain, a marriage broken off over a reckoning gone sour, a fourth household removed from ground it had held a hundred years to make way for a fifth; and he found, turning it over afterward at his own hearth, that this was the one word among them all he could not laugh at, though he could not have said, if anyone had asked him that year, exactly why a gathering of spears at a fort three days' ride east should have struck him differently from anything else the kingdom had counted and recounted in the whole of his own long lifetime.

The king, so far as anyone at Dùn ìseal could see, did nothing about any of it: no word sent east again, no muster called west of the vein, no change at all in the plain round of a man who held a hill in his own hand and heard whatever claims and reckonings the day brought him. He sat on the turf as he had sat since Catrìona Mhòr's death, and the kin went on counting their shares and marrying for them around him, and the spears gathered, slowly, at Dùn dhearg, the way a river gathers behind a dam it does not yet know is a dam at all.
