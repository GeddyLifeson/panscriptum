# Of the Tally Wall and the Half-Cut Stone

*GE 389 – 590*

*Of the wall and the stone Somhairle set down only this, and Clann na Ceiste have kept his telling exactly as he gave it, word for word, adding nothing after it and taking nothing away.*

## I. Of the Road East

Somhairle was three hundred and fifty-one years old and looked a boy of seventeen when his father told him to come down from the high valleys and walk the coast road with him.

He had been eight-and-forty years among Clann na Ceiste by then, long enough that the reciters no longer marked him out as the king's son so much as the one among them who forgot nothing, and set exact by exact into a boy's memory the Line of the Mason from the seven nights forward, the pots left cold under the oaks, the six households that came in first, the storm that broke the shell causeway at Seann Warr and was never mended. He had heard the causeway named a hundred times before he ever saw it, and had not thought, hearing it, that he would.

His father sent no reason with the summons, only a word carried up by a rider that the king rode east in three days and would have his son with him, and Somhairle came down off the slab where he had been sitting with old Aonghas going over the drowned years of the third generation, and walked south to the hill without asking why, for asking why was not a thing the king's messages generally rewarded.

They rode with a small company, no more than a dozen, along the road that ran below Dùn ìseal and out past the last of the valley households to the shore, a road Somhairle had walked in the telling many times and now walked with his own feet, noting as he went the things the telling left out: the smell of the bog before the bog was seen, a heron standing so still in a flooded ditch that two of the company took it at first for a grey stone, his father saying almost nothing the whole first day and then, on the second, telling a story about a horse he had once owned that would not cross running water after dark, which had nothing in it of kings or grooves or long years, and made the company laugh, and Somhairle understood, hearing it, that his father told such stories on purpose, the way a man puts a plain loaf on the table before a feast so that no one goes to the feast too hungry.

Seann Warr stood where the coast road ran out into shingle, a scatter of low turf-roofed houses set back from the water with their smoke going up thin and straight in the still morning, boats drawn up in a line along the strand, and beyond the houses, where the midden ground ran along the whole landward side of the settlement, Balla nan Stràc.

It was not tall. Somhairle had built it taller in his own mind from the telling, the way a boy builds most things he has only heard of, and found instead a course of flat stones no higher than a man's chest, running the length of two hundred paces or more along the midden's edge, each stone set on end and driven into older stones below it, so that the wall was not built so much as accumulated, one course laid on the last the way frost lays itself on a window a night at a time. He walked the whole length of it with his father before either of them spoke, and when he came near enough to see the faces of the stones he understood what he was looking at.

Every stone bore strokes. Some bore one, cut deep and old and softened at the edges with weather; some bore a dozen, cut close together in threes and fours the way a hand cuts fast when the hand does not want to stop; and near the wall's older end, where the stones stood furthest back and greyest, the strokes ran together into a texture Somhairle could no more count than he could count the grass of a whole field, thousands upon thousands, laid down one household's dead at a time over years past any hill man's reckoning.

"How long has it stood?" he asked, for it was the sort of question the Line trained a boy to ask.

"Longer than the wall at Dùn ìseal," his father said. "Longer, I think, than the grove." He said it without weight, the way he said most things, but he stood a while by the oldest of the stones with his hand resting flat against it, not moving, and Somhairle, watching him, thought for the first time that his father's stillness before a thing was itself a kind of answer, though he did not yet know what question it answered.

The households of Seann Warr, who were Seann-Dhaoine and had always been Seann-Dhaoine, went on about their morning as the king's company walked their midden's edge: mending nets, gutting fish on flat stones worn smooth with the work of it, a child carrying water up from the strand in a skin bag nearly as big as she was. None of them came near. None of them, so far as Somhairle could see, so much as turned to look, though he did not doubt they had marked every man of the company from the moment the dozen horses came over the last rise. His father had told him once, in the high valleys, that the Seann-Dhaoine were not a people who could be asked anything and answered, and Somhairle had taken it then for one more line of the Line, learned and repeated and not much felt. He felt it now, standing at the wall's edge with a whole people's dead cut into stone at his shoulder and not one living soul among them near enough to name a single one.

He set the wall down that night in the words the reciters used, spare and dated and without ornament, the way Coinneach had taught the first of them to set things down: *at Seann Warr the counting stones are built into a wall, some three thousand strokes in all, and the wall grows for many lifetimes.* It was the first thing of his own that Somhairle ever added to the Line, and he kept, all his life afterward, a particular carefulness about it, the way a man keeps a particular carefulness about the first thing he ever built with his own hands, whether or not it stood the straightest.

## II. Of Seann Tarr and the House over the Dead

Nine-and-thirty years went by before Somhairle saw the second of the three shores, and by then he had come into the years where a man of Clann na Ceiste was sent out on his own rather than at his father's stirrup, to keep the lines fresh at the edges of the kingdom where the reciters of the high valleys seldom rode.

He went to Seann Tarr in the north-east on a grey errand that had nothing to do with the Seann-Dhaoine at all, a dispute between two hill households over a stretch of grazing that had crept, as such disputes did, further than either household could honestly remember it ever running, and it was only after he had settled the grazing, a half-day's work that left both sides sullen and neither satisfied, which the reciters counted a fair outcome, that he walked down to the shore to see for himself what the tellings said of that coast.

Seann Tarr was a smaller place than Seann Warr, a dozen households at most, set close along a narrow strand under cliffs that kept the worst of the north wind off the boats. Its dead lay under the living rather than apart from them at a midden's edge: a long low house near the shore's centre, older than the houses around it by the settling of its walls, its floor paved with slabs cut and dressed and laid edge to edge, and on each slab, worked into the stone itself rather than merely cut across it, strokes gathered in fives, four upright and a fifth laid across them, the way a boy ties a bundle of rushes.

The house was lived in. A family sat at its hearth when Somhairle came to the door, an old man mending a net, a woman working something at a low bench he could not make out, two children asleep in a corner on a heap of skins, and none of them rose or spoke or gave any sign that a stranger stood in their doorway, though the old man's eyes came up once, held Somhairle's a moment with neither welcome nor refusal in them, and went back to the net.

He did not go in. Standing at the threshold with the smell of peat smoke and fish oil thick in the low room, he understood that he was looking at a household that had built its home upon its own graves and gone on living there generation after generation, the dead underfoot and counted, the living overhead and continuing; no hill custom he had ever been taught by the Line had prepared him for a people who would choose, of their own accord, to keep so close a floor.

He asked no question, for he had learned that much from his father's stillness at Seann Warr. He stood a while at the door and then walked back up to the cliff path, and set the house down that night as he had set down the wall, spare and dated: *at Seann Tarr the counting comes in fives, cut into the floor of a house, and the house is lived in over its own dead.*

## III. Of the Thickening

It was two-and-forty years further on, in his four hundred and thirty-second year, that Somhairle first heard from the coast road that something on the three shores had changed, and the word came, of all people, from an ordinary carrier of hides, a man he did not know, met by chance at a ford on the road south, who mentioned it the way a man mentions weather, in passing, to fill a silence while the horses drank.

"The stones are thick this year," the carrier said. "Thicker than I've seen them go in a season, and not at one shore only. I heard the same from a man who walks the Seann Tarr road, and another who does the southern coast. Three shores, all at once, and none of them saying why."

Somhairle rode to Seann Warr within the month, and found the wall grown by more courses in that one season than he remembered it growing across the whole of his own boyhood, fresh stone driven in among the old, strokes cut so close upon each other that in places a man could not set a fingertip between one groove and the next. He rode from there to Seann Tarr and found the floor of the house crowded past what its own slabs could easily hold, new stones laid at the room's edges where no stone had lain before, the family who kept it thinner by two than he remembered them, the old man who had mended the net gone from among them and no one at the hearth to say where.

He sent word to his father, less because the Line required it than because he did not know, that year, what else to do with a thing he could not yet set down as a plain dated fact; and the king came down himself, that summer, to Seann Warr, with no great company this time, only Somhairle and three others, and walked the wall's whole length alone while the rest of them waited at its far end.

Somhairle had seen his father stand still before a thing once, at that same wall, eighty years before. He had not seen him stand as he stood that day, a long while, longer than the walk of the wall itself could account for, one hand flat against the newest of the stones the way another man rests a hand on a sick child's brow, and the light off the water going from morning to nearly noon before he turned and came back down the strand to where his son waited.

"Athair," Somhairle said, for he had not called him father in the ordinary way of the hill since he was young enough to be carried on a shoulder, and the word came out of him now unasked, the way a boy's word comes out of a man when a thing has frightened him and he does not yet know it. "What did you see?"

His father looked at him a moment before he answered, and there was nothing in his face that Somhairle could afterward describe as grief, or fear, or any name a reciter might set down and have another reciter understand; only a stillness gone very deep, the warmth that other men felt from him when he took their hand banked low, like a hearth covered for the night rather than let go out.

"A people counting," he said.

He said nothing more of it that day, or, so far as Somhairle ever learned, to anyone else afterward. They rode back to Dùn ìseal in three days rather than the two they had come in, for the king set an easy pace and did not press it, and Somhairle, riding at his side, understood without being told that his father would not be asked again, and did not ask.

He set the visit down that autumn in the fewest words the Line would bear him use: *the king comes to the wall at Seann Warr and stands a long while before it. Asked what he saw, he answers: a people counting.* He added nothing to explain it, for there was nothing in him that could explain it, and he had begun, by then, to learn from his father's own example that the plainest telling of a thing a man cannot understand is often the only honest one.

## IV. Of the Half-Cut Stone

Somhairle did not go south to the coast at Seann Chwen until his four hundred and seventy-fourth year, some forty years after the wall at Seann Warr thickened, and he went then only because a carrier's tale reached the high valleys of a stone left unfinished, a strangeness even among strangenesses, and the reciters of Clann na Ceiste, who by then kept his three earlier tellings among the oldest of the new lines added since Coinneach's death, judged it a thing that wanted a witness rather than a third-hand telling.

Seann Chwen lay two days' ride to the south and west of Dùn ìseal, a larger settlement than either of the other two shores, its houses set close along a strand backed by low dunes, and Somhairle found it, coming down off the dune road in the low light of an evening, quieter than either Seann Warr or Seann Tarr had been at any season he had seen them: fewer boats drawn up than the strand had room for, fewer threads of smoke than the count of houses would account for, doors standing open on rooms he could see were empty from the road.

The midden's stones stood at the shore's southern end, a shorter ridge than the wall at Seann Warr but no less thick with strokes for its shortness, and it was near the ridge's newest end, where the stone should by every pattern Somhairle had learned from the other two shores have carried its freshest cut, that he found Clach nan Dà Stràc, the one the carrier's tale had named.

It stood no different from its neighbours to look at from a distance, flat, grey, driven into the ground at the same angle as the rest. Up close, the difference was plain. Two strokes ran clean and deep across its face, finished the way every other stroke on every other stone he had seen was finished, cut with a steady hand that knew where the groove would end before the tool ever touched the stone. The third stroke began beside them, cut perhaps a third of its length, the chisel's own marks still visible along the cut's floor where a practised hand had worked it, and then stopped, mid-groove, as cleanly as a man's voice stops mid-word when something reaches through a doorway and takes the rest of the sentence out of him.

Somhairle knelt by it a long while in the failing light, running a finger once, and only once, along the unfinished groove, though he had been taught by every teller before him that a hill man laid no hand on the Seann-Dhaoine's stones. He could not afterward say why he did it, only that the stone asked to be touched the way an open wound asks to be looked at, and he had never in four hundred and seventy-four years learned how to refuse a thing that asked so plainly.

No household stood near enough to the ridge that evening to be asked anything, had asking done any good, and Somhairle did not try. He slept that night in the lee of the dunes with his small company, and in the morning, before he rode north again, he walked the ridge once more from end to end and found no stone cut after the half-finished one, not there, and not, when he sent word in the following years to have the wall at Seann Warr and the house at Seann Tarr both checked again, at either of the other two shores.

He set it down as he had set down the rest, in the fewest words the fact would bear: *at Seann Chwen the last stone is left half-cut, two strokes finished and a third begun and forsaken, the chisel marks stopping in the midst of the cut.*

## V. Of the Silence

The last smoke any hill man saw rising from Seann Tarr was seen by a grazing dispute of Somhairle's own settling, the man who had first told him of the thickening years before, now grown old on the same road, who came up to the high valleys that autumn with a tale of finding the house over the dead standing open, its hearth cold, its net-mending bench bare, and no one, living or otherwise, anywhere along that narrow strand to say where the household had gone.

Somhairle rode down himself the following spring and found the man had told it true. The house stood as the carrier had found it, its roof not yet fallen, its door not yet fallen from its hinges, everything in it left as a household leaves a thing it means to come back to and does not; and along the whole shore, in the dozen houses that had stood there in his own remembering, no smoke rose at all, from any hearth, on a morning cold enough that smoke would have shown a mile off had there been any to show.

There was no need to go that year to Seann Warr or Seann Chwen, for word had already reached him from both that the same quiet had come to each in its own season, in no cause a hill man could point to and name, within the span of a few years of each other, until by his five hundred and thirteenth year, when he set the matter down for what he judged would be the last time, there was no shore on the eastern coast, the north-east, or the south where the Seann-Dhaoine still kept a fire.

He rode the coast road himself that last year, alone, from Seann Warr to Seann Tarr and down again to Seann Chwen, three shores in one long circuit no reciter before him had ever made in a single season, and found at each the same stillness: middens grown over at their edges where no new stone had been cut to keep the ground bare, boats left to rot on their keels or gone entirely, the low turf houses softening back, roof by roof, into the shape of the ground they had been raised from. No one at any of the three could tell him where a household had gone, for the households who might have told him were themselves gone, and the hill-folk who had traded that coast for generations had, by then, mostly given over walking to shores that no longer answered a knock.

He set down the silence in the same spare hand he had used for the wall and the house and the stone, and it was the shortest of his four tellings: *after the half-cut stone no work of the Seann-Dhaoine can be dated. Their middens stop growing, their harbours silt, and their strokes are cut no more. How they ended, this telling does not know.* Nothing was put before that last sentence that pretended to know more than it did, though he was asked, more than once in the years that followed, by reciters younger than himself who wanted a fuller answer than the Line was willing to give them. He told them what his father had told him without ever quite saying it in words: that some questions the world does not mean to answer, and a teller does himself no honour going on asking the second kind past the point where asking has anything left to find.

## VI. Of Cairistìona

It was in the ordinary run of things, and not by any turning the Line thought worth marking on its own account, that Somhairle came, in his five hundred and forty-second year, to marry.

She was Cairistìona, of a household in the high valleys that had kept sheep on the same slopes since before the king ever rode north with the six pots, and Somhairle had known her the plain way a man of the valleys knows his neighbours, across a dozen ordinary years of gatherings and shearings and the small business of households that live near one another, before either of them thought to call the knowing anything more than that. She was not of Clann na Ceiste, and had no wish to be; she kept sheep, and mended, and sang, when the mood took her, in a low clear voice that Somhairle had once heard carry the whole length of a shearing pen without anyone in it troubling to hush for her, and she asked him, the autumn he finally asked her, why a man who could recite four generations of a stranger's dead without missing a name had taken so many years to find the one of his own worth the asking.

He had no good answer for her, then or later, beyond the true one, which was that the years did not press on him the way they pressed on other men, and that he had let himself believe, without ever quite deciding to believe it, that there would always be time enough later for the things that were not the Line.

They were married at the turn of the year, in the high valleys, before the fires of both their households and what reciters of Clann na Ceiste could be spared from the slab, and it was, by every account that reached Dùn ìseal afterward, a plain wedding and a glad one, with nothing in it that wanted setting down for its own sake, and Somhairle set nothing of it down himself, for he had learned by then which of the things that happened to a man belonged to the Line and which belonged only to the man.

He did add, that same winter, one line more to the telling of the three shores, the last he ever added to it, for he judged, turning the matter over by his own hearth with Cairistìona asleep beside him, that a fact once told plainly needed no further ornament, whatever a man might privately go on wondering about it for the rest of his years. He wrote only that the coasts that had once answered a hill trader's knock now answered nothing at all, and that this was the whole of what any teller of the Line, king's son or otherwise, had ever truly seen, and set his hand to it, and added, beneath the last line, nothing more.
