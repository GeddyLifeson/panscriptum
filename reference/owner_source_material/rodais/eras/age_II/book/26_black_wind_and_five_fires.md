# Of the Black Wind and the Five Fires

*GE 2,417 – 2,420*

> *Chunnaic mi an teine, is thàinig mi dhachaigh.*
> I saw the fire, and I came home.
>
> — said of the eastern fire at Cuan ruadh, by more than one who came home by it

## I. Of the Wind that Took the Thatch

It came up out of the south-east in the black hour before a morning that never properly broke, and the fisher-folk of Cuan ruadh, who had weathered every wind the sea sent them with names for most and respect for all, had no name ready for this one until it was gone and they needed one to speak of it by. It struck the coast in the first days of an Gearran, in weather that had given no more warning than a wrongness in the swell the evening before, a heave that ran against the wind rather than with it, which the older boats' captains read and put their craft up on the shingle a full tide early for no reason they could afterward explain better than that the sea had looked wrong to them.

By midday it had stripped the thatch from every roof between Cuan ruadh and Ceann chiar, a stretch of coast a long day's walk and more, and driven three boats that had not been hauled high enough onto the rocks below the headland, and flattened, in one household's near ruin, a drying-rack hung with the better part of a season's salted fish that the sea took back grain of salt and flake of flesh together before anyone could reach it. The wind did not merely blow; it took, the way the old tellings say a wolf takes and not merely eats, choosing what it wanted and leaving the rest broken in the road. Children were carried indoors and held there bodily by mothers who could not otherwise keep them from the doorway to watch it. An old woman of Ceann chiar, who had seen eighty winters on that coast and outlived two husbands lost to lesser storms than this one, said afterward that she had heard the wind speak, though she would not say in what tongue, and no one on that coast ever doubted she believed it, whatever the rest of them made of the claim.

It blew out by the second dusk, going as suddenly as it had come, and left behind it a coast without roofs enough to shelter half the households that had had them two days before, granaries opened to the sky, and boats that would need a full season's mending before they swam again. Of Cuan ruadh's own eleven boats, four were fit for nothing but firewood, and a fifth would need a new keel before it saw water. The fishing that fed the coast through the dark months, which had never in living memory failed outright, failed that winter before it had properly begun, for a coast with half its boats broken and half its granaries roofless could neither catch nor keep enough against the cold.

It was the eldest folk of that coast, remembering other winds with other names and no name for this, who first called it A' Ghaoth Dhubh, for the sky it left behind was the colour of a smoored hearth for three days after, ash-dark from horizon to horizon though not a cloud stood in it, the way a fire's smoke hangs low over a valley long after the flame that made it is out. No reciter of the hill ever came to set the name straighter, and the coast never asked for a straighter one. It has been A' Ghaoth Dhubh on every tongue that has spoken of it since, the eldest storm whose name the coast still keeps whole, and every wind that has struck that shore in all the years since has been measured against it and found lesser.

No one of that coast died in the wind itself, a mercy the tellings are careful to set down plainly, for a wind that could take a roof whole could as easily have taken a life, and every household on the shore has its own small telling of how it came to be spared: a door that held when a neighbour's did not, a child called in from the shingle a breath before the worst of it struck, a boat left one turn later than it should have been on a rota that no one afterward could say why it had fallen out that way. What the wind took instead, it took slowly, through the winter that followed and not in the day itself: a season's food, a season's shelter, and, for more than one household, the last of a fishing life that a lost boat and a maimed hand between them made too hard to carry on. It was the winter after the wind, and not the wind's own day, that the coast came to reckon as the harder half of what A' Ghaoth Dhubh cost it.

## II. Of the Winter the Custom Reached the Coast

What saved Cuan ruadh that winter, when its own granaries lay open to the weather and its own boats lay broken on the shingle, was a custom the coast had never troubled to take up on its own account, having never before needed it. The sharing had begun generations before at Dùn ìseal, in the valleys under the hill, a grain-year's surplus carried in at Samhain and given back out by the count of mouths so that no household went into the dark months harder than its neighbour; and it had gone out from the hill slowly, the way such things go, along the roads that carried salt and flint and wool between one valley and the next, taken up first by households that had seen it worked at the hill and thought it good, then by households that had only heard of it and thought it good enough to try.

By the year of A' Ghaoth Dhubh it had reached, by whatever crooked road a custom travels, the settlements of that same coast north and south of the worst of the wind, Ceann chiar among them, and the inland valleys a day and more west of Cuan ruadh, households that grew barley and kept cattle on ground the coast had none to spare for, and knew nothing of storms that struck from the sea because their own hills stood between them and the worst of the weather. Word of what the wind had done to the coast reached those valleys within the week, carried by a trader who had meant to buy salt fish at Cuan ruadh and found instead a shore of broken boats and bare roof-trees. It was those settlements and valleys, with no word sent down from Dùn ìseal at all, that first sent grain east to a coast most of their own households had never set eyes on.

It went first as a scatter of single acts, one household sending what it could spare because a neighbour had told it what the trader said, and another following because the first had; and it was only in the second month of that winter, when the scatter had grown too large for any one household to account for on its own, that the valleys' own holders sat down together and agreed, in words close enough to the hill's own custom that whoever named it first is not remembered, to send it on as the hill would have sent it, weighed by the count of mouths at Cuan ruadh and given without asking what any one household there had managed to save from the wreck of its own store. Grain came down on carts and on the backs of the valleys' own cattle, salted meat in skins, wool enough to card and weave new roofing against the winters still to come, and none of it came with any reckoning kept of who at Cuan ruadh had lost the most and who the least, for that was not how the custom had ever been kept at the hill, and the valleys, having taken it up, kept it whole.

No household of that coast is known to have starved that winter, and the tellings of Cuan ruadh, when they came at last to set the year down, gave the custom no grander name than the coast's own folk gave it: the bringing-in, as it was called wherever it went, or, among the plainer-spoken, only the help, without further word for what it was or where it had first been thought of. It is told that not every household on that coast learned the custom's name or its origin that winter or ever after; it is told that every household learned, before the spring tide, that the grain had come, and had been fair, and had been enough.

## III. Of Lìleas, Who Watched the Boats Come In

Lìleas of Cuan ruadh was one-and-twenty that winter, the second daughter of a household that fished the water off the headland in a boat her father had built with his own hands and lost, along with two fingers of his left hand taken by a line under strain, on the second day of A' Ghaoth Dhubh. She had grown up on that coast knowing the sea the way a hill-child knows weather, by the smell of it before it showed itself and by what her elders' faces did when they looked at it, and had spent every autumn of her life since she was old enough to be trusted on the shingle watching for the boats to come home before dark, a habit the whole coast kept without troubling to call it anything, because a coast that fishes for its living watches its own boats home as a matter of course.

That winter, with her father's hand still healing crooked and the household's boat six weeks from swimming again, she had less to watch for than she was used to, and more time on the shingle than the work of the house strictly needed of her; and it was in that idle watching, as she told whoever asked her how the thing began, that she first came to notice what the coast had never troubled to put into words before: that a boat coming home in failing light, even a boat whose captain knew every rock of that shore by feel, ran a different risk in the last stretch before the beach than in all the miles before it, because the land, once the sun was down, gave a returning boat nothing to steer by but memory and the sound of the surf.

She had heard, from a trader who had come through with grain that same winter, of a fire kept somewhere on a headland of the western coast, built for a purpose she did not then understand well enough to repeat correctly to her own household, and had turned the tale over more than once through that long, roofless, half-fed winter without doing anything about it beyond turning it over, for it was, she said afterward, a stranger's tale of a stranger's coast, and she saw no reason a headland of Cuan ruadh needed anything a western headland had thought worth doing.

What changed her mind, so far as the telling of it can be trusted, was neither the trader's tale nor any single storm, but the ordinary run of that whole hard winter, watching boat after boat come in later than she liked in light that was worse than she liked, and reckoning, each time, how much of what she watched for on the shingle she could not in truth see at all once the light had gone, and how much she was only listening for and hoping. A coast that watched its boats home by daylight had never needed more than eyes for it. A coast whose boats, mended late and fished hard to make up a lost season, were coming home later and later into the dark as the days shortened toward midwinter, needed, she came slowly to think, something the eye alone no longer served.

## IV. Of the Fire She Kept on the Eastern Cape

She put it first to her own household, over a meal thin enough that winter that no one at the board had much appetite for argument, and her father, whose hand had healed crooked enough that he no longer trusted it fully on a line in rough water, was the first to say he saw no harm in it and perhaps some good, if she meant to do the work of keeping it herself and did not look to the rest of the household to carry wood up a headland on top of everything else the winter already asked of them. It was agreed, in the plain way such things were agreed on that coast, without any vote taken and without anyone troubling to write the agreement down anywhere but in the memory of the household that made it: Lìleas would keep a fire on the cape east of the village, in her own household's turn, and any other household that wished to share the carrying of wood, or the watching of it through a night, was welcome to, and none was asked to.

The cape stood a stiff climb above the village, a shoulder of bare rock with nothing on it before that winter but sea-birds and the low scrub that grew wherever the salt wind let anything grow at all, and it took her and her younger brother the better part of a month of short winter days to carry up enough driftwood and cut peat to keep even a modest fire burning through a single long night, let alone through the run of nights a whole storm season might ask for. She built the fire first in a shallow pit scraped into the bare rock, walled on the seaward side with stones carried up one and two at a time from the shingle below, so that the wind that would have scattered an open fire in an hour instead drove the flame and the smoke both out toward the water, where any boat looking for it would see it best.

The first nights she kept it alone, sitting by it through the dark with a skin of water and whatever her mother could spare from the household's own thin table, watching the black water below for a shape that did not come, for no boat of Cuan ruadh's own crippled fleet was out on the worst of those first nights to need finding by anything. She kept it anyway, through nights that gave her nothing back for the keeping but cold hands and a fire that burned down faster than she had reckoned it would, and it is told, though the telling allows this much may be kindness rather than plain memory, that more than one household of the village climbed the cape those first weeks with no boat of their own out at all, only to sit an hour with her and see for themselves what she was about, and went down again having seen nothing but a woman and a fire and the black sea giving back no more than its own darkness.

It was her brother, keeping the fire with her on a night neither of them afterward could put a precise date to, who first said aloud what neither had quite let themselves say before: that if the fire served no boat that winter, it would have cost them a month's wood and nothing more, and that a month's wood was a thing the household could bear to lose against the chance, however small that first winter made it look, of a night when it would not be wasted. Lìleas kept the fire the rest of that season on no better reasoning than her brother's, and no worse, and told anyone who asked her why she troubled with it exactly that: that it cost little enough to keep, and might cost the coast a boat and its crew if it were not there the one night it was needed.

## V. Of the Fires that Answered It

Word of the fire on Cuan ruadh's cape went along the coast roads that winter and the next the way word of any new thing travels among people who talk to strangers at every ford and market, faster and further than the fire's own small light could have carried it, and by the second winter after A' Ghaoth Dhubh, three other coasts had heard enough of it, and had troubled enough over their own boats in their own bad weather, to want fires of their own.

The northern point took to it first, where the coast turned hardest and the currents ran fastest against a boat trying to make the shore in the dark. The households there raised a fire on ground that had, without anyone quite planning it that way, already been scorched black for longer than living memory by shore-fires kept for other reasons in other ages; theirs burned steadier than Cuan ruadh's Teine a' Chlachair had, for they had Lìleas's own year of trial to learn from before they ever laid their own wood, and never once let it fail through a whole storm season once it was begun. The cape north-east of that, where the passage in toward the deeper water ran narrow enough that a boat astray by even a boat's length could find rock instead of channel, was next: a man named Seathan mac Pheadair took the fire's keeping on himself much as Lìleas had, alone at first and then with kin who came to share it, and cut, on a stone at the fire's edge, one stroke for every boat he ever saw pass safe by his flame, a tally that within a few years had grown longer than any one telling has troubled to count out in full.

Last of the four to be built, and by every account the surest of them, was the fire of the southern cape at Tobar dhearg, raised on a mound of stacked stone breast-high rather than a scraped pit as Cuan ruadh's first had been, so that the flame stood clear of the spray even in the roughest weather. The coast's own reciters were already saying, before the mound was finished, that with Cuan ruadh's fire and this one and the two between them, and the older fire that had burned so long on the Baile chrom headland in the north-west that no one living could say when it was first lit, the sea round the whole island now had five points of flame to answer it in the dark, cape answering cape the length of the coast, so that a boat lost in a storm off any shore of Dia-thìr had, for the first time within any telling's memory, a fire somewhere within its sight to steer toward. It was from that reckoning, made first by a reciter of Tobar dhearg who never troubled to give her own name to the saying, that the coast came to call them together the five fires, and has called them so on every telling since.

None of the four newer fires were kept the same way twice; each coast built its own to its own ground and its own weather, and none of the tellings that keep them troubles to say one was better tended than another, save that Cuan ruadh's was first, and Lìleas its first keeper, a fact the coast has never once let a traveller forget, whatever else about that hard winter has worn thin in the telling.

## VI. Of the Boat that Came Home by It

It was in the third winter of the fire's keeping, on a night that gave no more warning than A' Ghaoth Dhubh had given, that the fire did the one thing every night before had only promised it might. A boat out of a village two coasts north of Cuan ruadh, blown far off its own water by a wind that had turned three times in an afternoon and given its crew nothing steady to read, came upon full dark with a failing wind, a following sea it did not know, and a coastline it could no longer place by any mark its captain trusted.

Lìleas was on the cape that night with her brother and, for once, her father, whose hand had healed well enough by then to carry wood if no longer to trust a line, and it was her father who first saw, or thought he saw, a shape against the last grey of the water that was not quite the shape of a wave. He said nothing at first, for a fisherman who cries a boat where there is none costs his household nothing but a wasted watch, but he set more wood on the fire than the night's cold alone called for, and it burned up bright and high enough that within a little while none of the three of them any longer doubted there was a boat out there, low and struggling, working hard against a sea that wanted to put it onto rock rather than sand.

What the boat's own captain said afterward, to anyone who asked him in the villages he touched for the rest of his working life, was that he had given the coast up for lost an hour before, was steering by nothing better than the set of the wind against his face and a growing certainty that the next thing his keel touched would be stone, and had seen, low on the black land ahead of him, a single point of fire where he had expected nothing at all: no village lit for a feast, no house left burning by carelessness, nothing he could account for by any tale he knew of that shore, only a fire burning high and steady on a headland with no house near it that he could see. He steered for it because he had nothing better left to steer for, and found, coming in under it at last with his boat still whole and his crew all breathing, a beach he did not know and three figures on the cape above him who came down the path with torches to help him haul his boat clear of the tide before he had so much as told them his own name.

He told Lìleas, once he had breath enough to tell her anything, that he had not known the fire was kept for boats like his until he was already steering for it, and asked her, in the plain way of a man who has just been given his life back and does not yet know how to speak of the size of the gift, why a coast that had never seen him before would keep a fire burning all a hard winter's night for a boat that might never come. She told him, in words the coast has kept ever since as near to what she said as any telling of a moment like that can promise to be, that the fire was kept for whichever boat needed it, on whichever night it came, no more for him than for any other, and that this was the whole of the reason for it and had been from the first cold night she carried wood up the cape alone and found nothing there to answer her but her own fire and the dark. He slept that night, and three nights after while his boat was made fit to sail home in, in the household that had fed most of that coast through the winter of A' Ghaoth Dhubh and had lit, out of that same winter's need, the first of the five fires that now stood along every shore of Dia-thìr the sea could reach in the dark; and it is that fire, burning on the cape above Cuan ruadh through every storm season since, that the coast's own tellers point to first, of all the five, whenever they are asked which one began it.
