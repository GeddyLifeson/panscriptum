# Of Gormshuil at the Gate

*GE 2 – 106*

## I. Of the Watching Child

She was eight when the ditch was pegged round the crown of the hill, old enough to be given a task and trusted to see it through, which on Dùn ìseal in those years meant standing at the gate in the gap of the new ditch and counting who came and went, so that her father, who had never asked for a gatekeeper and had never needed one before the ditch was dug, would have someone to tell him at evening whether the day had brought anything worth his hearing.

Gormshuil took to the work the way some children take to a thing before anyone has decided whether it suits them: without being asked twice, and without complaint, and with a memory for faces that her mother said was not natural in a girl her age and was, all the same, glad of. She knew every household on the hill by the second summer, knew which dog belonged to which byre, knew the traders who came up from the valley with hides and the ones who came with nothing to trade and everything to ask. She stood at the gate in all weathers, wrapped in a plaid too big for her that had been her mother's, and watched the ditch deepen under the diggers' mattocks summer by summer, and the raw earth of the bank rise and harden until, in her fifteenth year, it closed at its gate and no longer looked like a wound in the hill.

Her father she saw less than the diggers saw the ditch. He rode out most mornings with a handful of men and came back most evenings, and some months he did not come back at all, and word of him arrived instead by whoever had last seen him, three valleys off or five, settling a quarrel or riding through country that had not yet troubled itself to send word of quarrels to anyone. When he was on the hill he sat at the crown of it in the low turf-roofed house that had been built for him and was already too small, and heard whoever came to be heard, and Gormshuil, who by nine had worked out that the gate and the hearing-house stood in a line she could watch both from, took to sitting on the bank between the two of them with her knees drawn up, watching her father the way she watched everyone else who came and went, as though he too were someone she had not yet finished learning.

It was in her twenty-fourth year that word came up from the west, carried by a trader with salt fish who had it from a trader with wool who had it from further off still, that a woman called Beathag Ruadh held a hill on the shore of the great lake, a red hill, he said, though no one on Dùn ìseal had seen it, and that she held it of her own right and not of any king's. Gormshuil, sitting on the bank with the fish-trader's words going round the hearing-house like smoke finding a draught, asked her father that evening whether the red hill's woman meant trouble for them. Her father said that a hill a long way off with its own line of holders was no trouble to anyone, and that trouble, when it came, came from nearer at hand and was easier to see coming than a woman on a lake shore three weeks' walk west. She did not understand then what he meant by it. She understood later, when she had learned that her father rarely answered a question with less in it than the question itself had carried.

## II. Of Her Mother's Dying

Muireall had never taken to the hill the way her daughter had. She had come to it already a grown woman, with a way of holding herself that the hill's own women, used to going where the work was and standing where it put them, found hard to place. It read as pride to those who did not know her. The truer word for it was the bearing of someone who had once expected her days to be minded by others and had found herself, past the middle of her life, minding her own, and her husband's besides, in a country she had not chosen. She kept the king's house as well as it could be kept with what the hill afforded, bore him two sons on the crown of it after Gormshuil, Dòmhnall in the second year of the bank and Fearchar some years after, and grew, through all of it, thinner and greyer at a pace that her daughter, watching everyone from the gate as she watched everyone, was the first on the hill to notice and the last to say aloud.

Muireall died in the twenty-fourth year of the hill, in an autumn that brought nothing else worth remembering, of no sickness anyone could name and no hurt anyone had seen taken. Gormshuil, thirty that year and past the age where a mother's dying is a child's grief only, sat with her the last two nights and heard nothing from her that the hill later thought fit to keep as a saying, only the ordinary things a dying woman says to a daughter who has sat up with her: where a particular comb had come from, whom a particular debt was owed to, that the youngest boy, Fearchar, was to be watched, for he had a temper his brother lacked and would want checking before it found him trouble he could not talk his way out of.

The king came in from the valleys two days after she was put in the ground, and stood by the mound a long while without anyone daring to go near him, and then went into the hearing-house and heard three cases that had been waiting on him and gave judgement on all three before dark, and never, in all the years his daughter afterward kept the gate and watched him come and go, spoke Muireall's name again where anyone could hear it. Gormshuil, who had expected grief from him and got instead the ordinary business of the hill taken up exactly where it had been left, understood only much later, when she had watched him bury more of his own than any one person should have to bury and go on burying them in the same fashion, what the going-back-to-work truly was: the whole shape grief took in a man who could not be seen to carry it the way other men carried theirs. Other men, in time, stopped carrying it. He never would.

## III. Of the Hard Winter and the Warm Ground

Two winters later came the hardest anyone then living on the hill had seen, snow lying from before Samhain until past the turn of the year, deep enough in the high folds of the valley to bury a standing sheep to the back, and the byres on the crown of the hill, never built for more than an ordinary cold, began by midwinter to empty of their own accord: not to wolves, for the wolves that year kept as much to shelter as the cattle did, but to the cold itself, working on beasts that had nowhere warm enough to stand and nothing left under the snow worth pawing for.

The king was in the north that winter, three valleys off and more, holding some quarrel down that had waited three seasons for him and would not wait a fourth, and no word could be got to him and back again before the herds were finished. It was Gormshuil, thirty-three that year and holding the hill for her father as she had by then held it more times than either of them troubled to count, who gave the order to drive what stock still stood down off the crown of the hill and along the frozen valley floor to the warm springs, where the earth steams the year round and the snow, however hard it falls, does not lie.

It was two days' driving, though the springs lie no more than a morning's walk below the hill in summer, through drifts that in places stood higher than the beasts themselves, with men and dogs both worked past what either would ordinarily be asked, and one night spent in the open on ground too cold for anyone to sleep sound, and Gormshuil walked the whole of it on her own feet at the head of the drive rather than ride, for a rider's beast, she said, ate what a walking man's did not, and every mouthful of fodder they carried was owed to the herd and not to anyone's comfort. They came down onto the warm ground on the second morning with the steam rising off the wet grass into air still hard with frost, and the cattle, smelling it before the drovers had done more than see it, went down the last slope at a run that no amount of driving had got out of them in two days of snow.

She lost none. It was said afterward, and is said still wherever the hill's own reciters keep the tale, that no herd of Dùn ìseal's had ever gone into a hard winter and come out the other side of it whole, and that no herd did again for a long while after; and it is said too, by those who keep the harder telling alongside the easier one, that Gormshuil came off that drive with two fingers on her left hand that never afterward bent all the way, and never spoke of them, and wore, from that winter to the end of her life, a glove on that hand in company and bare skin only among her own household, where no one thought to remark on it because everyone had always known.

## IV. Of the Gate and the Years Between

Her father married again in her thirty-sixth year, a woman of the valleys called Sorcha, younger than Gormshuil herself by a dozen years and more, which the hill noted and did not remark on aloud, for it had by then grown used to noting things about the king's household that it did not remark on aloud. Sorcha kept the house with less of Muireall's stiffness and more of the valley's easy give-and-take, and bore him two sons in turn, Somhairle first and Iain Dubh some years after, and Gormshuil, who might by rights have resented a stepmother younger than herself keeping the house she had half kept herself since her own mother died, found instead that she liked the woman, and that the liking cost her nothing, for by then she had other things pressing on her that a stepmother's presence did nothing to change one way or the other.

What pressed on her was the gate, and had pressed on her since she was eight, and did not let up now that she was a woman grown with a household and children of her own coming on. Her father rode out each spring with the thaw and came back each autumn with the first hard frost, as regular in it as the seasons themselves, and every year that passed, it seemed to Gormshuil watching from the bank where she had once sat as a child, he came back looking less like a man who had spent a winter riding through weather that aged other men, and more, if anything, like the same young man who had walked south so many summers before with a name the grove had given him and nothing else anyone could point to.

She did not speak of it to him, not in those years, for there was no way to raise the matter that did not sound like an accusation of something neither of them had chosen, and she was not, whatever else she was, a woman who raised what could not be answered. She spoke of it once to Coinneach, her father's brother, who had followed him north those first seven nights with food he would not eat and had stayed by him ever after, growing old in the ordinary way while his brother did not, teaching whoever would learn it the Line of the Mason as the Keeper had given it to Ailean in the grove, word for word, changing nothing, for he held, and taught Gormshuil to hold after him, that a true thing needed no improving.

"He does not age," she said to Coinneach, on the bank one autumn evening with her father not yet three days back from the valleys and the light going long and gold across the crown of the hill. "I have watched him thirty years since the grove and he does not age, and I am beginning to."

Coinneach, who by then had gone the colour of ash himself and moved more slowly than he once had, looked at her a long while before he gave her the nearest thing to an answer he ever would. "I was there the night the fires went out," he said. "What came into the grove, I saw. What it cost him, he has never told me, and I have never asked. Between the two of us, niece, that is a fair division of the matter: he keeps what went into him, and I keep from asking. Take the same division for yourself, if you can bear it."

She bore it, because there was nothing else to be done with it, and because Coinneach himself, who had borne it longer than she had and would not live to see how much longer it went on, seemed to think it bearable. He died the winter she turned sixty-one, an old man by then in every particular, the first teller of her father's deeds and the last of those who had stood outside the grove on the first night and could say what the waiting had been like from the near side of it; and Gormshuil, who took the Line of the Mason from him whole in his last winter, the way he had taken it whole from no one but his own memory of the grove, found that she had, without quite meaning to, become the second person on the hill after her father to carry the whole of it in her head.

## V. Of the Grandchildren Who Took Him for Her Son

By her sixtieth year Gormshuil's own hair had gone from the black it had been to a grey that no longer surprised anyone who looked at her, and her back, which had straightened proudly enough through her middle years, had begun to take on the particular stoop of a woman who has spent forty summers standing at a gate in every weather the hill could send. Her children by then had children of their own running loose about the crown of the hill, a generation that had never known a Dùn ìseal without its bank and ditch and took the shape of the place for as given as the hill itself; and it was among these grandchildren, more than anyone older and wiser ought to have allowed, that the confusion first took root that would trouble Clann na Ceiste's tellers for as long as they kept the tale.

The king came in at the gate each autumn a young man, unchanged from the young man who had ridden out each spring, and his daughter who met him there was by then old enough to be anyone's grandmother; and the children, who saw the two of them together perhaps a dozen days in a year and had no memory of a time before Gormshuil was old, took the young man at the gate for her son and not her father, and called him so among themselves, and were not, when the mistake was pointed out to them by an elder who had known both from the start, greatly inclined to unlearn it, for the mistake fit what they saw with their own eyes a good deal better than the truth did.

Gormshuil herself never corrected them past the first few times. It was, she told her eldest granddaughter once, easier to let a child believe a small wrong thing that harmed no one than to make her carry a large true one before she had the years to hold it; and besides, she said, and this she said only the once, and only to the girl, and never again to anyone, there were mornings now when she looked at the young man coming up the gate-track with the autumn light behind him and found the mistake easier to bear herself than the truth was, for a mother growing old beside a son who stayed young is only the ordinary sorrow of the world running backward, while a daughter doing the same beside her father has no shape that the world has a name for yet.

## VI. Of the Last Autumn

She was eighty-one the autumn she died, an old woman by every measure the hill kept, bent nearly double by then and blind in one eye from a cataract no one on Dùn ìseal had the skill to lift, and she had asked, some months before, to be carried to the gate one last time to see the year's herds brought up off the summer grazing, for she said she had watched every herd come up that track for near seventy years and did not care to break the habit on the strength of dying.

Her father was on the hill that autumn, having come in early, before the frost, as though he had known, though no one afterward could say how a man might know such a thing, or whether he had known it at all, or only chanced to be there. He sat with her the last two nights of her life in the low house where she had lived since her children grew and left it to her, and those who kept watch outside said only that no voice was raised in it, and no weeping either, only the ordinary small sounds of two people sitting together with a great deal between them and very little of it needing to be said aloud after so long.

It was on the second night, near dawn, that she asked him the thing she had carried since the evening on the bank with Coinneach, more than twenty years dead by then, and had never asked her father himself in all that time, for there had never seemed to be a right hour for it and now, she judged, there would be no other hour at all.

"Does it hurt," she said, "to stay young?"

He did not answer her. Those who were near enough to hear said afterward that he sat a long while with her hand in his, the hand with the two fingers that had never bent right since the winter of the warm ground. What he gave her instead, when at last he spoke, was her name, said once, in the way a man says a name he does not expect to have cause to say again. Whether that was answer enough for her, or no answer at all, or something a daughter and a father had settled between themselves long before that night without either needing the words for it, none of those keeping watch outside the house could say, and none of them ever claimed to know.

She died before the light came up fully over the crown of the hill. The king sat by her a night and a day after that, through the whole of the following day and into the second night, and spoke to no one who came to the door, and ate nothing that was brought to him, and when he came out at last it was to see her put into the ground with his own hands helping to lift the turf, which he had done for no one since Muireall and would do, in the long years that followed, for very few others; and he never, from that day to the last day anyone on the hill saw him, spoke his eldest daughter's name aloud again where another ear could catch it, any more than he had spoken her mother's.

It was then, and not before, that the hill understood in full what its own eyes had been telling it for seventy years and its tongue had been too careful, or too frightened, to say plainly: that the long years the king carried were the grove's alone, given once and given whole to the fires that burned in it, and that they did not pass by blood to a child got before the grove was ever lit, however much of the king's own bearing and will such a child might carry in every other particular. Gormshuil had carried his stubbornness, his stillness under a question he did not mean to answer, and something in the set of her shoulders at the gate that anyone who had watched him stand at the door of the hearing-house would have known at a glance for his; she had not carried the fire, and would not, and the hill, watching her go into the ground an old bent woman beside a father who looked her grandson's age, understood at last what it would mean for every child of his get after her, and for every child not yet got, and fell, for a season, unusually quiet on the matter, the way a hill falls quiet over a thing too large to be talked through in an evening.

## VII. Of Dòmhnall Taking the Hill

Dòmhnall was seventy-four that year, the eldest of the king's children born after the grove and the first child of his blood to draw breath inside the new bank, and he had none of his half-sister's stoop nor any grey in him at all, for he carried what she had not: a young man's face on a man three-quarters of a century old, and a young man's back besides, straight as a stripped sapling, so that those who saw him take up the seat in the hearing-house that his sister had held for near five decades saw, or thought they saw, no change at all, only the same shape of authority moving from one body to another in a manner too smooth to properly mark as a passing.

It was not, for all that, taken lightly on the hill. Dòmhnall had grown up under his sister's watch as much as under either parent's, for she had kept the gate through the whole of his childhood and a fair part of his manhood besides, and he stood at her grave-mound the morning after the turf was laid with an expression that those who knew him best said they had never seen on him before and rarely saw again, and said, to no one in particular, that he supposed he had better learn to count who came and went, since no one else on the hill seemed inclined to teach him and there was no one left alive who had learned it from the very start the way she had.

He took up the hearing-house within the month, for the hill's business did not wait on grief any more than it had waited on the king's grief for Muireall fifty years before, and the reciters, setting his name down after his sister's in the eldest of the spoken lists, called him third of the Stone Kings and left the gap between his naming and hers, eighty years and more by the tellers' later count, standing in the record exactly as long as it had stood in the life of the hill, for that, the reciters held, was itself a kind of truth-telling, and no one who came after them ever thought to shorten it.
