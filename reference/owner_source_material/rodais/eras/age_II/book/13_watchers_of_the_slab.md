# Of the Watchers of the Slab

*GE 1,071 – 1,174*

*In Na Glinn Àrda the keepers still say the first watch was kept by a man who did not need to sleep and sat it anyway, so that no one after him could claim the cold had been too much to ask.*

## I. Of the Line Kept in the Valleys

Na Glinn Àrda held no bank and no ditch and no hearing-house, and wanted none, for the households that lived there had never been households in the way the crown of Dùn ìseal understood the word: they did not gather grazing, and they did not marry outward more than they had to, and they kept, from father to son and from mother to daughter, a thing no other valley on the island troubled to keep at all, which was Sloinneadh a' Chlachair entire, every name of it, said the same way twice running or said again until it was. Somhairle had brought it up into the hills himself as a boy of a hundred and sixty who looked fifteen, taking no share of his father's kingdom and asking for none, and by the year this telling opens he had carried it the better part of a thousand years, in a memory that had by then grown old enough to hold more than any one household's business could have put into it.

The valleys knew him for that, and for little else. He was not a lord among them, for Clann na Ceiste had no lord; he was the one who came round at Bealltainn and at Samhain and sat by whatever fire would have him, and said the Line through from Ailean at the grove down to whoever in the house had been born that year, and let the house correct him if a name had slipped, though no house ever found one that had. He kept, besides the Line, the telling of the coal: how the mason had broken the vein at Tobar dhìreach and the lightning had fired it; how na Seachd Guail had been lit one by one down the generations, each by its own spirit; and how an gual dathach that ran through the rock under the hill country held all seven, though no one, not even Somhairle, who had asked his father once and been given no more of an answer than a look, could say what his father had carried out of the grove.

The slab lay above Tobar dhìreach on a shelf of ground that no household had ever built a byre against, a grey stone longer than a standing man, laid flat by the mason's own people over the broken vein when its fire had burned itself out, as the Line keeps it, with a stain at its lower edge the size of two cupped hands that had never, in all the years anyone kept count of such things, washed paler with any rain. Children were told, and told their own children after, that the stain was the mark of Teine a' Chlachair, which had burned in the vein beneath until there was nothing left in the break for it to burn; whether that telling was truer than any other telling of the stain, Somhairle himself would not say, holding, as he held of most things touching his father's blood, that a thing not given to him to know was not his to guess at aloud.

In the fifth year past Anna nic Coinnich's basket and her cupped hand at Achadh dhìreach, the ground a little below the slab's lower edge began, in a wet spring that brought down more of the hillside than any spring before it, to show a crack no wider than two fingers, running some yards toward the burn and then losing itself in turf that had not moved. It was nothing to trouble a herd over, and no house near it lost so much as a lamb into it in all the years it was watched; but it lay close enough under the slab's own stain that the valleys, who had learned by then to be careful of what the coal-marked ground did when it moved, set a line of white stones along it the way the coast had done at the crack Seasaidh's people knew, and left it at that for the better part of fifty years.

The handful had come up into Na Glinn Àrda the same road the tale of it had come, carried by traders and by Somhairle's own telling at the fires, and had taken there no differently than it had taken on the crown of the hill: a household that had never lost anyone to the coal took the custom on trust from households that had, and cupped its hand, and burned no more than the hand would hold, and asked no further questions of a rule that answered its own case well enough. Clann na Ceiste had, besides, more reason than most to keep the handful close, for they lived nearer the coal's own ground than any household of the lowlands did, in valleys where a herder turning peat for the winter fire might, on an unlucky day, turn up a vein no thicker than a finger running bright through ordinary black turf, and know, from the telling if from nothing else, to leave it exactly where it lay and cut his peat a spade's length further along.

## II. Of the First Turn

It was at Samhain in the eleven hundred and twentieth year that the households nearest the slab, gathered as they gathered every year at Samhain, fell to talking of the crack below the stain, and one among them, whose name the Line does not keep though the deed is kept in his stead, said that a thing close under the coal's own stone ought not to be left with no one minding it through the dark half of the year, the way a fire banked for the night is minded by whoever last built it up. No one could say what the minding was for, nor what it was minding against; but the thing was agreed the way most things were agreed in those valleys, by no one arguing hard enough against it to be worth outlasting, and it was settled that a household would sit by the slab from dusk to dusk, one household at a time, turn and turn again, from that Samhain until the light came back in the spring.

Beathan was among the first six to sit it, and no one thought it strange, for Beathan had by then lived among the herding households of Na Glinn Àrda the better part of five hundred years, looking, in all that time, no more than thirty, and had long since stopped being remarked on for it by anyone who had grown up seeing him about the fires. He was Somhairle's son and the king's grandson, and he took no more of a share than his father did, and he sat his turns the way any household sat them, wrapped in the same plaid, eating the same bannock, minding the same fire built low against the slab's foot so that its light would not run down the crack and its smoke would not hang where a watcher needed clear eyes.

The household whose turn followed his that first winter was a herding family from the shoulder of the hill above the burn, a man called Gilleonan and his wife Ìde, newly married that same autumn and glad enough, in the way of the newly married, of any night that put them alone together away from both their mothers' hearths, whatever the ground beneath them happened to be doing. Gilleonan had heard of Beathan the way every child of the valleys heard of him, as a fact of the place and not a wonder, the way a child hears that the burn runs cold even in summer; but he had never sat a night beside him until the two households' turns fell close enough together that autumn that Beathan, coming up early to see the fire well banked before he handed the watch on, found Gilleonan and Ìde already there ahead of their own hour, neither of them quite willing to say why, both of them glad of an excuse to sit a while longer by a fire that was, whatever else it was, warmer than their own hearth that first married winter.

The watch asked little of the body and a good deal of the patience. A household came up before dusk with peat enough for the night and food enough for two, built the fire low against the slab's own foot the way every watch before it had built it, and sat, turn and turn about within the turn, one waking while the other slept a little if the night allowed it, until the sky over the shoulder of the hill paled enough to call the thing done. There was talking, when the cold did not close a mouth too tight for it, and there was silence, and there was the ordinary work a hand can do sitting down, a distaff or a knife and a stick, and there was the slab, grey and close and no more talkative than any other stone, giving back the fire's low light along its stain and nothing else all night for year upon year. Six other households besides Beathan's and Gilleonan's stood in that first round, a shepherd's widow from above the ford of the burn, a household of basket-weavers who came up from lower in the glen only for their turn and grumbled at the walk every time, two brothers who split every watch between them rather than share it with wives who wanted no part of a cold night on a hillside, and a household so newly settled in the valleys that its turn, the first winter, fell to a woman who had not yet learned the names of half her neighbours and sat it, by her own later telling, more afraid of saying the wrong thing to Somhairle's son than of anything the ground might do. None of the six, in all the years the first round held, saw the crack do more than it had ever done, which was to lie there, unwidened, and let the frost silver its white stones along with everything else on the hillside; and it became, before the round was five years old, as ordinary a thing on a household's calendar as a lambing or a peat-cutting, a night set aside and kept, without anyone troubling any longer to wonder aloud whether the keeping served any purpose at all.

## III. Of Ìde Grown Old

They sat their turn every year after that, as their household's place in the round came round to them, and every year Beathan sat his own turn near enough to theirs, by no arrangement anyone could point to, that the two watches came to be spoken of together, Beathan's and the young herding couple's, as though the valleys had quietly decided the four of them made one turn between them rather than two. Gilleonan filled out through his twenties into the broad, slow-spoken man his father had been before him, and lost two fingers of his left hand to a fall of stone clearing ground the third year of his marriage, and mended, and went on herding with the hand he had left, cursing it only on the coldest mornings; and Ìde bore him three children in the years the crack was watched, two who lived and one who did not, and buried the one who did not on the hillside above the burn with Beathan standing by, silent, at the digging, the way he stood by at every digging in that country, for it was known among the households, though never said to his face, that a grave dug with the king's grandson standing over it was a grave a family need not fear would be disturbed.

In the ninth winter of their marriage a hard season drove wolves down off the high ground closer to the fires than any household there had known them come in living memory, and a pack of them was heard about the shoulder of the hill on three nights running while Gilleonan's turn stood at the slab, near enough that Ìde kept the youngest child inside her plaid the whole night through rather than let him sleep the arm's length away he usually slept. Beathan, whose own turn fell two nights after theirs that round, walked up a day early with a spear he did not often trouble to carry, and sat the intervening nights himself so that Gilleonan's household need not; and when Gilleonan protested that the turn was his to keep and not another's to take from him, Beathan told him only that a spear that had once turned back worse than wolves without being thrown could turn back wolves easily enough, and that Gilleonan's children had more need of a living father than the crack had of a second watcher that season. The wolves came no nearer than the sound of them, in the end, and drew off with the thaw as they had come with the frost; but it was the nearest the watch ever came, in all the years Gilleonan's household kept it, to guarding against a thing that could be named and fought, and the valleys told it after as the one winter the watch had proved good for something, whatever they made of the rest.

Once, and once only, in the fourteenth year, a light was seen along the crack that none of the six households on watch that season could afterward agree on the colour of: two said it was no more than the fire's own glow caught oddly in frost-mist, and two said it ran along the white stones themselves rather than above them, low and brief, gone before a third watcher roused from dozing could open his eyes on it, and the last two said only that they had seen nothing and doubted anyone else truly had. Somhairle, told of it at the next Bealltainn, asked each of the six alone and apart from the others, the way he asked when a thing wanted testing rather than repeating, and set down in no telling that has come to us any conclusion beyond the plain fact that six watchers had seen five different things, or four, or none, depending which pair was asked; and the valleys, who had little patience for a wonder that told four tales at once, let the matter drop inside a year and never raised it again in Ìde's hearing, nor, so far as the Line keeps, in Beathan's.

By the twentieth winter of the watch Ìde's hair had begun to go the iron grey her own mother's had gone at the same age, and by the thirtieth it was white, and her hands, that had once threaded a needle by firelight without troubling to hold it close, now held their needlework at arm's length and squinted at it all the same; and Beathan, sitting his turn beside hers as he had sat it every year since their first married winter, looked across the low fire at her some nights and found himself unable to say, watching her face against the slab's grey bulk, whether thirty years had passed since he first knew her or three, for his own face in the water of the burn told him nothing that thirty years ought to have told a man, and hers told him everything.

She never asked him the thing every other soul in the valleys learned young enough not to ask. She had her own guess, and kept it, the way her household kept most of its guessing, close and unspoken; but she told her grandchildren once, in a winter she was still strong enough to sit her own turn, that she had sat thirty years beside a man who had not aged a season in all of them, and that the strangest part of the whole business was how little strange it had come, after the first few winters, to feel.

It was in the twentieth winter of the watch that Somhairle gave Beathan Sloinneadh a' Chlachair whole, the same season his own hair, which had held black far past the age when any other man's would have turned, began at last to silver at the temple, the first sign the valleys had seen that even a son born after the grove would grey in the end like other men, though slower than any of them. He gave it to his son the way it had been given to him, by the fire, twice through without a name out of place, and told him afterward, in the only words the Line keeps of that giving, that a thing carried this long by mouth alone had better be carried by more than one head before the head that held it went into the ground; and Beathan, who had by then sat more winters at the slab than any man then living except his father, took the Line the way he had taken the watch, without asking why it should fall to him and not another, only that it had. He said nothing of the giving to Gilleonan or to any other household of the round, holding, as his father held, that the Line was a matter for Clann na Ceiste alone and the slab a matter for whoever sat by it, and that the two, though they lived in the same valleys, need not be carried by the same conversation.

## IV. Of the Question Gilleonan Asked

By then every household in the round had its own guess, held loosely and traded at the Samhain fire the way a household trades any tale it does not quite believe and cannot quite let go of. The basket-weavers from lower in the glen held that the crack led down to wherever the coal itself had first come to rest, and that the stone had been laid over it by hands older than the Line, to keep the coal from being carried off whole by whoever might otherwise have found it. The shepherd's widow held, more darkly, that something of the sìth's own company lay under it, sleeping rather than dead, and that the watch was kept lest it wake unminded; and she would say no more of it than that, however pressed, holding that a thing said too often aloud grew truer for the saying whether or not it had been true to begin with. The two brothers who split their turns held no view at all beyond the wolves and the cold, and said so bluntly whenever the others fell to guessing, which did nothing to stop the guessing but gave it, each time, someone to argue against. Somhairle, asked more than once to settle the matter with whatever the Line might know that the households did not, always answered the same way: that his father had given him the Line entire and had never once, in all the tellings, given him a reason for the slab, and that a son did not improve on what a father had chosen not to say.

It was Gilleonan himself, old by then in the ordinary way, his beard gone the same white as his wife's hair and his bad hand stiffer every winter than the last, who asked the thing at last, in the thirty-ninth year of the watch, on a night colder than most, with the fire burned low on purpose against the frost and the two of them alone at the slab while Ìde kept the house with a grandchild too young yet to sit a turn.

"I have sat here since I was a young man with two hands," Gilleonan said, holding up the maimed one to the firelight without complaint in it, only a kind of tiredness that came from further back than the cold, "and my father thought the crack worth minding before ever I sat by it, though he never said against what. You have sat it longer than any of us, Beathan, longer than my grandfather was alive to sit anything. Tell me plainly, for I am old enough now to be told plainly if there is a plain thing to tell: what is it we are watching for?"

Beathan was a long while answering, not from any wish to keep the thing back, for he had turned the same question over in his own mind more nights than Gilleonan had years to have asked it in, but because the honest answer, when it came, was smaller than a man who had given forty winters to a watch deserved.

"I do not know," he said at last. "My father does not know, though he has asked his own father, and been given no more than a look for the asking. I have lived in these valleys since before your grandfather's grandfather, Gilleonan, and I have watched the same crack you have watched, and it has never widened, and it has never closed, and nothing has ever come out of it nor gone down into it that any of us here have seen with our own eyes. I sit because the sitting was agreed, and it seemed to me, when it was agreed, no worse a way to spend the dark half of a year than any other. If there is a thing under that stone worth watching for, I have not been shown it, and I will not pretend to you now, after what your hand has cost this watch and what your wife's hair has cost it, that I know a reason I do not have."

Gilleonan took that the way he took most things, without complaint and without much surprise, for he had half expected, asking it, that the answer would be no bigger than the one he got. "Then we watch for nothing," he said, "and call it something, because a fire kept for nothing is harder to keep than a fire kept for a reason, even a false one."

"We watch," Beathan said, "because the ground moved once where the coal's own stain lies, and because a household that has watched forty years does not like to be the household that stopped the year before the thing it watched for finally came. That is not nothing, Gilleonan, whatever else it is."

They said no more of it that night, nor, so far as the Line has ever kept, any other night after; but it is told that Gilleonan sat his turns the four winters he had left in him with no less care than he had given the first, and that he taught the asking, if not the answer, to his grandchildren, so that the households of Na Glinn Àrda came in time to hold it as a settled thing among them that the watch was kept without knowing why, and that the not-knowing was no shame to anyone who had sat it.

## V. Of the King's Night at the Slab

The king came up into Na Glinn Àrda once in those years, in the eleven hundred and sixtieth winter, a year to the season after Gilleonan had asked his question of Beathan at the low fire, riding alone as he rode when he rode at all by then, and found the turn that season fallen to Beathan's own household and to a family new to the round, and sat down among them at the slab with no more ceremony than any traveller taking a place at a fire not his own. He said nothing of why he had come, and the households, who had long since learned not to press him for reasons he did not offer, asked him nothing. He sat the whole night through, wrapped in a plaid a herding wife had lent him without knowing whose shoulders it went round, and watched the same grey stone and the same thin crack the valleys had watched for forty winters, and when the light came up grey over the shoulder of the hill he rose, and thanked the household for the fire, and rode back down toward Dùn ìseal without having said, in all those hours, a single word touching the thing that had brought him.

It is the only night Sloinneadh a' Chlachair keeps of the king sitting a watch he had not been asked to sit, in a place that was not his own hill, among people who owed him no turn and had not looked for him. Whatever he had come to watch for, or to see, or only to sit beside a while before riding back to a hill full of quarrels no one else could settle, the households of Na Glinn Àrda never learned; and it is remembered of that night, when it is remembered at all, for the plain fact of it and no more, the way the valleys had by then learned to keep most things that came to them without an answer attached: that he came, and sat, and said nothing, and went away again, and the crack beneath the stone lay as it had always lain, neither wider nor narrower for the king's own night beside it.

Word of it reached Gilleonan's household within the day, the way word of anything touching the slab travelled fast in those valleys whatever else travelled slow, and Gilleonan, hearing it, said only that a man who did not know the answer to a question and a king who would not give one amounted, between them, to much the same silence, and that he was glad, all the same, to have asked his while there was still a Beathan to ask and not merely a slab to ask nothing of. He died three winters after, in the ordinary way at last, of no more than the years finally catching a body that had outrun most men's a long while, with Beathan sitting the last night of it beside his bed as he had sat so many nights beside his fire; and Ìde, who kept the house and the watch some years longer, told her grandchildren at every Samhain that the question her husband had asked was the one true thing anyone had ever got out of that stone in all the years it had been sat by. It had been asked plainly and answered plainly, she said, and a plain asking and a plain answer were more than most things in this world could claim between them. She sat her own last turn the winter her legs at last refused the climb, carried up by a grandson on a sledge and set down beside Beathan at the fire she had first sat beside as a bride glad of any excuse to linger from her own cold hearth; and the round went on after her without either of them, household following household as it had followed for fifty years and would follow for a hundred more, the crack lying under its white stones exactly as it had always lain, and the slab grey and silent above it, and no one, by the time this telling closes, any nearer an answer than Gilleonan had been the night he asked for one.
