# Of the Knapper's Son

*GE 1 – 2*

*At the flint-pit below Dùn ìseal, where the ground is still pocked with old workings, the spear-makers tell this among themselves, and have told it to their sons.*

## I. Of the Watch on the Lip of the Hill

The lad's father worked flint in a pit behind the houses, and had worked it there since before the lad was born, so that the boy grew up knowing the smell of struck stone the way another child knows the smell of bread. He was thirteen that summer, old enough to be set to watching and too young for much else, and it was his turn, with three others no older, to sit on the lip of the hill and watch the valley road for cattle-thieves and for worse.

The hill had no name then but the hill. It stood over the meeting of two streams, with byres and forty-some houses scattered along its shoulder and no bank round any of it, for the valleys had never needed one; the worst that came up that road in most summers was a hard bargain over grazing, or a drunk man from the next household with a grievance three years old. The lad sat with his knees drawn up and a spear across them that had been his father's, and had been his father's father's before that, and watched the road go down into the haze where the valley bent west.

He saw them a long way off: a string of figures on the track below the ford, twelve of them by his later count, walking without haste and without the shuffle of people driving beasts. No dust rose with them, for there had been rain three days before, and no baggage showed on any back that he could see. He watched a while longer than he should have, the way a boy watches something he cannot name a fear for, and then he ran.

He came down through the houses shouting that there were strangers on the road, twelve strangers walking, and by the time he reached the gate of his own house half the hill had heard him and were coming out with whatever lay nearest the door. His father came out of the pit still holding the antler tool he worked with, and looked at his son and then down the road, and said nothing, which the lad took worse than if he had shouted.

The holders of the hill gathered every spear they owned, which came to some sixty, old and new, well-made and poor, and stood in a crowd on the steepest part of the slope, where anyone climbing would have the ground against him. The lad stood among the men with his father's second-best spear, for the best had gone to his uncle, who had a longer reach. He was not told to stand there. No one told him to go home either, and by the time anyone thought of it the twelve had reached the foot of the hill, and it was too late to send a boy anywhere without being seen to do it.

## II. Of the Twelve at the Foot of the Slope

The twelve stopped where the ground began to rise, all but one of them, a man the lad's eye kept sliding past and back to, for he stood taller than the rest by a head and something more, though nothing else about him marked him out: no gold, no fine cloth, nothing in his hands. He came on alone, up the grass, at the pace of a man walking to his own door.

The lad said afterward, when he was old and had told the tale more times than he could count, that every man on that slope had meant to throw. He had meant to throw himself, and had thought about the throw the way his father had taught him, weight back, elbow up, and had been glad of the practice. The man came up within a long cast, and then well within an easy one, and the lad did not throw, and could not afterward say why not; only that somewhere between one breath and the next a warmth had come onto his face, as though a door had opened on a kiln a field away, and his arm, which had been ready, simply did not do what he had told it.

Nor was he alone in it. Beside him the eldest of the hill's holders, a man who had led them through two bad winters and a feud with the next valley and had never in the lad's knowing been slow to act, let the point of his spear down into the grass, and then let the shaft go after it, and sat down where he stood with his face in his hands, as a man sits down who has been struck somewhere no wound shows. One by one the rest did something of the kind: let their spears fall, or turned and went back down into the houses without a word to anyone, walking the way a man walks from a hearth he has just been told is not his any longer.

The tall man walked through the middle of them as a man walks through standing barley, touching nothing, looking at no one long enough to be looked back at, and went on up to the crown of the hill, and stood there turning slowly, taking in the valleys on every side as though he were counting them. After a while he called down the slope, in a voice that carried without being raised, for someone to bring him water.

No one moved. The lad found that his legs, which had failed him in one way a few minutes before, were willing enough now, and he went up the hill with the water-skin his mother kept filled by the door, because it was nearer to hand than thinking twice, and because a boy of thirteen does not weigh the same things a grown man weighs.

## III. Of the Water and the Spear

Up close the man was less strange and more so both together. His face was a young man's face, unweathered in a way that did not fit a life spent walking hill roads, and the lad, who had an eye for ages from watching his father's customers come and go, put him at four-and-twenty and never once, in all the years he knew him after, found cause to change the guess. His eyes, when they came round to the boy, were patient in a way the lad had no word for then and only found one for much later, when he was old himself and had watched enough patient men. He took the skin and drank, unhurried, and handed it back, and looked at the boy properly for the first time.

"What is your name?"

The lad gave it.

"Is that your father's spear?"

"His second-best," the lad said, before he had decided to say anything at all. "The best went to my uncle."

The man looked at the spear a moment, not long, the way a man who knows tools looks at one that is neither good nor bad. "Take it back to him," he said. "Tell him I shall want a great many more."

That was the whole of it. He turned from the boy and looked out again over the valleys, north and south and along the line of the streams, and the lad went back down the hill with the water-skin empty and the spear heavy in his hand in a way it had not been an hour before, though nothing about the spear had changed.

The houses talked that night as the lad had never heard them talk, in low voices round hearths where the doors were shut earlier than usual, and every household had its own account of the moment its spears had gone down, none quite agreeing with its neighbour's, and none of them, the lad noticed even at thirteen, willing to say plainly that they had been afraid. His father sat a long while turning the spear over in his hands after the boy gave him the king's word, saying nothing, and at last hung it back on its pegs over the door and told his son to bank the fire and go to bed, in the same voice he used on any ordinary night, though the lad lay awake past the last of the talk outside and heard his father still sitting by the hearth when he finally slept.

## IV. Of the Households Coming In

What came after was slower than the taking, and stranger in its own way for being slow. Through that first summer and into the next, the households of the valleys about the hill came in to the tall man one after another, a family at a time, like water finding its level with no one to drive it there. No word went out that the lad ever heard of; they simply came, walking up with whatever they thought a king should be given, which varied enormously, for none of them had ever given anything to a king before. Some brought hides. One old woman brought a pot of her own making and nothing else, and the man took it and thanked her by name, though no one had told him her name, and she went home and told everyone who would listen that he had known her before she spoke.

They told him where their grazing ran and asked him to hold the telling of it, for a valley that had quarrelled every spring past memory of the oldest man living had begun, within a season of his coming, to want someone outside every household to say where one man's grass ended and the next man's began. They brought their quarrels too, old ones some of them, older than the lad, disputes over a strip of ground or a dead man's cattle that had gone unsettled so long that both sides had half forgotten what had started them and kept them running out of habit more than grievance.

He heard the first of these on the open ground below the crown of the hill, where the grass was already beaten thin from the households' coming and going, and the lad was there among a crowd of forty or fifty who had come to watch, for there was nothing else that summer worth leaving off work for.

## V. Of the First Judgement

The two households in it held land on either side of a strip of pasture between two becks, and each swore the strip for its own, and each brought an old man to say so, and the old men disagreed with each other in exactly the words their fathers were said to have used, so that the lad, listening, had the strange feeling of hearing an argument that had never once changed in forty years.

The king, for so the hill had begun to call him among themselves though no one had heard him claim the word, let both sides finish. He asked one question only, of the elder of the two old men: which household's cattle grazed the strip the summer the boundary stone at the beck's mouth had last been moved. The old man answered without thinking, the way a man answers a question he has never once considered might matter, and in the answering gave away the case he had come to argue, for his own memory put the other household's cattle on the ground in that year, before the stone was moved.

"Then it is theirs," the king said, "and has been since before either of you were troubling your fathers about it. Move the stone back and be done."

That was the whole of the judgement, said as a man says which way the wind is, and it left both households standing where they were for a moment as though a wall they had leaned on all their lives had been taken away without warning. The one that lost the strip went home saying nothing worse than that the old fool had talked himself out of his own land, which was true and did not comfort him. The one that won it went home quieter than a household should go home from a victory, and the lad's father said, that night, that he had never in his life seen men so glad to be told what to do, nor so frightened of the man who had told them.

Other judgements followed through that year and the next, on smaller matters mostly, a stolen calf, a well two families both claimed to have dug, a marriage promised and not kept, and each was settled in the same handful of words, without appeal and, so far as the lad ever heard, without a single household going back afterward to argue the finding a second time. The valley people began to say among themselves, half in complaint and half in something nearer relief, that a man could no longer nurse a grudge on that hill the way his father had nursed his, for there was somewhere now to take it and be rid of it in an afternoon.

## VI. Of the Warmth with No Fire

It was in that first winter, when the snow lay longer on the hill than the oldest people could remember it lying, that the thing happened which the hearths of Dùn ìseal still tell before any other, though the lad himself did not see it and had it only at second hand, from a girl a year or two older than himself who swore to it all her life and was believed.

She had gone out past the houses one clear, bitter night to see to a beast that had strayed near the king's own fire, which he kept apart from the rest and let no one tend, and found herself standing nearer him than she had ever stood, closer than she had meant to come. She told it that she had expected to feel the cold worse there, in the open, away from any hearth of her own; instead she felt warm through, as a person feels warm sitting close by a good fire banked high, though there was no fire near her at all, only the king standing with his back half to her, looking at nothing she could see. Neither of them spoke a word. She stood there long enough to grow uneasy at her own staying, and went back to the beast and the byre, and was warm, she said, the whole walk back, though the night did not warm around her; only she did.

She told it once to her mother and was told to keep it to herself, and did, mostly, until she was old and telling it cost her nothing anyone could use against her. By then half the hill had a story like it, of standing near him on a cold night, or a hot one, and coming away changed in temperature without a fire to blame; and no one who told such a story could say why they had never thought, at the time, to ask him about it.

## VII. Of the Pit and the Word Kept

The word the king had sent back with the lad's spear was not forgotten on either side. Through that autumn the flint-pit behind the houses worked longer hours than it had in the lad's memory, his father and his uncle and two men taken on from a household that had come in late, cutting and knapping spearheads by the score where before they had made them a few at a time, as they were needed and no faster.

The lad was set to help before the leaves were fully down: fetching stone from the outcrop above the pit, sorting the good nodules from the flawed, learning under his father's hand the strike that split a piece clean and the strike that shattered it for nothing. He was not good at it that first year, and his father told him so without unkindness, the way a man tells a boy a true thing he expects him to outgrow. What the lad was good at, from the start, was watching: he had watched the taking of the hill from close enough to remember every part of it, and he watched now, at his father's elbow, the way a spearhead came out of a piece of stone the way a fish comes out of water, all at once and not at all by accident.

Word came up from the king's own steward, before the first snow, of how many heads the pit was to have ready by the turning of the year, and the number was larger than the lad's father had ever cut for one order, larger by a good deal than the whole hill had needed for its own use in a generation. His father took the order without complaint and without haste either, and set to teaching his son faster than he might otherwise have done, for there was more work now than two men and two hired hands could easily carry, and a third pair of hands that could be trusted, even a clumsy pair, was worth the teaching of it.

By the depth of that winter the lad could strike a passable head three times in five, which his father allowed was better than most boys managed in a year, and the lad understood, without being told in so many words, that the pit behind his father's house had become, in the space of one summer and one autumn, the place spears came from for a king who had said he would want a great many more, and had meant it.

## VIII. Of the Ring Pegged in the Snow

The bank was begun in that same hard winter, which surprised the hill more than the king's coming had, for no one builds earthworks in frost if it can be helped, and the ground on the hill's crown was iron by the turning of the year. Yet it was in the snow, not after it, that the king walked the crown of the hill with a bundle of pegs cut from ash and set them himself, one after another, pacing out a line that took in the old byres and left room, he said, for houses that were not built yet.

The households worked it in shifts through the worst of that winter, breaking frozen turf with mattocks that blunted faster than they could be sharpened, and the lad's father sent flint scrapers up to the diggers when the antler picks gave out, so that for a stretch of days the bank of Dùn ìseal was cut as much with worked stone as with anything else, an oddness the old people of the hill still mention when the bank is spoken of at all. The lad went up himself on the days his father could spare him, and stood in the line passing frozen clods hand to hand along the ditch, and remembered afterward mostly the cold in his fingers and the steam that stood over sixty or eighty people working close together in the freeze, so that from a little way off the whole crown of the hill seemed to smoke like a fire banked for the night.

The king worked among them on the days he was there, which were not all of them, for he was already riding out to valleys beyond the first ring of them and coming back only every so often; but on the days he set his own hand to the ditch the work went faster, not because anyone slacked when he was gone, the lad's father always said, but because no one wanted to be the one found idle when he came back unlooked-for. By the time the frost broke, the line of the ditch ran round the crown of the hill, a raw scar in the turf a spade deep, marking room, when the last house should be fitted into it, for some forty households. It was a scar and no more. The bank itself, raised to a man's height and closed at its gate, was eight more summers in the building, for the households dug when the grazing and the harvest let them and not otherwise; but the lad always said that the ring was made that winter, in the snow, and that the rest was only earth.

## IX. Of Those Who Were Brought Down

It was in the first days after the frost broke that a small company came down out of Na Glinn Àrda to the north, where no one on the hill had thought to look for anyone belonging to the king, for he had never spoken of a household of his own, and the hill had half decided, in the way a hill will decide things without anyone saying them aloud, that he had none. It learned afterward that he had gone north himself in the first autumn, on one of the ridings it had taken for the bringing-in of further valleys, and stayed a month in Na Glinn Àrda with his own, and come back alone to the digging.

There was a woman among them, somewhere in her middle years, with the bearing of someone used to being minded rather than minding, and a girl of some seven or eight years walking close beside her with a child's face set hard the way a child's face sets when it has learned to be careful; and there were others with them, kin and a few hands to carry what little they brought, for they had come a long way and light. The lad, who was at the gap in the new ditch that day mending a hurdle, watched them come up the same track the twelve had come up the summer before, though slower, and with none of the twelve's stillness; the woman looked about her at the new-cut ditch and the raw turf as a person looks at a place they have been told of and find smaller, or larger, or simply other than the telling.

Word went round the hill before the company had reached the gate that this was the king's own wife and his daughter, brought down at last from wherever he had left them before ever he walked south with eleven others and a name the grove had given him; and by evening it was known, the way such things become known on a hill where everyone's business is everyone's, that the woman was called Muireall, and the girl Gormshuil, and that there was a child not yet born who would be born on the hill itself before the year turned again, the first child of the king's blood, it was already being said, to draw breath inside the new ring.

The lad did not speak to any of them that day, nor for some while after, for a knapper's son and a king's wife had little enough cause to cross paths on a hill of forty houses. But he remembered, all his life, the particular quiet that fell over the crown of the hill as the small company passed through the gap left for a gate and the households stood back to let them by, the same quiet, though no one on the hill ever said so aloud, that had fallen on the slope the summer before when sixty spears had come down together without a hand raising one of them; as though the hill had learned, in less than a year, to know a thing worth being quiet for when it saw one coming up the road.

The child came before the summer was out, a son, born inside the new ring as it had been said he would be, and the hill took it for a settled thing after that, the way a household takes a roof for settled once the last thatch is laid: that the king had come to stay among them, and had brought what a man brings when he means to stay, a wife, a daughter, and now a son born on the ground he had taken. The lad's father cut a spearhead that week finer than any he had cut that year, for no order and no payment, and gave it to a neighbour to carry up to the king's fire, and would not say afterward why, only that it had seemed to him a thing that wanted doing. Whether it ever reached the king's hand, or what he made of it if it did, the lad never learned; but he kept, until he was an old man teaching the strike to his own grandsons, the plain conviction that some things are given because the giving is owed, and not because anyone is waiting to be thanked for them.
