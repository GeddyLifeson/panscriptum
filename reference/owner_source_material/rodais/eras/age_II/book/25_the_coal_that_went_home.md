# Of the Coal that Went Home

*GE 2,409 – 2,416*

> *Chaidh an gual dhachaigh.*
> The coal went home.
>
> — the households near the slab, of the coal Gilleasbuig took, as the hearths of Tobar dhìreach still say it

## I. Of Gilleasbuig's Turn

Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill had kept the slab six years and more when her knees first failed her on the path up from the spring, and though she said nothing of it that winter, the household that sat with her saw her rise from the cold ground by the stone stiffer each month, and leaning harder on the arm offered her, until it was plain to anyone who watched her walk what a night on wet ground was costing her. She did not wait to be told. In the last mild days of a year the hill afterward had no cause to remember for anything but this, she stood up before the families at the clearing and said, in the flat voice she kept for matters that could not be argued with, that her legs had done with the slab even if the rest of her had not, and that the lot should be drawn again while she could still stand to watch it drawn.

No one argued with her. It was the custom's own rule, said aloud at its very making, that the keeper held the stone until death or infirmity called for another lot; and infirmity had called, plain as a broken bone, in a woman who could no longer kneel at the coal's edge without help to rise again. She kept her cup. She kept her place among the families near the crack, and was asked her word on most things touching the slab for the rest of her days, for six years of keeping had made her the nearest thing the custom had to a living memory. But the bag of pebbles was filled again, one marked among the rest, and passed hand to hand among the households who stood the watch, and it was a man named Gilleasbuig Beag, of a family that had come to the near valley only in his father's time, whose hand came out holding the white stone.

Ciorstaidh Bhàn stood in that ring with the rest of her household the day the lot fell to him, a young woman not yet thirty with a husband two years married and no child yet at her skirts, and she remembered after, though it meant nothing to her at the time, that Gilleasbuig had gone pale rather than glad when he opened his hand and saw what was in it, the way some men do at good news as readily as bad, and that his wife beside him had laughed once, sharply, and then put her hand over her own mouth as if the laugh had got out before she meant it to. He was a broad, quiet man who cut good hazel withies and mended a roof as well as any three men on the near slopes, and no one standing in the clearing that day would have named him first, or last, among those it might fall to. That was the whole of the custom's mercy and its whole danger both: that the stone did not choose by what a household already knew of a man.

He kept the watch as any keeper kept it, through that first year and into the second, sitting the cold nights at the slab with whichever of his own kin cared to sit beside him, walking the clearing at first light to see that nothing lay disturbed, carrying water up from the spring for whoever came to fill a cup against Oighrig's. Ciorstaidh's own household lived near enough the clearing that she saw him often in those years, going to and from his own door with an axe or a creel on his back like any other man of the hill, and she thought no more of him than she thought of any neighbour whose turn had fallen on him rather than her.

It was in the second winter of his keeping, a hard one on the near slopes with the grazing thin and the storm-season sharing stretched further than any household liked to see it stretched, that a woman of Gilleasbuig's own kin, going into his house on some other errand while he was from home, found a basket set back under the bed-place with a cloth over it, and lifted the cloth thinking it food, and found instead a basket of the coloured coal, broken small, more than any hand could have gathered by accident and more, by a long way, than the handful any hearth was allowed. She did not touch it. She put the cloth back as she had found it and left the house and told no one that day, being kin to him and slow to believe her own eyes; but she told her husband before she slept, and by the third day it was known past any hope of its being unknown, the way such things always were on the near slopes, where one door stood close enough to the next that a secret kept in one house was a secret only until the wind carried a word of it as far as the well.

## II. Of the Judgement at the Slab

The families sent for Gilleasbuig to come to the clearing on a morning when the frost still lay white in the shadow of the stone and had not yet burned off the open ground where the sun reached it, and he came, and Ciorstaidh, standing near the back of the gathering with her husband's arm through hers against the cold, thought afterward that she had never seen a man walk so far to reach so short a distance. He did not deny it when it was put to him. He stood with his hands loose at his sides, not folded, not fisted, and said only that the basket was his and the coal in it was his, taken by his own hand from the crack he was set to watch, and that he had meant, when he took it, to use it against a winter he could see coming a long way off, for a mother of his who was old and slept cold, and for nothing else that he would name.

No one at the clearing that morning doubted that this much was true. It was the *use* of the coal that shook them, not the greed of it, for there was no greed to be seen in a man who had taken it to warm an old woman's bones and not to sell it on any road; and it was for that reason, more than any other, that the judgement took as long as it did, the families standing in the cold arguing among themselves in low voices while Gilleasbuig waited in the middle of them and said nothing further, as if the matter had already left his hands the moment the cloth came off the basket.

It was an old woman of Cnoc chaol, who had walked the better part of a day to be at the judgement because her own household held one of the four cups and felt the matter touched her as nearly as it touched the slab's own valley, who said at last what none of the near families had quite been willing to say first: that the coal was not Gilleasbuig's to spend on any love, however true, because it had never once, since the crack was first watched, been any single household's to keep behind a cloth under a bed. The custom had never asked a keeper to love the vein less than his own kin. It had only ever asked him to hold nothing of it back from every hearth that had a claim on the handful, his own included, and take no more for one house's fire than the vein gave to any other's.

They sat him at last under judgement the way the hill had always judged such things when a household was put out for hoarding grain against the storm-sharing: by the silence that follows, once every voice in the clearing has said its piece and no voice rises after to say different. The silence held long enough that morning, with the frost gone from the open ground and the sun well up, that there was no mistaking it for anything but agreement. Gilleasbuig was told he would be rowed out to Eilean chrom, the low island that lay off the near coast with nothing on it a household could live from, and left there without a boat to bring himself back in; and the coal, the same voice went on, once the old woman of Cnoc chaol had named the island, would go neither to any fire nor back into any basket a living hand might carry home, but would be carried instead to the slab's own edge and given back to the ground it had come from.

Gilleasbuig said one thing more before they took him down to the shore, and it was the only thing Ciorstaidh, standing near enough by then to hear it plainly, ever heard him say with any heat in his voice at all: that his mother would sleep cold that winter whichever way the judgement fell, whether the coal burned in her hearth or lay under a stone she would never see, and that he did not understand what the stone gained by having it back that the old woman did not lose by going without it. No one answered him. It was not, Ciorstaidh thought afterward, a question the clearing had ever troubled to answer, and perhaps for that reason no one there was willing to invent one on the spot, with the man standing before them waiting to hear it.

## III. Of Eilean chrom

They rowed him out in the afternoon of that same day, three men of the near households taking one boat between them with Gilleasbuig sitting in the stern and saying nothing more the whole crossing, by the telling of the men who were there and told it after. Eilean chrom lay low and treeless a short pull off the coast, grazed once by nobody's sheep and fished by nobody's nets even before that day, a spit of grass and grey rock with a spring at its middle that never failed and nothing else on it a person could live from beyond what the tide gave up. They put him ashore on its lee side, where the landing was easiest, and pulled the boat back out into open water while he stood on the shingle and watched them go, and none of the three who told it afterward could agree on whether he had raised a hand to them or not, though all three agreed he had not called out.

Ciorstaidh did not see the crossing herself. She heard it told twice over in the days after, once by one of the three oarsmen at his own door and once by his wife, who repeated it word for careful word as if the repeating were its own kind of keeping. But she saw the fire.

It stood on Eilean chrom's low rise that first night, small and steady against the dark water, and half the near coast came out to its own doors to look at it, some out of pity and some out of nothing kinder than the wanting to see a thing everyone else was seeing. It burned again the second night, smaller, Ciorstaidh thought, though she could not have sworn to it across that distance and in that light. On the third night it was there again at dusk, no more than a coal's worth of red low down near the shingle, gone before the deepest part of the dark, and after that no one on the near coast, watching night after night through a week and then a month of watching, ever saw a fire on Eilean chrom again.

No boat went out to it. The judgement had left him there without one, and no household, whatever it felt in its own heart about the man or the coal, was willing to be the one to row out and learn for certain what three nights of fire and no fire after meant. Eilean chrom stood empty from that season on, ungrazed and unfished, the way it had stood before Gilleasbuig was put on it and the way, so far as Ciorstaidh Bhàn ever lived to see, it went on standing after. Children on the near coast were told, in time, not to ask about the island with the spring on it, and most of them grew up asking anyway, the way children always did about the one thing they were told not to, and were given the same short answer each time: that a man had kept the watch there once, and did not keep it well, and that no one had gone near the island since, and no one would.

## IV. Of the Second Lot and the Basket Unburned

The basket itself came to the slab's own household before the month of the fires was out, carried by the same kin who had first lifted the cloth from it, set down just inside the door of the keepers' house the way a thing is set down that no one wants to be the one to have carried the whole way in and put where it belonged. It sat there through the rest of that season under its own cloth, and every household near the crack knew it was there, and not one of them, in all the low talk that ran up and down the near valley in those weeks, ever proposed that it should be burned. It had not been theirs to burn before Gilleasbuig took it, and his taking it had not made it burnable; if anything the judgement had settled, past any further arguing, that it never would be.

The lot for a new keeper was drawn within the same season, at the clearing, in the same bag of pebbles that had given the stone to Oighrig and then to Gilleasbuig, and this time it was Ciorstaidh Bhàn's hand that came out of the bag holding the white stone. She had stood at enough of these drawings by then, her own household near enough the crack that she was seldom far from one, to know what was expected of whoever the lot found; what she had not known, until she felt the pebble cold against her palm and understood from the faces around her before she had properly opened her own hand what it was, was how little warning the stone gave before it changed a life. Her husband put his arm round her the way Gilleasbuig's wife had once nearly laughed; Ciorstaidh did neither. She looked at the stone a while, the way Oighrig was said to have looked at hers years before, and then closed her hand on it and said she would keep it.

She moved into the keepers' house at Tobar dhìreach before the month was out, her husband and, before long, a first child with her, and took up the night-watches and the water-carrying and the weighing of cups against her own the way every keeper before her had taken them up. But the basket was hers now too, set inside the door under its cloth, and she found in the first weeks of her keeping that she could not walk past it without her eyes going to it, the way a sore tooth draws the tongue whether the tongue means to go there or not.

It was on a still evening near the year's turn, with the frost already down and the last of the light going grey over the clearing, that she lifted the cloth herself and looked at what was under it: broken coal, small pieces, no different to the eye from any handful any hearth on the near slopes was owed by right, and yet made wrong, entirely and past mending, by nothing more than whose hand had gathered it and how. She thought of Gilleasbuig's mother sleeping cold that winter regardless, as he had said she would; she thought of the fire on Eilean chrom, three nights and then none; and she understood, kneeling there with the basket open in front of her in the last of the light, that there was only the one place left on the whole island where that coal could go that would not make a liar of the judgement that had sent a man to an island without a boat over it.

## V. Of the Coal that Went Home

She carried the basket to the slab's own edge on the first clear morning after, kneeling on the same cold ground where she had knelt through half a year of night-watches by then, and set it down beside her, and began to lay the coal back under the stone's lower lip, piece by piece, pressing each fragment into the dark gap where the slab met the earth until her fingers found the give of the loosened soil and the piece would sit and hold. It was slower work than she had looked for. The gap ran narrow in places and wide in others, and more than once a piece she thought settled worked itself loose again when she reached for the next, so that she had to go back and press it in a second time, and a third, before it would keep. She worked through most of that morning and came back to finish it the next, the basket half empty and then near empty and then, on the second day, holding nothing at all.

No one had told her to do it that way, piece by piece with her own two hands rather than tipped out all at once the way a lazier keeper might have emptied it and called the matter closed. She did it slowly because it seemed to her, kneeling there with the cold coming up through the ground into her knees the way it must have come up through Oighrig's, that a thing taken wrongly, however small each piece of it, ought at least to be given back with the same care it had been taken with, if not more; and because, though she never said this to anyone in so many words, she wanted, when it was done, to be able to say truly that her own hand had put every last piece of it back where it belonged, and not merely that the basket had been emptied.

It is from that unhurried morning, and the second morning after it, that the hearths near the slab keep the four words that are still said of the whole matter, short enough that a child can carry them whole after hearing them once: that the coal went home. Ciorstaidh Bhàn never claimed to have made the saying herself, and by her own telling, given late in her life to whoever asked her about those years, she could not afterward say for certain who had said it first, in her hearing or out of it; only that by the following summer it was already going round the near valley as if it had always been there, the way the truest sayings on the hill always seemed to arrive already old.

## VI. Of Calum Ciar and the Lot at Dùn ìseal

Word of the judgement, and of what the new keeper had done with the basket after, travelled the salt road and the flint road both before that year was out, and reached Dùn ìseal in the mouth of some traveller or other long before any of the households near the slab thought to send it there on purpose, for the crack had never asked the hill of the Stone Kings for leave to judge its own. It was two summers later, by Ciorstaidh's own reckoning, that Calum Ciar himself came up the valley to the clearing, the elder son of Eilidh Ghlas and eleventh to hold Dùn ìseal under the king, a broad grey-eyed man well past his hundred and fiftieth year who carried himself, even walking alone and unarmed among households that owed him no more than any household owed the hill, as a man used to being listened to.

He did not come, he said, standing where Gilleasbuig had once stood to hear his own judgement, to question what had been done to Gilleasbuig or where the coal had gone after; both, he allowed, had been done as the custom asked, and he had no quarrel with either. What he asked was smaller, and larger, both at once: that the next time the bag of pebbles was filled, it should be filled and drawn at Dùn ìseal, in his own sight, so that the hill that held the grazing and called the gatherings and answered to the king for both might see with its own eyes that the lot which decided who kept the vein was drawn as honestly as the lot which had once, in Oighrig's year, decided the same thing at the clearing.

Ciorstaidh stood near enough that day to see his face while the families gave him their answer, and she thought, though she never said so where it might travel back to Dùn ìseal, that he had not looked for the answer he got. The households near the crack held their silence the way they had held it over Gilleasbuig, long enough and level enough that there was no mistaking it, and it was the same old woman of Cnoc chaol, grown older still, who said at last what the silence had already said: that the lot had been drawn at the slab since the custom first began, and would go on being drawn there, in the sight of whoever near the crack cared to stand and watch it, and that Dùn ìseal's sight, however honestly meant, was not a thing the vein had ever asked for or needed.

Calum Ciar heard it out and did not argue it there in the clearing, which Ciorstaidh set down afterward, when she spoke of that day at all, as the one thing in his favour that she never forgot. He thanked the families for hearing him and went back down the valley the way he had come, and it was only later, from travellers again rather than from any word Dùn ìseal troubled to send the slab, that she learned he had carried the same question on to the king himself, at the ford that same summer where the two hills met each year over salt and wool.

## VII. Of the Unwritten Line

What passed between Calum Ciar and the king at the ford, Ciorstaidh Bhàn never heard first-hand, and neither, so far as she ever learned, did anyone near the slab; it came to the near valley the way most things from the ford came to it, worn a little smoother with every mouth it passed through on the way. But the words that reached her, and that she went on repeating to whoever asked her of it in the years after, in nearly one shape from the first telling to the last, were these: that the king had heard Calum Ciar out as the families at the clearing had heard him out, without anger and without haste, and had said at the end of it only that the slab was older than the hill, and older than the grove where he himself had first been crowned, and that he would not overrule a thing that old for the asking of a man not two hundred years in the world.

Calum Ciar went home from the ford, the telling said, and asked nothing more of it. The lot went on being drawn at the slab, as it had been drawn for Oighrig and for Gilleasbuig and for Ciorstaidh herself, and no household near the crack ever again heard Dùn ìseal ask to stand over the bag of pebbles while it was filled. The hill ruled the grazing in those years, and called the gatherings, and answered for both to the king; the keepers ruled the vein, and answered for it to no one the telling could name, not even, when it came to it, to the king himself. It was a line drawn nowhere on any stone and sworn to in no words either side troubled to write down, and it held, so far as Ciorstaidh Bhàn lived to see it hold, for every year of her keeping after; and when she was old, and her own knees had begun to fail her on the path up from the spring the way Oighrig's once had, she told whoever sat with her at the last of her watches that she had never once, in all her years at the slab, seen a holder of Dùn ìseal come up the valley to ask it a second time.
