# Of the Stones in the Valley and the Chair of Birch

*GE 107 – 227*

*At Dùn ìseal the boundary stones still stand in the grass below the bank, and the holders of the valley tell this against their own name, for it is a telling that gives them no credit.*

## I. Of the Setting of the Stones

Dòmhnall Clachach had held the hill a little over thirty years when he first thought of the stones, and he thought of them the way a man thinks of a thing he has put off too long, for the idea was old with him already, and it was the quarrels that brought it back each spring, wearing at last through his patience, that gave it a shape worth acting on. He was a son born after the grove, and though he did not yet look it, he had crossed a hundred years, and something in a hundred years of hearing the same two households swear the same strip of grazing to different fences had taught him that a king's word settled a dispute for exactly as long as it took the disputants to forget who had lost.

He came to it standing over Gormshuil's grave one morning in early summer, which he did more often than he told anyone, for she had held the hill before him and he had loved her as a man loves an elder sister who dies old while he stays young, a grief that has no clean place to sit. Two households of the eastern slope were at their fences again that week, the third time in four years, over a strip of poor grass between two becks that neither side wanted badly enough to fight for and neither would give up for pride. He judged it as his father judged such things, in a sentence, and watched both households go home no gladder than they had come; and it came to him, walking back up to the bank in the last of the light, that his father settled quarrels one at a time because there was always a father to settle the next one, but that a valley needed something that did not depend on a man being there to ask.

He set the stones himself, that summer and into the one after, walking the boundary of every household's grazing with two men to carry and a third to dig, and setting into the ground, at every place the old men of both sides could agree the line had always run, a stone too heavy for one man to shift without help and too plain to be mistaken for anything but what it was. The stones he chose with care, flat-topped things from the outcrop above the flint-pit, and had each one cut with a single mark, not a name, for he did not want the stones to belong to anyone; he wanted them to belong to the ground. It was slow work, for every stone needed both sides' word before it went into the earth, and there were places where the old men could not agree even standing on the very ground, pointing at tussocks that had shifted a hand's width in forty years of frost and thaw and swearing each tussock had been exactly there since before their fathers were born.

By the end of the second summer there was a line of grey stone down the whole of the eastern slope and along the beck to the ford, and Dòmhnall stood at the last of them with turf still fresh at its foot and believed, for perhaps a season, that he had done something that would hold.

## II. Of What the Stones Did Not Settle

What the stones did was move the argument, not end it. Within a year the eastern households had stopped quarrelling over where the grazing lay, for that was written now in stone that did not forget and could not be argued with by an old man's memory; but they had begun instead to quarrel over the stones themselves, whether a stone stood a pace to the wrong side of where it should, whether a beck had moved its bed since the setting and left a stone stranded on the wrong bank of its own line, whether a man's grandfather had truly agreed to a boundary his grandson now swore had been forced on a bad year when the household was too weak to argue.

Dòmhnall judged the first of these himself, in the ninth year of the stones, standing over a marker two households both claimed had been moved in the night, though neither could say by whom, and both swore the beck ran different from where it had run at the setting. He walked the ground a long while before he spoke, and when he did it was to say a thing he had not planned to say: that the stone stood where it had been set, that he remembered setting it himself with his own hands and could point to the exact tussock it had gone down beside, and that the beck, not the stone, had moved.

It was true, and both households knew it was true the moment he said it, for a man with the long years does not forget where he put a stone the way an ordinary man forgets, and there was no arguing memory against a man who did not age between the setting and the asking. But he saw, walking home from that judgement, that he had settled the quarrel by being the sort of witness no one else on the hill could ever be, and that when he was gone north, as his father sometimes was, or gone into the ground, as his father was not, the eastern slope would have only the stones themselves to argue with, and stones, unlike a king, cannot be asked which of two stories is true.

He said as much to no one, then. It sat in him the whole of that year, the plain fact that he had traded one kind of quarrel for another and called it a settling because the second kind was quieter and took longer to come round. He kept the stones. He never told the hill that he doubted them.

## III. Of the Wars of the Valleys

His father was, in those years, seldom on the hill at all. The kingdom of the godkin that had begun as forty houses inside a single bank had by Dòmhnall's thirtieth year on the hill grown to take in valleys three and four days' walk from Dùn ìseal, brought in, for the most part, by the same warmth that had stilled sixty spears on the first slope; yet not every valley came in warm. There were households in the folds of land north and west that had heard what had happened at Dùn ìseal and did not believe it, or believed it and resented it the more for believing, and among those the king rode with few, for he never took a great host anywhere, and what fighting there was, the reciters afterward called Cogaidhean nan Gleann, the wars of the valleys, though it was nothing any teller of true wars would recognise: a burnt byre here, a dozen spears crossed there, three men dead in one valley and none in the next, and everywhere, in the end, the same silence that had fallen on the first hill, sooner or later, in every household the king came to himself.

Dòmhnall did not ride with him in those years, for the hill needed holding and someone of the blood needed to hold it, and it fell to him as it had fallen to Gormshuil before him. He heard the wars of the valleys the way the hill heard them, in the words of traders and the sons of households that had come in, garbled a little in the telling, so that the same skirmish might reach Dùn ìseal in three different shapes within a season, each one sworn true by the man who brought it. He learned to listen for what stayed the same under the different tellings, which was always the small thing and never the large: a name, a place, the colour of a wound, and from those he built for himself a truer picture than any single teller gave him, though he never told anyone he did it, being a man who kept his methods to himself the way his father kept his fires.

It was in the eighty-second year of the stones, when Dòmhnall himself had begun to notice that the eastern slope's grandsons called him uncle without knowing quite why, that word came down from the north-west of trouble in a valley whose name he had heard only twice before, where a household under a hard old man called Griogar had turned back two of the king's riders with spears and no words at all. The king went himself with a dozen men, no more, for he never took more than a dozen anywhere he rode himself; and it was there, in a fight Dòmhnall never saw and heard of only after, in the hundred and thirteenth year of his own holding of the hill, that his younger brother Fearchar took a spear through the leg that never healed straight.

## IV. Of Fearchar Lamed

Dòmhnall was not at Griogar's valley, and no telling that reached him afterward agreed on whether the king himself had been within reach of the blow that laid Fearchar down, though most said he had not, that he had been elsewhere in the fight when it happened and came to his son only after, and knelt by him a while on the trampled ground, and said nothing that anyone standing near enough to hear could afterward report. What is certain, and what Dòmhnall heard from Fearchar's own mouth years later, sitting by him at Dùn ìseal with the birch chair already built and Fearchar's leg already a thing he no longer troubled to hide the shape of, was that the wound should by any ordinary reckoning have killed him, for it took him in the thigh and bled a long while before anyone could stop it, and that it did not kill him, and did not heal straight either, so that he walked the whole long remainder of his years, however many they proved to be, on a leg that bent wrong at every step.

"I asked him once," Fearchar told Dòmhnall, the two of them sitting outside the bank in the last of an autumn evening with the fires being lit below them one by one, "why he let it set crooked, when he can do what he did for you and me both, the long years and all the rest of it, and my leg wanted only a little of the same mending to be as good as it ever was."

"What did he say?"

"Nothing. You know how he answers a question he does not mean to answer." Fearchar shifted his weight off the bad leg, the way Dòmhnall would watch him do ten thousand times more before either of them was done with the world. "I have thought since that he cannot mend a thing that is already done, only keep a thing from being undone. Whether that is true I could not swear to you. He never offered, and I never asked him twice."

Dòmhnall said nothing to that either, for he had wondered the same of his own long years, whether they were a mending or only a keeping, and found no more of an answer in himself than his brother had found in their father.

## V. Of the Storm

The first named storm came in Dòmhnall's seventy-first year on the hill, in a season that had given no more warning than any other autumn, and it broke on Dùn ìseal in the dark of one night with a wind that took the thatch off six houses and laid a seventh flat to its footings, and did not slacken until the second day was well begun. Dòmhnall spent that night going from house to house within the bank with what men he could gather, holding roofs down with ropes and bodies both, and lost no one, which he counted afterward the one mercy the storm allowed him, though he lost, besides the houses, most of the summer's cut hay and two of the flint-pit's best sheds.

The name the hill gave the storm that night, in the fright of it, was lost within a generation, the way such names are, for a storm remembered by its fear rather than by what it did rarely keeps the word fear gave it; but the memory of the naming outlived the name, so that the oldest tellers of the hill still say, when a bad wind comes up, that it is nearly as bad as the one they named and forgot, without any of them able to say what they had called it.

It was traders coming up from the west that winter, driven off their usual road by flooded fords, who brought word that the storm had done worse on the coast than it had done at Dùn ìseal: that the shell causeway the Seann-Dhaoine had built long before any of the hill-folk's grandfathers were born, out at Seann Warr, was broken through in three places and the marsh behind it standing full of lake water that should have been kept out of it, and that the Seann-Dhaoine themselves, whose causeway it was and whose land it flooded, had not come out to mend it. Dòmhnall asked the trader twice whether he had that right, for it seemed to him a strange thing for any people to let stand broken, a causeway being the kind of work a people mends before the frost sets a second time; and the trader, who had walked the broken ends himself and touched the old stone with his own hand, said only that the Seann-Dhaoine had watched him do it from a little way off and said nothing and done nothing, and that he had not liked to ask them why.

Dòmhnall thought about that longer than the news seemed to deserve. He had grown up on tellings of the Seann-Dhaoine as a people who built well and kept what they built, the shell roads and the stone landings older and truer than anything the hill-folk had yet raised for themselves; and a broken thing of theirs left broken sat oddly with him; but he was sixty miles and more from Seann Warr with a hill of his own needing him, and he let it go, as a man lets go a strange thing that is not his to mend.

## VI. Of the Household That Stood

There was a household on the western slope, some three years after the storm, that Dòmhnall came, in his own mind, to think of afterward as the first true test of the stones, for it was the first quarrel in his time that was not, at bottom, about grass or a fence-line at all. The household was old on that ground, older than the bank, and its elder, a broad grey man named Labhrainn who had never in his life been asked to give an account of himself to anyone, took it into his head that year that a boundary stone set on his own western fence marked less grazing than his household had always held, and refused outright to stand by the line Dòmhnall had walked forty years before.

It was not, Dòmhnall came to understand over the course of that summer, truly about the ground at all, though Labhrainn argued the ground with more heat each time they spoke of it. It was that Labhrainn had watched, over the forty years since the stones went in, the hill's judgements settle themselves more and more by the stone and less and less by the elder men of each household standing on the ground and arguing their memory against each other, the way it had always been done before Dòmhnall's father took to writing boundaries in rock; and he had come, slowly and without ever quite saying it to himself, to resent a settling that no longer needed his own word to stand.

Dòmhnall judged against him in the end, for the stone was where the stone had always been and Labhrainn's own father had stood by its setting with his own hand on the digging-bar; but he judged it gently, and afterward, rather than let the matter close the way most of his judgements closed, with both sides going home and neither speaking of it again, he rode out himself to Labhrainn's byre the following spring and sat with the old man a whole evening over nothing more than a jug of the household's own brewing, and asked him about the ground itself and what it had carried in his grandfather's time and what it carried now, and let him talk himself through most of an evening about a thing no one had asked him to account for in years.

It did not mend everything between them. Labhrainn never again spoke warmly of the boundary stones, and told his own sons, dying, that a king who needed rock to remember a man's word was a king who did not trust the man; but he never again brought a quarrel against the line itself, and Dòmhnall understood, turning it over on the ride home, that he had not won the old man over so much as given him back, in an evening's listening, a little of what the stone had quietly taken from him.

## VII. Of Somhairle Who Took No Share

It was in that same stretch of years that word came up to Dòmhnall of his youngest half-brother, Somhairle, son of Sorcha, who had by then seen some hundred and sixty years and looked, by every account that reached the hill, no more than a boy of fifteen summers, slight and quiet and given to standing at the edge of any gathering rather than in the middle of it. Somhairle had asked their father, so the telling went, not for a share of anything, neither land nor herds nor a place among the holders, but only leave to go and live among the households of the high valleys, Na Glinn Àrda, where Clann na Ceiste kept their herds about the crack in the ground that none of them could name the naming of, and to be counted there as nothing more than one more mouth at one more hearth.

Their father had granted it without, so far as anyone heard, a single word of argument, which struck Dòmhnall, when he heard of it, as stranger than the asking itself, for their father argued little about anything but rarely gave leave so quickly to a son who wanted nothing in return for the giving. Dòmhnall rode up himself, the following autumn, to see his brother settled among the high valleys, and found him living in a hearth no larger than any other, tending goats with a household that treated him, as far as Dòmhnall could tell in three days among them, as an odd, quiet, useful boy and nothing more, none of them seeming to know, or to care to know, whose son he was.

"You could have had the hill from me one day," Dòmhnall said to him, the evening before he rode back south, the two of them sitting outside the byre with the smoke of the household's fire between them and the crack in the ground somewhere off in the dark, silent as it had been silent as long as either of them had heard tell of it. "Or land of your own, wherever you asked for it. Why this?"

Somhairle was quiet long enough that Dòmhnall thought he would not answer, which was a thing, he was coming to notice, that ran in their father's blood more than any of the rest of it. "Someone has to remember," Somhairle said at last, "who everyone is, and whose son follows whose, once there are enough of us that no one hearth can hold the whole of it in one old man's head. I do not want a share, because a man who holds land judges his own kin a little differently than a man who holds nothing but names. I want only to be believed, when I say who someone's grandfather was."

Dòmhnall thought of the stones then, though he did not say so, and of Labhrainn, and of the plain fact that he himself had traded one kind of memory for another and never been sure, since, that he had traded up. He said only that he hoped his brother would be believed, and rode home the next morning turning the thought over the whole way, that his father's line seemed to be filling itself, generation by scant generation, with sons who wanted no share of anything, as though the getting of land had already, this early, begun to seem to some of them a smaller thing than the keeping of a true account.

## VIII. Of the Chair of Birch and the Riding North

Fearchar's leg never straightened, and by the thirtieth year after Griogar's valley he had stopped pretending it might, and the household carpenters of Dùn ìseal built him, without being asked, a chair of woven birch with long poles fore and aft so that four men could carry him to the gatherings he would no longer walk to, and he took to it without complaint and, Dòmhnall thought, with something almost like relief, for a man who has hidden a limp for thirty years finds a certain ease in being carried openly instead.

It was at one of those gatherings, in the hundred and fifty-first year of Dòmhnall's holding, with Fearchar set down in his chair at the edge of the crowd and the fires of the bank burning behind them, that Dòmhnall went to his father and asked him for more than the hill.

He had thought long about how to put it, and put it plainly in the end, the way his father answered best. He said that the hill was settled now as settled as stone and habit could make it, that its quarrels had grown smaller than the quarrels of a valley twelve days north where the king's word had never yet reached in any lasting way, and that he, Dòmhnall, had held one hill for a hundred and fifty years and wanted, before his own years grew heavier than they yet felt, to hold ground that was not yet settled at all.

His father heard him out without moving, in the way that had stilled sixty spears on a slope more than two hundred years before, and when Dòmhnall had finished he was quiet long enough that Dòmhnall began to wonder whether he had asked wrongly, or asked for a thing that was not his to ask for.

"The north is not held," his father said at last. "Griogar's valley and the ones like it took a spear of your brother's and gave me nothing back but a name I do not trust yet. Go and hold what has been won there, and what has not been won yet, and do it your own way; I will not tell you the stones you should set."

It was, Dòmhnall understood even as he heard it, both more and less than he had asked for: more ground, and no guidance at all in how to keep it, which was, he came slowly to see over the years that followed, the only kind of gift his father ever gave any of them. He left Dùn ìseal that same season, with a company no larger than the twelve his father had once walked south with, and Fearchar, from the chair of birch, watched him go with the flat, unsurprised look of a man who has already begun to understand that he himself will hold this hill a long while yet, on a leg that never healed and a patience that, unlike the leg, would have to.

The hill took Fearchar for its holder without a word of dispute, for he had sat at its judgements at his brother's side since his wounding and the households knew his voice as well as they had known Dòmhnall's; and the boundary stones of the eastern slope, which Dòmhnall had set with his own hands in the thirty-first and thirty-second years of his holding, stood on into a rule that was not his, arguing, in their own plain way, long after he had ridden north, the case he had made for them before he was sure himself that he believed it.
