# Of the Flint Road

*GE 1,747 – 1,892*

*At Cnoc àrsaidh the flint-pit keepers still let the first flake of a new working go free to whoever asks for it, and say only that it was a promise once made to a king's son.*

## I. Of the Road He Chose

For sixteen years after his mother was put into the ground at Dùn ìseal, Murchadh sat in the hall his father kept and watched the hearth-smoke go up through the same hole in the same roof it had gone up through every evening of his life, until, in his fifty-fifth year by his own true count, he found he could sit there no longer. No stranger meeting him on the road would have set him past a young man grown, and the hill had begun, in the way a hill will, to speak of him a little uneasily behind his back, careful of him the way people are careful of a thing they do not yet know how to hold. Beitris's only living son, and the only child either of his parents had raised to see grown, he was; and a hill that had buried one queen after another without much trouble found it did not, after all, know quite what to do with a prince who showed no sign of being buried himself.

So he told his father he meant to go north with the flint-carriers, giving no better reason than that he wished to see the pit the grey flakes came out of, which was true as far as it went and left out the larger part of the truth, that he wished, for a season or two, to be a man doing a man's ordinary work among men who did not watch him for signs. His father heard him out standing at the door of the hall with the low warmth he carried with him into every room he stood in, and said only that a road was a road and Murchadh was free of it as any man was, and that he should mind, going north, that the households up there judged a stranger only by the work in his hands, whatever blood ran under it, which was, Murchadh understood even then, as near to a blessing as his father commonly gave.

He went out that summer with a train of carriers bound for Cnoc àrsaidh in the north-west, the only hill on Dia-thìr that gave good flint, a grey stone that flaked clean and held an edge no other stone on the island matched, and had been worked, by the time Murchadh first saw it, for longer than any single household's memory could account for. The pit was a wound in the hillside as much as a working, a hollow of raw earth and broken stone with three generations of chippings banked round its lip like the tide-wrack round a shore, and the men who worked it, stripped to the waist in all weathers, greeted the carriers with the particular rough courtesy of men who know their stone is wanted and mean to be paid fairly for the wanting. Murchadh took up a carrier's yoke among them without a word of who he was, and no one at Cnoc àrsaidh troubled to ask; a strong-backed stranger who worked a full day without complaint was welcome on that hill whoever had got him, and it was three seasons before the first man there learned, from a trader passing through, whose son he had been carrying flint beside.

On his way north that first summer he came down through Ros dhomhain, where his mother had once stood on the harbour wall with him small at her side and watched the boats come in on a tide the rock in the harbour mouth had been cut to measure. The rock stood where it had always stood, and the keeper's line of cuts had crept a hand's width further up the stone since he had last had it pointed out to him as a child, so slowly that no single year's mark could be told from the year before or the year after, and only the whole row together said plainly that the water climbed. He stood before it the better part of an hour, in a stillness the keeper's grandson, watching him from the harbour steps, took at the time for no more than a traveller resting his legs, and said nothing of what he thought there to any man on that coast.

The keeper himself, an old man by then and glad of company, came down at last to stand beside him and named the marks off one by one without being asked, this one cut in a hard winter, this one the year the salmon failed, this one the year his own father died and he had cut it crooked for grief and never straightened it. Murchadh asked him whether he minded that the sea seemed, by his own rock's telling, to be climbing toward his door a little further with every generation that kept the count.

"I'll not live to see it reach the door," the keeper said, "and my grandson's grandson can mind it then, if minding does any good by that time. A man keeps the rock he's given to keep. What the rock says is its own business." He looked at Murchadh a while longer than the words needed, as a man looks at a stranger he half-recognises and cannot place, and said no more, and Murchadh, who had not given his name at the harbour and did not give it then, thanked him and went on north before the light failed.

He walked the last stretch to Cnoc àrsaidh alone, by a hill road he had never travelled before, sleeping the two nights under nothing but his own cloak and a dry bank, and came down into the flint country on the third morning with his boots worn through at one heel and no coin on him beyond what a carrier travelled with, which he judged, rightly, to be the surest way of arriving anywhere on the island unremarked.

## II. Of the Flint Road and Ailig Spor

The road the carriers wore between Cnoc àrsaidh and the ford gathering ran the length of the island's northern quarter and back, a season's walking each way with a laden yoke, and it was on that road, over years rather than seasons, that Murchadh, ten years and more into his carrying, came to know Ailig Spor, born at a household by a shallow river-crossing in the flint road's northern flats and grown to a carrier himself before he was twenty. Ailig was a short thick-armed man with a laugh that carried further than his voice had any right to and a memory for a joke told once ten years before that he would produce again, word for word, at the one moment it would do the most damage to whoever it was aimed at; he took to Murchadh from the first season they walked together, not knowing or, once he did know, much caring who Murchadh's father was, and told him so plainly the first time another carrier tried to make something of it.

"A yoke doesn't care whose back it's on," Ailig said, "and neither do I. You carry your share or you don't, and so far you carry it."

It was, Murchadh told his father years later, the truest thing anyone outside the hall had ever said to him, and he kept Ailig's friendship the whole of the years the road gave them, walking the same stretches season after season until the track between Cnoc àrsaidh and that crossing, worn already by generations before either of them was born, sank a full yard below the level of the ground on either side of it, so that a man walking it in a low sun cast no shadow beyond its banks, and a stranger cresting the rise above it for the first time was, more than one carrier swore, apt to mistake the whole line of it for a dry ditch dug on purpose rather than a road worn by nothing but feet and yokes and the years. Murchadh and Ailig walked that sunken stretch together more times than either ever troubled to count, Ailig always a half-step ahead on the narrow going and always, without being asked, dropping back to walk level with Murchadh again once the road widened, a small courtesy neither of them ever spoke of and neither ever failed to keep.

It was on that same stretch, in a winter neither of them ever forgot, that a late storm came down off the sea with no more warning than an hour's dropping wind and caught their gang two days from any household with shelter enough for a full yoke-line. The snow came sidelong and did not let up through a whole night and the better part of the following day, and it was Ailig, not the eldest carrier of the gang nor the one who had walked the road longest, who thought to have every man dig into the sunken road's own bank rather than try the open ground above it, packing the yokes across the top of the cut for a roof and the men beneath them for warmth, so that the gang came through the storm with nothing worse than cold fingers and one man's frost-nipped ear, where an older carrier swore afterward they would have lost two or three had they tried to walk on through it. Murchadh dug beside him the whole of that night without once being told to, and it was there, packed shoulder to shoulder under the borrowed yokes with the snow hissing along the bank above their heads, that Ailig first asked him, not who his father was, for by then half the gang half-knew that already and said nothing of it out of the same rough courtesy that had first welcomed him, but only whether he meant to walk the road all his days or whether some other life was waiting on him somewhere further south.

"I don't know that any life is waiting on me anywhere," Murchadh said, "further south or otherwise."

"Then you're luckier than most of us," Ailig said, "and needn't tell me why, if you'd rather not," and turned over and slept, or seemed to, and Murchadh lay a long while after listening to the wind and thought that of all the answers he might have been pressed for on that road, that was the gentlest any man had ever let him off with.

It was on that same circuit, some years after, that an old keeper of the Cnoc àrsaidh pit-mouth, a man both of them had known the whole of their carrying lives, died in his own bed in the ordinary way of an old man whose work is done, and the two of them walked out from his burying together saying little, until Ailig, turning a half-charred stick in the embers of that night's fire rather than looking at Murchadh as he said it, spoke first.

"I've buried my own father," Ailig said, "and I'll own the ground took no more care over him than it took over that old keeper today, for all the years he gave the pit. A household forgets a man within three generations and calls the forgetting respect."

"Your father had a household to stand over him at the end," Murchadh said, "which is more than some are given."

"It is," Ailig said, "and I'll not say it isn't worth the having. I only say a man ought not to look for more from the ground than the ground has ever given anyone." He threw the stick into the fire whole and said nothing further, and Murchadh, who thought of his own mother's grave more often on that road than he ever told Ailig, found there was more truth in the saying than either of them was minded to sit with that night, and let the fire burn down without adding to it.

## III. Of the Years Between

The years went as years go on a road walked often enough that the road becomes its own kind of calendar, marked less by seasons than by which stretch had washed out that winter and needed re-cutting, which household along it had a new roof up since the carriers last passed, which old man at which hearth had died and which son now sat in his place at the door when the yoke-gang came through. Ailig married a woman of a household by that same crossing in his thirtieth year and brought two sons and a daughter up on flint-carrying wages, all three of them walking the near stretches of the road themselves before they were grown, and Murchadh stood godfather, in the plain way the hill kept such things, to the eldest of the three, a boy who grew, in his turn, to carry a yoke as unremarkably as his father had.

It did not escape the households along that road, in time, that Murchadh walked it decade upon decade with no more sign of the years on him than a carrier three seasons in shows on his first walking, and there were carriers, as the decades wore on, who would not share a fire with him for reasons they gave badly and mostly did not give at all; but Ailig never once, in all the years the road gave them, asked Murchadh the plain question every other man on that circuit sooner or later wanted to ask, and Murchadh, for his part, never once offered him an answer unasked, which both of them, without ever saying so to each other, understood to be the whole of the arrangement between them and the better part of why it held.

"You'll bury me on this road one day," Ailig said to him once, an ordinary evening with nothing in it to have prompted the saying, the two of them sitting out the last of a fire on the sunken stretch below Cnoc àrsaidh. "And you'll walk on with the next lot the season after, same as you walked with me."

"I'll bury you wherever your own people want you buried," Murchadh said, "and I'll walk the road a good while less easily after, whatever lot I walk it with."

Ailig laughed at that, the big carrying laugh that had first drawn Murchadh to him on a road neither of them had walked together yet, and said no more of it that night, and it was the only time in all their years that either of them came so near the question and let it lie unasked.

Murchadh's godson, the eldest of Ailig's three, grew from a boy who ran errands between the carriers' fires to a man walking a yoke of his own, married in his turn, and buried his own father's father, a keeper of that crossing whom Murchadh had known as a young man on his first summer north; and it was watching that ordinary chain of burying and marrying and being born again go round three and four times over on the one road that taught Murchadh, more plainly than any word his father had ever given him, what it was his father's own long life must look like from the far side of it, a single unmoving figure watching a whole hillside of households rise and fall around him like grass under a wind he himself never felt. He had not thought, going out with the carriers at fifty-five, that he was choosing his father's road as much as escaping his father's hall; by the time the thought came to him plainly, on a spring morning watching his godson's own son toddle after the yoke-gang with a play-yoke of bound sticks across his small shoulders, it had already been true a long while.

## IV. Of Ailig Spor's Death

Ailig aged as an ordinary man of the hill ages, slowly through his middle years and then, past his sixtieth, faster than either of them liked to remark on, his back bending under a yoke it had carried without complaint for forty years, his laugh coming a little shorter of breath each season though it lost none of its carrying power to the end. He gave up the road in his sixty-fourth year, handing his yoke to his youngest son and taking instead the lighter work of a keeper at the Cnoc àrsaidh pit-mouth, tallying what came out of the ground rather than carrying it away, and Murchadh, who could by then have claimed a dozen honest reasons to walk the far circuits instead, found himself somehow always coming back through Cnoc àrsaidh at the turn of a season, to sit an evening at the pit-mouth with a man grown grey and slow where he himself had not changed at all.

He was sixty-eight, and had been four years a keeper and not a carrier, when he did not wake one autumn morning, found by his own daughter with no mark of pain on his face and nothing in his last evening's talk, by her account, to say he had known it for his last. Murchadh was two days' walk south at the time, on the near stretch of the road with a fresh gang of young carriers who scarcely knew his name, and did not hear of it until a rider caught him up on the third day; he turned back at once, walking the two days north in one, and stood at the graveside among Ailig's grown children and grandchildren with a face the household, watching him, read for the first time as something nearer his own father's stillness at a grave than any young man's want of understanding, though none of them, then or after, ever said so to him plainly.

He stayed at Cnoc àrsaidh the better part of a month after the burying, walking the pit-mouth alone in the evenings where Ailig had sat tallying, and told the keeper who took Ailig's place that the pit should go on giving its first flake of any new working free to whoever asked for it, as it always had, and that he would see to it himself, from whatever he had, that the custom did not lapse for want of a man to keep it; which the pit-keepers of Cnoc àrsaidh have done, by their own telling, from that year to this, though most of them, asked why, can no longer say more than that it was a promise a carrier once made and that no one since has troubled to unmake it.

## V. Of the Bird Carved at Àth mhòr and the Wolves of Tobar fhiadhaich

He walked on alone after that, taking whatever gang would have him and giving no household of the north cause to remark on him beyond his usual steady carrying, and the years he walked without Ailig beside him seemed to him, though he never said as much to any living soul, longer than all the years he had walked with him put together. The road threw off paths of its own into every household that wanted flint, and it was on the longer of these circuits, far east of the pit, that he came, in his hundred and forty-eighth year, to Àth mhòr, where a young man of that household was cutting a hawk into the lintel over his father's door, wings closed and the head turned back over the shoulder as though it had heard something behind it and had not yet decided whether to fly. The carver worked at it through three evenings after the day's work was done, by firelight and by the little seal-oil lamps that had by then reached even that far corner of the north, and would say, when Murchadh asked him plainly why a hawk and not some other bird, only that the shape had come to him whole one night and would not leave him alone until he had cut it. It was, so far as any carrier ever heard tell afterward, the first bird cut into wood or stone on the whole of Dia-thìr, and Murchadh, standing in the doorway's shadow with a full yoke still on his shoulders, thought it a strange, small, patient thing to have watched come into the world, and wished, walking on, that Ailig had lived to see it, for it was the kind of small thing his friend had always had more patience for than most men gave him credit for.

At Tobar fhiadhaich, many years on, in his hundred and eighty-fifth year, he came upon a household burying a wolf. It lay on its side in a grave dug to a man's depth, its legs drawn up under it as a sleeping wolf draws them, and the household stood about the grave in the ordinary hush of a burying, an old woman among them saying over it words Murchadh did not know. He asked her, once the earth was turned in, why a wolf and no other beast was laid so, and she looked at him a long while before she answered, the way a person looks at a stranger deciding how much of the truth is worth the giving.

"The wolf keeps its own dead," she said. "We have found them at it, on the high ground, more times than I could tell you and told no other soul but the household. A creature that will not leave its own to the crows has earned better than the ditch from us, whatever a stranger thinks a wolf is owed."

Murchadh said nothing for the whole walk back down to the road, thinking of an old carrier put into ground that had taken no more care over him than it took over any other man, and of a friend of his own put into ground with more grief over him than either of them had ever let on to expect, and could not, that evening, have said with any certainty which of the two ways of being buried he thought the better bargain.

## VI. Of the Asking and the Refusal

He came south to Dùn ìseal the following spring, in his hundred and eighty-seventh year and looking, to any stranger's eye, no more than a well-set man in his middle life, and asked his father, plainly and without the long working-up to it that another man might have thought the asking deserved, for the north to hold as his own: the whole stretch of country the flint road ran through and beyond it, from Cnoc àrsaidh to Àth mhòr and Tobar fhiadhaich and the households between, which he had walked, by then, more times than any man living and knew, he said, better than any man living knew it.

His father heard him out in the same low warmth with which he heard every asking, standing at the same door of the same hall he had stood in the day Murchadh first told him he meant to go north with the carriers, and was a long while answering, long enough that Murchadh let himself believe the answer he wanted was coming.

"The north is Dòmhnall's to hold," his father said, "and has been these forty years, by my giving and his own good keeping of it. I will not take a share from a man who has kept it well to give it to a son who has only walked it, however far he has walked."

"I have walked it further than Dòmhnall has ridden it," Murchadh said, and heard, saying it, how much of his mother's plain temper and how much of some other temper entirely had gone into the saying.

"That may be true," his father said, "and it changes nothing I have said. A share given is a share I do not take back for a better claim that comes after it, or no man on this hill would ever again trust a giving of mine to hold. Ask me for the north when Dòmhnall no longer holds it, and I will hear you as I heard him."

He gave no other reason, and Murchadh, who had walked half the length of the island more times than he could number without once being told by any keeper or carrier or grieving daughter that his asking was unwelcome, found in his father's plain refusal the first thing on the whole of that long road that had ever been flatly refused him. He stood a while longer at the door before he answered, and told it afterward as the hardest work he ever did to hold his voice as level as his father's own.

"Then I have walked it for nothing," he said.

"You have walked it for itself," his father said, "which is the only reason walking a road has ever been worth a man's while, and the only reason I ever gave you leave to go. If you went out looking for a hill at the end of it, that was your own asking of the road, and not mine, and not the road's."

Murchadh had no answer ready for that, and gave none, and his father did not press him for one, only stood a moment longer in the doorway with the same low warmth he had stood there with the day his son first went out, before turning back into the hall to whatever business the hall still held for a man who did not age. Murchadh said nothing further that evening, and rode out again within the week without asking a second time, north by the road he knew better than any road on the island, past the sunken stretch below Cnoc àrsaidh where a keeper's grandson still gave away, at Murchadh's own standing order, the first grey flake of every new working to whoever came asking for it; and it is told, by those few at Dùn ìseal who claimed afterward to have marked it, that he rode out that year with a face his father's household had not seen on him before, neither the young man's face he had worn going north the first time nor the stillness he had worn at Ailig Spor's grave, but something nearer pride than either, and, close under the pride, the first grievance the hill ever had cause to lay at a son of Ailean's own naming.
