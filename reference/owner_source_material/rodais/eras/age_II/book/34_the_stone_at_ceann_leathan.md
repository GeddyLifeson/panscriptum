# Of the Stone at Ceann leathan

*GE 3,031 – 3,495*

*This is kept at Ceann leathan among the wrestlers themselves, passed hand to hand at the fires the night before the contests, and carried down to the ford by whichever of them walks there after.*

## I. Of the Southern Shores

In those years the south filled slowly, the way a shallow dish fills last from a jug poured too fast. Households came down to Ceann mhin within sight of Làrach an Dùin on its headland, and built their houses with their backs to it and their doors to the sea, and would not climb the path up to the broken walls even to gather the good stone lying loose there for the taking. An old woman of that shore was heard to say that a wall raised by hands other than one's own kept its own quarrel, and no one troubled to ask her whose quarrel she meant.

Further round the coast the south had its own old harbour, unnamed by Seann-Dhaoine who first laid its stones and unrenamed by the Dia-thìrich who took it up after them, so that it was known only as the southern Seann Skell, the old landing. Within a few generations of its resettling it was the busiest quay on that whole shore, boats going out with the tide and coming home heavy, and the hook-makers there shaped bone hooks faster than the fishing wore them out.

Achadh shean had stood empty since the long drought, its fields cracked and its old well silted to mud, and when households came back to it they did what the ones before had not: they sank a new well first, deep into the rock, before they broke a single furrow, and planted only after the water had held through a dry summer. The well did not fail them again in all the years of that age, and the households there told it as a lesson to their children long after the reason for the telling had gone out of most other memories: dig first, plant after.

On the eastern cliffs a scattering of houses grew up at Inis mhin, where the birds nested in their thousands on ledges no goat could reach, and the people there lived as much by egg and feather as by anything grown in the ground, and carried baskets of down and quill inland to trade for grain and salt. And on the high ground above the upper waters, where the rivers that fed half the south had their beginning, households settled at Muileann chaol around a fold built for cattle in bad weather, a low stone pen with a turf roof over one corner of it, nothing more. A cairn was raised there for each head of a household who died, on the slope above the fold, one stone laid at a time by whoever had known the dead, until by degrees the slope came to have as many small heaps of stone on it as the fold below had cattle in a good year.

East again, past the middens of an older ruin the settlers called Seann Dunn and never dug into, calling the ground around it empty though it plainly was not, Baile Mòr ruadh grew up in the shadow of nothing at all, a broad red-earthed rise with no history that its own people troubled to ask after. And inland from all of these, on the eastern hills, stood Làrach an Teampaill that no one built and no one claimed, which the Dia-thìrich of that country passed without stopping, taking no stone from it though its stone was as good as any other for a house wall, until a path had worn round it at a careful distance, as a stream wears round a rock it will not cross.

These were the years, too, in which the island lost the last of the king's first sons. Fearchar Bacach, who had held Dùn ìseal for the king long before and had gone lame in Cogaidhean nan Gleann so long before that no one living remembered him whole, died in his own bed in the year three thousand one hundred and forty of the grove, having carried his lameness near three thousand years and lived three thousand one hundred and thirty years in all. His house mourned him quietly, the way a fire is kept without anyone remarking that it is being kept.

His brother Dòmhnall Clachach, who had been sent north out of that same hill the year Fearchar took it, and had grown old in the north country among people who called him only the stone-cutter's son and did not trouble themselves with what he had once been, died there in the year three thousand three hundred and ten, at three thousand three hundred and eight years of age, the elder of the two brothers by eight years at birth and by a hundred and seventy-eight at death. Word of it came south slowly, as word from the north always did, and reached Dùn ìseal a full season after the burying, so that Fearchar's house heard of their kinsman's death only after they had already marked, without knowing it, the anniversary of it.

A boy of Inis mhin once climbed down past the ledges after eggs and did not come up again by dusk, and the whole small settlement went out along the cliff-path with torches half the night calling his name into the wind, until they found him wedged on a shelf below the highest nests, unhurt but too frightened to climb either way, and had to lower a rope woven that same hour out of whatever cord the households owned. After that the cliffs were never worked alone, by custom rather than any law laid down, two to a rope always, one above and one below, and the old woman who had led the search with the loudest voice that night was, for the rest of her life, asked her advice on every matter to do with the birds, whether or not it had anything to do with ropes.

At Làrach an Teampaill on the eastern hills a shepherd once let his flock wander too near the fallen stones while he slept in the sun, and woke to find every sheep standing stock still along the outer edge of the worn path, none of them grazing, none of them crying, simply standing, as though some line had been drawn there that even beasts without words for it knew better than to cross. He drove them home without letting them so much as put a hoof past the path's edge, and told it after as the day his sheep had more sense than he did, and few who heard it thought he was wrong to say so.

Between these two endings, in the south, a son was born to the king in the year three thousand two hundred and four, and named Ìomhar, and called Dearg for the colour that came into his hair as a boy, redder than his father's or any of his brothers'. He grew up about the southern shores and the eastern marches with no love of the hill and no patience for lists and lots, and was already, before he had lived his first century, known along that coast for asking, wherever he went, whether a man might not simply be given ground and left alone to hold it, without a share given him and taken back again by a father who never explained the giving.

## II. Of Alasdair, Son of Torradh

Among the households of the southern shore that came down to settle near Clachan nan Gaoth at Ceann leathan, there was a man named Torradh who had two daughters and, late in his life, one son, born in the year three thousand three hundred and eighty, whom he named Alasdair. The boy grew large from his first years, in the plain way some children do, not sickly and not clever with his hands at any trade his father tried to teach him, so that Torradh in the end gave up trying to make a potter or a net-mender of him and let him run with the cattle instead, where his size was no hindrance and his slowness with words no shame.

By the time he had seen twenty summers Alasdair stood a head above every man of that shore and most of the women too, and had the kind of strength that came without his seeking it, so that he broke more than one tool in his father's shed simply by using it as another man would, and had to be told, more than once, to set a thing down gently rather than merely set it down. He was not quick-tempered. If anything he was slow to anger past the point most men would have reached it, and slower still to use the strength he had against another person, having learned early, from the one time he had shoved a boy his own age in a boyhood quarrel and put him in the dirt six feet off, that his own arm did not know its own weight the way other men's did.

It was Clachan nan Gaoth that first gave him somewhere to put it. The eight stones stood in their ring on the point above the harbour, weathered and grey, said by the old people to mark Na h-Ochd Gaothan and set there before anyone now living could say by whom, and below the ring, on the flattest ground of the headland, was held every year a contest of falls and grips and the lifting of stones that drew men down from half the southern coast to try each other, and drew everyone else to watch them try. Alasdair went to it first as a boy at his father's heel, carrying water for the older men between falls, and went to it next as a competitor before he had grown his full height, and put three grown men in the dirt in his first year at it, to his own evident surprise and everyone else's.

His mother, who had thought her one son would follow his father into the boats like her own brothers, gave up any such hope the year he came home from the shore with a net he had meant to mend torn clean through where his own hands had pulled the cord too hard, and told him, not unkindly, that the sea would have no use for arms like his and he had best find somewhere that would. It was his father, in the end, who walked him up to Clachan nan Gaoth one autumn and stood him at the edge of the ring to watch the contests through from first light to last, more to give the boy somewhere to be than from any thought of what would come of it.

He lost some years too. He was thrown twice himself, once by a smaller man from the southern Seann Skell who knew a trick of the hip that Alasdair had never troubled to learn, and once by his own overreaching, catching his foot on a stone half-buried in the turf and going down under his own weight before his opponent had done more than raise his hands. But he won more often than he lost, and by the time he had seen his tenth gathering he was known the length of that coast simply as Alasdair Làidir, Alasdair the Strong, a name given him by other men rather than claimed by himself, which was, among wrestlers, accounted the only kind of name worth having.

## III. Of the Stone That Would Not Be Lifted

Every contests ended the same way, once the falls were done and the light had begun to go out of the day: with Clach a' Neirt. It lay at the point where the headland narrowed to a lip of bare rock over the sea, a black boulder half again as broad as a man was tall, smooth on every face as though the sea had shaped it, though the sea at that height had not touched it in the memory of anyone's grandfather. Every year, at the close of the contests, whoever had won the falls that day was given the first try at it, and after him whoever else cared to, and every year, for as long as the telling went, it did not move.

Men had strained at it until the veins stood in their necks. One, in the years before Alasdair was born, had put his back into it so hard that something in his back gave way before the stone did, and he never walked upright again after, and the stone was left alone for two whole gatherings out of respect for him before anyone tried it again. Torradh himself had put a shoulder to it once, as a young man, and told his son afterward that it had felt, for the length of one long breath, as though he had set his shoulder against the headland rather than against any stone lying on it.

In the year three thousand four hundred and three, Alasdair Làidir won the falls at three-and-twenty, the youngest man in memory to take every bout of the contests from first light to last, and by custom the first try at the stone was given him. He set his hands to it, braced his feet in the turf worn bare by every year's failed lifting before his, and heaved until the sky went dark at the edges of his sight and his own breath roared louder in his ears than the sea below him. The stone did not stir. He stepped back at last, his hands scraped raw, and said, to no one and to everyone, that he did not think any man born of a woman would ever lift it, and sat down hard on the turf to get his wind back.

It was then that a stranger came forward out of the crowd along the edge of the ring.

He was a big man, though not so big as Alasdair, grey through the hair and the short beard he wore, dressed as plainly as any herdsman of that coast and carrying nothing that marked him as anyone in particular, a stick in one hand that he set down in the grass before he came to the stone. No one on that headland knew his face, though a good half of every crowd at Ceann leathan was strangers from somewhere else on the coast, so his being unknown troubled no one at first. He looked at the stone a while, the way a man looks at a thing he means to lift rather than a thing he merely means to try, and then he set his hands to it much as Alasdair had, low and wide, and lifted it.

He did not strain as Alasdair had strained. The stone came up off the turf as though it had been waiting only to be asked properly, and he held it a moment at the height of his knees, black and dripping the loose soil of centuries from its underside, before he set it down again, not with the crash and grunt every failed heave before it had ended in, but gently, the way a man sets down a sleeping child he does not wish to wake, so that it settled into the print it had worn in the turf without so much as a thud.

The whole headland had gone silent. Even the sea below seemed, to more than one who told it after, to have gone quiet for that one breath.

Alasdair, still sitting where he had dropped to get his wind, found his own voice before anyone else's. "Who are you?" he said, or something near it, for by his own account afterward the words came out of him before he had decided to speak them at all.

The stranger looked at him, and there was nothing unkind in the look, nor anything proud in it either, only the plain regard one man gives another after a day's work shared rather than a contest won and lost. "A traveller," he said. "No more than that."

"You lifted it," Alasdair said, getting to his feet, though he came up more slowly than he had ever come up from anything in his life. "No one lifts it."

"It only wanted lifting properly," the stranger said, and there was, in that, the ghost of something that might have been dry humour in another man, though his face gave little enough away to be sure of it. "You had the strength for it yourself. You set your hands too high."

"Will you give your name to the reciters?" someone called from the crowd, for by custom whoever lifted the stone had his name taken down and carried to every gathering after, to be spoken before the falls began each year until another man matched him.

The stranger picked up his stick from the grass. "I have no name that would mean anything carried," he said, "and I would take it kindly if none of you troubled to carry one for me." And with that he turned and walked off the headland the way he had come, unhurried, and was lost among the fires and the crowd before anyone thought to follow him, though half a dozen young men swore afterward that they had tried.

Alasdair stood by the stone a long while after the stranger had gone, and put his own hand flat on the print it had left in the turf, which was, he said later, warm, though the day had been no warmer than any other autumn day on that coast and the stone itself, when he touched it, was cold as any stone taken from the sea.

## IV. Of the Years That Followed

The tale went along that coast quickly enough that first winter, told at every fire from Ceann leathan to Achadh shean and back, and for a season or two it was told much as it had happened, a grey stranger, a stone lifted and set down gently, a name refused. But tales worn between enough fires wear smooth the way Clach a' Neirt itself was smooth, and within a few years some tellers had made the stranger taller, some had him lift the stone one-handed, and one teller at the southern Seann Skell, drunk on someone else's beer, swore the stranger's hands had glowed like coals in a hearth while he held it, which no one who had stood on the headland that day would allow, least of all Alasdair, who took to correcting that particular embellishment wherever he heard it, more sharply each year he heard it repeated.

He did not lift the stone himself again after that year, though he tried twice more before he gave it up, telling his own sons, when he had them, that a man who has once seen a thing done properly does not care to go on failing at it in front of witnesses. He married a woman of Achadh shean, Mòr, whose people kept the new well, and had three children by her who lived and two who did not, and went on winning the falls at Ceann leathan on and off for another five-and-twenty years, until a younger man's hip-trick put him in the dirt for good and he took, after that, to carrying water at the edge of the ring the way he had once carried it for the men before him, an old joke he made of it himself before anyone else could.

His eldest son, born the summer after the stranger's visit and named Torradh for his grandfather, grew up hearing the tale so often at his own hearth that he could recite it in his father's voice before he had seen ten summers, and did once, to a fire full of his father's own friends, word for word down to the setting-down of the cup, until Alasdair laughed so hard at hearing himself mimicked that he could not finish his own supper, and told the boy afterward that he had got the throat-clearing wrong but the rest near enough right to trouble him. That son took no interest in the wrestling himself, having his mother's build rather than his father's, and went instead to keep bees on the ground above Achadh shean, but he told his father's tale to his own children in turn all the same, throat-clearing and all, long after the man who cleared it that way was in the ground.

He never stopped telling of the stranger, though. Younger men at the fires learned to recognize the particular way he set down his cup and cleared his throat before he began it, and some of them, as the years passed, took to finding some other fire to sit at when they saw him settling into the telling, having heard it enough times that they could have told it themselves, word for word, without being there at all.

## V. Of the Ford

In the year three thousand four hundred and fifty, old now and slow in the hip himself, Alasdair Làidir walked north from Ceann leathan to Cruinneachadh an Àtha for what he told his wife would likely be the last time his legs allowed him the road. The ford by then drew households from every coast of the island, its ground long since parted by custom into three, the salt people downstream, the hill people upstream, and the flint people on the rise beyond the river, more crowded each year than the year before, so that a man could stand at its edge of an evening and hear a dozen different accents of the same tongue called across the water at once.

The ground he walked to reach it had changed since he had last made the journey as a younger man, houses standing now where he remembered only open pasture, and twice he lost his own bearings on a road he had walked a dozen times before, until a girl minding geese by the wayside set him right again, unable herself to say how long the new houses had stood there, having been born among them and knowing no other way of it.

He found a fire of strangers that first night, southern men mostly, a few of them young enough to have been born after the year of the stone, and when the talk turned, as talk at a fire will, to feats of strength and stones no one could lift, he told it as he always told it: the ring at Ceann leathan, his own failed heave, the grey stranger stepping out of the crowd, the stone set down gentle as a sleeping child, the name refused.

Most of the young men at that fire heard him out politely and said nothing after, in the particular silence people keep for an old man's tale they do not believe and do not care to argue with. One of them, bolder or drunker than the rest, asked him outright whether he was certain it was not merely a strong man he had seen, dressed plain and travelling light, and not any stranger worth remarking twice.

But an older woman at the same fire, grey herself and further travelled than any of the young men there, said she had heard the very same tale told at a fire near the southern Seann Skell thirty years before, near enough word for word as Alasdair told it now, from a different mouth entirely, a woman who swore she had stood on that same headland the very year it happened and seen the stone set down with her own eyes. She said this without taking a side in the matter one way or the other, only setting the fact down beside the doubt, the way a stone is set down that a walker has turned over once and found something curious under after all.

Alasdair said nothing further to the young man who had doubted him, only reached for a stick and settled the fire, the way a man does who has told a thing often enough to know that the telling itself is worth more to him, by then, than whoever happens to believe it. He sat a while longer at that fire, an old man among strangers at the edge of a river crowded with more people than he had ever seen in one place outside the ring at Ceann leathan, and watched the flames a long time before he went to his bed that night, thinking, he told his wife afterward, of nothing in particular, only of a stone set down gently on a headland forty-seven years gone, and of hands that had known, better than his own ever had, exactly how much weight they carried.
