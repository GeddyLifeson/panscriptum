# Of the Sand on the Hearths and the Opened Ground

*GE 770 – 901*

*At the new houses above Cnoc chaol they still measure a child's growing by Seasaidh's own count: so many years since the sand, so many since the valley, so many since the stones.*

## I. Of the Sand

Seasaidh was seven years old the night the sea came into the house.

She had no memory afterward of hearing it come, only of being carried, wet and half asleep, in her mother's arms up a slope she did not know the household owned, while behind them a sound went on that she took at first, muddled with sleep, for wind in a bad season and only later, turning the night over and over across the rest of her life, understood to have been water moving where no water had ever moved in the memory of anyone then living. Cnoc chaol stood, in those years, low along a shingle rise a stone's throw back from the tide line, a scatter of houses no different from a dozen others on that coast, and the sea had kept, for as long as the oldest households could reckon, to a plain and honest habit of coming up the shingle at the flood and going back down it at the ebb, so that no one had thought to ask what the sea might do if it once forgot the habit.

It forgot it that night. There was no storm to give warning of it, no wind worth remarking, only a stillness some of the older folk said afterward they had felt before the water came, a stillness in the air that a few had remarked on at the time and none had known what to make of, and then the sea rose past every mark it had ever left and came in across the low ground faster than a grown man could run from it, taking two households whole and leaving, on every hearth it reached and did not carry away with it, a sheet of grey sand laid smooth as if a hand had smoothed it, a hand's depth thick, and threaded through with the broken shells of creatures no one on that shore had ever pulled up in a net, the shells of water deeper than any of them fished.

Seasaidh's father did not come up the slope with them. She learned this only in the grey hour after, standing in the ruin of what had been the lower houses with the sand already drying pale under the first sun and her mother's hand gripping hers hard enough to hurt, and she did not properly understand, at seven, what the word meant when her mother said it, only that a shape in her life that had always been there was now, without any ceremony to mark the change, not there, and that everyone around her had gone quiet in a particular way that told her not to ask more of it than she had already been told. Twelve of the coast's households were counted dead when the counting was done, a number that meant nothing to her at seven beyond the fact that among them was the shape that had been her father, and that her mother's face, in the days that followed, wore an expression Seasaidh had not seen on it before and would see again, at longer and longer intervals, for the whole of the rest of her mother's life.

The old folk, sifting through what the sand had left standing, found little worth the digging. A quern-stone, a scatter of the small clay counters children played at knuckle-games with, the charred end of a roof-beam still faintly warm where the sand had not quite smothered the last of a hearth-fire before it went out under the weight of the water. No one went back to rebuild on the same ground, though it was, as more than one of the older men said standing on it in the days after, as good a stretch of level shore as the coast offered and would offer again once the sand had settled and grassed over, which in time it did. But there was a fear laid into that low ground now that no amount of good level shore could talk a household out of, and the survivors of Cnoc chaol carried what could be carried up the slope behind the old site, to a shelf of higher ground looking down over the drowned houses, and began again there, in the first weeks with nothing more than what had been snatched up on the way out and what could be cut and carried in a hurry, and in the seasons after with houses built properly, walls of stone this time where the old houses had been turf and wattle, as if stone alone might argue the sea out of a second visit.

## II. Of the Slope

Seasaidh grew up on that slope, in a house her mother and her mother's brothers raised with their own hands in the first winter after the wave, and she grew up, as children do who lose a father too young to properly grieve him, with the loss folded into the ordinary shape of her days rather than standing apart from it: a story told at the fire on certain nights, a silence that fell over her mother at certain turns of the tide, a habit, which Seasaidh kept all her own life after without ever quite deciding to keep it, of counting the households on any shore she came to and reckoning, without meaning to, how far up the ground they stood from the water.

The new houses held. Seasaidh was thirteen, fourteen, old enough to help at the nets and the drying racks, before she stopped waking at every ordinary sound of a high tide working at the shingle below, and older than that again before she stopped, on the anniversary nights the household kept without ever naming them anniversaries, looking down from the new slope at the pale drowned ground below and feeling the particular cold that was not the sea's cold at all. The village above the old site never took a name of its own separate from the name the drowned ground had carried before it, for there seemed to the folk who built it no reason to call the new houses by any word but the word the coast had always used for that stretch of shore, so that Cnoc chaol came to mean, without anyone deciding it should, the slope and not the sand, the living houses and not the dead ones, though every soul who grew up there in Seasaidh's years knew exactly which ground the older word had first belonged to, and pointed it out to their children in turn, low ground, drowned ground, not to be built on.

It was on that slope, in her nineteenth year, that Seasaidh first met Pàl, a quiet, broad-shouldered young man come down with drovers from the inland valleys, wool to trade for salt fish, third son of a household that held grazing two days inland along a river Seasaidh had never seen and could not, that first year, well imagine, a country of grass and slow water and no tide at all to reckon anything by. He came back the following year, and the year after that with less excuse for coming than wool alone gave him, and by her twenty-second year Seasaidh had agreed, to her mother's mixed grief and relief, to go inland with him and take up a household on ground she had never set eyes on, a decision she thought little enough of at the time, being twenty-two and in that particular kind of love that makes distance feel like nothing more than a thing to be walked through, and understood a good deal better once she had lived some years on the ground she had gone to.

## III. Of the Valley

The valley Pàl's household held lay a long two days' walk from the coast by the tracks Seasaidh came to know well over the years that followed, a green trough between two low scarps with a stream running the length of its floor and grazing enough on either slope to hold three households in fair comfort, which was, in those years, as much land as any inland household thought to ask for. It had no name of its own beyond what its own folk called it among themselves, the valley, the long field, words that served well enough for people who rarely had cause to speak of it to anyone who did not already know which valley was meant, and Seasaidh, coming to it a coast woman with salt still in the creases of her hands, found it at first a strange, closed sort of country, hemmed in by its own slopes the way the coast was never hemmed, no horizon of open water to look to, only grass and more grass and the stream's steady, unhurried talking.

Love for it came slowly, the way love comes for a thing not chosen for oneself, first through Pàl and the children who came after him, four of them living past their first winters out of six born, and then through the valley's own quiet virtues: its shelter from the coast's winds, its grass holding green longer into a dry summer than any pasture she had known as a girl, its stream that never once, in all the years she kept house beside it, rose higher than a channel a child could jump. Seasaidh told her children more than once, meaning it kindly and meaning it, so far as she then knew, truly, that the valley was a safe country, dry-footed, its only weather the ordinary weather of wind and frost and the occasional hard winter that thinned the flocks but never the households.

She was fifty-seven years old, her hair more grey than not and her hands stiffening a little in the cold mornings the way hands do at that age, and her youngest grandchild not yet walking, when she woke one night in early autumn to a sound she had not heard in fifty years and had never expected to hear again on ground so far from any tide: a long, low groaning, felt through the floor of the house as much as heard, that went on, off and on, through the better part of that night and left, by morning, a crack running clean across the floor of the valley a little below the household's own byre, wide enough in places for a grown man to set his boot into and lose it to the darkness below, and running, as the light came up fully and Pàl and the neighbouring households walked the length of it, further than any of them cared to walk to find its end.

## IV. Of the Opened Ground

It was not, that first morning, a thing any of them had words ready for. A storm, a bad winter, even a fire, these were misfortunes the valley's folk had inherited a stock of sayings for, ready-made comfort passed down from households that had weathered the same before them; but no one living in that valley, nor any teller any of them could call to mind, had ever spoken of the ground itself coming open under a household's feet, and the strangeness of it sat on the valley folk for days afterward heavier, in its way, than the plain damage would have warranted on its own, for the crack had taken no life and thrown down no house, only opened, silently and for no cause any of them could name, across ground they had walked without a second thought every day of their lives.

Seasaidh, standing at the crack's edge that first morning with Pàl's hand hard on her arm holding her back from the loose lip of it, felt the old cold come up in her the way it had not come up in fifty years, the exact cold of the slope above Cnoc chaol looking down at drowned ground, and understood before anyone else in the valley said it aloud that this was, for her, the old fear come round again by a different road, the world's ground proving, a second time in one life, less solid underfoot than a person is raised to believe. She kept it from Pàl that morning, for there was, in the moment, no way to say it that would not have sounded, even to her own ear, a stranger claim than the crack itself: that she had felt this exact cold before, on a coast two days' walk away, over water and not over ground at all.

The spring that had, for as long as the household could remember, risen at the valley's upper end and run down along the near slope to join the stream below the byre, moved in the days after the ground opened, its old channel running dry by inches until within a season it ran altogether elsewhere, breaking out a hundred paces downhill in a new hollow none of them had marked before as ground a spring might choose, so that the valley folk found themselves, in the space of one autumn, relearning a stretch of ground they had thought as settled and as known as their own hands. No house stood near enough the crack to be lost to it, which the valley folk afterward counted a mercy plain enough not to need much saying, the byre nearest it standing a good long stone's throw off and no dwelling closer than that; but grazing was lost along the crack's whole run, sheep would not be driven near it and cattle liked it no better, and for a season and more the valley's folk went about their ordinary work with a wariness that had not been part of the valley's temper before, glancing at ground they had never before thought worth a glance.

Word of it went out along the drover roads the way word of anything went, carried by whoever next had reason to travel, and it was carried, that winter, all the way to the hill, though none of the valley's own folk had thought to send it there on purpose; a drover simply told it at a hearth where it was overheard by a man travelling on to the hill for other business, and he told it again where he next stopped, and so, by the slow accident of ordinary talk rather than by any deliberate asking, the king came to hear of a valley two days inland where the ground had opened and a spring had moved.

## V. Of the King at the Crack

He came in the spring following, on foot and with no more than two men behind him, word of his coming reaching the valley only a day ahead of the man himself, which gave the household barely time enough to be afraid of the honour before the honour was standing at their gate. Seasaidh was sixty that year, old enough by the valley's own reckoning to have earned a settled place at any gathering, and old enough, too, to remember plainly enough what a king's coming had meant on the coast in her own girlhood, though she herself, a child then, had never once seen him with her own eyes; and she stood that morning at the edge of the yard with her grown children about her and felt, watching him walk up the last stretch of track from the ford, the same thing every teller she had ever heard tell of him agreed on and that she had never, until that morning, quite believed simply for having heard it so often: a warmth on the air around him that had nothing to do with the season, felt at ten paces and at five and closer, as though the man carried some small part of a well-kept hearth folded into his ordinary clothes and none of it showed on the outside of him.

He asked to be shown the crack before he would take food or rest, and walked its length with Pàl and two of the valley's older men, Seasaidh a little behind them with the rest of the household, and said little as he walked, stopping twice to crouch at the lip of it and look down into the dark the way a man looks into a well he means to judge the depth of, though he let down nothing to measure it by, only looked. He asked how the spring had moved and was shown its new channel, and asked whether any beast or soul had gone into the crack in the season since it opened, and was told, truthfully, that none had, the valley folk having kept clear of it by instinct before anyone had thought to make a rule of keeping clear.

"You have done well enough on your own," he said at last, standing at the widest part of the crack with the two men who had walked it beside him and the rest of the household drawn up a little way off, "better than most would, coming on a strangeness with no one to tell them what it meant." He looked along the crack's run a last time, the way, Seasaidh thought, watching him from where she stood, a man looks at a thing he expects to see again rather than a thing he expects to be done with. "I'll not tell you to fill it in, nor to leave it. The ground will do as the ground does, here as anywhere else I've stood on it. Keep your beasts back, and your children, and build no house nearer it than you'd build to a drop of the same depth cut by a man's own hand, and you'll have done all that wants doing." He said nothing more of it, neither warning nor blessing beyond the plain sense of what he had already said, and Seasaidh, hearing it repeated at valley fires for years afterward in tellings that grew, as tellings do, a little grander with each retelling, thought privately, and said so to her own grandchildren when she was old enough to say what she liked, that the grandest thing in it had been how little he had made of a thing the valley had spent a whole season being afraid of.

He stayed the one night, eating at Pàl's own hearth without ceremony and sleeping, so far as anyone in the household could tell, an ordinary man's sleep, and was gone again by the next morning's light, walking back the way he had come with his two men and no more said of the crack than had already been said at its edge. Seasaidh never saw him again in the years left to her, though she heard, as everyone on that road heard sooner or later, of him at other gates, other gatherings, other years, always the same face, always the same warmth reported by whoever had stood near enough to feel it, while her own face, and Pàl's, and the faces of every soul she had grown up beside on the coast or married among in the valley, went on ageing the ordinary way faces age, so that by the time she herself was old she had long since stopped finding it strange and had come, instead, simply to expect it, the way a person comes to expect the tide.

## VI. Of the White Stones

The crack neither closed nor widened greatly in the years that followed, though it moved, by inches and by seasons, the way such ground moves, a little here after a hard frost, a little there after a wet spring, so that a line drawn along its edge in one year would not quite serve to mark it ten years on. The valley folk kept clear of it as they had from the first, by habit more than by any rule laid down, until Seasaidh, old now, her own children grown and settled with households of their own along the same valley, took it into her head, in the winter of her seventy-seventh year, that habit alone was a poor thing to trust a crack's whole length to, memory being shorter than a valley's life and children being born every year who had not stood, as she had, at the lip of the ground on the morning it first opened.

She began the stones herself, carrying the first of them up from the stream bed one at a time, more than her stiffening hands and her grown children thought wise for a woman of her years, and set them along the crack's outer edge a good stride back from the drop, white stones chosen for their whiteness rather than their size, so that even a child running in poor light might see the line of them and know, without needing to be told twice, where the safe ground ended. Her sons and daughters, seeing what she was at and unable to talk her out of it, took up the carrying themselves before she had done more than the first stretch, and grandchildren after them, so that the work, begun as one old woman's stubbornness, became within a season the whole valley's doing, households from both ends of the settled ground coming to add their own stones to the line until it ran the crack's full length, broken here and there where the ground itself had already made the danger plain enough without a stone to say so.

Pàl had died the winter before her seventy-fifth year, an ordinary death of an ordinary old age, and Seasaidh kept the house alone after him with a grandson's family settled near enough to see to her, a life she found, in the telling of it to those same grandchildren, no harder to bear than any other loss a long life brings, having by then buried a father, a mother, and a husband, and having come, in the slow way a person comes to such things, to hold that grief widened rather than emptied a person, leaving room, each time, for one more thing worth minding on behalf of those still to come after.

"The ground keeps its own counsel," Seasaidh was fond of saying to her grandchildren in those last years, over the white stones, "and a wise household keeps a stride back from it"; and the children of the valley grew up, in the years after, knowing the crack the way Seasaidh herself had once known the drowned ground below Cnoc chaol: a plain fact of the country they lived in, marked, minded, and left otherwise alone.

She died in her eighty-second year, in the same house Pàl had first brought her to sixty winters before, with her children and most of her grandchildren about her, and was buried on the near slope above the valley within sight, though not within a stone's throw, of the white line she had begun; and the valley folk, telling of her afterward at their own fires, told less often of the coast she had come from or the wave that had taken her father than of the stones themselves, so that within two generations more it was chiefly as the woman who had marked the crack that her name was kept at all, the sand and the drowning folded quietly into an older telling behind it. No house was raised, in all the years that valley folk afterward kept the ground, within a stone's throw of the crack on either side of it, and the white line, mended where frost or flood undid a stretch of it and never once let go entirely to grass, ran the whole visible length of the opened ground, still tended by hands that had never known Seasaidh living, when every soul who had stood at its edge the first morning was gone to the hill's own reckoning of the dead.
