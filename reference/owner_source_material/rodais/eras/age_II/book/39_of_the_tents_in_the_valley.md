# Of the Tents in the Valley

*GE 4,550 – 4,603*

*This is kept in Na Glinn Àrda among Clann na Ceiste, who had it first from Gille-Brìde himself, in his own age, before he was old.*

## I. Of the Eve as It Stood

In the year four thousand five hundred and fifty the island stood as it had stood, by then, for longer than any one household could remember it standing otherwise. The handful was kept at every hearth from the northern capes to Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh, each burning its own small measure in the dark of the year and none troubling another with the manner of the burning. Luchd-faire na Lice were nine, as they had been nine since the lot was last drawn, and the sharing before the storms was kept on every coast that had a boat to share it from, and the saying cut long before on Calbh Cnoc ghorm was spoken aloud, plainly and without dispute, wherever two or three of the blood or of the common households came together at a fire: *we did not come; we were here.* None of it had been set down as law. No oath had been sworn over the vein, and no oath had been asked. The king held the hill in his own hand, as he had held it since Catrìona Mhòr's death took the last of Rìghrean na Cloiche' list out of the world, and his kin waited about him, patiently or less patiently as it suited each of them, for shares that were not yet given and could not yet be counted.

It was a few years before this, in the year four thousand five hundred and forty-three, that a son was born in Na Glinn Àrda to Gille-Brìde of Somhairle's line. The child was called Ceallach, after no one that the mother could name, and he was a quiet child, slower to walk than others of that blood and slower still to run, though when he did at last run he ran, his father said, watching him cross the floor of the high valley on legs that had taken the better part of two years to trust themselves, as though he meant to make up in a single afternoon what he had been given late. It was told back afterward, when the boy was grown into whatever he grew into, that this had been the first sign anyone in Na Glinn Àrda troubled to remark on: that the years were coming down faster in him than they had in his father, and faster in his father than in the father before that, a shortening that no one in the valleys named aloud, because there was no cause yet to name it, and because the years a man had were his own business until the manner of his dying made them everyone's.

Gille-Brìde was by then the seventh of Somhairle's line after Beathan, a man past a hundred years of his own, a big quiet man who kept to Na Glinn Àrda among Clann na Ceiste and went down into the lower country only when the asking or the giving of some particular thing required it of him. He held no hill and claimed no share, and it was said of him, by those few of the king's blood who troubled to think of him at all, that he had got the family's patience and none of its hunger, which some of them meant as a compliment and most did not. His own father, an old man then in the second century of his life, came down from the upper valley to see the child that same autumn with a gift of worked flint in a bag on his back, and sat with the boy on his knee by a low fire while Gille-Brìde told him, in the flat voice of a man reciting a thing he had said to himself too many times already to feel much about it any longer, that the fever which had taken two of his own brothers young had not touched this one, and that the boy, if the years ran true, might see out the whole of whatever remained to the line before they ran out altogether.

"They will run out," the old man said. It was not a question.

"They will," said Gille-Brìde. "Not in me. Perhaps not in him. In someone."

Clann na Ceiste, who kept Na Glinn Àrda about the slab and had kept them since the keepers were first chosen by lot, took little account of the king's kin's counting of years, having little enough occasion to count their own beyond the ordinary span of a life and a death, and it was one of their number, an old woman who had sat that year's watch at the slab and walked down afterward to see the new child for herself, who said to Gille-Brìde what he came, in time, to think of as the truest word spoken over the boy in the whole of his infancy: that a child born short-lived among the long-lived would grow up knowing early a thing neither his father's people nor hers had ever needed to learn until late, how much of a life could be made to matter in however much of it a body was given. Gille-Brìde kept that word to himself and did not repeat it to his own father, judging that an old man newly certain his line was running out wanted no philosophy laid over the plain fact of it, however kindly meant.

He watched the boy grow through his first years, and found the child each season a little further from what he had expected and a little nearer to something he had no word for yet: quiet where Niall Òg's blood ran loud, careful where Ìomhar's ran quick to grievance, watching the fires of a hearth that was not his own the way a man watches a thing he has been told he will not keep for long, though no one, least of all the boy, had yet told him any such thing in so many words.

## II. Of the Father's Three Hundred and Eighth Year

Gille-Brìde's father died at Samhain in the year four thousand five hundred and ninety-nine, in his three hundred and eighth year, which was fewer years than his own father had had, and far fewer than the six hundred his grandfather had counted, and fewer by a long way than the plain thousand-odd of the generations further back still, whose names Na Glinn Àrda kept only as a list recited at a fire and not as men anyone living had known. He died as most of that line had learned, by then, to die: without illness, without a wound, simply slower each year at rising from his own hearthstone until one morning he did not rise from it at all, and the household found him sitting upright with his eyes shut and his hands folded as though he had meant, at the last, to make as little trouble of the thing as a man could.

Gille-Brìde stood at the burying, and it was there, standing over the turned earth with Ceallach a solemn boy of six-and-fifty years holding his hand, that he first heard the word going about the fires that the kin of the king's own blood were, at long last, coming down out of their scattered holdings to gather in the valley below Dùn ìseal, each with tents and a fire of its own and a steward set over its own people, to wait there upon whatever the king should say of the shares that had gone uncounted since Catrìona Mhòr's death. It was said as news, and said, too, with the particular relish that news of the king's kin always drew from households that were not of it: that after so long a waiting, some matter or other was at last to be settled, or at least to be waited for in one place instead of many.

"You should go down and see it," said an old woman of the burying party, who had no love for the king's blood and every love for a spectacle involving it. "It will not come again in your lifetime, whatever it comes to."

"It may not come to anything," Gille-Brìde said, "in my lifetime or another's."

But he thought of it walking back up into Na Glinn Àrda with the boy's small hand still cold in his from the graveside, and he found that the old woman's word had lodged in him in a way her spectacle had not: not the gathering itself, which he had little enough curiosity for, but the plain, strange fact that after so many hundreds of years of a hill held quietly by one old man who asked nothing of anyone, the kin should choose, now, to come down and sit under it and wait.

It was in that same season, though the news of it was slower reaching Na Glinn Àrda than most news of the king's own household, that word came of another death nearer the vein than the hill: Fearghal Donn, who had held Dùn dhearg since before Niall Òg was sent to him with a message and sent back with nothing, died in his own bed at the fort in the year four thousand five hundred and seventy, an old man by the reckoning of the common blood though he had never claimed any other, and left the fort and its long unsettled quarrel with the hill to a son grown to manhood inside that quarrel and knowing, by his own later account, no time before it. The households east of the vein buried Fearghal Donn as they had buried Ailis two generations before him, without word sent to Dùn ìseal and without any expecting one to come back, and his son Artair took up the holding of the fort in the same season with no more ceremony than his father had used to take it up before him, and was heard to say, at the burying itself, that he meant to hold what his father had held and ask nothing further of it until the day the hill asked something of him first.

## III. Of the Tents and the Steward to Each Line

They began coming down in the summer of the year four thousand six hundred and one, household by household and line by line, no single word sent out to call any of them, each deciding on its own that the waiting had gone on long enough to be done nearer the hill than far from it. Murchadh Mòr's people came from the northern plain in a column that took the better part of three days to pass any one point on the road, eleven sons' worth of grandsons and their own grown children driving cattle before them and carrying, some said, more tent-cloth on their backs than the valley below Dùn ìseal had ever seen brought to one place at once. Eithne's people came from the plain below Làrach an Dùin-fhaire with less show and no less certainty, her own knot-keeper walking at the head of them with the household's cord wound about his arm as though he meant to be seen keeping count of every soul who set foot on the new ground, which he did, from the first day to the last. Ìomhar Dearg came from the eastern marches with his own household and a grievance he had been nursing, by then, for the better part of a hundred years, over grazing along the river that he judged his by use if not yet by any word of the king's, and he pitched his tents nearer the vein than any other line thought wise, which was, everyone agreed, exactly like him.

Niall Òg came laughing, as he had come laughing to every gathering of his life, claiming through both his mother's blood and his father's a share no one else troubled to dispute with him in the open, though more than one household said behind its own hearth-smoke that a claim made through two lines at once was, by the same reckoning, no more than half a claim doubled, and worth exactly what a claim through one line was worth and not a grain more. An old white-haired daughter of the king's own body came too, older than Eithne by some centuries, and pitched her tent apart from the rest with a small household of her own, and was seen at no fire but her own for the better part of that first summer, which some read as grief for shares long since given and taken back, and others read as nothing more than an old woman's wish to be let alone in her own tent for once in a very long life of not being let alone in it.

And Sìne nan Snaidhm came down from the hill as the household's own knot-keeper, claiming no ground of her own, carrying Sreang nan Teallach that had counted every soul under the king's roof for longer than any single line's memory reached, and she walked the new valley in its first weeks the way she had always walked the hill, checking each new tent against the cord in her hands and each steward's tally against her own, so that within a season there was not a fire pitched in that valley, of the king's blood or of any household following it down, whose count she did not keep somewhere on the knots she carried.

A steward stood over every line by the second summer, a man or a woman set by each household, holding no ground the king had given and holding no lordship over any but its own, only charged to speak for it at the common fires and see that its own people did not go hungry or unhoused while the waiting went on, which by then had gone on long enough that the valley below Dùn ìseal had come to look, from any rise above it, less like a gathering that would soon disperse and more like a town that had simply forgotten, or declined, to raise walls around its own fires.

## IV. Of Gille-Brìde Come Down

Gille-Brìde came down himself in the last summer of it, the year four thousand six hundred and three, more out of the same plain curiosity the old woman at the burying had first put in him than out of any claim of his own, for he had none, and wanted none. He walked the two days out of Na Glinn Àrda with Ceallach beside him, a young man now of sixty years and already, though neither of them said it aloud, walking a little more slowly than a man of sixty of the older reckoning ought to have walked, and they came down over the last rise together at evening and stood a while looking at what the valley below Dùn ìseal had become.

He had not seen a gathering of that size in his life, and said so. Fires ran the whole length of the valley floor as far as the last light let him see, more of them than he could have counted in an evening's standing, and the smoke of them lay along the ground in the still air the way mist lay along a river at dawn, so that the tents seemed to rise straight out of it rather than to stand upon the grass. He found Murchadh Mòr first, an old grey giant of a man surrounded by sons and grandsons who all shared his own build about the shoulders, holding forth to a knot of them about grazing rights on the northern plain in a voice that carried the length of three tents in either direction without troubling to be raised for the purpose. He found Eithne after him, seated outside her own tent with her knot-keeper beside her going over the day's tally by firelight, and she greeted Gille-Brìde with the particular courtesy of a woman who had long since stopped expecting to be surprised by anything a gathering of her own kin might produce.

Ìomhar Dearg he found arguing, as he had somehow known before he found him that he would be arguing, with a steward of one of the common households over the exact reach of the river-grazing he claimed, a dispute that had, by the sound of it, been running since the first week of the gathering and showed no sign of any conclusion either man expected to reach that night or any other.

"It was given me by the king himself, six hundred years since," Ìomhar was saying, loud enough that half the fires nearest his own could follow the argument without troubling to walk to it, "and I have grazed it every year since without asking leave of any steward set over any household that came to this valley after I did."

"It was given you the near bank," the steward answered him, patient in the particular way of a man who has said the same thing many times before and expects to say it many times again, "and you graze the far one besides, and call the difference nothing because no one has yet troubled to measure it."

"Then measure it," said Ìomhar, "and we will see whose nothing it is." Neither man moved to fetch a rope or a rod to do any such measuring, and Gille-Brìde, passing on beyond the firelight, judged that neither man expected the other to, and that the dispute itself, rather than its settling, was what each of them had come down into the valley to keep alive.

Niall Òg he found laughing at someone else's fire, as ever, telling some tale of Rathad an t-Salainn to a ring of younger cousins who had not heard it before and would, by the look of them, be glad enough to hear it again; and when he saw Gille-Brìde standing at the fire's edge he broke off long enough to call across to him, cheerfully, that a man of Somhairle's line who came down into the valley with no claim to press was either the wisest soul in it or the only fool who had not yet worked out what he was owed, and that he, Niall, had not yet decided which, and would let Gille-Brìde know when he had. Gille-Brìde said only that he was content to be thought either, so long as no one troubled to settle the question that same evening, which made Niall Òg laugh again and turn back to his cousins and his tale.

The old white-haired daughter he saw once at a distance, sitting alone outside her own tent with her hands folded, and did not go to her, judging rightly, he thought, that she wanted no company that evening and would not thank a near-stranger for offering it.

It was Sìne nan Snaidhm who found him in the end, coming along the fires with her cord wound about her wrist as she went, and she stopped when she saw him and looked at him a long moment before she spoke, as a woman does who is putting a face to a name she has heard and not yet seen.

"You are of Somhairle's line," she said. "The one who keeps to Na Glinn Àrda."

"I am," he said.

"Your name is not on any cord I carry," she said, not unkindly, "because you have asked for no ground and no share, in all these years, from anyone who might have given you one."

"I have not," he agreed.

"Then you are the only soul in this valley tonight of whom that can be said," she told him, and went on with her counting, leaving him to think, as he stood there among the fires of a gathering he had wanted only to see, that she had not meant it as praise and had not meant it as blame either, only as the plainest fact she knew about him, set down the way she set down every other fact that came to her along the cord.

Word ran through the valley that same week, from fire to fire faster than any steward could carry it formally, that Sluagh an Ear had begun to move. It had been gathering at Dùn dhearg, by all the telling that reached the western fires, since the spring of that year, under Artair, the same son of Fearghal Donn who had taken up his father's holding thirty years and more before with the plain word that he would ask nothing of the hill until the hill asked something of him; and it was said, by those who claimed to know his mind and by those who only claimed to know his household, that the hill's own moving that summer, the king going down at last among his people after so long a stillness, was the very thing Artair had told himself, thirty years before, that he was waiting for. What Sluagh an Ear meant to do once it had gathered, no one in the valley below Dùn ìseal could say with any certainty, and the tellers who claimed to know said different things depending on which fire they sat at, so that by midsummer there were as many accounts of the east's intent going about the valley as there were fires to tell them at, and most of the households simply went on with their own waiting and let the accounts settle themselves, or fail to, as they would.

## V. Of the King's Answer

It was the king himself who settled the question of his own household's part in it, and he settled it, as he had settled most things in his very long life, without asking anyone's leave to do so and without troubling to explain himself past the plain fact of the doing. He came down from Dùn ìseal on a morning near the end of that same summer with a handful of his own people about him, no more than would fill three tents, and word went round the valley before he had gone a mile that the king was going to meet the east himself, and that every line camped along that valley floor meant, upon hearing it, to follow him within a day or two, once tents were struck and stewards set to keep the ground, once this or that last matter of a household's own was seen to.

Gille-Brìde stood at the edge of Murchadh Mòr's fire that morning and watched the king go down the valley on foot among his handful of household, a grey-headed man with a young man's face, walking at a pace that did not look, from where Gille-Brìde stood, like the pace of a man who expected to be followed soon or gladly. He went to the king before he had gone far, not knowing himself quite why he did it until he had already done it, and asked him plainly whether he, too, should come down to the ford with the rest.

The king stopped and looked at him a while, the way a man looks at something he has not troubled to look at closely before and finds, on the looking, not unfamiliar after all.

"You are Somhairle's," he said. It was not a question either.

"I am what is left of that line that still walks Na Glinn Àrda," Gille-Brìde said, "and asks nothing of anyone."

"Then go home to them," said the king. "Whatever comes of this, it will want telling afterward by someone who was not in it, and your people have always been better at the telling than the coming."

Gille-Brìde said nothing more to that, because there was nothing in it that asked for an answer, and the king went on down the valley among his handful of household, and did not look back, and every line behind him along that valley floor went on saying, at its own fire and in its own time, that it would follow him in a day or two, as each of them had been saying since the news of the east's moving first ran the length of the tents.

Murchadh Mòr said it that same evening, standing at the edge of his own fire with a grandson on either side of him, that he would bring his own people down within the week once the cattle were seen to; and Eithne said it more quietly to her knot-keeper, who set it down on the cord as though a word of intent were as good, for her purposes, as a soul already counted; and Ìomhar Dearg said it loudest of all, to no one steward in particular, that he would follow the king to the ford himself and see the matter of the river-grazing settled once and for all where it could be settled by more than argument, though which matter he meant by that, the grazing or the gathering of spears at Dùn dhearg, he did not trouble to say, and no one troubled to ask him. Niall Òg said it laughing, as he said everything, and the old white-haired daughter said nothing at all that anyone at any fire heard her say, and struck her own tent two days later to pitch it again in the upper camps, apart from the noise of the rest, and did not come down from them.

Sìne nan Snaidhm alone said nothing of following, because it was no part of her charge to follow the king anywhere; her charge was the cord, and the cord stayed at Dùn ìseal, in the hands of whichever household the king left behind him to keep the hill while he was gone, and she was seen, in the days after his going, walking the emptying rows of tents with the same care she had walked them full, as though a valley half-struck were no less hers to count than a valley pitched from end to end.

Gille-Brìde took the boy Ceallach by the arm that same afternoon and turned north, back up out of the valley of tents and its smoke lying low along the grass, toward Na Glinn Àrda where no ground was claimed and no share waited to be counted, and he did not look back either, though he thought, walking, that he would remember the smoke of that valley for whatever years were left to him, however many or few they proved to be against his father's three hundred and eight, and that someone, as the king had said, would have the telling of what came after, and that it would not, after all, be him who came to it, only him who walked away from where it was about to happen and carried the boy home.
