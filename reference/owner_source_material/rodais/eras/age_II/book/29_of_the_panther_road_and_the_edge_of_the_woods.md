# Of the Panther Road and the Edge of the Woods

*GE 2,428 – 2,460*

> *Nuair a philleas na cait, philleas an cuideachd leotha.*
> When the cats turn back, wise hands turn back with them.
> — said of the passes, after Iain Buachaille

## I. Of the Bone Pipes and the Turning of the Cats

At the southern skirt of Cnoc àrsaidh, where the flint road ran down off the high ground and lost itself among the last of the birch before the grazing opened out, there lived that year a man named Gilleonan, who had no craft the households about him could put a plainer name to than the making of small useful things out of whatever came to his hand, and who that winter came to hand on a heap of hollow bird-bones left over from a winter's snaring and took a notion, turning them over by his own fire of an evening, to bore holes along the length of the longest of them and see what came out of blowing across the end. What came out, after a week of ruined bone and burnt fingers and more silence than sound, was a thin high note that carried further than any voice raised across an open field, and by the second week he had bored four holes rather than one and could raise and lower the note as he pleased, and by the third the children of every household near enough to hear him practising had taken to gathering at his door of an evening the way children gather at any door where something new and strange is being made. No one before him is remembered to have made a thing on Dia-thìr that could be played rather than merely blown, and the pipes he cut that winter out of hollow bird-bone are the oldest instruments the island's tellers count, and Gilleonan, asked in his own age how he had come to think of it, would say only that a bone with a hole through it already wants to sing, and a man with enough patience and enough ruined bone will find where the rest of the holes belong.

It was in that same season, while Gilleonan's thin note was still new enough to draw children from three households at a call, that the word came down from the passes above Cnoc àrsaidh that the great cats were turning back on the panther road before ever they reached the foothills. Every year of any household's memory the cats had come down out of the high ground with the last hard frost of the spring, in ones and twos and sometimes in a string of them following the same worn track a day or two apart, crossing the passes on their own road as they had crossed them since before any teller's grandfather had words for a beginning, and going on south to whatever ground held them through the cold months, and every year the herders of the hill households that lay along that road kept their beasts well clear of it while the crossing lasted, for a cat on the move wants nothing of a man or his cattle and will do neither harm if neither stands in its way, but a cat turned or crowded is another matter, and no household along the panther road needed telling twice why the custom stood as it stood. That year the cats came as far as the narrow throat of the highest pass and no further, three of them seen by different watchers on three different days, each pausing at the same turn in the rock, each looking down the long fall of ground toward the foothills a while before it swung about and went back the way it had come, and by the fourth such turning the households nearest the pass had stopped waiting to see whether a fourth cat would finish the crossing and had begun instead to ask each other what a cat knows about a coming winter that a man does not.

The old answer, given by every old woman and old man who had outlived enough winters to be asked, was that a cat which will not cross into ground it has crossed every year of its own life is a cat that already knows the ground it is leaving is the kinder ground that year, and that a household which reads the turning aright and lays in double against a winter no cat will risk crossing into does better than a household that waits for its own eyes to be sure. The households along the panther road that year laid in double: double the dried fish, double the stored roots, double the cut peat and the split wood, working through the whole of that summer and autumn at a pace that left little time for anything but the storing, so that by the time the first true frost came down off the high ground behind the cats that had not come, every hearth within a day's walk of the pass had more laid by than it had ever laid by before against a winter that had not yet shown its own face. The winter that followed was as hard as any teller then living had words to measure it by, snow lying on ground that commonly saw none, and the burns running thin and slow under rims of frost, and it is told, though no single teller claims to have counted it exactly, that not one household along the panther road that had laid in double against the cats' own reading of the season went hungry before the thaw, while further east, where the cats' turning had been heard of only as a tale carried late and thin, more than one household came near the end of its own stores before the ground softened again.

## II. Of Iain Buachaille and the Cattle in the Passes

Not every household along the panther road heeded the cats that year, and the households of Muileann bhàn, a scatter of byres and huts set along the last good grazing before the ground climbed into the passes proper, were among those that did not, or that heeded too late to matter. The grazing about Muileann bhàn had run thin that spring before ever the cats were seen turning, the winter before having left the lower slopes bare and slow to green, and the herders there had already begun, before the first cat was so much as spoken of, to eye the high meadows inside the passes themselves, ground no household of that stretch had grazed within living memory because it lay along the cats' own road and the custom forbade it for the length of the crossing season, whatever grass stood ungrazed there while the custom held.

Among those herders was Iain, son of Iseabail of Muileann bhàn, three-and-twenty that spring and already known along that stretch of the road for a temper that took slights to the household's grazing harder than most men took slights to themselves, a fault his own mother had despaired of curing in him and had mostly stopped trying to. When word came down that the cats had turned back a second time at the throat of the pass, Iain heard in it a chance too good to waste, for a road the cats have given up crossing, he said to whoever would listen at his own hearth and at two or three neighbouring ones, is a road with nothing left on it to fear, and grass standing ungrazed a day's drive above Muileann bhàn while his own household's beasts grew thin below was a waste no custom, in his reckoning, had any business asking a hungry household to keep. His mother told him plainly that a custom kept by every household along that road since before her own grandmother's day had not likely been kept so long for no reason at all, and that three turnings of the cats in one season meant, to her mind, a road with a fourth turning still owed it, not a road with nothing left on it to fear. Iain heard her out, as a son will hear out a mother he does not mean to obey, and two mornings after went up into the passes with four other men of the household and near forty head of the household's thinnest cattle, meaning to graze them a week on the high meadows and bring them down fattened before the cats' season was out.

They had the cattle up and grazing by the second day, on grass richer than any of the five men had seen standing anywhere below the passes that whole dry summer, and for three days more nothing troubled them but the cold, which came down harder each night than the last, and the wind off the high rock, which none of them had properly dressed against, having gone up in the light gear of men driving cattle a short way rather than men meaning to camp a week among stone. It was on the fourth night, with the fire burned low and two of the five men asleep and Iain himself keeping the first watch more from restlessness than any sense that watching was needed, that the panthers came down on the herd, a whole string of them together, the last and largest cats of that year's crossing, driven onto the road at last, the tellers say afterward, by a hunger of their own that the turning back had only put off and not ended. Iain heard the cattle before he saw anything, a sound no herder mistakes for any other sound cattle make, and had the other four on their feet and the fire built high before the first cat broke into the firelight after a beast at the edge of the huddled herd, and what followed, by Iain's own telling of it afterward, he could give no clean account of, for a night fight with cats among cattle in the dark leaves a man with pieces of what happened and no whole cloth to lay them in: a beast down and screaming, a shape low and fast crossing the firelight, his own spear thrown and lost, and then the ground going out from under him as he threw himself sideways from a claw he never afterward could say he truly saw coming, his leg twisting under him against a stone he had not known was there, and a white pain up through the hip that put him on the ground for the rest of that night no matter how the fight round him went.

By first light the cats were gone, south along their own road as though the night's killing had settled whatever debt they had come down to collect, and the five men counted, in the grey after, thirty head of the household's forty taken or scattered beyond finding, and Iain unable to stand on the leg that had gone from under him in the dark. They carried him down off the high ground on a hide slung between two spear-shafts, the other three driving what was left of the herd before them, and it was three days before any of the five was fit to speak much of what the night had cost, and longer than that before Iain's leg, set as well as the household's own healing hand could set it, showed any household at Muileann bhàn what it had truly cost him: a leg that healed crooked at the hip no matter how it was bound, that shortened against its fellow by the width of two fingers by the time the swelling was fully down, and that left him, from that winter on, a man who walked with a stick and a roll to one side for every step he took, in a household of herders where a man's legs are as much his living as his hands. His mother said nothing to him of the custom he had broken, then or ever after that she is remembered to have said, and Iain himself said little of that night to anyone for a long while, beyond the plain account any household along that road would ask of a man who had lost it thirty head, and it was left to the road, over the seasons that followed, to turn what had happened to him into the shorter saying it kept: that the cats had turned back three times that spring to tell every household along the road what one household had not troubled to hear the first time.

## III. Of the Gatepost Sign and the Boats Named for Wind

It was in the years just after, while Iain was learning to make a lame man's work of a whole man's trade, that the custom the households of Muileann bhàn had broken began, further south, to be kept in a new way that no household along the panther road had thought to keep it before. At Baile dhomhain, where the grazing lay well clear of the passes but where the crossing season still shaped the year as surely as it shaped it anywhere the cats' own road ran, the households took to cutting a mark into the gatepost of every byre and yard at the season's start, a short straight cut no deeper than a knife needs to make its point, and scraping it away again at the season's close, so that a man riding through Baile dhomhain in the crossing months could read at a glance, gate by gate, which households stood mindful of the season and which did not trouble, and a household whose gatepost went uncut two years running found itself, without any word said outright, asked fewer favours and lent fewer beasts than a household that kept the mark faithfully. No household before that is remembered to have cut a sign for a season rather than for an event, a birth or a death or a boundary settled, and the mark at Baile dhomhain, plain as it was, is counted the first the island kept for nothing more than the turning of the year.

Further north, where the coast ran along the mouth of the river the fishing households called simply the blue water, the households of Caol bheag came in those same years to a custom of their own that answered no cat's road but the same restless weather that governed every boat on that stretch of coast. Each household there had long since taken to naming its boats, as households everywhere name the things they build with their own hands and trust their lives to, but it was at Caol bheag that the naming first fixed on the winds a boat was built and blessed to fish in, so that a boat built for the wind out of the north bore a north wind's name and was held unlucky if put out under any other, and a boat that went down under its own true wind was never, by the custom that grew up there, given its name again, for a name once drowned was held to carry the drowning with it into whatever boat next wore it. The fishing households up and down that coast came in time to know each other's boats as much by name as by build, and a man newly wed into a household there learned the winds' names for boats before he learned much else of his wife's own kin, for a boat wrongly named, in the reckoning of that coast, was a debt a household paid for sooner or later, whatever the weather did or did not do to collect it.

## IV. Of the Wind-Stone and the Broken Grave

At Seann Chwen, midway along the same stretch of high ground the panther road crossed, there stood, older than any household near it could put a beginning to, a low grey boulder cut about with marks no living hand had made and no living eye could read whole, the same close, careful, patient marks the Old Ones had left on stones the length of the island wherever the ground stood high enough and bare enough to take a chisel, and it was on that stone, in the years after Iain's leg had set crooked, that a cutter of that household first set one of the eight winds' names beside the older marks, in the plainer, broader cuts the hill-folk's own chisels made, so that the boulder came to carry, on the one face, both a telling no living hand could read and a telling any household passing could read at a glance. Few stones on the island came to bear the marks of both peoples so close together, for the households that raised their own signs commonly chose ground the Old Ones had left bare, out of a wariness that had less to do with any harm believed to lie in the older marks than with a plain unwillingness to set a new thing hard against an old one nobody living could account for; the cutter at Seann Chwen, asked why he had chosen that stone over any bare boulder near it, said only that the wind-name wanted a stone already old, and the oldest stone he knew stood already there.

A day's walk south of Seann Chwen lay the old burial ground the households of that country had kept, in one fashion or another, longer than any single line among them could claim to have kept it alone, a wide low field thick with thorn and bramble where the dead of a dozen households lay under cairns of every age from freshly turned to grass-grown past finding, and it was in those same years that a family newly settled nearby, kin to none of the households that already had dead lying there, took on the clearing of the thorn from the older rows, cutting it back each spring so the cairns beneath could still be found and the paths between them still be walked, though they laid none of their own dead among the ones they tended, having none yet to lay, and meant, by their own account, only to keep a ground that plainly wanted keeping and had no one household whose plain business it was. They kept it faithfully enough that within a few years no household of that country thought to clear the thorn any longer, trusting the keeping to the family that had taken it up unasked, and it was diggers of that same family, cutting a new row further back than any row before it had reached, who broke one spring into a grave older than any they had themselves opened, lined on every side with cut slabs set close and true in a manner no household of that country built its own graves in, a manner the diggers knew at once, without needing to be told, for the manner of the Old Ones. They closed what they had opened as carefully as they had opened it, moving the new row a spear's length further off rather than cut any nearer, and no digger of that ground, then or after, is remembered to have tried that older grave a second time, the family's own reckoning being, as the eldest of them put it, that a ground kept for the dead does not want its keepers asking after graves it was never given to keep.

## V. Of the Pilgrims and the Child Lost at Cnoc bheag

East of Seann Chwen, where the ground fell away into woodland thick enough that no household had yet cleared more than its own edge, there stood a stretch of forest the hill-folk had long held sacred without any household near it being able to say plainly when the holding had begun or why that wood rather than any other had been set apart from the ordinary clearing and grazing every other stretch of woodland on the island was put to sooner or later. In the years after the wind-stone was cut at Seann Chwen, people from households that had never before troubled to walk so far began coming to the edge of that wood in small numbers, singly and in pairs, to sit through a single night at the line where the trees began without ever passing in among them, keeping a small fire that burned itself out before morning and going home again at first light with nothing to show for the walk but whatever the night had given them to carry back. No household could say who had begun the walking, nor what any single pilgrim hoped to find at the wood's edge that could not as well have been sought at his own hearth, and the pilgrims themselves, asked, gave answers as various as the households they came from, though most agreed on this much: that a night spent at the edge of that wood, awake and watchful and asking nothing of it, left a man quieter than the same night spent anywhere else was likely to leave him.

Further east again, past a stretch of country still mostly given over to grazing, lay another wood held sacred in the same fashion and for the same unremembered reasons, and it was at the edge of that wood, some years after the pilgrims had begun sitting at the first, that settlers newly come down from the Red Hill raised the earliest house of what would grow, in the years that followed, into a small settlement of its own at Cnoc bheag, taking their timber, as the custom of that country already held for ground so near a sacred wood, only from the far side of the river that ran between the settlement and the trees, and never from the unclaimed wood, however near and however tempting its unfelled timber stood. The settlement grew slowly, a house and then two and then a handful more over several years, each household keeping, without needing to be told twice, the same line the first house had kept between its own clearing and the trees, a line marked at last, once enough households stood there to agree on marking it, by a row of unhewn stones set at the wood's edge from the river to the higher ground, a line no child of that settlement was raised without being told, before it was told much else, never to cross.

It was past that line, in the year the settlement had grown to six houses, that a child went missing, a boy not yet seven summers old, last seen by his own mother at play near the stones and next seen by no one at all, though the whole settlement and every household within reach of the news spent three days searching the wood as far in as any of them dared go, calling his name through undergrowth thick enough that a man three strides off could not always be seen, let alone a boy small enough to have gone to ground under any fallen branch or into any hollow the wood offered him. Iain, near six-and-forty that year and walking still with the stick and the roll to one side that the panthers had left him three-and-twenty years before, was among the searchers who came from Muileann bhàn and the country about it to help hunt a wood he had never before had cause to enter, a lame man's search being slower and shorter-ranged than a whole man's but no less willing for that, and it was Iain, by more than one account, who first said aloud what every searcher among them had by the third day begun privately to think, that a wood no household of that country would fell timber from nor build a house nearer than the far bank of its own river was not, whatever the boy's own mother might in her grief still hope, a wood that gave back what it had taken, and that a search which had by then gone three days past the line of stones without one sign of the child was a search that had done, and would go on doing, everything a search could rightly be asked to do. The searchers stopped at the line of stones on the evening of the third day, no one among them commanding it, each in his own time and by his own reckoning coming to the same stopping as Iain had spoken first, and the boy was never found, in that wood or any other, and no teller of that country, then or after, ever laid the stopping at the searchers' own door, for a wood held apart from every other wood on the island for reasons no living household could name was, in the plain reckoning of that country, no wood a man could be blamed for failing to bring a child safely out of.

## VI. Of the Warm Springs

Iain carried the leg the panthers had left him for seven-and-twenty years after that first night in the passes, through the raising of his own household and the burying of his mother and the long slow work of teaching younger men of Muileann bhàn a herder's trade he could no longer himself practise at more than half a whole man's pace, and in all that time he tried, as any man in his place tries, every cure his own household and every neighbouring one had ever heard spoken of: bindings of every plant the hill country grew that anyone claimed had ever eased a joint, a winter spent wrapping the leg each night in hide soaked warm from the fire, a season's walking on a crutch cut shorter than his stick to see whether the leg, forced to bear more of its own weight, might in time straighten, which it did not. By the year the boy was lost at Cnoc bheag he had stopped, by his own later account, expecting any cure at all, and had come to think of the leg the way a man comes in time to think of any hurt that has outlasted every hope of mending it, as simply the form his own life had settled into and would go on wearing until it wore him out.

It was four years after the search at Cnoc bheag, in a spring when the leg had begun to pain him worse than it had in a long while, the cold and the wet of that season finding every old crookedness in it that fair weather let him mostly forget, that a neighbour of his, back from a journey south for reasons of the neighbour's own, told him of a warm water that rose from under the mountain a long way off, ground his own kin had once passed through and camped a night beside, water that came up out of the rock already heated and pooled shallow enough for a man to sit in it, and that more than one traveller resting there had come away saying the water had eased an old hurt no ordinary washing ever touched. Iain heard the telling the way he had by then learned to hear most tellings of cures he had not himself tried, without much hope and without much unwillingness either, and said nothing of going for the better part of that year, until the leg's own pain, worse that spring than any spring before it, gave him less to lose by the walking than he had ever before reckoned he had.

He went that same summer, on the stick he had carried seven-and-twenty years, a journey of many days for a whole man and a great many more for a man who could put no real weight on one leg, staying at whatever households along the way would have a lame stranger by their fire a night, and came at last to the warm water on an evening when a handful of other travellers were already camped about it, some there, as he learned by their own telling round the shared fire that night, for the same hope that had brought him and some merely to rest a night on a long road elsewhere. At first light the next morning, before the others were properly stirring, he eased himself down into the water onto a shelf of rock the water covered to the waist, and sat there a long while, by his own account longer than he had meant to, the heat working into the crooked hip in a way no binding or wrapping he had ever tried had come near to matching, and when at last he rose to climb out he found, standing on the rock in the cool morning air with the water running off him, that the leg under him held its own weight in a way it had not held it in seven-and-twenty years, not straightened, for no water on the island could undo what a night of panthers had done to the bone, but eased past whatever had kept it locked and grinding at every step, so that he walked the length of the pool's edge and back again without once reaching for the stick he had set down on the rock beside him.

He stayed three days more at the warm water, going into it each morning and each evening, and walked home to Muileann bhàn, by every teller's account of it since, on his own two legs and without the stick he had carried south, a journey that took him near half the days the same road had taken him going, a lame man's walk cut down, near enough, to a whole man's pace. His own household, seeing him come up the last rise toward the byres without the roll to one side they had watched him walk with for seven-and-twenty years, took him at first, more than one of them said after, for some other traveller entirely, a man of Iain's own build and no more, until he was near enough to call his own name across the yard and be known by his own mother's grandchildren, who had never in their own short lives seen him walk any other way. It is that walking, and not the water, that the tellers of that country kept as the whole of what happened at the warm springs that year, a herder who came to them lame and walked home from them whole, and it stands, of every cure any teller of Dia-thìr has since claimed for that water, the oldest and the first, told wherever the springs themselves are told of, in words that have changed little from the plain shape Iain's own household first gave them: that he came lame, and he walked home.

## VII. Of the Hollow Man and the Covered Hearth

In the years just after, two herders of a household near the hills where the Hollow Man was oldest told of, driving cattle home later than they had meant to and caught by full dark still some way off, saw a shape walk the ridge above them with no breath showing in the bitter cold, though every breath of their own hung white before their faces the whole way down, and neither of the two, by their own telling after, could say the shape had done anything at all beyond the walking, no word spoken, no hand raised, nothing but a going along the ridge line at a man's own ordinary pace until the dark or the ground took it out of their sight. They had lit no fire that evening, having pressed on for home rather than camp so near those hills after dark, and it was that, the telling holds, and no virtue of their own, that let them come home at all to tell of it, for every older telling of that shape agreed on the one plain rule any household near those hills kept without needing telling twice: that a fire lit on those slopes after dark was a fire the Hollow Man came to, sooner or later, and a household that lit one did not always sit by it undisturbed until morning.

It was in those same years, spreading out from those same hills toward the settlements that lay nearer the eastern woods, that a new manner of hearth began to be built in household after household, a fire pit dug below the level of the floor and roofed over with a low stone lid that could be drawn across at need, so that a household could bank its coals for the night and cover them close, leaving no open flame nor rising smoke for anything abroad in the dark to be drawn toward, and wake in the morning to coals still live enough to be raked open and blown to flame again with no fresh striking of fire needed. The covered hearth spread faster among the households nearest those hills than anywhere else on the island, and it was there, before it was anywhere, that the custom hardened at last into something near a law that every household on those slopes kept whether or not it had ever seen anything walk a ridge in the dark: that no open fire was lit there after nightfall, coal-banked or covered or none at all, a rule no council had made and no holder had set his hand to, kept simply because every household near enough to those hills to be asked had, sooner or later, heard from some neighbour's own mouth a telling near enough to the two herders' to want no fire of its own left open to whatever the telling was a telling of.

Iain Buachaille lived on at Muileann bhàn many years after his own walk home from the warm water, on legs that carried him, to the end of his days, further and more evenly than any household there had thought, in the long years of his lameness, he would ever walk again, and it was said of him along that stretch of the panther road, for as long as any household there kept the saying, that he alone among the men who had broken the custom that hard year had lived to be mended of what the breaking cost him, though no teller of that country ever claimed the mending answered the breaking, nor said the cats had given him back what they had taken, for a leg does not forget, whatever ease the warm water worked into it, the night it was first twisted under a man in the dark, and Iain himself, asked in his own old age whether he still felt the passes in bad weather, would say only that he did, and that a man who has walked home once from a hurt he had stopped hoping to be rid of does not, after that, trouble much over what a little weather still asks of him.
