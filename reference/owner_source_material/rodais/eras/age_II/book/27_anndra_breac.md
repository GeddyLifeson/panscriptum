# Of Anndra Breac

*GE 2,421 – 2,423*

> *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.*
> We did not come; we were here.
>
> — cut on Calbh Cnoc ghorm, and spoken at the ford from the year after Anndra Breac

## I. Of the Watch at Seann Skell

Four men kept the tower at Seann Skell that autumn, as four had kept it every autumn since the hill-folk first put a roof back on Seann-Dhaoine' old watch-stones and set a household there to mind the western water: the island had no enemy worth the name; a coast that watches its own sea is a coast that is never taken wholly by surprise, and the hill-folk had learned that much from every people whose ruin they had walked through on the way west. The tower stood at the northern shoulder of the bay, round-walled in stone older than any teller could put an age to, and from its top a watcher could see the whole reach of water from the last rocks of the point to the grey line where the sea gave up and the sky began.

It was Tormod Chaol who saw it first, in the last light of a day that had already turned the water the colour of a knife, and he did not at first believe what his own eyes told him, for a man does not, seeing such a thing for the first time, trust himself over the water he has watched every evening of his working life without seeing anything stranger than a whale or a drifting spar. What rose off the point, a long way out and low against the light, was a back, dark and wet and shining, longer by half than any whale he had ever helped haul ashore or heard sung of by men who had, and it came up slow, the way a thing comes up that has all the time in the world and no fear of anything the shore can do to it, and went under again without breaking the water into foam, as a stone goes under that is let down rather than thrown.

He called the other three up from the fire below, and by the time they had climbed the narrow stair and crowded the parapet the water had gone flat and grey and empty again, giving nothing back but the ordinary chop of an ordinary evening, and for a moment each of the four half hoped, in the private place a man keeps such hopes, that he alone had seen nothing and the others would say so. None of them said so. Each had seen enough, in the last of the light, to swear afterward to a length, a colour, a manner of going under, and no two of their four tellings agreed in every particular, as no two tellings of a fast and half-seen thing ever do; but all four agreed on the two matters that mattered, that the back was long as three boats laid end to end, and that whatever wore it was not a whale, for a whale blows before it goes down and this had made no sound at all, only the low hiss of water closing over something that did not choose to be heard.

Word of it went down to the households below before the four had so much as come down from the tower, carried by the youngest of them, who could not keep it to himself the length of the stair, and by the time the tale had gone twice round the bay it had grown a head and a neck that none of the four had sworn to and lost nothing of the length they had. The old women of Seann Skell, who kept between them more tellings of the western water than any four men had years to have heard, said only that the sea had shown itself before to watchers who had the luck or the misfortune to be looking at the right hour, and would show itself again to others after these four were dust, and that a wise household neither doubted the watchers nor put out a boat that evening on the strength of the doubting. It is the first sighting the tellers ever tied to a place a man could point to and a count of men who could be asked, and it went into no telling grander than the bay's own, until a man who had never climbed that tower came to hear of it the following spring, at a gathering fifty miles from any water Tormod Chaol had ever wetted his feet in.

Tormod Chaol himself kept, for the rest of that autumn, a shorter watch and a longer silence than the other three, climbing the tower each evening at the hour the light had caught the thing and standing at the parapet a while after his turn was properly done, though he never again saw anything the water gave back but the ordinary chop it had always shown him before that one evening. Asked, in his own old age, whether he wished he had never climbed the stair that night, he would say only that a man who has seen a thing the sea does not commonly show cannot afterward unsee it, and that he did not know whether that was a burden or a gift, and had stopped trying to decide which.

## II. Of the Planks on the Western Sand

It was that same season, before the tale of the tower had properly finished going round the bay, that the wrack along the coast south of Seann Skell began to give up planks the like of which no household there had cause to build or buy. They were narrow, shaped with a care no coracle or hide-boat of the island's own building troubled to spend on a plank that would be under water half its life anyway, and stitched rather than pegged, sinew or cord drawn through holes bored close along each edge in a line as even as a weaver's selvage, so that a length of two boards lashed together lay along the sand like something sewn rather than built. No two pieces that washed in over that season matched exactly, as though the sea had broken up more than one hull and scattered the wreckage of each the length of the shore before any of it came ashore, and no household along that coast, asked afterward, could say it had lost a boat of its own building answering to any of the pieces, nor lacked a man, woman or child it could not account for among its own dead and living both.

That last was the part the coast turned over longest, more even than the planks themselves. A wreck without a body is not, on that coast or any other the tellers knew of, an ordinary wreck; the sea that breaks a boat commonly keeps something of what was riding in it, a length of rope still knotted to a thwart, an oar with a hand's wear worn into the grip, or, in the worst weathers, worse than an oar. What came ashore that season was wood alone, stitched wood broken small, and not one scrap of anything that had ridden in the wood, no cord that was not ship's cord, no tool, no bone, nothing a household could grieve over or bury, only planks enough, gathered and laid out along one low wall by a fisherman with more curiosity than most, to account for at least two hulls and perhaps three, and no drowned man's face to put to any of them.

The fisherman's name was Micheil Beag, a name half the coast bore by then, and it was he who first thought to walk the whole stretch of shore a second time with the planks already laid out, matching what he found against what he already had, the way a man pieces a broken pot back together to see what shape it kept before it broke. He got, by the end of that walking, two lengths of hull curved enough to show him the shape of the boats they had come from, narrower and longer in the beam than anything built on that coast, built for open water rather than the close fishing the coast's own boats were cut for, and he said so to anyone who would listen, at the tower, at the shore households, and later, because he was a man who liked an audience more than most, to a teller who had come that way with nothing better to do that season than listen to whatever the western coast had to tell him.

The teller's name was Anndra Breac, and he listened to Micheil Beag's planks the way he had, by then, taken to listening to everything that coast had to offer him, turning it over for longer than the matter seemed at first to deserve.

## III. Of Anndra Breac, Who Made a Telling

Anndra Breac was three-and-forty that year, a teller by trade in the loose way the word was used on that coast, meaning a man who carried news and old tales both from household to household in exchange for his supper and a place by the fire, welcome everywhere and rooted nowhere, with no wife the tellings ever named and no household of his own beyond whichever one had last fed him. He had walked the western shore, on and off, for the better part of twenty years, and knew its households, its old wrecks, its half-forgotten burial mounds and its Seann-Dhaoine' ruins better than most men born to any one of them, for a man who belongs everywhere a little comes in time to know a coast the way its own people, each rooted to their one stretch of it, rarely trouble to learn the whole.

He had heard, over those twenty years, a great many tellings of where the hill-folk had come from, most of them agreeing on nothing beyond the plain fact that Ailean Mòr had walked out of a grove at Doire ghlas with a warmth in him no man before or since had carried, and had gone south to take a hill, and that everything else the coast's own households believed of their own beginnings before that grove was a scatter of half-tales that no two households told alike: that the hill-folk had always been on the island, as the elders of Cnoc ghorm held and had carved into a stone before any teller now living had learned his trade; that they had come up out of the bare ground in the first spring after the world was made; that they had walked dry-shod over a sea-floor since drowned, from a shore no one now living had seen. Anndra had carried all three tellings in his own head at different times without troubling to choose between them, the way a man carries several coins of different mintings without asking which is truest, for a teller's business, as he understood it, was to carry a tale, not to settle it.

What the planks on Micheil Beag's wall did, over the weeks Anndra spent going back to look at them, was give him a fourth telling he had never carried before, and one he found, turning it over by whatever fires would have him that autumn, that he could not afterward put down. A boat, he came to think, watching the low light catch the stitched seams Micheil Beag had laid out for him a third and a fourth time, is not a thing a people builds to walk out of the ground with, nor a thing a drowned road leaves behind it. A boat is a thing built by hands that already knew water, built to be lost on water, and two or three boats broken small enough to leave nothing whole but their planking, with not one body to grieve, argued to his mind a crossing rather than a wreck close to shore, made once, long before any telling now kept remembered it being made, over water wide enough that whatever the sea did to the hulls and the people both, it kept what it took and gave back only wood.

He did not, by his own account afterward, come to the thought all at once, nor with any confidence he was right; he came to it slowly, the way a man comes to trust a bridge he has crossed several times without it giving way, and he tried it out first on Micheil Beag himself, who heard it out, turned it over as long as Anndra had, and said at the end of it only that he did not know, which was, from a man who knew that shore's wrecks better than any other living soul, as near to agreement as Anndra was going to get from anyone that season. He tried it next on two or three households up and down the coast who had known him twenty years and would hear him out on the strength of that alone, and got from most of them the same careful not-knowing, and from one old woman of a household near Seann Skell, who had heard every telling the coast possessed and outlived most of the tellers who carried them, a long look and the single remark that a tale a man cannot leave alone is a tale worth telling somewhere it will be properly heard, whatever comes of the telling.

One household alone turned him from its door over the telling before he had carried it further than the shore, a fisherman near Caol bheag who kept the old carving of Cnoc ghorm's saying cut small into his own lintel, as a few households of that coast did, and who told Anndra plainly that a man who came to his hearth to say the hill-folk had crossed a sea from somewhere was a man who might as well tell him his own father was a stranger, and would find no meal and no bed under that roof for the saying. Anndra took the door shutting on him without argument, as he had, by his own account, taken every door shut on him in twenty years of walking for one telling or another, and thought no more of it than he thought of a wet night with no roof at all, until the same closing came at him from every door at once the following spring.

It was the watchers' back in the western water, more than the planks, that gave Anndra the shape the telling finally took, for a coast that had just seen, with its own four pairs of eyes, a thing long enough and strange enough to argue for a sea with room in it for things no teller had words ready for, was a coast that would hear a tale of a long crossing without laughing him off its threshold before he had finished the first sentence of it; and by the turn of that year Anndra Breac had a telling whole in his head, worked and reworked by a dozen firesides until the words came to him without his troubling to reach for them, in which the hill-folk had not always stood on the island nor come up out of its ground nor walked over a drowned sea-floor, but had crossed the sea in boats, once, out of some shore no living telling named, and had lost more of themselves to the crossing than any telling now kept remembered losing, and had come ashore few and had grown many.

## IV. Of the Ford

He carried the telling east that spring, the length of the island, to the yearly gathering at the ford, where the hill's own holder and An Cnoc Ruadh's met each year to settle what wanted settling between the two, and where, over the generations since Raghnall Ceannfhionn first waded that water to talk salt for wool, every kind of household on the island had come to bring its own business, its own goods, and, more than either, its own news, so that a teller with a telling worth hearing could be sure of a crowd there such as no single coast, however long he walked it, would ever give him.

He waited, as the custom of that gathering ran, until the business of the day was done and the fires of evening lit, when tellers of every kind took their turn by whichever fire would have them, and when his turn came he chose the largest fire he could get a footing by, well clear of the small fires at the edge of the crowd, close enough to the king's own fire that men of the hill and men of An Cnoc Ruadh both stood within easy hearing of him. The telling came out as he had shaped it through the winter, plainly and without apology: the crossing, the loss, the coming ashore of few where many had set out. By every account that has come down of that evening he told it well, with the ease of a man who has said a thing to himself so many times by so many firesides that the words no longer cost him anything to find.

The crowd did not hear him out to the end. It began, as such things begin, with one voice from somewhere near the back of the gathering, an old man of a hill household who called out that no teller with any claim to his trade told the households of Dia-thìr that they had come from anywhere, for they had not come, they had always been, and Calbh Cnoc ghorm said so in words cut there before any living grandfather was born; and where one voice led, in a crowd already restless with a full day's business behind it and drink enough in it to loosen tempers that might otherwise have kept their own counsel, others followed fast, until Anndra's own voice, still carrying the last of his telling, could no longer be heard over the shouting that had risen to drown it. He did not, by every account, raise his own voice to match theirs, nor try a second time to finish what he had begun; he stood where he was until it was plain to him and to everyone else that no more of the telling would be heard that night by anyone who wanted to hear it, and then he stepped back from the fire and left the crowd to its shouting.

What followed, in the way of such gatherings, was less a judgement than a driving-out that no one household troubled to claim as its own doing: households that had fed him gladly enough on his way east that spring would not have him at their fires that night nor the next morning, and by the time the gathering broke and the households went their own ways home, it was understood on every road out of the ford, without any one voice having said it in so many words, that Anndra Breac was not to be fed at a hill hearth again, nor given a place by any fire that called itself of Dia-thìr's own telling, for the rest of whatever years the sea or the road left him. No one struck him. No one needed to. A teller lives by welcome, and the ford that evening withdrew his, coast by coast, faster than any single household could have managed alone, and by the following spring there was not a hearth from the eastern cape to the western shore that would own to having fed him since.

## V. Of the King, Who Laughed and Then Was Quiet

The king had stood the length of the telling at his own fire, near enough to hear all of it before the shouting rose, and those who stood nearest him that evening told afterward, each in his own words but all agreeing on the shape of it, that he had laughed, once, short and low, at some point in Anndra's telling that none of them could afterward agree on exactly, a man's low private laugh rather than any mockery meant to carry, the kind a man gives a jest that has caught him by surprise rather than one he saw coming and braced against. He did not join the shouting when it rose, nor did he move to still it, standing where he was with the firelight on him while the crowd did the driving-out it did without any word from him one way or the other; and when it was done, and Anndra had stepped back from the largest fire to find no smaller one that would take him either, the king was seen to be quiet, in the particular way those who had watched him thirty years and more knew to read as more than ordinary silence, a stillness that sat on him the rest of that evening and did not lift with the fire's dying.

He said nothing of it that night to anyone who left a telling of what he said, and nothing the next day either, by any account that has come down; and it is told, though the telling allows this much may owe more to the shape a tale takes in the mouths that carry it than to any one witness's sworn word, that a woman of his own kin asked him once, some time after, plainly, whether the teller had spoken true, and that he answered her only that he had heard a great many tellings of where his own people came from in all his own years, and had never yet troubled to choose one over another, which she took, as she told it after, for neither a yes nor a no but a door he did not mean to open for her that day.

Whatever the laugh had been, and whatever the silence after it was, the king made no move that spring, nor any spring after, to call Anndra Breac back to a hearth, nor to still the coast that had driven him from one; and the households that had shouted him down took his silence, as households will, for the answer that suited each of them best.

## VI. Of the Saying Spoken at the Opening

The gathering that met at the ford the following spring opened, for the first time within any telling's memory, with a saying spoken aloud before any business of salt or wool or marriage was so much as raised: the words cut on Calbh Cnoc ghorm, which the people of that hill had kept by mouth across more generations than any one line could count, spoken now not as a thing believed quietly in each household's own heart but as a thing said together, aloud, at the start of the year's meeting, by whoever stood nearest the fire when the crowd had gathered and the business was ready to begin. We did not come; we were here. No one teller is remembered as the first to have said it that spring in place of any other opening; it is told only that it was said, and that it was said again the spring after, and every spring after that, until no household coming to the ford could remember a gathering that had opened any other way, and the saying that had stood carved on a stone at Cnoc ghorm for longer than any living telling could account for became, from that year, a thing spoken at the opening of every gathering the ford ever held, a rule of the telling and not only a belief held apart in each hearth's own quiet keeping.

Of Anndra Breac himself, after that first spring, little more is told with any certainty. He was seen that same year along the western shore south of Seann Skell, walking the strand below the tower where Micheil Beag's planks still lay against the wall he had stacked them on, though whether he stopped to look at them again no teller who saw him passing troubled to ask him, and none who saw him that day spoke to him or was spoken to, for the coast that had fed him gladly twenty years running had by then taken up, coast household by coast household, the same closed door the ford had shown him first. He walked on south along the strand and did not look back at the tower nor at the man who watched him go, and it is that walking, and no other end to it, that the western shore's own tellers kept of him: a man going on along an empty beach in the direction the tide was running, out past the last household that would have fed him and on toward whatever shore lay beyond the one the tellings could still follow him to. No teller of that coast, then or after, claimed to have fed him again, nor buried him, nor marked where he stopped walking; and none, asked why the coast let a man who had first given them their fourth telling of themselves go so unremembered afterward, ever gave an answer better than that a coast which will not keep a tale will not trouble long over the man who carried it.
