# Of the Fosterling's Return

*GE 2,424 – 2,428*

> *Dh'fhalbh e 'na ghille; thàinig e dhachaigh 'na dhuine.*
> He went a boy; he came home a man.
> — the salt-road's own telling, of the fosterling never named

## I. Of the Paired Marks at Ros dhomhain

*This is told at the river-mouth below the Red Hill, where the rock still stands and the marks are still read, though few now living can say which keeper cut which.*

He was thirty years old that spring and had raked salt on the lake shore since he was eight, and it did not occur to him, walking down to the river-mouth on an ordinary morning to see what Seònaid Mhòr had done to the tide-rock, that he was walking toward the last four years he would spend calling the Red Hill his only home. Seònaid was his own age within a season, a broad, quiet woman who had kept the rock since her mother's hands grew too stiff for the chisel, and she had taken, that year, to a habit no keeper before her had troubled with: where every mark since the rock was first cut had recorded the one thing a keeper thought worth recording, the high water that crept a little further up the stone with every generation that watched it, Seònaid cut two strokes for every tide instead of one, a mark for the water's highest reach and, beside it, a shorter mark for its lowest ebb.

"One tells you the sea is rising," she said, when he asked her why, crouched at the rock with her chisel still in her hand and salt dust white on her knees. "It says nothing about how far it falls back. I want both told, so whoever reads this after me has the whole of it and not the half that frightens."

He turned that over as he walked back up to the drying platforms, the way he turned over most things Seònaid said, slowly and more than once. It was, he thought, a small enough change to a stone that had stood plain and single-stroked since before either of them was born, and yet it was the kind of change that the Red Hill's own people, unlike the hill of the Stone Kings fifty miles east, seemed to make without asking leave of anyone: a woman decided a thing was worth doing more fully than it had been done, and did it, and the doing became, within a season or two, simply how it was done. Her pairs could be read that year and every year after; the keepers who came to the rock once she was gone went back, in time, to the single stroke, finding the doubled work more than the plain record needed, but no reciter of that shore ever troubled to say her pairs had been wrong to make. They were only, in the end, more than the custom wanted kept.

## II. Of the Third Rock at Inis thais

Word of the paired marks went along the coast road the way most things went along that coast, in a carrier's mouth rather than in any letter, and reached Inis thais, two days' walk to the south-west round the shoulder of the lake, before the season was out. He never learned who first told it there, or whether the telling that reached Inis thais even kept the doubling that made Seònaid's marks worth remarking on; what came back to Ros dhomhain, the following year, was only that the households of Inis thais's own harbour had begun cutting marks of their own on a rock at their own harbour mouth, at the same spacing Seònaid used, though none of them could say why that spacing and no other, since none of them had ever troubled to ask the keeper who had first thought of it or the reasons she gave for the width between one mark and the next.

It struck him, when the word came back, as a strange way for a thing to travel: not as an idea carried whole, with the mind that made it still attached, but as a bare fact of practice, a spacing on a stone, taken up by people who kept the habit and lost the reasoning behind it in the same crossing. Three rocks stood, by then, along that shore and the next, cut at the same interval and read by three different hands, and no one at any of the three harbours could have told a stranger why the interval was what it was, only that it had always, within any keeper's memory, been kept so. He said as much to Seònaid once, sitting with her at the river-mouth while she cut a mark he had watched her cut a hundred times before, and she only shrugged and said that most customs, if you dug down far enough under them, turned out to have started the same way: someone thought a thing worth doing, and enough people agreed without ever quite saying so aloud, and after that it was a custom and no longer a thought, which was, she said, the whole of how anything at the Red Hill or anywhere else on the island ever came to be done at all.

## III. Of the Landing of Laid Stone

The river-mouth itself changed under his own hands that same year, more than the tide-rock ever had. The Red Hill's boats had put out from an open shingle beach since before Lachlann Ruadh's grandmother's grandmother held the hill, hauled up above the tide-line each evening on rollers cut fresh from whatever driftwood came to hand, a way of landing that worked well enough in fair weather and cost a boat or a bruised shin in foul; and it was Peigi Dhubh, not her father, who first said aloud, at the household's board one wet autumn evening, that a hill whose whole wealth now travelled by water deserved better than a beach any winter tide could rearrange without asking.

Lachlann agreed readily enough, being by then a man past sixty who had grown willing to let his daughter's plans stand alongside his own, and the work went to every household of the hill that could spare a season's labour from the salt-rakes: stone carried down from the low quarry above the lake, laid course on course at the river-mouth where the fresh water of Loch chrom met the tide coming up from the sea, until by the second summer there stood a landing a boat could come alongside at any state of the water without a single hand needed to haul it clear of the mud. He worked the stone himself through most of that second summer, his hands grown hard with salt-rake work but new to the different ache of setting a course of laid stone true, and it was there, more than at any tide-rock or salt-pan, that he came to think of himself as a man of the Red Hill's own building rather than a boy the hill had merely taken in.

It was the hill's first way onto open water that did not depend on a beach's mercy, and word of it travelled, as the marks had, along every road the salt-carriers walked: that the Red Hill now had a landing of laid stone at its own river-mouth, and boats out of harbours further down the coast began, within a few years, to put in there themselves rather than carry on to the open beaches beyond, so that the houses that had stood scattered along that shore for longer than any list could number began, almost without anyone deciding it should be so, to draw in closer round the landing and the rock together, until the scatter had become, by the time he was thirty-four, something a stranger walking down from the high road might have called, without much argument from anyone who lived there, a town: some sixty houses, by the count Seònaid kept as carefully as she kept her tides, standing round the tide-rock and the stone landing both, its people fishing now from boats of their own building rather than working the shallows on foot as their grandparents had.

## IV. Of Lachlann's Dying

Lachlann Ruadh had held the Red Hill only a little while when the boy first came to it at six, a broad man just past forty then, and he held it four-and-twenty years more after, through the boy's whole growing and the raking of a hundred seasons' salt, until the winter the stone landing's second course was laid, when a chill he took wading the shallows to set a boat right settled in his chest and would not lift with the spring. He was five-and-sixty years old, which the household reckoned, when they spoke of it at all, an age past complaint for a man of no king's blood who had spent his whole life wet to the knee in salt water and cold wind off the lake; but it came hard all the same, in the way any death comes hard to a household that has never known its holder any other way, and the fosterling, sitting with the rest of the hall through the last of it, found himself grieving a man not his own father with a plainness that surprised him more than the grief itself.

Lachlann said little, in his last days, that the Red Hill's own reciters ever troubled to keep whole; what they did keep was a single evening near the end, when Peigi sat by him with the salt tallies still in her hand from the day's weighing, and he asked her, not for the first time that season, whether she meant to send the fosterling home once she held the hill in her own right, and she told him she had not yet decided, and he said, in words the hearth still repeats though no one now living heard them first-hand, that a fostering kept a season too long stopped being a kindness to either house and became only a habit no one had troubled to end, and that she should not let her own fondness for having him about the place be the reason the peace of the ford went one day longer without its proper marking. He died within the month, in the same red-earthed hall he had been born in, with Peigi's hand in his and the fosterling standing at the door among the rest of the household who had no closer claim on the bedside than the years they had given the hill.

## V. Of Peigi Dhubh's Holding

Peigi took the hill at forty years old, the same plain, dark, quick-eyed woman who had told him, at the gate on his first evening there, that he was thin for a king's son and she supposed they would have to feed him, grown now into a holder her father's own steward said kept the tallies straighter than Lachlann ever had and forgave a bad season's raking no more readily than he had. She held it the way she had done everything since childhood, without ceremony and without much patience for anyone who expected one; the household's own telling of her taking the hill runs to a single line, that she sat down at the board the morning after her father's burial and asked for the tallies before she asked for anything else, and no one at the Red Hill ever thought to ask her to do it any other way.

It was the better part of a year before she said anything to him of what her father had asked her, on the last evening he had strength to ask it, and when she did she said it plainly, the way she said most things, walking with him along the new stone landing in the last light of an evening in his thirty-second year: that her father had been right, and that a fostering kept past its season served neither hill, and that she meant to send him home before the year turned, with as much salt as a household could carry as gift and not as trade, so that Dùn ìseal would know the Red Hill sent him back with honour and not merely let a hostage of peace go once he had grown too old to trouble keeping. She watched his face while she said it, in the frank way she had watched him at the gate four-and-twenty years before, and told him afterward, though only once and never again in his hearing, that she had put off the saying of it longer than her father would have liked, because the hill had grown used to him in a way no one at the Red Hill had troubled to put into words until the words were needed.

## VI. Of the Leaving of the Red Hill

He spent the last month before the train set out doing the ordinary work of the hill as though nothing were ending, raking salt at the drying platforms beside rakers he had taught himself, setting a last course of stone at the landing that he would not be there to see finished, for Peigi had said plainly that she wanted no long leave-taking made of it, that a man who knew he was leaving in a month worked worse in that month than a man who did not, and the hill had salt to rake whether or not one of its own was about to walk fifty miles east and not come back.

Seònaid came to him the evening before the train left, at the tide-rock rather than at the hall, and cut, while he watched, one more pair of marks on the stone, though the tide that evening called for none, a high stroke and a low stroke set close together at the rock's edge where no keeper after her would think to look for a reading. She told him, when he asked what they were for, that she had not decided, only that the rock had marked every tide of his years on that shore and she saw no reason it should not mark the last evening of them as well, whatever the water was actually doing. He did not know, walking away from the river-mouth that night, whether to call it a kindness or only Seònaid's own plain way of saying a thing she would not otherwise have troubled to say aloud, and decided, by the time he reached the hall, that it was likely both.

Peigi herself walked with the train no further than the gate, which surprised no one who had known her a season, and gave him, in place of any long farewell, the same flat, appraising look she had given him as a boy of six arriving thin and friendless at that same gate four-and-twenty years before. She told him the salt was weighed true and the beasts were sound and that the Red Hill would hear, by whatever road word travelled, how he did; and then, because she had never in her life been a woman to let a silence do work a sentence could do better, she said that her father had been right to send him, and righter still to wait as long as he had waited, and that neither hill would ever trouble to thank her for either, which was, she said, exactly as holding a hill ought to go.

## VII. Of the Road East

He went with three of the Red Hill's own household and a train of pack-beasts loaded with salt enough to weigh down a lesser hill's whole season's store, walking east along the same salt road that had carried him west at six years old on his own two feet, and it struck him, somewhere on the second day, that he remembered almost nothing of that first crossing beyond the shape of it: a woman named Rìonach who had knelt to his height and spoken of forked rivers, a low green mound Donnchadh would not walk near, the smell of the sea reaching him before any sea he could see. He looked for the shelters along the road that Donnchadh had slept them under and found most of them standing still, kept swept, as they had always been kept, by whoever passed last, and slept in one of them himself on the third night with a strange, quiet satisfaction in finding the road had not changed while he had.

The near valleys came up on the fifth day, country he had crossed as a child without troubling to look at it closely, and he found himself now noticing what a fostered boy of six had had no eye to notice: the boundary stones his brother, he had heard, once judged a quarrel by; the households that sent their surplus east at Samhain and never asked what came back to them in a lean year; small plain customs, kept without law, that he had grown up thinking belonged only to the Red Hill's own shore until he saw, walking through them himself, how far they ran. He came in sight of Dùn ìseal on the seventh evening, the hill standing up dark against a sky gone gold the way he had been told, twenty-four years before, that the Red Hill would stand against its own western light, and found he did not know, walking up the last stretch of road, whether the hill ahead of him was the home he was coming back to or another hill he was only passing through on the way to some third place he had not yet found.

## VIII. Of the Brother Who Did Not Know Him

Calum Ciar met the party at the gate, for Eilidh, who had given the hill into his hands some years before, was at the far end of the valley that evening, and it fell to her elder son to greet whatever the Red Hill sent east. He looked the fosterling over, salt-carriers and pack-beasts and all, with the plain courtesy a holder's son owed any train that came in under a gift-load, and asked, when the greetings were done, which of the household he might be, for a face burned dark by lake wind and a Red Hill way of standing had left nothing in him that his brother's eye could fasten on as kin. It is told, and the hill's own reciters do not trouble to soften it, that the fosterling stood a long moment in the gate saying nothing at all, watching his own brother wait for an answer to a question that should never have needed asking, before he told him plainly who he was; and that Calum Ciar's face did something then that no one standing near enough to see it ever troubled to name, somewhere between shame at not knowing and a plainer grief at how completely a boy could be lost to a household that had never once stopped loving him.

Eilidh knew him before he had crossed half the yard. She came to the hall door when word reached her that the Red Hill's train was in, and she looked at the man coming up from the gate, salt-dark and grown a head taller than the boy she had knelt to at Àth shean four-and-twenty years before, and said his name, the one name she had never stopped using though no reciter of either hill had ever troubled to write it down, before he had said a single word to tell her who he was. She wept then, which the hill's own telling allows her, being by that year past caring what a hall full of her own household thought of her weeping, and held him a long while at the door before she would let anyone, even her elder son, come near enough to greet him in turn.

## IX. Of the Songs Sung at the Pans

He stayed the winter at Dùn ìseal and rode down, before the spring turned, to see the salt-pans at Baile ghorm, for it was there, among rakers who worked salt the sea-shore way and had never learned the lake-shore turn of the wrist Lachlann taught him at eight years old, that he found himself, one evening at their fires, teaching what the household at Ros dhomhain had taught him without meaning to teach anything at all: a refrain the Red Hill's own rakers sang at the crust, four short lines whose meaning even the Red Hill's own eldest could no longer wholly give an account of, though every hand on that shore had sung it at the raking since before Seònaid's grandmother's grandmother kept the tide-rock. He sang it for the Baile ghorm rakers as Peigi's own household had sung it for him, and one of them, an old woman who had raked that shingle sixty years, wrote nothing down, for no one at Baile ghorm troubled to write a working song down, but she kept the four lines whole from that single hearing and taught them to her own daughter's daughter before she died, so that the song ran on at the sea-shore pans as it had always run at the lake, in a tongue near enough the island's own that a raker of Baile ghorm could sing it without knowing she sang words the Red Hill itself could no longer wholly construe.

*Salann geal, an cuan dorcha,
Làmh a' snìomh 's a' cur air ais,
Dachaigh ann an gach gràinne,
Cha chaill an fhairge a càs.*

What could be made of it, the Red Hill's own eldest said when he asked them, before he ever carried it east, was something of white salt and a dark sea, of a hand that turns and gives back what it takes, of a home held whole in every single grain; the fourth line no keeper of that shore, in all the years anyone could ask after, had ever managed to render the same way twice, and some said only that the sea does not lose what is its own, and others that the sea does not count the cost of its own losing, and no one thought either wrong enough to correct the other. It did not trouble him, singing it at Baile ghorm's fires with rakers who had never set foot on the western lake shore, that the song's own meaning had worn thinner than its tune; a thing four generations of hands had sung at the same work needed, he thought, no more warrant than that.

He was thirty-four years old that spring, and stayed at Dùn ìseal the rest of his mother's life and some while after, neither holding the hill nor troubling anyone with a wish to, a man of the king's own blood who had gone west at six and come home at thirty grown, by every account, more the Red Hill's than his own mother's hill had ever managed to make him in the six years it held him first. The Stone Kings' own list, when the reciters after Eilidh came to set down her reign and her sons in order, gave Calum Ciar his place among the holders as it gave every holder before him; of the one who had gone to the Red Hill and come back with salt in his hands and a song no one could wholly construe, it set down nothing at all, no year of birth and no year of death, no reign and no deed with a date fixed to it, so that a stranger reading the list a hundred years after would have found no trace that Eilidh Ghlas had borne more than the one son. It was the Red Hill's own telling, not the hill of the Stone Kings, that kept what little of him is kept at all: the boy who went, it says still, at every hearth that sings the salt refrain without knowing whose hands first carried it home, and did not come back the same man, and was never, by either hill's own reckoning, given a name.
