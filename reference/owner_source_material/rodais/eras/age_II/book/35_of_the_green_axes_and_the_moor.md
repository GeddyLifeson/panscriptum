# Of the Green Axes and the Moor

*GE 3,496 – 3,999*

*Part Six: Catrìona Mhòr and the Ford*

> *Tuagh uaine, air uaigh; cha do bhuail i riamh, is cha bhuail i tuilleadh.*
> A green axe, on a grave; it never struck, and it will strike no more.
> — said of the axes, wherever one is laid

## I. Of the Axes That Were Never Swung

In those years there came out of the north-west a fashion the households of every other coast were slow to understand and quick to want: axes of a green stone, ground and polished until they held a shine no flint or common rock could hold, cut at a single quarry above Àth àrsaidh and carried from there in packs on men's backs, in baskets on ponies, in the bottoms of boats going the long way round by sea when the land road was closed with snow. They were beautiful, and they were nearly useless. A green axe would take an edge finer than any grey one, and lose it faster, chipping at the first knot in a green log, and every household that owned one soon learned to keep it from the woodpile and give it, instead, to the dead: laid whole across the chest in a grave, or set under the corner-stone of a new house where no floor would ever wear it, a thing too fine to spend on work and too dear to leave undone something with.

Ìomhar Dearg was two hundred and ninety-two years old the autumn he first held one, at a wake in the north where an old man of the household was given his green axe to carry into the ground with him, and Ìomhar, who had already buried more kin of his own than he cared to count on days when the wind sat wrong, turned the thing over in his two hands before it went under the turf and found himself, not for the first time in his life, wanting a use for a thing everyone else was content to spend on the dead. He was the king's own son, one of the youngest of them then living, and he had grown, in nearly three centuries of watching households rise and fall and rise again about him, into a man who liked a thing to earn its keep before it earned its grave. He asked the old man's son, plainly, whether he might have one cut for himself, to carry and use every day until it broke, since none of his own kin were close enough to burying to need it spent that way.

The son said he might ask at the quarry itself, and so Ìomhar did, walking the length of the north-west coast that same winter to stand at Àth àrsaidh among the chips lying knee-deep about the cutters' huts, green dust on every threshold and every child's bare foot for a mile round. The cutters heard him out, and one of them, an old woman who had worked the stone longer than anyone else living there, told him plainly that no axe of that green stone had ever been cut to be swung, only to be shown, and that a man who wanted an axe to fell a tree with had best go to any flint-knapper on any common coast and leave the green stone to the graves it was already promised to. Ìomhar did not press her further. He had asked, and been answered, and he was old enough in the ways of the island's small customs to know that a custom held by an entire quarry's worth of households was not one man's argument to win in an afternoon.

He took her advice all the same, and had a knapper of the ordinary grey stone shape him an axe before that same winter was out, plain-hafted and quick to dull, which he used and had reground and used again for the better part of forty years until the head cracked at last on a root he had misjudged, and he buried that one himself, when it broke, in the same ground he had first turned it, judging that a tool which had earned its whole life in use had earned, too, the same rest the green stone was given for having earned none of it at all.

## II. Of the Quarry Forsaken and the Moor That Came Down

The quarry at Àth àrsaidh did not last out his own lifetime's watching of it. Within fifty years of that winter the households who worked it, and traded in nothing else besides, found the seam of the true green stone running thinner year on year under their own cuts, whatever they tried of following it deeper or sideways along the hill, until the day came, in the year three thousand five hundred and forty-seven, that the last cutter of the old skill died without having taken an apprentice, his own sons having gone to herding rather than stone in a decade when the stone itself had stopped paying for the teaching of it. The chips lay knee-deep still, and would lie so for longer than any teller then living could foresee, but no new axe came out from among them after that year, and what had been made before it went on being buried, one grave and one corner-stone at a time, until the graves of the whole north-west coast held between them more of the green stone than the quarry itself had ever held ground still uncut.

It was in those same decades, the tellers of the north say, that the winters themselves began to lengthen, though no single year could be named as the one where it started. The oak that had stood since before any household's grandfather began, about Doire ghlas and the low ground north of it, to fall back from ground it had held without contest for longer than the reckoning went, and heather and birch and the soft grey moss of the bogs came up in its place, spreading south a little further with every generation that did not trouble to burn it back. The groves that gave the whole north its many names built on the word doire, the oak-grove, shrank to the sheltered valleys where the wind could not reach them, and stand there yet, and the households of that coast told it to their own children simply as the time the winters began to run longer than a man's memory could hold two of exactly the same length together.

Ìomhar passed through that country more than once in the years the moor was taking it, an old man to look at by then though his own years ran further than any household he passed could reckon, and remarked once, to a woman of Doire ghlas who gave him a night's shelter and asked nothing of his name in return, that a country changes its face the way a man does, slowly enough that no one living in it sees the day it happens, and only a stranger returning after a long absence can say plainly that the thing before him is not the thing he left. The woman answered that she had been born among the heather herself and had never seen an oak-grove larger than the one behind her own house, and did not know, until he told her, that the ground under her own byre had once stood thick with trees a man could not see through. Ìomhar said nothing further on it. He had learned, of the moor as of the green axes, that a change too slow for one life to feel is not a grief to the life living in the middle of it, whatever it might be to a life old enough to remember the ground before.

## III. Of the Stepping-Stones and the Drowning at the Ford

The households along Abhainn fhionn had for as long as anyone's telling went crossed the river where they could, at a shallow bend below the willow flats, wading it in low water and going the long way round by the upper shallows when the water ran high, and it was in this same stretch of years that a family of that stretch, tired of wet feet and lost baskets, began laying flat stones across the shallowest crossing, one at a time as each summer's low water allowed the laying, until within two generations a man could cross the whole width of the river dry-shod, stone to stone, in every season but true flood. It was the first crossing of any river on the island made by hand rather than found by wading, and households from three days' walk in either direction came to see it before they believed the telling of it, and went home to lay stones of their own wherever their own rivers ran shallow enough to try.

No such stones were ever laid at the fords of Abhainn chaol, further south, where the river ran narrower and the ground on either bank rose steep enough that no household troubled to build what the current would only carry off again at the first spring spate. It was at those same fords, in the year three thousand six hundred and eighty, that a party of twelve crossing with salt on their backs, bound for the upland households that paid well and asked no questions of a trader's route, were taken by a spate that came down without the warning a slower rise would have given them, rain having fallen three days running in hills the fords themselves could not see. Ìomhar was two days' walk south of the fords when word reached him, carried by a boy on a pony who had been sent, he said, to fetch anyone of standing who might know what should be done with twelve bodies and no household near enough to claim more than two or three of them as its own dead.

He came as fast as the road allowed, and it was not fast enough. By the time he stood on the bank of Abhainn chaol the water had dropped back to its ordinary height, running clear and shallow over stones that gave no sign at all of what they had carried through them three days before, and the households of both banks had already done what could be done: five bodies found and given to their own kin, four more found further downstream and given, since no one closer could be found to claim them, to the nearest household willing to dig, and three never found at all, the river having taken them wherever a river takes what it does not choose to give back.

He walked the bank a while before he would let himself be led to the graves, looking for some sign of the crossing itself, the salt-baskets, a length of rope, a shoe, and found nothing the river had troubled to give back beyond a single pack-strap caught on a root a half-mile down, empty, the buckle still fastened as though the shoulder it had crossed had simply stepped out from under it. A woman of the upland households, come down to claim one of the four unclaimed dead as near enough kin to bury properly, told him that the twelve had crossed at that ford every season of her own life and her mother's before her, and that she did not think any of them had so much as looked upstream at the sky before they went in, the rain of those three days having fallen on hills none of them could see from the water's edge. Ìomhar asked her whether the households of that ford had ever thought to watch the upper hills for exactly such a rain. She said they had not, until that year, and that they did now. Ìomhar stood at the water's edge a long while, an old man of the king's own blood who had come two days too late to be any use to the living or the dead, and said, to the household elder who had walked down with him, that he had crossed rivers enough in his own centuries to know that a ford is a debt every household pays sooner or later, and that the only mercy in it was that this one had been paid all at once rather than one drowned traveller at a time across a hundred years. The elder answered only that no household on either bank had crossed those fords in the wet season since, and none has crossed them so in any year told of after.

## IV. Of the Twenty-Two Marks, and the Ground That Would Not Sit Still

Further round the coast, at Ceann chiar, the households rebuilding after the Black Wind that had scattered their roofs a generation before set, in the year three thousand seven hundred and twenty-six, a stone in the midst of their new-laid houses and cut on it a mark for every house the wind had taken: two-and-twenty marks, plain strokes in a plain row, no names beside them, for the households that raised the stone held that the dead of one wind were better counted together than singled out one from another, a wind having taken no more notice of who it took than a wave takes of which shell it turns. Ìomhar saw the stone some years after it was cut, on a journey east that had no other purpose than his own restlessness, and stood before it counting the marks himself, twice, as though a third count might make the number smaller than the first two had left it, which it did not.

It was a restless country in those years in more ways than the wind alone. A summer came, in that same stretch of seasons, when the rivers of the eastern plain ran so low that the households of Dùn ìseal and Muileann bhàn drove their cattle to the warm springs to drink rather than lose them to a stream too thin to serve a herd, and the surplus each household had kept against exactly such a year held long enough that not one death was counted for the thirst of it, which the households themselves accounted, once the rains came back, less a mercy of the weather than a debt the sharing custom had quietly paid on their behalf. The sea, too, came in again over the low ground at Cnoc chaol, a second wave lesser than the first the coast still told of, and found the new houses standing higher on the slope than the old ones had stood, so that this time the water took only empty byres and left every hearth it reached for standing dry above it. And further west the old crack in the ground that no household there had ever fully trusted moved again in its sleep, as the reciters of that coast liked to say of it, shifting the spring back uphill and breaking the line of white stones that marked the danger in two places, so that the households had to walk out with fresh stone and lay the line again exactly as their grandmothers had laid it the first time, no wiser for the moving than their grandmothers had been, only more patient.

At Cnoc bheag, on the bank of Abhainn ìseal, households settled that same generation a short walk from the standing stone the place had already borne a name for, and set their own houses, without any law telling them to, so that no door among them faced the stone directly, a custom no one could afterward say who had begun but that every household coming after kept without question, the way a custom of that kind is kept once the first house has set the pattern for every house built after it. And on the lower reaches of the same river, at Inis bhàn, the households that lived by the seal-rocks off the river mouth settled their own quarrel over them the plain way island custom settled most such things: one rock to a household, taken in its turn, and a rock taken out of turn was, by every teller's account of that stretch of coast, the commonest cause of hard words between neighbours who agreed, on every other matter, rather better than most. Ìomhar was shown the turn kept for it once, a tally of notched sticks held by whichever household's turn had last passed, and asked the man who showed it to him what was done when two households both swore, in good faith, that the turn stood with them. The man said that the elders of both households sat down together at the tally and counted the notches aloud from the first stick to the last, and that this had settled every such quarrel so far without needing anyone to raise a voice past the counting, which he offered, Ìomhar thought, with more quiet pride than the small matter seemed at first to deserve, until he considered how many quarrels on other coasts never came to a tally at all.

## V. Of Niall, Born to Raonaid

Ìomhar had a half-sister he loved better than any other of his father's many children, Raonaid nic Ailein, a daughter of the king born to a wife of the eastern shore a hundred years before Ìomhar himself, and married, long before this telling reaches her, to a man of the king's blood by the line of Fearchar Bacach, a quiet herder of the river-lands below Dùn ìseal whom the eastern shore knew better by his flocks than by his name. It was Raonaid's house, more than any house of his own, that Ìomhar came back to between his wanderings, and her hearth he sat longest at, for she had the gift, rare among the king's children, of asking a man where he had been without asking him why.

Her son Niall was born in the year three thousand seven hundred and three, in that house on the eastern shore, with the midwife's fire built up high against an autumn that had come in colder than the last three running, and Ìomhar, by then close on five hundred years old, held the boy the first evening of his life and told his sister afterward that he found in him more of her own quickness in the eyes than of anything he recognised from the long line on either side. Niall grew laughing from his first year onward, the kind of child who found the small mishaps of a household, a dropped pail, a goat loose in the wrong field, a neighbour's dog barking at nothing, funnier than most children found anything at all, and it was said of him even before he had seen ten summers that a fire with Niall at it was a fire that stayed lively past the hour it would otherwise have gone quiet. He was the king's grandson by his mother and of the king's blood again by his father, and he learned to say both lines before he could say much else; the household laughed to hear a child of five tell a stranger at the door that he was owed twice.

Ìomhar watched the boy grow with the particular attention of a man who had already watched a great many children grow and outlive him, and told Raonaid once, on an evening when Niall had run the length of the yard three times over some joke of his own making that no one else quite followed, that he did not know yet how long the boy's years would run, and that he had decided, for his own part, not to trouble himself asking it aloud where the boy might hear the asking and grow strange about it before he had any cause to. He had a son of his own by then, Dùghall, born to him in the year three thousand nine hundred by a wife of the river-lands whom the tellings do not name, a grave, red-haired child with none of his cousin's laughter in him; and it was Niall, not his own boy, that the eastern shore came to think of as Ìomhar's shadow on the road.

## VI. Of the Share Ìomhar Asked For

It was in the year three thousand nine hundred and sixty, Niall by then a young man of two hundred and fifty-seven years who looked a youth of twenty, and already known the length of the eastern shore for the same laugh he had carried since the yard, that Ìomhar went up to the king himself and asked, plainly, for a share of his own: not the wandering right his blood had always given him to sit at any hearth on the island and eat from any household's table, which he had used all his long life and never once needed to ask twice for, but ground with a hill on it, held in his own name and passed, when his own years ran out, to whichever of his children the custom favoured. He had never asked such a thing before, in nearly eight hundred years of watching households given their ground and their grazing and their rights of way while he himself held nothing but the wandering right that came to him by blood alone, and he told the king, standing before him on the hill with his sister's son Niall a little apart, laughing quietly with one of the king's own grandsons over some jest of the road, that a man who had buried strangers at fords he came too late to and counted marks on stones cut for other households' dead had come, in his own slow way, to want a piece of ground that answered to his own name before it answered to anyone else's.

The king heard him out the way he heard out most such askings, without haste and without the warmth some visitors expected of him and did not always get, and gave him, before the day was out, the river-grazing on the eastern marches, ground between the last settled households and the open country beyond them, thin soil but good grass along the water, and told him only that a share given was a share that could be taken back, as every share on the island had always been and would go on being, and that Ìomhar, of all the king's own blood, understood that condition better than most men his ground might have gone to instead. Ìomhar said he understood it, and thanked him, and took his household, with his sister's laughing son riding along for the pleasure of the road, and went east to see the ground for himself before the season turned.

He stood on the river-grazing at the end of that same day, the light going long and gold across the water the way it goes in the last hour before a clear autumn evening turns cold, and looked east along the river toward ground he had not yet walked and households he had not yet met, thin grass under his own two feet for the first time in nearly eight centuries of walking on ground that answered, always, to some other name than his. He did not know, standing there, what would come up that river out of the east in the years his own line had still to run, only that the ground he stood on faced that way and no other, and that he had asked for it himself, wanting a thing at last that would need his own name spoken over it before anyone else's blood could rightly claim it back.

The green axe he had turned over at a wake near five centuries before came back to him that evening, too fine to spend on any wood it might have cut and given, instead, to the ground. His own grey axe was long since buried too by then, worn to a stub and cracked at the haft, spent to the last year it had in it before he let it go. Standing on grass no one else yet held, he judged a share of ground a different order of thing from either: too plain to fear the wearing, too lasting to wear away in one man's using of it, a thing that would go on answering to a name, his own or his son's or a name not yet given, for as long as any household troubled to keep the custom that had granted it. That, he thought, was longer than any axe of any stone, green or grey, had ever been asked to last.
