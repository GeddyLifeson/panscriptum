# Of the Sharing before the Storms

*GE 2,328 – 2,390*

*Part Four: The Lot and the Saying*

> *An làmh a roinneas, cha teirig i; an làmh a dh'fhalaicheas, falbhaidh i.*
> The hand that shares does not fail; the hand that hides must go.
>
> — said at Dùn ìseal after the sharing, and after nothing else

## I. Of the Custom that Had No Beginning

No one at Dùn ìseal could say who first brought grain to the bank before the storms, or in what year, or why. The telling only holds that in one year a household with more than it needed carried the surplus to the hall and set it down before the holder without being asked, and that the households with less were glad of it, and that the year after, another household did the same, unbidden, because the first had. By the time it was a custom no one living remembered it not being one.

It was not a law such as the reciters kept at the ford, cut into no stone and sworn to no name. It asked only this: that before the season of the black-wind storms, when the boats came in for good and the passes closed and a household's stores were what they would live on until the grass came back, whoever had more than enough should say so, and bring it in.

Tormod Liath, who held the hill in those years and had held it longer than any before him, let it be done in the open. At the gathering of Samhain the households brought their surplus within the bank: grain in skins, dried fish strung on rods, salted meat in its own fat. It was weighed by the count of mouths in each house and given back out, so that every hearth went into the dark months even. A household that brought little because it had little lost nothing by it. A household that brought little because it had kept more back lost the thing it had kept, once, and after that lost more.

It is told that in the third year of the custom a woman of the shore households, whose net had taken more mackerel than her family could eat or smoke before it turned, carried three baskets of it up to the bank herself rather than let it spoil unshared, and that Tormod came down from the hall to help her set them down, an old grey man carrying fish like any hand of the household. After that the custom needed no one to ask.

There was no cutter's mark for it, no knotted cord to count it by, and no word among the Dia-thìrich for the thing itself; it was known only as the sharing, or the bringing-in, or, among those who did it least willingly, the asking, though no one asked, and that was the whole of its strength. A household that gave more than its share got back no more than any other; a household that gave nothing because it had nothing was not shamed for it, and a holder who tried to shame such a household was, in the one telling that gives the case, made to bring his own surplus in double the next year by the plain silence of his neighbours, without a word said against him that any reciter kept.

For thirty years and more it went so, and the island beyond the hill knew little of it, for it belonged to Dùn ìseal and its valleys and had not yet gone out along the roads the way the lot and the cup would go out after it. Elsewhere households still hoarded against the storms as they had always done, burying what they could not eat, and thought no worse of themselves for it. A household that dug a floor-pit in those years did so as its grandfathers had, against wolves, against a bad neighbour, against nothing it could have named; it was only where the sharing had come first that a hidden pit became a different thing, without the digger changing a stone of it.

## II. Of the House at Àth shean

Àth shean stood where the salt road forded the shallow river below the hill, a scatter of houses on the near bank with byres cut into the slope behind them, no different from a dozen fords like it save that its ground was good for barley in a wet year and bad in a dry one, and that its households answered to Dùn ìseal the way the near valleys did.

Beasag was born there in the household of a man whose name the hill's own telling does not keep, only that he was a careful man, close with what was his, and that his neighbours called him so without meaning it as praise. He had built his grain pit himself, lined with hurdles and roofed with turf, under the floor of the byre where the cattle stood over it in winter and no stranger would think to dig; and he had built it before ever the sharing custom came down the valley to Àth shean, in the years when a hidden store was only good sense and no one asked a man to open his floor to his neighbours.

By the time Beasag could walk, the sharing had come to Àth shean too, carried down by households that had seen it done at the hill and thought it good, and her father brought his share to the bank each year with the rest, a little less than his neighbours guessed he had, though none could have said by how much.

She grew up knowing the pit the way a child knows the shape of a house without being told its walls have a name. Her father lifted the third flagstone from the door on nights when the household ate better than its neighbours, and laid it back after; and long before Beasag had words for understanding it, she understood that this was not spoken of outside the walls.

She remembered it best as a smell before she remembered it as a thing: the byre at night smelled of cattle and old rushes and, on those nights, for a little while, of turned earth and grain-dust, and under it the tallow smoke of the one lamp her father allowed himself for the lifting. He never spoke while he did it. He knelt on the flag beside the third stone with a length of ash wood for a lever, and worked it up an inch at a time so that it made no more noise going than a cow shifting its feet, and he never once, in all the years she watched him from the loft ladder with her chin on the top rung, looked up to see whether she was there. She thought, when she was very small, that this was a game fathers played, a secret kept the way a secret is kept from younger children about a feast day; it was some years before she understood that it was kept from the ford as well as from her, and some years more before she understood what that meant.

"A careful man keeps his own counsel," he told her once, the only time he ever spoke of it to her directly. It was said, on that one occasion, of a neighbour who had asked him plainly, at the ford, whether his barley had come in as thin as everyone else's. "What a man has under his own floor is between him and his floor." She was young enough then to think it a kind of wisdom, the way a child takes most of what a parent says for wisdom until the day it stops sounding like any.

Her mother had died birthing the last of Beasag's brothers, three winters before this telling opens, and after that the house was her father's and his alone to keep as he judged fit; and what he judged fit was to keep more back each year, a little at a time, the way a man tests how far a rope will stretch before it breaks. He was not unkind to his children. He fed them well in years others went hungry, and thought, in his own reckoning, that this was the whole of what a father owed.

## III. Of the Lean Year

The storm season that came in the forty-ninth year after the custom began was the leanest Àth shean had known. The grazing had failed the summer before along the whole of the near valleys; the barley came in thin and short-headed; and the fishing boats, which in a poor year on land could always be sent out again for what the sea still gave, lost three weeks running to a wind that would not turn.

Beasag was seventeen that year, old enough to be sent to the bank at Dùn ìseal with the household's share and to see with her own eyes what a lean year looked like gathered in one place: baskets half the height of other years, byres led up with cattle too thin to slaughter, old people who came to stand by the bank and be counted though they had nothing to bring. She saw the division made by the count of mouths, saw a widow with four children carry home more than she had brought, saw a household of two old men carry home less; and she understood, watching it, what her father's floor held that these people did not have.

She saw one thing more that day that stayed with her longer than the counting itself. A boy not much younger than her own brother stood near the bank with an empty sack over his shoulder, waiting his household's turn, and when the measure was poured into it he stood a moment holding the weight of it in both arms, where the other children ran home with theirs at once, and laughed, once, at nothing anyone else could see; and an old woman beside Beasag said, not unkindly, that it was the boy's first winter with a full belly in it, and that he did not yet trust it to last the walk home. Beasag thought of her father's pit, deep and dry and dark under the byre floor, holding more than that boy's whole sack twice over, and said nothing, then, to the old woman or to anyone.

She said nothing that year. It was not in her, yet, to say it. She told herself, walking home behind her father and brother with the household's own thin share on her back, that it was not her floor and not her word to give, and that a daughter did not measure her father any more than a son did; and she went on telling herself so through the winter that followed, a winter in which Àth shean buried two of its old people and her own house ate meat twice a week and bread every day, and no one at her hearth asked why, because no one at her hearth wanted to know the answer badly enough to ask.

But the year had marked her, and she went home turning it over the way a stone is turned over in a pocket, worn smooth by handling before it is ever thrown. She began to watch her father's face when he counted the household's share into the skins for the bank, and to notice what he set aside first and did not put in, and to reckon, as well as a girl of seventeen could reckon, how many mouths at Àth shean had gone hungrier that winter than they need have, for the sake of what lay under three courses of turf beneath the byre floor.

Her elder brother, who slept in the loft above the byre and had grown a man's shoulders that year, knew of the pit as she did and had made his own peace with it long before; it was their father's floor, their father's grain, and a son did not measure his father. He told her so plainly enough, the once she tried to speak of it to him, and after that she did not try again with him.

## IV. Of the Telling

She told it at the ford rather than the hill, and to a woman mending net on the bank rather than to any holder, in the first mild days after the storms broke, when Àth shean's household came down to the water again to see what the winter had left of the boats. The woman's name is not kept in the hill's telling, only that she was old enough to have buried a husband and a son that same winter, and that she asked Beasag, in the way of an idle question between neighbours who have both come through a hard season, whether her father's byre had fared as thin as the rest of the ford's.

"No," Beasag said, before she had thought whether to answer at all, and then, because the one word sat badly on its own, "there's a pit under the floor. There has been since before the sharing came to us. He never brought it all in."

The woman went on with her mending for a moment as though she had not heard, the way people do when a thing is too large to take in at the speed it was given. Then she asked, quietly, whether Beasag was certain, and Beasag said she was, for she had watched her father lift the flag with her own eyes more winters than she could count; and the woman put down her net and looked at her a long while before she said anything more.

"You know what this will cost him."

"I know."

"And you still say it."

Beasag gave that one no answer. The hill's telling holds, in the one line it gives to the moment itself, that she stood on the bank with the wind off the water in her face and found, turning the words over once they were out, that she felt lighter for having said them than she had felt in a long time, and that this frightened her more than her father's anger would, when it came.

She did not tell it to bring trouble on her father. She told the truth of what she said afterward, when she was made to say it again before others, in a plainer way: that she had been asked, and that she had grown tired, some time in that lean winter without her quite marking the day it happened, of watching her neighbours go thin while her own house did not, and that once the word was out of her mouth she found she did not want it back.

The woman told her own household, as neighbours do, meaning no more harm by it than Beasag had meant in the telling; and from there it went the short way a thing goes in a scatter of houses at a ford, hearth to hearth, until it reached Àth shean's own holder, who sent word up to Dùn ìseal, because a matter under the sharing custom was not his to judge alone.

Tormod Liath was an old man by then, older than any who had held the hill before him, and it is told that he heard the matter out in silence and asked only one question of the men who came to lay it before him: whether the girl had told it of her own will, unbeaten and unbribed. When they said she had, he nodded once, as a man nods at something he expected, and sent them to open the floor.

They found the pit as she had said it would be, lined and roofed and dry, and in it grain enough to have fed two households through the lean winter that had just passed, kept whole while the hall counted out its shares by the mouth. Her father did not deny it when they brought him before the holder's man at Àth shean. He said only that it was his own pit, dug with his own hands before ever the custom came to the ford, and that a man's floor was his own to keep as he judged.

The holder's man told him that the pit was his, and always would be; the grain in it was the households' now, by the custom the ford had kept for fifty years, and the household that had hidden it from the sharing would keep nothing at Àth shean past the day. Until dark he might take from his own house what he and his children could carry on their backs, and no more, and be off the ford's ground by morning.

Before the man turned to go, Beasag's father asked who had told it, though he must already have guessed, for there was no one else in the house who had known where the pit lay and lived to speak of it besides his own children. The ford kept no custom of naming the teller to the one told on, for fear of what might follow such a naming in a house before it was even emptied, and he got no answer to it. But he looked at Beasag across the yard as the man spoke, once, and she did not look away from him, and it is told that this was answer enough for both of them, and that no more words passed between father and daughter on the matter, that day or any day after.

## V. Of the Road

Beasag walked out with her father and her brother and the two younger children in the last light of that day, each of them under as much as they could bear: a folded hide, a cooking pot, what grain had not gone into the pit, a spindle, a knife, the small things a household gathers in a generation and cannot leave behind though it can carry almost none of them. No one at Àth shean came out to see them go, though she felt, walking the lane between the houses, that every door had someone standing just inside it.

Her father did not speak to her on the road, that night or the next, and it is not told in the hill's own telling whether he ever did again as they had spoken before. Her brother walked beside her without a word either way, carrying more than his share and setting his own pace to hers when the youngest flagged, which was the only kindness either of them had it in him to give just then.

They slept the first night in the open, under a hedge-bank within sight of Àth shean's own smoke, because the youngest could go no further, and Beasag lay awake listening to her father's breathing on the far side of the fire and could not tell, in the dark, whether he slept or only lay still. In the morning he was up before any of them, and led them east without a word about which way, as though the road had always run only that one direction and none of them had ever thought otherwise.

They went east, the way households went who could not or would not hold ground close under the hill: past the near valleys, past the last of the bank-ditches Tormod's line had thrown up, out to where the vein under the mountain gave way to common rock and the king's blood held no hall and claimed no grazing. They walked eleven days by Beasag's later count, though she owned that she had lost the true tale of them somewhere past the sixth, footsore and thin-shod, sleeping under whatever bank or overhang the evening found them near, eating the grain they had carried at half what they would have eaten it at home, because none of them yet knew how far east they would need it to last. There were households there already, scattered and thin on ground poorer than Àth shean's, who had gone east a generation before for their own reasons and asked no questions of those who came after with the same look about them. Beasag's father built a house on a slope above a stream with no name that she was ever told, and did not build a pit under its floor, though whether that was shame or only that there was nothing left worth hiding, she never said, in all the years the ford afterward kept her name.

It is told that she stayed east of the vein the whole of her life, and raised her own children in a house with no door to any stone, and that whenever travellers came through from the hill she would ask them, before any other news, how the sharing had gone that season, and whether any household had been found keeping back; and that she never once said why she asked, though everyone she asked already knew.

## VI. Of the Hill in Its Old Age

Tormod Liath held the hill twelve years more after the matter of Àth shean's pit, into the last winter of his life, by which time his hair and beard had gone the colour of the stone the reciters named him for and his hands shook at the cup though his judgment did not. He had outlived three heirs of his own body before that, and buried each one himself at the bank he had built, and it was said of him at the end that no one now living at Dùn ìseal remembered a holder of the hill who was not Tormod.

He died in his own hall, in his sleep, with no wound and no fever on him, in the three hundred and eightieth year of his holding, and the hill mourned him the length of a season, for there was scarcely a household on it whose grandfather he had not judged.

The holding of the hill passed, as the list gives it, to Eilidh Ghlas, of the king's own blood, who had lived three hundred and forty years in the valleys under Dùn ìseal without any thought, her own telling says, of ever holding anything but her own hearth. She took up the hill in the year of Tormod's death, and the sharing custom with it, and it is told that the first thing she did, before the bank was even swept for the season's grain, was to send to Àth shean and ask after the pit under the byre floor that everyone at the ford still called by the name of the man who had dug it, though he had been gone from the ford for twelve years and more.

She was answered that the pit stood empty, and had stood empty since the day it was opened, for no household at Àth shean since had cared to dig one of its own; and it is told that she said only that this was well, and asked nothing further about it, then or after.

Eilidh had come to the hill an old woman already in the world's reckoning, though young beside Tormod's long holding, and those who had known her in the valleys said she brought to the hall a plainer hand than his: she weighed the sharing herself in the first years rather than leave it to a steward, and stood at the bank through the whole of the counting even in wet weather, so that no household could say the hill judged them from indoors while they stood wet outside it. Of the man who had gone east with his children she asked nothing more than the one question, and the ford's telling, which watched her closely those first seasons the way a ford watches any new holder, set that down as neither harshness nor forgiveness, but only as the manner of a woman who had watched the custom work for sixty years before it was ever hers to keep, and saw no cause to remake it.

Under her the sharing went on as it had under Tormod, brought in and weighed and given back by the count of mouths, and no household at Dùn ìseal or Àth shean was ever again found keeping a floor that lied about what lay beneath it. The house east of the vein, and the woman who had opened her father's pit with a word at a riverbank, the hill's own telling does not follow further; that thread the ford at Àth shean kept for itself, and told, when it told it at all, in its own words and not the hill's.
