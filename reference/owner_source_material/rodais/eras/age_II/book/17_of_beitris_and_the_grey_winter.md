# Of Beitris and the Grey Winter

*GE 1,541 – 1,746*

*At Muileann leathan the weavers still leave one thread of the warp uncut at Bealltainn, and say only that it was Beitris's custom before it was theirs.*

## I. Of the Lamps, the Traps and the Standing Frame

In the twenty-first year after the households of the Hawk bay first stood looking at open water, a household of Caol leathan set a hollowed stone on its hearth-ledge with seal oil in the hollow and a wick of moss laid along the rim, and lit it, and found that it gave a small steady flame that made almost no smoke and needed no wood at all. No one on that shore had lit anything but a hearth before. The stone was passed from hand to hand at the next gathering of the households, turned over, sniffed, held up and admired the way a new thing is admired before anyone has decided whether to trust it, and within three winters every house on that stretch of coast kept one burning through the dark months by its bed-place, where a hearth's heat would have been wasted and its light had never reached. The old people said the light was too small to be worth the oil it drank. The young people, who slept nearer the door where the hearth's glow had never quite reached them, said nothing at all and kept their lamps lit.

Down the coast at Caol fhiadhaich, in much the same years, the households of the river-mouth drove a fence of woven wattle out into the ebb where the salmon ran, angled so that a fish coming down on the falling tide was herded rather than caught, penned at last in water too shallow to turn in. The first fence took two households the better part of a season to stake and weave, and washed out twice before the third setting held; but the line of stake-holes it left in the mud, once cut, was used again every year after by whoever held that stretch of bank, so that a channel first fished by a man's grandmother was fished in the same channel, by the same worn holes, by his own grandchildren, and no one troubled to ask any more whether the holes could be moved to better water, for the fish, it seemed, had long since learned the fence was there and came down anyway.

It was at Muileann leathan, in the north, that the standing frame was first raised, in a year the reciters give as the six hundred and twenty-eighth after Caol leathan lit its first lamp. Cloth on Dia-thìr had until then been twined by hand, a slow business worked in the lap or against a knee, a length grown a finger's width an evening if the light held and the worker's back did not give out first; the frame stood the warp upright instead, weighted at the foot with loops of fired clay so that the threads hung taut of their own weight rather than needing a hand to hold them so, and a worker could stand at it, pass the weft through with both hands free, and beat it home with a wooden sword faster than any lap-work had ever gone. The clay weights themselves, when a house burned or fell in, lay afterward in rows on the floor exactly as they had hung from the warp, and a stranger digging into an old house-floor at Muileann leathan a thousand years on would find them still in their rows and know, without being told, that a loom had stood there and fallen straight down when the roof gave out above it.

There was, in those same years, a further thing begun that no teller has ever put a name or a face to. Someone, on some shore no reciter can point to first, began to cut a mark on a fixed rock at the line the highest tide reached, and to cut another beside it at the next high tide, and the next, until a row of marks ran up the rock face at intervals no fisherman's ordinary carefulness would have asked for; a fisherman need only know that the tide is coming or going, and does not, as a rule, care by how much more this month's flood beats last month's. Whoever began the cutting cared. The marks kept no other record than themselves, told no story a listener could follow, answered no question anyone thought to ask of them; and yet the habit spread, in the way a habit spreads on an island where everyone's business becomes everyone else's within a season, until more than one shore kept its own rock and its own count, and no household that kept one could have said, if asked, what it was counting toward.

These four things, the lamp, the fence, the frame and the mark on the rock, belong to no single teller and no single hearth; they are given here together because a chronicle that told each on its own night would tell four small things and miss the one large thing they say together, which is that in those years the hill-folk had begun, without any king's word and without any law, to make for themselves the ordinary furniture of a settled life: light apart from fire, food taken by cunning rather than by chasing, cloth grown fast enough to clothe a household without breaking the maker's back over it, and a way of setting a mark against the world's slow changes so that a change too slow for any one life to see could, all the same, be seen. None of the four made anyone rich. None of them was decreed. They simply were, one year, where they had not been the year before, the way the hill's whole way of living had come to be everywhere at once out of no single mouth's saying so.

## II. Of the Weaver

Beitris was born at Muileann leathan in the year the reciters count as the six hundred and seventy-first after the crossing to the Hawk bay, into the third household to raise a standing frame on that stretch of the northern stream, and she grew up under the shadow of her mother's loom the way another child grows up under a father's boat or a smith's forge, knowing the smell of wet wool and hot stone before she knew much else. Her mother worked a frame with two others of the household in a shed built onto the north side of the house, out of the wind, and Beitris was set to carding before she could properly hold the comb, and to the spindle before she was tall enough to reach the frame's upper bar without a stool under her. By her twelfth year she could spin a thread even enough that her mother stopped checking it against her own; by her twentieth she had her own frame, raised in the same shed against the wall her mother's stood against, and a name on that stretch of the stream for cloth that lay flatter and held its dye better than any other hand's.

She was a tall woman, black-haired, with hands that by her thirtieth year had gone hard and quick at the knuckle the way a weaver's hands go, and a face that people who did not know her called plain and people who did know her called honest, which came, most who had dealt with her agreed, to much the same thing said two ways. She was not, in her own household's telling, a woman who went looking for anything beyond the shed and the stream and the yearly walk down to the salt gathering with the household's cloth loaded on two pack-beasts; she went because the household's cloth had to be carried somewhere to be traded, and she carried it herself rather than trust the carrying to a brother who could not tell a well-set warp from a badly one, and that was the whole of her reason, or so she told it afterward, more times than she can have believed it herself by the end of the telling.

The ford gathering in those years drew households from every part of the hill's growing country, and the king walked it, as he had walked it since the first summer it was agreed, buying nothing that anyone at Muileann leathan could remember him buying, looking at everything with the same unhurried attention a man gives ground he means to farm rather than ground he is only passing through. Beitris had seen him at a distance twice before, in the ordinary way anyone who came to the ford enough summers running saw him, a tall plain-dressed man the crowd gave way from without seeming to notice it had moved; she had never spoken near him, had never particularly wished to, and would have said, asked beforehand, that a weaver's business at the ford was with the salt-traders and not with kings.

It was her cloth that brought him to her stall the summer she turned nineteen years past her fortieth, not her face nor her name, which he did not yet know. He stopped at the length she had laid out on the trestle, a bolt dyed the deep blue-black that only the northern lichen gave, and ran two fingers along the selvedge the way he ran a thumb along a flint edge, testing nothing a stallholder could see him testing, and said, after a silence long enough that the woman at the next stall had time to nudge her neighbour and nod toward him, that he had not seen cloth lie so flat off a frame in longer than she would believe if he told her the number of years.

"It is the weights," Beitris said, because it was the truest answer and the only one she had ready. "Clay, fired hard, and hung even. A frame that is not weighted even pulls the warp crooked, and a crooked warp makes crooked cloth no matter how straight the hand that works it."

He asked her, then, whether she had made the weights herself, and she said she had, her mother having taught her the firing of them along with everything else the shed held; and he asked whether the shed at Muileann leathan could be found again by a man who did not know the stream well, and she said, drily, that a shed with three looms in it and the whole household's washing hung to dry beside it was not commonly mistaken for anything else on that stream, whoever was looking for it. It is the only exchange between them that any teller at Muileann leathan claims to have had whole from a witness who stood near enough to hear it, and the weavers of that stream have told it, ever since, near enough word for word, adding nothing and, they say, taking nothing away.

## III. Of the Wedding and the Grey Winter

He came to Muileann leathan himself the following spring, with no more company than two men leading spare horses, and stood in the shed a full morning watching Beitris work a frame while her mother, who had by then guessed a good deal more than her daughter had told her, kept the rest of the household well away from the door on one excuse and another. What passed between them in that morning no teller has ever claimed to know; what is told is that he left before noon, and that Beitris did not touch the frame again that day, and that within the year she had gone south to Dùn ìseal as his wife, in the sixteen hundred and ninetieth year of the hill, the household at Muileann leathan keeping her old frame standing in the shed against her return, which some of them, in the early years, still half expected.

She wove at Dùn ìseal as she had woven at Muileann leathan, raising a frame of her own in a shed built to her asking on the south side of the king's house, and the hill's women, who had twined their cloth by hand or worked smaller frames copied badly from travellers' tellings, came to her in twos and threes to learn the standing weights properly set, so that within a handful of years the craft had spread from her own shed to a dozen others on the hill without her troubling to teach it as a teaching, only as a woman teaches a neighbour who stands and watches long enough. She did not hold herself above the hill's other wives for being the king's; she held herself, if anything, rather below them in her own reckoning, being a weaver's daughter among households that could trace their blood, some of them, back to the six pots under the oaks, and she never once, in any telling that survives her, put on an airs a weaver's daughter had no true right to.

In the tenth year of her marriage, in a winter that began no differently than any winter she had known on the hill, the king's hair went grey.

It went in a single season, not thinning first the way an ordinary man's hair thins toward grey over years of small changes no one notices until they are done, but all at once, dark to grey between one moon and the next, as though a frost had come down on him the way frost comes down on bracken, browning it all at one stroke rather than leaf by leaf. He said nothing of it, and no one at Dùn ìseal that winter was fool enough to ask him plainly what had brought it on, though it was noticed, and talked of low at every hearth on the hill, that he did not sit as still that winter as his custom was, and was seen more than once standing alone at the bank in weather no sensible man stood out in for pleasure. The grey did not go on. It came that one winter, took him entirely, and stopped, so that from that season to the end of any telling that reaches so far, he wore the one grey and no more of it, neither balding nor whitening further as an ordinary man's grey goes on to do, a man who had aged, once, exactly as far as one winter would carry him and then stood still again.

Beitris never asked him the cause of it either, not that winter nor after, though she was near enough to him, by then, that a lesser woman might have thought the asking her right. She told her own mother once, years later, on one of the two visits she ever paid back to Muileann leathan, that she had lain awake more nights that winter than she had lain awake in the whole of her life before it, though not from any fear of him, for she had none; only from a plain unwillingness to close her eyes on a man whose face had changed in his sleep once already and might, for all she then knew, change again before morning. It did not change again. By spring the grey had settled into him the way a colour settles into dyed wool, and the hill, which had talked of little else through the dark months, found other matters to talk of by Bealltainn and let this one rest, as a hill will.

Murchadh was born the following year, her only child by him and, as it happened, the only child either of them would raise together to see him grown, a black-haired boy from his first hour with his mother's hands already showing in the shape of his own small ones, quick fingers that closed round whatever was put into them and would not easily be made to let go. He was given his father's warmth from his first winter, though no one on the hill ever said so plainly in his hearing; the women who nursed him alongside their own children remarked only, low, that he ran hot for an infant and slept sound through cold that had other babies crying half the night, and left it at that, the way the hill had learned to leave a good many things at that.

## IV. Of the Rock at Ros dhomhain

In the eighteenth year of her marriage, with Murchadh not yet three and already walking further than his mother thought wise, the king took Beitris west with him, the two of them and the boy and a small household of the king's own choosing, to a harbour on a wide sea-loch called Ros dhomhain, where a fisher's household had lately begun to cut a mark against the tide on a boulder in the harbour mouth, in the same manner as the first mark cut, years before, on some rock further south whose keeper no one at Ros dhomhain had ever met. Word of the first rock's marks had travelled up the coast the way such word travelled, by trader and by kinsman visiting kinsman, and a young keeper at Ros dhomhain, hearing of it, had gone down to his own harbour mouth and begun a rock of his own, cutting the marks at what he judged, by ear and by memory of the telling, to be the same spacing as the first, though he had never seen the first rock with his own eyes and could not have said, pressed on it, how he knew the spacing to copy rather than invent his own.

The king stood before that second rock the better part of an afternoon, in much the same stillness Beitris remembered from the ford, running a finger down the row of cuts without pressing hard enough to mark the stone further, and said, when at last he turned from it, that whoever had carried the habit up this coast had carried a thing worth more than the fish it never counted. Beitris, standing by with Murchadh's small hand in hers, asked him plainly what he thought the marks were for, since no keeper of either rock had ever given her a straight answer to the question.

"For whoever comes after and wants to know how far the water has climbed," he said, "though I do not think the man who cut the first mark knew that was his reason, any more than the man who cut this one knows it now. A thing can be worth doing before anyone has worked out why."

It was, Beitris told her mother on the second of her two visits home, the longest single answer she ever had from him on any matter that was not a household's grazing or a stranger's quarrel, and she carried it the rest of her life the way another woman might have carried a gift he had given her outright. She watched the keeper's daughter, a girl not yet ten, standing at her father's elbow that same afternoon with the same unhurried attention the king himself gave the rock, and thought, though she said this to no one for years, that the habit of watching closely was its own kind of inheritance, passed from a father's hand to a child's eye with no more ceremony than the standing frame had passed from her own mother's shed to her own.

They stayed at Ros dhomhain the better part of a month, longer than the king's business at any one harbour commonly ran, and Beitris wove nothing the whole time she was there, content, for once, to sit on the harbour wall with Murchadh and watch the boats come in on the tide the rock had been cut to measure, the first true idleness, she said afterward, she had allowed herself since she was old enough to hold a comb. It did not last. Within the year she had a frame standing again, this time in a shed at Dùn ìseal built larger than the first to hold two more of the hill's women who had come, by then, to learn from her rather than merely to borrow her pattern of weights; and the harbour at Ros dhomhain kept its rock and its keeper's line long after the king and his household had ridden east again, the marks creeping upward summer on summer at a pace too slow for any one watcher to swear to, though every watcher who kept the count across two generations or more could point to the difference and say, plainly enough, that the water stood higher on the stone than it had at the first cutting.

## V. Of the Names She Was Given

Beitris aged as her own mother had aged, and her mother's mother before that, in the ordinary way of a woman born after the grove but not born of the king's own blood: slowly at first, so slowly that Murchadh, growing through his own first thirty years faster in every visible way than his mother grew old in any of them, did not remark it; and then, past her hundredth year, more plainly, her black hair going the iron-grey of an ordinary long life rather than the single frost-stroke that had taken her husband's, her hands stiffening at the knuckle past what even a weaver's hard use could account for, her back bending a little at the frame where it had once stood straight through a full day's work without complaint. She kept the loom to the end, working shorter lengths in her last years and letting the younger women she had taught finish what her own hands could no longer hold taut, and she never once, in any telling the hill kept of her, spoke of the difference between her own slow going and her husband's stopped clock as anything but the plain order of things, no more worth resenting than the tide is worth resenting for going out.

It was in her hundred and thirty-ninth year, with Murchadh a grown man of thirty-nine already spoken of on the hill for the north he meant one day to hold, that she asked him, on an ordinary evening with nothing in it to have prompted the question beyond her own long turning of it, whether he would forget her once she was gone the way a man forgets an old winter's cold once summer has come round again properly.

He did not answer at once, which was, by then, no more than his ordinary way with any question that mattered to him; and when he did answer, he gave her a name rather than any plain word either way. He said it quietly, the way a man says a name he has said to himself many times and rarely aloud, and then he said another, and then a third, and Beitris, listening, understood before the fourth name that he was giving her every wife he had had before her, in the order he had had them, going back further than any list the hill itself had ever troubled to keep whole.

She tried, at first, to hold the count on her fingers, the way she had counted threads on a warp her whole life without thinking about the counting; she lost it somewhere past the second hand, the names coming steadier and more quietly than she could keep pace with, some she half knew already from the hill's own old tellings, Muireall and Sorcha among the first of them, and others she had never once heard spoken in all her years on the hill, names that belonged, she understood as he went on, to wives the hill itself no longer kept any memory of at all, buried and mourned and forgotten by every mouth but his own. He did not hurry through them, and he did not slow for effect either; he said each one the way a man reads a tally he has read to himself so many times that the reading has stopped being an effort and become instead a kind of breathing. When he had done, he said nothing further, no word of what the naming meant or did not mean, and Beitris, who had asked the question hoping for comfort and had been given instead something closer to the truth than comfort commonly is, found that she no longer wanted an answer to the question she had actually asked. She had her answer. It was that he would not forget her, because he had not forgotten any of them, and that this was, for a man built as he was built, neither a kindness nor a cruelty but simply the shape of the thing, the way a warp holds every thread it was ever strung with whether the weaver remembers stringing each one or not.

She died four years after, in a mild autumn with the harvest well in and nothing wrong with her that any teller ever named beyond the plain wearing-out of a life lived its full ordinary length, in her hundred and forty-fourth year and Murchadh's thirty-ninth, a black-haired man who looked, to any stranger who did not know his true years, no older than fifteen summers past his boyhood, standing at her graveside with a stillness that the hill, watching him, took at first for a young man's want of understanding and only slowly came to read as something nearer his father's own. The king buried her with his own hands, as he had buried Muireall and Sorcha before her and would bury few enough others after, and sat by the turned earth a night and a day, and spoke, in all that time, only once, low enough that only Murchadh, kneeling near, ever claimed to have heard it: that a warp holds what it is strung with, and that he had never yet had to learn a cloth again from the beginning. He said nothing further of her after that day to any ear the hill's tellings reach, any more than he had spoken again, in all the years since, the names of the wives who came before her.
