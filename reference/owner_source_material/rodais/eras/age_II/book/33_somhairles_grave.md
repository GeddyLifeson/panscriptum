# Of Somhairle's Grave

*GE 3,001 – 3,030*

*Clann na Ceiste say the Line was given whole to one who was not of Somhairle's own blood only once in all the age, and that this was the once.*

## I. Of the Child Who Counted Names

Diorbhail was born at Tobar dhìreach in the year after the great gathering, in a house two hundred paces below the slab, and she was not three years old before the households of Na Glinn Àrda had begun to remark on her the way a valley remarks on any child who does a thing early: she could say her own line, mother to grandmother to great-grandmother, four generations back, before she could reliably tie her own shoe, and she said it, her mother told the fire more than once, the way another child said a rhyme it liked the sound of, for the pleasure of the saying and not because anyone had asked her twice.

Somhairle heard of her the way he heard of most things in the valleys, late and sideways, from a herding woman who mentioned the child to him at a Bealltainn fire as an oddity worth a laugh, no more; and he might have let the matter lie there, as he had let a hundred smaller oddities lie in the long years he had gone round those fires, had he not, some months after, sat an evening at the same house himself and heard the girl, then five, correct her own grandmother on the order of two cousins' births without being asked and without seeming to know she had done anything worth remarking. He watched her a while after that without saying why he watched, the way a man watches a colt in a field before he has decided whether it is worth the breaking, and he came to no such decision aloud for some years more.

She was seven when he first sat her down by the slab, on a morning her mother could never afterward say he had asked for in so many words, only that he had come to the door and said the child might walk up with him if her mother had no need of her that morning, and her mother, who like every household in the valleys had long since stopped questioning what Somhairle wanted with an hour of anyone's time, had let her go. He gave her nothing that first morning but the names of the slab, what it was and where its stain came from and why no household built a byre against it, told the way he told any child the same thing, in the plain voice of a man handing over a fact rather than a mystery; and he sent her home before noon with nothing more asked of her than that she come again if she cared to.

She cared to. She came again the next week, and the week after, walking up the two hundred paces on her own once her mother judged the path safe enough for a child that age, and by the time she was ten the valleys had begun to say of her, in the flat way they said such things, that she went to the slab the way another girl went to the burn for water, as an errand too ordinary to remark on twice. What passed between the old man and the child on those mornings was not, in those first years, the Line, for Somhairle held, as his own father was said to have held before him, that a thing given too soon to too young a head is a thing given badly; he gave her instead the small tellings that ringed the Line the way bark rings a tree, the tale of the coal fallen in seven pieces, the tale of the crack and its white stones, the names of the watch-households back three rounds, small enough matters that a wrong word in the telling cost nothing worse than his patient correction and a telling again.

## II. Of the Years Before the Fire

Beathan's son sat the watch those years as his father had sat it before him, and as his father's father had before that, a broad quiet man the valleys had stopped, long since, remarking on for anything but the ordinary steadiness of a household that kept its turn without complaint; and he watched the old man take up the girl the way a man watches a neighbour's matter, closely enough, but from outside the fence of it. It was known in the valleys, though never said outright at any fire Diorbhail sat by, that the watch and the reciting had always run in different hands, the one kept by whoever of Somhairle's own blood cared to sit it, the other given to whoever of Clann na Ceiste's wider households could carry it best; and if Beathan's son thought once or twice, in those years, that the reciting might in time have come to his own house rather than a stranger's, he gave the thought no more room than a man gives a draught under a door he has learned to live with, and sat his own turns at the slab with no less care for the not having it.

Diorbhail turned twelve, and then fifteen, growing tall and quick-tongued in the way of a child raised half by her own mother's hearth and half by an old man's patient repetitions, and Somhairle, who by then had begun himself to feel the years in his hands on the coldest mornings, though he said nothing of it to anyone, gave her, a little at a time, the outer branches of the Line: the households that had married into the king's blood and out of it again, the names the Line kept only because a marriage had once touched them and then let them go. She learned quickly, and corrected him twice in those years on a name he had himself begun to give wrong, both times a small slip of an ordering he had held the same way for centuries and had, without noticing, let time wear at like a stone worn at by the same running water; and both times he thanked her plainly and set the name right in his own next telling, without any shame in the setting right that she could ever afterward remember seeing on his face.

There was a boy of the basket-weavers' household, a cousin of hers by the shoulder of the hill and no relation at all by the Line, who went up with her twice that same year out of plain curiosity and came away both times saying he could make no more sense of it than of the crack under the slab, and would rather learn to weave a creel that held water than a string of names he could not use to catch so much as a fish; and Diorbhail, who liked him well enough at the ordinary fires, found she had no answer for him either, beyond that the names had never once, in all her going up, felt to her like a weight she was made to carry, only like a shape she already knew the feel of before she was given the words for it. He went back to the baskets, and neither of them thought the less of the other for the going, and it became, as such partings do in a small country, only one more fact of the valleys: that the slab took some and let others alone, and asked no household to explain which was which.

By her sixteenth year she had begun, without being told to, to test her own memory against the households' own telling of themselves at the ordinary fires, asking an old woman for her mother's mother's name before anyone had thought to volunteer it, and setting it beside what Somhairle had already given her to see that the two held together; and twice in that testing she found a small gap, a name a household kept differently from the way the Line kept it, and brought both tellings up to Somhairle rather than choose between them herself, for she had learned early that a gap brought to him whole was worth more than a gap already guessed shut. He heard her out both times without hurry, turned the two tellings over against what his own father had given him, and settled each in its turn, once for the household's telling and once for the Line's, explaining to her afterward, so that she would know the reason and not only the answer, why the one had held truer than the other.

It was in her eighteenth year that the valleys first heard him say, at a Samhain fire with the whole round of watch-households gathered close against the cold, that he did not know how many more Samhains he would sit among them, said in the plain voice he used for any fact and not as a thing wanting comfort in return; and the households, who had never in living memory heard him say such a thing, fell quiet round the fire the way a gathering falls quiet when an old man says a true thing nobody present is ready to hear said aloud. Diorbhail, sitting that night at the edge of the fire's light with the other young of the round, heard it and understood, better than most of the children beside her, what the saying meant for her own two hundred paces up the hill each week, though she said nothing of it that night to anyone, and went home and lay a long while awake before she slept.

## III. Of the Line Given Whole

He gave it to her whole in her twentieth year, on a night in the dark half of that year when the watch-household's fire was banked low against the slab's foot and Beathan's son sat that turn, close enough to hear what passed between them though he had not been asked to and did not ask afterward what he had heard. Somhairle had told her, some days before, only that she was to come up after dark and to eat well before she came, for the thing he meant to give her that night would want the whole of a night to give and take no shorter telling than that; and she had gone up her two hundred paces with her heart going harder than the climb alone accounted for, not from fear of the thing coming to her, for she had known for some years now what was coming to her, but from the plain weight of being the one it was coming to.

He began, as his own father had given it to him and as he had given it to Beathan before her, with the grove and Ailean at the fire that did not go into any other hand, and worked forward from there, generation on generation, wife on wife and child on child, the short lines and the long, the ones who had died before their fathers and the ones the Line kept only as a bare name and a bare year because no telling had come down with anything fuller; and he said it through once without a pause long enough to let her ask a question, the way it had always been given, twice through and no name out of place, and only at the second telling did he stop, here and there, to let her say a stretch of it back to him and hear for herself where her own memory had already caught it and where it had not.

The night ran long past the middle of its dark, and Beathan's son fed the fire twice more than his own turn usually asked of it, saying nothing, only watching the old man's mouth working through names that had, by his own reckoning, been said at that same slab longer than any watch-household's memory reached back; and when at last Somhairle came to the end of the second telling, to her own name set at the foot of it, he sat a while without speaking at all, his hands loose over his knees, and told her only, in a voice gone hoarse with the length of the saying, that she had it now the way he had had it, and that what she did with it from that night forward was hers to answer for and no longer his.

She walked home in the grey of the morning with the whole of it turning over in her own head, generation on generation, the way a person walks home turning over a weight they have only just learned they are strong enough to carry; and it is told, though the Line does not keep who first said it, that from that morning the households of Na Glinn Àrda began, without any of them quite deciding to, to bring their own children's names to Diorbhail at the fires rather than to Somhairle, the way a valley turns by degrees from an old well to a new one dug beside it, the old well no less good for the turning, only no longer the one a hand reaches for first.

## IV. Of the Withering

The years after that giving were kinder to no one's hands than the years before them, least of all to Somhairle's own. His face, which had held the same lines for so many centuries that the valleys had stopped remarking on it the way they had stopped remarking on Beathan's son's steadiness, began at last, in the seasons after the great giving, to fall in the way a fruit falls in that has been kept a season past its ripeness, the skin drawing close over the bone beneath it until an old woman of the watch-households, seeing him at a distance one Bealltainn, said to her own daughter, not unkindly, that he had come at last to look like nothing so much as a withered apple left too long on the sill; and the saying, once said, travelled the valleys the way such sayings do, and stuck to him the way a byname sticks to a face that has worn the same look too long to shed it, until the households spoke of him by it as easily as they spoke of the slab.

He kept coming to the fires all the same, though slower on the path than any of them had known him, leaning at last on a stick a herding boy had cut him from the hazel above the burn, and he sat still through whole tellings without correcting a word, listening to Diorbhail say back to him a branch of the Line he had given her years before, nodding when she had it right and saying nothing at all when, once or twice, a small thing had shifted in her keeping that had not been in his; for he had come, by then, to hold that a Line kept forward by a living mind was worth more than a Line kept exact by a dying one, and he let her small changes stand without troubling either of them with the correcting of them.

Beathan's son watched the old man fail with the plain grief of a household that has sat beside a fire so long it has stopped imagining any other arrangement, and did for him, in those last seasons, what no one asked him to and he never named aloud: walked up before each Samhain to see the path cleared of the season's first fallen branches so an old man's stick would not catch on one in the dark, and sat longer at the slab on the nights Somhairle came up to it than his own turn strictly asked of him, so that there would always be a fire built up and someone by it if the old man wanted company on the climb down.

It was in the last of those years that Somhairle told Diorbhail, at a fire with no one else near enough to hear it, the one thing his own father had never told him: that he did not know, himself, what waited for a man of the grove's own blood who had never taken a wife of the king's giving nor a share of the king's kingdom, once his own years ran out; that his brothers and sisters who had taken wives and shares had each, in their dying, been carried into some telling that named them by their hall or their household, and he had no hall and no household to be carried into, only the slab and the Line and the valleys that had let him sit their fires the better part of thirty centuries without once asking what he was to them beyond a teller of names. Diorbhail had no answer for that either, being twenty-eight by then and no wiser in such matters than he was, and she told him only that the valleys would know what to make of his going when it came, the way they had always known what to make of everything else that came to them without warning, and he laughed at that, the first time she had heard him laugh in some years, and said she had more of Clann na Ceiste in her than her own mother's line accounted for.

## V. Of the Grave

He died at Tobar dhìreach in her thirty-fifth year, in the season the valleys keep for turning peat, and it is told that Beathan's son found him at the slab in the grey of a morning, sitting against the stone the way he had sat against it a thousand mornings before, his stick fallen just out of reach of the hand that had held it, and no mark on him anywhere that anyone could point to, only the stillness of a man who had finished a long day's walking and sat down at last to rest from it. Word went down from the valleys before noon that same day, carried by a boy on the fastest pony the round could put under him, and it went, as such word had always gone in that country, straight to Dùn ìseal and to no other hall first. He had lived two thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven years, by the Line of the Mason's own counting, and died before his father all the same, as every child of the grove would in the end.

The king came up from the kingdom on the third day, on foot the last two miles of it though he had ridden the rest, and the old people of Clann na Ceiste stood about the grave they had by then already dug, close by the slab on the side away from its stain, and looked at the two of them, the father with his straight back and grey hair and the son laid out in his shroud with a face like a withered apple, and none of them, not even Somhairle's own reciters, could find a word worth saying into that silence. Diorbhail stood among them with the whole of the Line sitting in her own head the way it had sat there fifteen years now, and found that she too had nothing to say, though she had, an hour before, half believed there would be some line of the telling that would rise up in her and fit the moment; there was none, and she let the not-having-one stand rather than force a thing that did not want forcing.

The king put his son into the ground himself, kneeling at the grave's edge in the plain herdsman's clothes he had worn, by every telling that has ever come down of him, since long before Diorbhail's own grandmother was born, and he did the work with his own two hands the way any household buried its own dead in those valleys, taking no help offered him though three men of the watch-households stood ready to give it. Then he sat down on the turf beside the closed grave with his arms round his knees, like a boy, and stayed there. The households drifted back toward their own fires as the light went, one by one and then in twos, until only Beathan's son and Diorbhail were left standing near enough to see him, neither of them willing to be the one who went first, and it was full dark before one of the older reciters, sent up at last by the others waiting below, went to him and asked whether he would come in to the fire.

"I have buried a wife in every century," said Ailean Mòr, "and a son before this one; and I shall bury them all. That was the bargain. I did not know it was the bargain when I made it."

He said nothing further that the Line has kept, and after a while he rose of his own accord and came down to the fire the reciter had asked him to, and sat among the households of the round until the grey of the next morning, saying little, listening more than once to Diorbhail say back a stretch of the Line at another's asking, and once, when she came to Somhairle's own name set near its middle, closing his eyes a moment the way a man closes his eyes against a light too bright to look at straight, though the fire that night gave no more light than any other fire.

## VI. Of the Keeping

He went back down to the kingdom within the week, and the valleys of Na Glinn Àrda closed over the going the way water closes over a stone dropped into it, quickly on the surface and slower underneath, where the households went on for years after speaking of the old man's dying at odd hours, over a distaff or a half-turned peat, the way a household speaks of a thing it has not finished carrying yet. Beathan's son took up, without any household troubling to make it a formal thing, the watch at the slab as his own to keep entire, the way his father had kept it before Somhairle's giving had ever divided the watching from the reciting between two hands; and no one asked him whether he minded that the reciting had gone, these fifteen years past, to a household not his own, for by then it had long ceased to be a thing anyone in the valleys thought worth asking after.

Diorbhail kept the Line the way she had been given it, whole and by mouth, adding to its foot each year the names the fires brought her and setting nothing down on any leaf or hide that a fire or a flood might one day take from her, for that was the way it had always been kept and she had been given no reason, in all her years at Somhairle's own knee, to keep it any other way. She said it through, twice each Samhain as he had said it, to whichever of the watch-households cared to sit and hear the whole of it rather than only their own branch of it, and she took, from the households of Na Glinn Àrda and from Dùn ìseal both, no share of anything for the keeping, holding, as Somhairle had held before her and told her plainly enough in her twentieth year that she must go on holding, that a Line taken up for a share was a Line a household soon began to shape toward that share, and that the valleys had kept theirs unshaped this long only by keeping it, and its keepers, apart from any hall's giving.

By the year the reciters set at the close of that decade the households of Na Glinn Àrda had stopped, in their ordinary speech, calling her the girl who had learned it from Somhairle, and had begun instead to call her only the reciter, the way they had once called him only the keeper of the slab, a name worn down at last to the office and away from the person who had first worn it; and it is told that when a stranger came up the two hundred paces one Bealltainn some years after, asking whether the old man who kept the Line of the Mason might be found at the slab, Diorbhail answered him herself, in the same plain voice Somhairle had used for any fact worth handing over and no other, that the keeper he asked for had gone into the ground some years since, and that the Line he had kept was kept still, by her own mouth now, and by no hall's leave.
