# Of Salt for Wool

*GE 1,174 – 1,273*

*At the ford it is still said that the water remembers two men better than it remembers any king, though neither of them was one.*

## I. Of the Hill Without Salt

Raghnall Ceannfhionn was four hundred and fourteen years old the summer he first rode down to look at his grandfather's salt-kists and found three of the six empty and the other three going the same way, and he was, by the reckoning of Dùn ìseal, still a young man; his hair was the fair, pale colour that had given him his name from a boy, and it had not so much as dulled in all the years he had held the hill for the king, which by then was the better part of four hundred and fifteen of them, though no one troubled any longer to say when the holding had begun, only that it had, the way a river is said to run and not to have started running.

The hill in those years wanted for nothing that grass could grow. Dòmhnall Clachach's grandsons and Fearchar's, and Raghnall's own sons besides, had between them more sheep on the slopes above Dùn ìseal than any household could shear in a season without borrowed hands, and more wool piling in the byres each spring than the weavers could work before the next clip came down on top of it; a man could walk from the gate to the high pasture and never be out of sight of fleece, grey and black and the pale undyed white that the hill favoured, and think, if he had never travelled, that wool was the one thing the world was short of nowhere. What the hill did not have, and had never had in any quantity a household could keep a winter on, was salt. What little came up from the coast came dear, carried on the backs of traders who took as long over the hills as the weather let them and charged for every mile of it, and a bad autumn for the traders was a hungry spring for anyone who had salted less meat than the winter asked of him.

It was old Gormshuil's stores Raghnall had opened that morning, or what had once been hers and had passed, kist and key, through Dòmhnall and down to him with the rest of the holding, and it was the sight of them near empty, when he had thought there was salt enough laid by for two winters yet, that put the thing in his mind properly for the first time, though he had known the shape of it since he was a boy young enough to still be growing. He stood in the low stone room a long while with the lid up and the smell of brine gone stale in his nose, and thought less about the salt than about a five days' ride to the west, on the shore of the lake, there sat a hill that had salt running out of its own ground in pans the sea filled twice a day for nothing, and sheared no wool worth the name, and that the two hills had sworn to the same king for close on nine hundred years and had never in all that time sent so much as a basket the one to the other.

He rode to Dùn ìseal's own gate the same week and put it to the king plainly, the way he had learned to put things to him, which was without wrapping. "The Red Hill has salt it doesn't want and we have wool it does. Let me go and settle it. They are both yours; I will only be doing what you'd do yourself if you rode there."

The king was mending a bridle at the time, sitting on the low wall by the well with the leather across his knee and an awl in his hand, and he did not look up from it for the length of Raghnall's whole speech, which was simply how he attended to most things that were not directly in front of him, and no unkindness. When he did look up it was to say, "They are both mine. I will not do their talking." And then, before Raghnall could take that for a refusal and go, he added, in the same flat voice he used for everything, "You can do it, or it can go undone. It makes no difference to me which."

It was as close to a blessing as the king's kin had learned to expect from him on such matters, and Raghnall, who by four hundred years old had long since stopped waiting for a warmer one, took it as such and rode home to choose his company.

He had asked, before he left the well, whether the king would send word ahead of him, a name or a token the Red Hill would know for the king's own, and the king had said no to that too, in the same flat way. "If they'll take wool from you for what it is, they'll take it. If they need my name on it first, it was never worth carrying." Raghnall had ridden away turning that over more than he turned over most of what the king said to him, for it seemed to him, on the road west, less a refusal than a kind of trust laid on him sideways, the way the king laid most things he meant to matter: never handed over outright, only left within reach of a man willing to pick it up and carry it himself. The hill was fifty-five miles from the Red Hill by the straightest road a horse could keep to, and the ford lay some forty-three of those miles out, nearer the lake than the moor, at a crossing neither household had built and neither, so far as Raghnall could learn, had ever needed to ask the other's leave to use.

## II. Of the Wading at the Ford

He took nine men with him and no more, for a company of any greater size would have looked, on a road that had never in living memory carried the hill's spears anywhere near the lake shore, less like a settling of business than a raid got up in wool merchants' clothing, and Raghnall had no wish to be met at the water by men already counting his numbers against their own. It was a five days' journey by the shortest road he knew, west and a little south, over the moor where Dòmhnall's boundary stones still stood in their quarrelling rows and down through country the hill's shepherds walked every summer without ever once, so far as Raghnall could learn by asking, having spoken a word to the households on the far side of it. Word had gone ahead of him by a faster rider all the same, for the Red Hill's own herders had eyes, and by the fourth day Raghnall's company was passing through country where children stood well back from the road to watch them go by and said nothing to anyone until they had passed.

They came down to the ford on an evening of early summer, the river low and clear over its stones the way it ran in that season before the autumn rains filled it, wide enough at that crossing that a man standing in the middle of it could not easily be reached from either bank without getting wet to do it, which was, Raghnall thought afterward, very likely why it had been chosen for whatever had been settled there before his own people ever came to it, though he never learned what that earlier use had been, if it had one. On the far bank, drawn up along the shingle in a loose line that was trying not to look like a line, stood a company near the size of his own, and at the front of it, alone a pace ahead of the rest, a broad, weathered man past his middle years whose name the reciters of neither hill, then or after, ever troubled to set down, though both lists kept the deed whole. Whether the omission was the man's own choosing, a holder who wanted the meeting remembered and not himself, or only an accident of two households each waiting for the other to ask first and neither ever doing it, no one now living could say, and it had not seemed, at the time, worth anybody's asking.

The two companies looked at each other across the water for longer than was comfortable, long enough that one of Raghnall's own men muttered that they might as well make camp and settle it in the morning, and it was the river, running loud enough over its stones to cover any silence a man cared to stand in, that made the waiting bearable at all. Then Raghnall walked his horse to the edge of the shingle, dismounted, handed the reins to the man beside him, and walked into the ford alone, without spear or shield, his cloak gathered up out of the water in one fist and the cold of it climbing his legs with every stride until it stood at his knees in the middle of the crossing, where he stopped.

He had thought, walking out, that he might have to stand there some while before the other made up his mind to answer it, and braced himself for the discomfort of the water without complaint, since a man who has asked another to trust him ought at least to be cold about it himself; but the Red Hill's holder was already moving before Raghnall had properly settled his feet on the gravel, wading out with the unhurried step of a man crossing his own dooryard, no cloak held up, no ceremony made of the wet, until the two of them stood face to face in the middle of the river with the current pulling at both their legs and nine men on either bank watching close enough to see, though not to hear.

What passed between them the reciters give short, not, Raghnall thought later, because there was more to it that had been lost, but because there had truly been little enough said, the whole of it fitting easily inside the time it takes cold water to numb a man's shins past feeling them. The hill would send wool, come the autumn shearing and every autumn after, and take salt for it, measured out however the two households agreed a fair measure was; neither would bring spears to the ford again, not that year nor any year after, so long as both sides held to the bargain; and if either hill's people broke faith with the other over the trade itself, it was the two hills' own quarrel to mend and none of the king's, since he had said as much himself and meant it. The Red Hill's holder put this last part to Raghnall almost as a question, watching his face for whether the king's own grandson would balk at being told his grandfather's word bound him, and Raghnall, who had heard the king say it to his own face not ten days before, only said that it did, and that he for one was glad of it, since a bargain a man had to keep because no one bigger would keep it for him was worth a great deal more to him than one he could run crying to the hill about. At that the older man laughed, once, short and surprised, as though he had not expected the king's blood to say a true thing about its own blood so readily, and put out his hand.

They clasped arms in the water, each man's grip made clumsier than it should have been by the current and the cold and by hands too numbed for a proper hold, and waded back each to his own bank without either of them once looking round to see whether the other made it, which struck Raghnall, drying his legs by a fire someone had got going with more speed than the evening strictly needed, as a kind of trust in its own right: that a man could turn his back on you in the middle of a river and not trouble to look and see you weren't drowning behind him.

## III. Of the Ground the Water Never Dried

The wool came that autumn as promised, and the salt in return, and if either side thought it had been cheated in the weighing neither said so loudly enough for it to reach the other hill's ears, and by the second year no one on either bank had troubled to ask whether they ought to meet again, only where and when, as though the question of whether had been settled for good in that one cold crossing and needed no further deciding. Raghnall did not go himself every year; there was a hill to hold and quarrels enough on his own side of the moor to keep a man from five days' travel each way whenever the wool was ready, and after the third meeting he sent his eldest son in his place more often than not, and after the sixth he sent whoever of his household happened to be free of other business, for the ford by then wanted less a king's grandson to stand in it than it wanted someone, anyone, of the hill's own blood or trust, to be there at the appointed time with the wool loaded and the scales honest.

It was the honesty of the scales, more than anything either holder had said in the water that first evening, that Raghnall came, as the years went on, to think had made the thing hold. A trade that cheats slowly dies slowly and no one troubles to mourn it; this one, weighed fair on both banks from the first autumn, grew instead the way a well-tended thing grows, without anyone deciding it should. By the fourth or fifth year traders who were no part of either household had begun coming down to the ford on the appointed days with flint from the eastern diggings and dried fish off the salt shore and pots thrown by potters neither hill had ever heard of, setting their goods out on the shingle a little apart from the wool and the salt as though asking permission of a custom not yet old enough to have a name for what it permitted; and no one turned them away, for turning a man away from a bargain that cost the hill nothing to allow was not a thing either Raghnall or the Red Hill's holder, nor after him his own son who took up the same watch, could see the sense of.

In the thirty-fourth year of the ford's meetings, which the reciters of Clann na Ceiste mark by counting back from a thing that happened rather later and is easier to fix a year to, a young man of the hill and a young woman of the Red Hill's people, who had between them done no more over three or four autumns than stand near enough to each other at the weighing to be remarked on by both their mothers, were married on the trodden ground, with both banks' households standing for it and the river running past unconcerned a few strides off, and it was the first marriage the ford ever gave anyone, though the reciters differ, as reciters will, on whether it was also the least remarkable, since by then half the young people on either bank had begun finding reasons of their own to be at the crossing on the appointed days whether they had wool or salt to weigh or not. Raghnall was there for that one, an old custom of his own by then, to see the ford give something back besides trade; and standing a little apart from the crowd of both households mixed together on ground that had been nothing but shingle and cold water thirty-four years before, he found himself thinking, not for the first time and not for the last, that a thing agreed between two men standing waist-deep and shivering had somehow, without either of them meaning it to, become a thing that no longer needed either of them at all to keep it going.

There was one year, the eleventh or the twelfth of the meetings, that the wool came down thin and poorly washed, a bad clip after a wet shearing season, and the Red Hill's people, who had weighed fair salt against fair wool every autumn before that one, said plainly at the ford that they would not weigh the same measure of salt against wool that stank of the fleece still on it. Raghnall was away in the north that year; his son stood on the hill's side of the scales and heard it, and who rode home afterward expecting, by his own later telling, to be sent straight back to the king to ask what was to be done about a bargain the west meant to break. Raghnall heard him out and asked only one thing in return, which was whether the wool truly was as poor as the Red Hill said. His son allowed that it was. "Then they're not breaking the bargain," Raghnall told him. "They're keeping it exactly as it was made, which was fair wool for fair salt, and this year we brought them the wrong half of it." He sent his son back the following spring with a better clip and, besides it, enough extra fleece to make up what the thin year had shorted them, and said nothing to the king about any of it, there being nothing in it that wanted a king's settling; and the Red Hill, for its part, never afterward pressed harder than a fair measure asked, nor did the hill ever again send down wool it had not first looked over with its own hands. It was the nearest the ford's custom ever came, in Raghnall's lifetime, to being tested, and it held by nothing written and nothing sworn beyond the two hills' own wish to keep being able to trust each other the following year, which Raghnall came in time to think was worth a good deal more than anything a law could have bound them to.

## IV. Of Murchadh Gearr on the Salt Road

The Red Hill's holder who had waded out that first evening held it a further sixty years and some, and was followed by a son and then, the son dying young of no more than an ordinary fever, by a grandson who held it only a little while before the hill passed, by whatever reckoning the west kept of such things, to a kinsman of the old holder's line named Murchadh Gearr, a short, thick-set man with a laugh too big for the size of him, who came to the ford for his first weighing already known on both banks as a man who walked everywhere he might as easily have ridden, on the grounds, he said, that a holder folk could see coming a mile off on his own two feet was a holder they trusted more than one who appeared among them suddenly from horseback, as though he had something to hide in the arriving.

He took the habit further than any before him. Every year from the salt-pans of Baile ghorm down to the ford, the length of what the traders by then had begun to call the salt road without any of them quite remembering who had first called it that, Murchadh Gearr walked it on his own feet in the weeks before the autumn meeting, stopping at every household along it whether they had wool or salt to send that year or not, eating what was set in front of him and asking after names he plainly already knew the answers to, so that by the time he came down to the water there was not a child on the road who could not say they had seen the Red Hill's holder pass, on foot, in the flesh, with his own eyes. Some of the older folk of both hills said he did it only for the love of being seen doing it, which was likely true and no fault in a man; others said, and this too Raghnall came to think likely, that a holder who let the folk forget him between one autumn and the next was a holder whose word, when it came time to weigh salt against wool, carried less than a stranger's, and that Murchadh Gearr, whatever his vanity, had understood that plainly enough to walk fifty miles a year rather than risk it.

Raghnall himself, by the year Murchadh Gearr first walked the road, had held Dùn ìseal the better part of five hundred years, and looked, so far as anyone below sixty could judge such a thing by eye, no older than the fair-headed young man who had waded into the ford at four hundred and fourteen; his own children by then had children with grey coming into their hair, and those children in turn were bringing wool to the ford themselves without any memory of a time the crossing had not been kept, so that to them the ford was simply a thing the world had always had, the way a hill has always had a river running past it, and not a bargain their grandfather's grandfather had once stood shivering in cold water to strike. It was a strangeness Raghnall had grown used to without ever quite growing easy in it, watching a thing he remembered making pass, year on year, out of anyone's living memory of its making and into the kind of fact a child simply knows without being told when he first learned it.

## V. Of the Trodden Ground

He came down to the ford himself for the last time in his hundredth year past the wading, an old habit more than a needed one by then, since the meeting by that year ran on custom thick enough that neither hill's holder needed stand in it to see it kept faith. It was a cool morning with mist still lying along the water when he arrived, the shingle already crowded with more traders and more households than he could remember it holding even ten years before, wool and salt and flint and pots and dried fish all laid out in their long accustomed rows, children of both banks running between the stalls in a way that made no distinction any longer between the hill's side of the water and the Red Hill's, and somewhere among the crowd, though Raghnall did not trouble to seek him out, Murchadh Gearr's big laugh sounding over the general noise the way it always did.

He stood a while on the bank, at the same stretch of shingle, near enough as made no difference, where he had stood before wading out the first time, and looked at the ground the ninety-nine autumns since had worn bare and hard of any grass, packed down by so many feet coming and going that it held, even in the wet of that morning, no mud to speak of, only a smooth dark path running down to the water's edge on both banks alike, wider now than any road the hill could show. It came to him, standing there, as an old thought finally said plainly, the way a thing known a long while sometimes waits years for its own saying, that the ground under his own feet would go on being trodden by feet that had never known his name and would not miss the knowing of it, long after the last of the people now crossing it had gone the way of everyone but him and the man mending a bridle by a well who had told him, on a morning near a hundred years gone, that the settling of it was Raghnall's to make or leave unmade and no business of his own either way. The king would outlast the ford, Raghnall thought, the way he had outlasted the grove and would outlast whatever came after the ford too; and Raghnall himself, four hundred years old the day he first stood shivering in that water and near five hundred and fourteen the morning he stood dry-shod on its bank watching strangers weigh salt against wool without so much as a glance toward the old man who had once made the weighing possible, understood, with no particular grief attached to the understanding, that he would be gone from the ford a long while before the ford was done with anyone.

He did not say any of this to the men who had ridden down with him, nor to his own son, who found him some while after, still standing on the same stretch of shingle and asked, not unkindly, whether he meant to stand there all morning or come and see the year's weighing properly begun. Raghnall said that he was coming, and went, and put his hand once, briefly, to the packed dark earth of the trodden ground before he did, the way a man touches a doorpost on his way out of a house he does not expect to enter again by the same door; and the ford, which had by then long since stopped needing either hill's holder to keep faith with the other, took no more notice of the gesture than the river had taken, all those years before, of two men wading back to their own banks without once looking round to see whether the other had made it safely home.
