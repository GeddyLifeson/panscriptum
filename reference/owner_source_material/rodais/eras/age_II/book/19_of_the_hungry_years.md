# Of the Hungry Years

*GE 1,893 – 2,020*

*At Dùn ìseal the grain-keepers still cut their tally by the count Sìleas Bhàn left them, and say so, though none now living remembers why the count runs the way it does.*

## I. Of the Store within the Bank

Sìleas Bhàn had held the hill the better part of four hundred years before the grazing failed her, and in all that time the list that kept her name had asked nothing more of her than that she go on holding it. She was of the king's blood, the eighth name in the list of those who had held Dùn ìseal, counting the king who took it first, and she had come to the hill already old enough in her own reckoning that the households below it had stopped remarking on her face and begun instead to measure their own lives by the fact that hers did not change; a woman married under her rule and buried under it still, and Sìleas no greyer for the burying.

She was a plain holder, by every account the hill has kept of her: quarrels judged the way her father's father's fathers had judged them before her, a turn taken at the ford gathering each summer, no law made and none broken. If any household asked what Sìleas Bhàn had done with her four hundred years, the honest answer, and the only one anyone at Dùn ìseal troubled to give, was that she had done what was asked and nothing more.

The store within the bank was not her own thing. It had stood since Dòmhnall's day, cut into the earthwork itself on the sheltered side, three pits lined with withies and clay against the damp, and it had been filled in good years and drawn down in poor ones for longer than any single holder's memory could account for by itself; but no one before Sìleas had ever emptied all three pits to the withies in a single winter, and no one, in the telling that came after, ever forgot that she was the one who did.

The winters before it had been ordinary winters, the summers ordinary summers, and no household on that ground, going into the spring of her three hundred and ninety-third year, had any reason to look at the sky over the high pastures with more than a farmer's usual wariness. The failure began in that spring, a spring so wet the high pastures would not dry enough to carry the flocks before Bealltainn, the rain coming in off the west for the better part of two months with hardly three dry days running between one soaking and the next, and by midsummer the grass that should have stood to a man's knee lay flattened and yellow, rotted at the root from standing water no one had seen the like of. Sìleas rode the near valleys herself that year, as she had never troubled to before, and found households already killing beasts they would ordinarily have kept through the winter, for want of anything to feed them; and she gave the order, without asking leave of anyone, that the store within the bank should be opened to every household of the hill's own ground by the count of mouths at its hearth, whatever the size of its herd.

Grain had come out of those pits before, in earlier lean years. This was the first time it had come out by that measure, and the households said so at the time, some of them with more thanks than others; a household with sixty beasts and four children found itself given no more than a household with four beasts and four children, and the man who had the sixty beasts rode up to the hill to say so to her face.

"You have taken from me and given it to a man who kept nothing," he said, standing in the yard with his hat still on, which was itself a kind of insult and both of them knew it.

"I have taken nothing from you," Sìleas said. "The grain in that pit was put there against a year like this one, by every household on this ground together, yours no less than his. It belongs to the year that made it needful, and to every hearth the year touches."

He kept his hat on to the end of it and did not ride up again, and by the second winter of the grazing's failure the households had begun, without any further order from her, to bring their own surplus to the pits before she asked for it, the way water finds the lowest ground without being told to.

The opening of the pits itself she never forgot, though nothing about it was remarkable to anyone who watched. Two men of the hill's own household went down into each pit in turn with baskets, working by the light let in through the mouth of it, and came up smelling of wet withy and old clay, a smell that clung to a man's clothes for a day afterward and that every household on that ground learned, that year, to know from a distance before the cart carrying the grain was near enough to see. Sìleas stood at the mouth of the first pit herself while it was opened, though there was no custom that asked it of her, and watched the level of grain in the baskets against what she remembered of the year it went in, saying nothing to the men below her, only counting.

By the second year the hunger had eased enough that a household or two began, on their own accord and without her leave, to bring back into the pits a little more than the pits had given them the winter before, a kind of paying-forward that no one had asked for and that Sìleas let stand without remark, for she judged, rightly as it turned out, that a custom left alone to grow its own root held firmer than one she might have ordered into being.

## II. Of Baile chrom

The grazing came back within three years, as such failures generally did on that ground, but the hunger it started did not stop at Dùn ìseal's own valleys, and the worst of it, when it came, came far off, on the north-western coast, at a scattering of hearths the hill-folk called Baile chrom for the crooked shore it sat on.

Word of it reached Sìleas the way word of most far things reached the hill in those years, slowly and by more than one mouth, each mouth a little more certain of what it had heard than the one before it: that the fish had failed there two seasons running, that the household stores had gone to bark and boiled seaweed before the second winter was out, that the dead were being laid in doubled rows because the ground could not be dug fast enough to keep pace with them, and that nothing so bad had ever, in any telling the coast itself kept, been tied to one place before.

The king rode north-west himself with the first grain that could be spared, in the last month of that same year, taking a road that had never carried him before and that the pack-horses found harder going than any road nearer the hill; he was gone the better part of a season, and no teller who rode with him ever said afterward what he found at Baile chrom when he came to it, only that he came back grey about the mouth in a way that had nothing to do with his hair, which had not changed, and that he gave no order when he returned beyond telling every hill of the kingdom to look to its own stores before the next storm season, and not to wait for hunger to ask first.

Sìleas did not go with him. She held the hill in his absence, as she had held it through every absence of every king before this one that the list remembered, and she sent after him what her own stores could spare, three cartloads of grain and a herd of goats too small to be missed from the near valleys, with instruction that they were to go wherever along that coast was worst served and not to Baile chrom alone, for she had heard, by then, that the crooked shore was not the only hearth burning low. The cartloads reached the coast a full month behind the king's own grain, and by every count that ever came back to the hill, they were not wasted.

The man who drove the foremost cart was still telling of it at Dùn ìseal's own hearths years afterward, an old carter by then and glad of an audience, and Sìleas herself sat through the telling more than once without saying she had heard it before. He told of hearths with no smoke over them that a passing man took at first for empty houses, until a dog came out from under one to bark at his horses, thinner than any dog he had ever driven cattle past; of a woman standing at her own door who would not take the grain into her hands until he had counted it out to her in front of her neighbours, for fear, she said, that a neighbour might call a full measure given behind a closed door a lie about by evening; of the grave-rows themselves, which he had seen from the road and would say no more about than that they ran two deep where at any other season they would have run one, and that he had made the sign against ill luck three times passing them and been glad of the horses' pace picking up of their own accord once the graves were behind. It was the doubled grave-rows of Baile chrom, more than anything the king himself said of that ride, that the hill-folk kept afterward as the measure of how bad a hunger could be tied to one place; and it is told that from that year forward, no household within a day's ride of Dùn ìseal let its own store run below a season's worth of grain again, whatever the grazing did, for fear of being asked one day what had become of it.

## III. Of Achadh shean

The eastern springs failed differently, and slower, and it took longer for anyone at Dùn ìseal to believe what the households of Achadh shean were telling them.

The first word came the year the wells there ran a hand lower than any digger's memory reached, which nobody thought worth a rider's time; the second came the year after, when the wells ran lower still and a shepherd's boy walked the whole way to the hill on his own two feet to say the upper field banks had cracked from the dryness, banks his own grandfather had raised and that had never once, in three generations of telling, cracked from anything but frost. The boy had walked the better part of three days to bring the word himself rather than send it by a rider who might, he said, get the shape of the cracking wrong in the telling, and he stood in the yard at Dùn ìseal with his feet raw through worn shoes and would not sit down to eat until he had said his piece through to the end. Sìleas had him fed and his feet looked to before she asked him anything further, and sent him home again on a borrowed pony rather than his own two feet, which the boy's household spoke of for longer, in the years after, than they spoke of the drought itself.

Sìleas sent no grain east that year, for there was, as yet, no hunger to send it against, only dry ground and a boy's report; but she sent a rider to look, and the rider came back saying the springs of Achadh shean had not merely run low, they had stopped, the whole visible flow of them, at a season when they had never in living telling stopped before.

By the third dry year the field banks east of the springs stood forsaken, the barley in them dead in the row and never cut, and the households that had worked that ground for longer than the list could reckon by name walked off it and cut new fields on another line, lower on the slope, where a smaller spring still ran thin but steady. They took nothing from the old banks with them but their own tools and the stones of their own hearths, and left the dead barley standing where a passing rider could see it for years after, going grey and then black with the weather, a field of dead grain kept upright by nothing but its own roots long after the households that had planted it were gone.

The moving itself took the best part of a month, one household at a time rather than all together, for no household would leave its own hearthstone cold a day longer than it had to before the new one was laid and lit; a woman of that ground, carrying her own infant on one hip and a bundle of tools on the other, was heard by more than one neighbour to say, passing the old banks for the last time, that the ground had given her mother's mother a living and given her nothing, and that she owed it no more grief over the leaving than a hall owed a guest who had stopped setting a place at the table. The saying went round the new fields for a season or two and then, as such sayings did, stopped being anyone's in particular and became simply a thing that was said of Achadh shean, by people who could no longer have told a stranger who had first said it.

Sìleas rode out to see it herself once, in the last year of the drought, and stood a while at the edge of the forsaken banks with the rider who had brought her the first word of it two years before, and neither of them said much. The rider asked her, at last, whether she meant to have the banks pulled down, since no one would work that ground again while the springs stayed dead.

"No," she said. "Let them stand. A bank tells a household what the ground once gave, and what it stopped giving. That is worth more standing than pulled down."

The banks stood. They are standing yet, by every account that has come down of that coast, grown over now with gorse and bramble in lines a stranger would take for nothing more than the shape of an old hedge, though the households of Achadh shean, cutting their new fields lower on the slope, have never once forgotten what the old lines mark.

## IV. Of the Deed and the List

It was in her five hundred and sixth year, with the drought at Achadh shean not yet a season closed and the grave-rows of Baile chrom forty winters behind her, that Sìleas Bhàn asked the king, at the ford gathering of that summer, for a thing no holder of the hill before her had ever troubled to ask for.

The ford that summer ran low and clear over its stones, the way it always ran in a dry year, and the households had pitched their fires further back from the water than usual for want of the shade the willows gave closer in; salt and wool and dried fish changed hands as they always did, and a marriage or two was agreed on the trodden ground, and nothing about the day marked it out from any other gathering Sìleas had sat in her four hundred years, until the crowd thinned toward evening and the cook-fires began to smoke low and steady into a sky gone the colour of a hearth's own embers. She had watched the king greet the households at the river as he greeted them every summer, no different to look at than he had been the first year she herself came down to the ford as a girl of thirty, and when the crowd had thinned she asked leave to walk with him a way along the bank, which he gave her without asking why.

"Dòmhnall set the boundary stones," she said, when they were far enough from the fires that no one else could hear the shape of it. "Fearchar rode with the valleys and was lamed for it. Uisdean burned a palisade twice and built it a third time. Mòrag raided the salt shore and kept the names of her dead. Iain Dubh died on the shingle for it. Raghnall waded the ford and made peace with the Red Hill that has held ever since. Every one of them has a deed the list keeps by. I have held the hill through a bad spring, a coast's worth of hunger I could only watch from too far off to mend, and a drought I answered with a rider sent to look at dry ground. When the list comes to my name, what will it say I did?"

The king walked on a few paces before he answered her, his eyes on the water rather than on her face, in the way she had noticed, over four hundred years, that he looked at most things he meant to answer plainly.

"It will say you held," he said. "That is not nothing, Sìleas. Dòmhnall's stones only moved the quarrels they were meant to end. Uisdean's palisade burned twice before it held once. Mòrag's raid cost seven lives to answer a want that a fair season would have answered for nothing. Every deed the list keeps was also a thing that might have gone the other way, and some of them did, before they went the way the list remembers. You gave grain by the mouth and not by the herd, and a man in a temper rode up to tell you so to your face, and the households have kept your measure since without being told to. Carts went a coast's width from your own ground to a hunger that was never yours to answer, and you did not wait to be thanked for sending them. A dead field stands at Achadh shean because you let it, so that a household two hundred years from now will know what its own ground once gave. That is the deed, Sìleas. Dòmhnall's stones and Mòrag's raid were each one thing done once; yours was four hundred years of not failing, in a country where failing was always the easier road."

"That is a hard thing to put in a list," she said.

"Most true things are," he said. "The list is not troubled by that. It will put it in four words, the same four it has kept for every holder who did not fail: she held the hill. I have never asked the list for more than that of anyone, and I will not begin with you."

She said nothing further to him about it that evening, and the reciters, when the telling of that summer's gathering came down to Dùn ìseal, kept nothing of the walk along the bank at all; but it is told, in the hill's own keeping rather than the reciters', that Sìleas Bhàn never again asked what her deed would be, and that in the four years left to her holding, she gave out grain, judged quarrels, and rode to look at dry ground exactly as she had before, without troubling herself further over what the list would say of any of it.

## V. Of the Giving of the Hill

Sìleas gave the hill to Tormod Liath in her five hundred and tenth year, on a morning in early spring with the near valleys still carrying the last of that year's snow on their northern faces and the yard at Dùn ìseal churned to mud by every household on the ground that could send even one pair of feet to stand in it. The giving followed the shape every holder before her had given it who was not carried out of the hall feet first: standing before the assembled households, in the same plain wool she judged quarrels in, and saying that the hill was his to hold now, and that she would answer no more quarrels from that day, and that the store within the bank was to be counted before witnesses that same week so that the new holder took it up knowing to the basket what stood in it.

Tormod was a son of Iain Dubh, born in her own father's time, a broad quiet man of thirteen hundred and seventy years who had spent the better part of them at the hill's own margins rather than at its centre, minding cattle on the poorer ground to the north and asking the hill for nothing beyond what any household asked of it. He took the giving without ceremony beyond what the custom itself required, knelt to Sìleas in the mud of the yard though she waved him up again before his knee had properly settled, and told the households, in his first words as their holder, that he meant to change nothing about the store within the bank, for he had watched it feed two hungers in his own lifetime from the outside and saw no reason a new holder should improve on a measure that had already held. It was the longest speech anyone at Dùn ìseal had yet heard out of him, and the last of any length for some years after.

Sìleas did not go far. She took a house in the near valleys, within a morning's ride of the hill, and kept a small garden there that asked less of the ground than a herd would have, and more than once in the years that followed a household came to her own door rather than the hill's, out of old habit, to ask a judgement she no longer had any right to give; she sent them back to Tormod every time, and told no one, in all the years the sending went on, what it cost her to do it. She never told the households that the rule they kept about the grain-pits, the one about the mouth and not the herd, had been hers and no one else's before it was theirs; it did not occur to her that it mattered whose it had first been, once it was kept without her having to ask for it. When at last her own reciters set down what the list would carry of her holding, they gave her the four words the king had promised her at the ford, no more and no fewer: she held the hill. The withy-lined pits are cut to her measure in that country still, and the households that fill them and empty them by turns have long since stopped asking after the woman who first ordered it so.
