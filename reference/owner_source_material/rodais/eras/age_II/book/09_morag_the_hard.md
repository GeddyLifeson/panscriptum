# Of Mòrag the Hard

*GE 672 – 719*

*At Dùn ìseal the reciters give her the one deed and no other, and say that one is enough to carry a name the length of the list.*

## I. Of the Hill Without Salt

Mòrag Chruaidh was thirty-eight years short of four hundred and had held the hill for her grandfather the better part of a generation before she gave the household its first war. Dòmhnall Clachach had ridden north in the hundred and eighty-ninth year of his own holding to see what could be made of ground his people had only lately come to, and he had been gone, by the time Mòrag's tale properly begins, longer than most of the households below the bank could remember him being present; so that to the children born on the hill in those years the king's blood that sat in judgement there and settled the grazing and heard the quarrels was, from their first memory of it, a tall, dark-browed woman who never once let slip that the hill was not, in the fullest sense, her own.

It was not her own, and she knew it better than anyone said aloud. Dòmhnall held it of the king; she held it of Dòmhnall; and beneath all the holding, further back than any bank or ditch, the hill and the valleys round it were the king's, in the plain sense that he had taken them with his own hand and never once given the taking away. Mòrag judged, and levied, and rode out with a spear at her side when a spear was needed, and slept easier for knowing that whatever she failed to settle could, in the last account, be carried to a man whose years never told on him and whose memory held every name once given him. It was a good arrangement for a woman with the temper her grandfather's line ran to, and she had never yet found the want in it.

The hill Mòrag held was not the hill her great-grandfather had taken. The bank her father's father Dòmhnall had set the boundary stones for still ran round it, but three more rings of houses had grown up outside the first bank in the years since, and the valley road that had once ended at the gate now ran on south and west to a dozen daughter settlements that sent their grazing quarrels up to the hill the same as they had always done, only in greater number and with less patience for waiting their turn. Mòrag heard them in the same low stone hall her grandfather had built, sitting the chair he had sat, and gave judgement in the plain, unhedging way the household had come, over three generations, to expect of anyone who sat it: a sentence, seldom more than a sentence, and the hearing closed. She had grown up watching her grandfather do the same and had never once, as a child, imagined she would be doing it herself before he was dead, or even much aged; it was one of the small, unspoken strangenesses of a household of the king's blood that a holder might sit a chair for a hundred years and more and see her own grandchildren grow grey while she herself was only beginning to feel the first weight of the years in her knees of a cold morning.

She found the want the autumn the hill went without salt.

It was not the first such autumn. Salt was a thing the hill bought or bartered for, brought up from the southern shore by traders who kept the terms of the trade to themselves and raised the price a little each year the hill's herds grew and the hill's hunger for it grew with them; and there had been lean seasons before when a bad crossing or a bad harvest on the trading side left the hill's larders going into winter with meat that would not keep and no way to keep it. What was new that autumn was the manner of the going without. Three separate traders, in the space of one summer, had come up the valley road empty-handed and sworn, each in his own way, that the southern shore had stopped selling; that the folk who worked the salt-scrapes at Baile ghorm had grown proud of what they made and meant, this year, to keep the whole of it for themselves and their own kin along the coast, and had said as much, plainly, to any hill trader who came asking.

Mòrag heard the third of these traders out in the hall her grandfather had built, a low stone room hung with the antlers of beasts long since hunted out of the near valleys, and said nothing for a while after he had finished, turning the matter the way she turned most matters that came to her, slowly and from more than one side before she let herself speak on it. "They have salt and no king," she said at last, half to the trader and half to herself. "We have a king and no salt. Between us that is no bargain at all, only two households that have not yet learned which one is the stronger."

She did not send to her grandfather in the north before she decided what to do about it, and told herself afterward, when there was time to tell herself anything, that a season's sailing would have brought his answer back too late to matter and that a holder who could not judge for herself in her holder's own absence was no holder at all; both of which were true, and neither of which was the whole of why she went. The whole of why she went she kept to herself, then and after, save that once, very old, she told a great-grandchild that she had wanted, that autumn, to know for certain whether the strength that had taken every valley the hill now held had come down to her at all, or had stopped somewhere short of her father's father and left her nothing but the name.

## II. Of the Shingle at Baile ghorm

She took eleven men south along a coast track the hill's herders used for driving beasts to the winter grazing, and came down on the salt-scrapes at Baile ghorm in the grey hour before a summer dawn, meaning, she told her own men more than once on the road, to take the salt and nothing more: no house burned, no beast lifted that was not already a working ox needed to carry the load away, no blood shed that the salt-folk did not themselves choose to shed defending it. It went otherwise, as such things generally do once the first spear leaves a man's hand rather than his intention.

Mòrag had never seen the sea before that road brought her to it, and she said afterward, to the one or two she ever spoke to plainly of that morning, that the first sight of it in the grey light, flat and enormous and giving off a smell like nothing the hill valleys had ever taught her to expect, had unsettled her more than the fighting that followed. Her men, drawn from households that drove beasts to the winter grazing within sound of that same shore, took the sight as no more than a familiar landmark reached at last, and it was one of them, a broad, unhurried man named Griogair who had made the drive a dozen times and never once thought to raid at the end of it, who first pointed out to her, in a whisper pitched not to carry, the low red glow strung along the strand ahead of them where the salt-hearths still burned through the last of the night, banked but not let die, exactly as a household bank a hearth-fire it means to want again before morning.

The salt was made in those years over open hearths set along the shingle above the tide line, shallow stone-lined pits kept burning through the drying months while women fed them driftwood and raked the crusting brine, work that blackened the hands and thickened the skin of the forearms in a way Mòrag noticed, afterward, on every salt-worker she ever after had cause to look closely at. The hearths could not be carried off. What could be carried off was what they had already made, heaped in baskets and skin bags under low turf shelters at the shingle's head, and it was these Mòrag's men went for, working fast in the half-light while she herself stood watch at the shelters' mouth with four spears about her and told herself the thing was nearly done.

It was not nearly done. Mòrag stood at the shelters' mouth in the last dark listening to her own men working the baskets free of the turf with a care that struck her, even at the time, as oddly gentle for men in the act of a raid, as though the habit of handling salt without wasting a grain of it had come over them the moment their hands closed on the baskets, whatever else was in their minds that morning. She had told them, on the road down, to take nothing they did not need to carry the taking off cleanly, and she watched them keep that word with a thoroughness that later struck her as almost comic beside what came after: men stepping carefully over a sleeping dog's blanket so as not to wake it, a basket set down and re-tied when its cord slipped, as though the whole enterprise were no more than an ill-mannered borrowing that decent men would see set right before they left.

A dog gave tongue somewhere up the strand before the last basket was clear of the shelters, and the salt-folk came down onto the shingle in twos and threes and then in a rush, men first and then, close behind them, women who had been raking the hearths since before light and came still holding the long wooden rakes they raked with, which proved, in the event, weapons enough at close quarters against men encumbered with baskets. Mòrag's people got the greater part of the salt away up the strand before the fighting closed round them properly, but the getting away cost seven lives on the hill's side before it was done: three cut down on the shingle itself, one taken by a rake-blow to the skull that no one on either side had thought could kill a man and did, two more overtaken on the track above the strand by salt-folk who knew the ground better than any hill man could in the dark, and the last, a boy not yet twenty summers who had begged Mòrag to let him come because his elder brother was already one of the eleven, drowned trying to swim a inlet he had judged, wrongly, narrower than it was.

Mòrag herself killed no one that morning, a fact she never afterward either claimed as mercy or denied as failure, saying only, when asked directly by one of her own grandchildren many years on, that she had spent the whole of the fighting getting her people and the salt clear of the strand and had had no leisure that morning to keep a tally of who fell to whose hand. What she did do, standing at the shingle's head with the last basket loaded and her own men falling back around her in a ragged line, was turn once, before the light was fully up, and look back down the strand at where her seven lay among stones already dark with more than seawater, and she made, in that turning, the one decision of the whole raid she never afterward doubted: that whatever the hill said of the salt, and whatever the king said when word of it reached him, the seven names would not be let go the way a lost beast or a broken tool is let go.

## III. Of the Bank Behind the Shingle

She kept that decision through eleven days' ride home with the salt on the hill's own carts and her dead carried the shorter way, by boat along the coast to the nearest point a cart track could meet them, and she kept it through the winter that followed, in which the hill ate salted meat again for the first time in two years and no one below the bank asked her too closely what the salt had cost to bring. At the turn of that winter, on the night the hill kept for its dead in the ordinary way, she stood at the fire her grandfather had built the hall's hearth from and named all seven aloud, one after another, in the order they had fallen so far as anyone could tell it: Donnchadh Bàn first, her own father's oldest belted man, who had gone down under the first rush at the shelters' mouth; then the three of the strand; then the two of the track; then, last and youngest, the boy who had drowned asking to be let come. She named them again the winter after that, and the winter after that, until the naming was a thing the hill did every year at the same fire without anyone having to be told to do it, and the reciters keep it still, said each year now by households who know none of the seven by anything more than the name itself.

Word of what had answered her raid reached the hill more slowly, brought up by the same traders who had once brought salt and now, for a season, brought nothing at all. The salt-folk of Baile ghorm, it was told, had lost the raid's night but had not, in the reckoning that mattered to them, lost the salt itself, for salt is a thing the sea goes on making whether or not any given year's hearths stand. What they had lost, and set about mending within the same season, was the manner of keeping it safe: a fire that burns for a season burns in one place, tended by hands that must sleep sometime, and can be come at in the dark by anyone willing to risk the raking-women's rakes. Within two summers of Mòrag's raid the folk of Baile ghorm had raised a low bank of shingle and turf behind the tide line, and inside it had dug shallow clay-lined basins that needed no fire at all, only sun and wind and the slow work of months, the brine thickening on its own where no torch had to be kept burning to give a raider his mark in the dark. It was the first work of its kind the hill-folk had ever heard of, and it changed, within a lifetime, how salt was made the length of that shore; and though none of the tellers who carried the news up to Dùn ìseal ever said so in as many words, every one of them understood, and Mòrag understood best of all when she heard it, that the bank behind the shingle had been raised as much against her as against the sea.

## IV. Of the Red Hill and the Pebble Bag

Word of the raid reached the king before word of the bank did, carried, as such things were carried in those years, faster than any single messenger could have carried it, by the ordinary passing of the news from hearth to hearth along the roads Mòrag's own carts had used. He came to Dùn ìseal himself the spring after, riding down out of the north with no great company about him, and Mòrag met him at the gate the way she had met few enough kings' visits in her life, having held the hill so long under her grandfather's long absence that she had half forgotten what it was to stand second to anyone on her own ground.

He heard the whole of it from her own mouth, sitting the evening through in the hall her grandfather had built, and asked few questions, and made, when she had finished, no judgement at all in the sense she had braced herself to hear one. "The south is not mine yet," he said, when she pressed him at last for some word of blame or of praise, one or the other, so that she would know which way to carry the matter forward. "You took salt from a people who owe me nothing, and lost seven doing it, and they answered you well enough that you will not take salt from them that way again. I will not tell you that was rightly done, and I will not tell you it was wrongly done, because it was neither mine to forbid nor mine yet to avenge." He was warm where his arm brushed hers crossing the hall's threshold, warm the way a hearthstone is warm long after the fire has been let sink, a thing she noticed and did not remark on, having grown up hearing it remarked on by everyone older than herself until the remarking itself had worn thin.

What he did instead of judging was take her west with him, a week's riding and more along tracks that grew narrower and less certain the further they went from the hill's own valleys, through country Mòrag had never had cause to see, low wooded ridges giving way at last to open water that lay so still under the evening light she took it, the first hour she saw it, for a second sky fallen to earth. The king rode the whole way in no great haste and said little of where they went or why, and Mòrag, who had never before travelled at his side for longer than an evening's hearing, came in those days to understand something of what the household meant when it said, of him, that he was easier company on a road than in a hall: he asked after her grandfather's health in the north, and after the boy who had drowned in the inlet, whose name he had already by heart though no one had told it him twice, and once, on the third evening, sang a verse of something in no tongue she recognised, low and to himself, that she never afterward heard him sing again and never once asked him what it had been.

They came at last to the lake shore where the Red Hill stood, an old holding of its own line that had never once sworn to Dùn ìseal and never once needed to, owing the king directly and no one between. Seonag nic Uisdein held it then, of Uisdean Mòr's blood though born, the list already said of her, more than three hundred years after he died, and she met the king at her own gate with a worn leather bag at her belt that Mòrag took at first for nothing more than the ordinary purse a holder might carry to a meeting of consequence.

It was not a purse. Seonag opened it before the king without being asked and weighed it once, twice, in her open palm, her lips moving a little as she did, and said that the bag held one pebble for every household on the hill and along the shore below it, set in when a household was first counted and taken out again, without ceremony, the season a household failed or moved on or was absorbed into another; so that she needed no reciter and no written tally to know, at any hour a man cared to ask her, how many hearths answered to the Red Hill, only the weight of a bag she had carried at her own belt since she was a girl learning the count from her mother. The king took the bag from her hands and weighed it himself, the same small motion, and gave it back, and said only that he was content; and it was the whole of the business between them, no more words spent on it than that, though Mòrag, watching, understood she had been brought the whole week's ride west to see it.

She turned the pebble bag over in her mind the length of the ride home, weighing it, she told herself more than once, the way Seonag weighed her own. Seonag held a shore that had never raised a bank against a raid because it had never given the sea's folk cause to raise one; held it, so far as Mòrag could see in the short days she spent at the Red Hill's gate, by knowing exactly what she held and claiming not one pebble's weight beyond it. Mòrag had gone south with eleven spears to take what was not owed her and had come home with seven names to carry the rest of her life for the taking of it. She did not, in the years after, ever raise another raid, though she never once said, to anyone who asked her plainly why not, that the pebble bag was the reason.

## V. Of the Years That Followed

The years after the raid went the way most years on a settled hill go, told afterward less as a string of single days than as a handful of things a household remembered doing for the first time. Word came up from the south-west coast, in the seven hundred and fifth year, that households at Doire shean had begun firing clay pots in open bonfires, coil upon coil built up and baked hard in a single burning, a craft that within a few lifetimes spread the length of the southern shore and put an end, wherever it reached, to the older habit of carrying water and grain in skins that split and baskets that rotted; Mòrag heard of it from a trader glad, for once, to be carrying news rather than salt, and had two of the first pots brought up to the hill's own hearth, where they sat, chipped and much mended, longer than she herself sat the hill. Eleven years after that, at the landing on the green river now called, by the folk who used it, Cidhe Beag, a boat was hollowed whole from a single oak trunk, long enough to carry six men and their gear, and sunk at its mooring and left there, the first boat of its kind anyone then living had seen made in one unbroken piece rather than built up plank by lashed plank; and though it lay a long way from Mòrag's own hill and she never once saw it with her own eyes, the telling of it reached her all the same, as the telling of most new things on the island by then reached whoever held Dùn ìseal, sooner or later, by one road or another.

In the meantime the hill went on eating salt bought rather than salt taken, for the traders of Baile ghorm, whatever they thought privately of the household that had raided them, found within a few seasons that a bank of clay basins made more salt than seven hearths ever had, and that a hill with silver or hides to trade was worth more to them standing than raided. Mòrag herself never again sat across a bargaining fire from any of them, holding that a holder who had once taken by the spear had no business haggling afterward as though nothing had passed between the two households; she left the trading to men of her own who had not ridden south with her, and took what salt came up the valley road without asking too closely what price had been struck for it. It was, she told herself, no more than the ordinary distance a holder kept from the ordinary business of her own hill, though more than one of her own household noticed, without ever saying so where she could hear it, that she kept that particular distance more carefully than any other.

She held the hill forty-seven years in all, counting from the raid, and gave it up at last with her strength still whole in her, having judged, as she told the household plainly enough when she told them anything, that a hill was ill served by a holder who stayed past the age when she still had something to learn from it, and that her grandfather's line, by then, had another of its own grown to the holding. The one who came after her was her father's own brother, Iain Dubh, a son of the king himself and older by every reckoning than she was, though he looked, that year, no more than a man in his middle strength beside her own greying and lined face; and it was Iain Dubh, taking the hill from her hand at the gate in the seven hundred and nineteenth year, who asked her the one question no one else in the household had thought, in all the years since, to put to her directly.

"How did you do it," he said, not naming the thing he meant, because there was only the one thing between them worth asking after, "with eleven spears against a whole shore, and come home with only seven lost?"

Mòrag looked at him a while before she answered, the way she had looked at most things worth the trouble of an answer, and said at last that she did not rightly know how she had done it, only that she had gone down meaning to take salt and had come home understanding that a household's strength was never so plainly its own as it liked to believe, and that the seven she had lost had bought her that understanding at a price she would not, if the choosing were given her again, judge worth paying twice. It was the whole of what she gave him, and the reciters, who kept little enough of her beyond the one deed, kept that answer with it, and no more.
