# Of the Eight Winds and Tormod the Grey

*GE 2,021 – 2,210*

*At Ceann leathan the boat-builders still touch the third stone from the landward end before they step aboard, and none of them, asked why that one, can tell you more than that it is always the third.*

## I. Of the Hill Given Over

Tormod was the son of Iain Dubh, who had died on the shingle at Baile ghorm with a salt-man's spear in him and been carried home to the cairn his own people raised. Tormod had been a boy of eighty-seven when that news came up from the coast, old enough to stand at the front of the crowd and young enough that the older men put their hands on his shoulders rather than asked anything of him, and he had grown to a man's full years, and then past them, on a hill that had already buried one holder younger than himself and gone on to a woman who was not of his line at all before it came round to him.

Sìleas Bhàn gave the hall over to him in the one thousand three hundred and seventieth year of his life, which is a great age for a man not of the king's own body but no age at all beside hers, for she had held Dùn ìseal through years when the grazing failed two summers running and the store within the bank was opened to every household that had none of its own, and she had done it, the tellings say, with no deed to her name greater than that she had held and not let go. She did not die when she gave the hill over. She went to live among her own kin in the valley below the bank, and was there still, an old woman among older kin than herself, when Tormod's own hair began at last to go the colour it would keep for the rest of his days on the hill.

He came to the hall a man past a thousand years old and looking, those who saw him say, no more than forty, dark-haired still, broad through the shoulders as his father had been broad, and he took up the holding the way a man takes up a tool that has been left lying about for some time and finds, turning it over, that it still answers to the hand. The failed years were behind him. He set himself, in his first seasons, to putting right what the hunger had let go: the bank mended where two winters of neglect had let the ditch silt and the palisade lean, the boundary marks between the hill's own fields and the households' walked and set again where a season of each man taking what he could had blurred them past any two neighbours agreeing. He judged as his grandfather's grandfather had judged, in a sentence, standing, and went back to his mending. It was not a great beginning. It was the kind that does not get itself told, and the tellers of Dùn ìseal, pressed for a first deed of Tormod's holding, will generally say only that the hill stopped losing ground and start their story somewhere further on.

## II. Of the Winds Given Names

It was in his twenty-sixth year on the hill, by the count his own household kept, that word came up from the coast of a thing done among the boat-crews that no one holding land had asked for and no one holding land could stop, being a thing of speech and not of law. The eight winds of the year, that every sailor from the Hawk bay to the eastern cape had always known by their feel and their season without troubling to call them anything beyond "the one that blows off the ford" or "the one that brings the fish in," had been given names, all eight together, in one telling, by a man or a woman no one afterward could put a face to.

How it was done, and by whom, is not agreed between the coasts that tell it. At the salt shore they say it was an old woman of no family, blown off course in an open boat and put ashore half-drowned, who named the winds one after another as she came to herself by the fire, the way a fevered person names the dead; that the fisherfolk who pulled her out wrote nothing down, being fisherfolk and not reciters, but that every one of them who heard her went home with all eight names whole in his head and never afterward forgot one. At Ceann leathan they say there was no woman and no boat, only that the names had always been there, waiting under the tongue the way a word waits before a child is old enough to need it, and that all that happened in that year was that the whole coast began, on the same tides, to say them aloud where before they had only felt the winds without naming them. Tormod himself, asked once by a trader who wanted the eight winds' names written on a hide to carry inland, said that he did not know where they had come from and did not think it a question a landholder was fit to answer, the sea and its winds belonging to those who lived on it and not to those who held ground above the tideline.

What can be said plainly is this: before that year, the eight winds went unnamed on every coast of Dia-thìr that any telling reaches. After it, every coast used the same eight names, in the same order, for the same winds, and no coast since has changed one of them, though what the names themselves mean, if they ever meant anything beyond the wind each stands for, is dark even to the sailors who say them oftenest and could, any one of them, tell you at a glance which wind was rising and which was dying by the smell of the air alone.

A carrier of the hill's own salt, a man named Fearchar Gobha who ran the road between Dùn ìseal and the coast twice in every month of his working life, brought the eight names up to Tormod himself within the year, having heard them first at the fair from a crew he had never met before and never met again. He had gone down to the strand meaning only to trade, he told Tormod, and had come away with eight words in his mouth that he had not owned when he went down, said in an order he found he could not say them out of even when he tried it as a jest, the way a man cannot say the days of a week backward without stumbling. Tormod asked him to say them once more, standing in the hall with the fire between them, and Fearchar said them, and Tormod said nothing for a while after, and then only that a landholder had no business owning what belonged to the sea, and sent Fearchar back to his road with a measure of grain for his trouble and no more said on it. It is the one telling of that year that gives Tormod a voice at all in the matter of the naming, and it gives him, as the tellings of that hill are generally content to give him, very little.

## III. Of the Stones at Ceann leathan

The stones came after the names, and came, unlike the names, from a place and a hand that the tellings agree on. Ceann leathan stands on a point where the coast turns, a long low finger of rock run out into water that is calm on one side of it and broken on the other, and the boat-crews of that shore had always put out from the calm side and come home to it, so that the point itself was, before anything was set upon it, already a place where every crossing began and every crossing that went well was seen to end.

In the twenty-ninth year after the naming, the households of that point set eight standing stones in a ring on the seaward end of it, each a man's height or better, and gave one to each of the eight named winds, so that a sailor going out could lay a hand on the stone of the wind he wanted and, the telling has it, ask it for that wind and no other. Whether the wind ever came for the asking is not a thing any telling troubles to say either way; what is told is that the custom held from that year, and holds still on every coast that has heard of it, and that no sailor of Dia-thìr, however far past believing in the working of it he may claim to be over his own hearth, has ever been known to walk past the ring at Ceann leathan without laying a hand on one stone or another as he goes down to his boat.

The setting of the stones is remembered, unlike the naming, as the work of named hands: three brothers of the point, Aonghas, Griogar and Ailig mac Torcaill, who quarried the eight stones from a single outcrop above the landing rather than bring them from elsewhere, reasoning, Griogar is said to have said, that a stone that had always stood on that point would know its own wind better than a stone dragged in from a hill that had never felt sea air. It took the three of them the better part of two years to cut and drag and raise all eight, and the ring was not set true at the first standing: the stone of the wind that blows hardest off the open water was found, after one bad winter, to have settled crooked in ground softer than the rest, and had to be dug out and reset on a footing of smaller stones packed beneath it, which is why, of the eight, it alone stands markedly straighter and newer-looking than its fellows, a thing every boat-builder on that coast can point to and none of them, until told, ever thinks to ask the reason for.

The wind-names crossed water before that summer was out. Eilean fhada, the long island that lies off the northern cape and takes the worst of every storm that comes down out of Manannan's mist before the mainland feels it, kept no stones and had no point shaped for a ring of them, but its people cut the eight names as notches into the rock of its highest ground, one notch facing each way the wind could come from, so that a man caught on the height in weather too thick to see the coastline by could still lay his hand along the rock and find, by the run of the cuts under his fingers, which way he stood. The notches are there yet, cut deeper by every generation that has found them worn shallow and cut them again, and no one on that island now living remembers a time when the eight ways were reckoned by anything else.

## IV. Of the Heirs He Buried

Tormod married in his hundred and ninetieth year on the hill, a woman of a household under the eastern shoulder of Dùn ìseal named for nothing that survives beyond her own children's naming of her as their mother, and she gave him three sons before she died an old woman in her own right, having lived out a full span and seen her eldest son go grey before her.

That eldest son died first, in Tormod's hundred and nineteenth year of holding, by the same slow failing that takes any mortal man who lives past his three-score-and-ten without being of the king's own blood to carry him further, and Tormod stood at the burying dark-haired still, and heard himself named by his own son's children as their grandfather's father, which none of them, being young, thought strange, not having any other grandfather's father living to measure it against.

The second son, who had never cared for the hill's holding and had spent his whole life on the western grazing with the sheep his brother thought beneath him, died sixty years after the first, an old man himself by then, and was buried, at his own asking made long before, not at the hill's own ground but on the slope where he had herded, with a cairn no larger than any shepherd's. Tormod rode out to see it raised and stood by it a long while, and was heard to say only that a man ought to be let choose his own ground if nothing else was left him to choose, which the tellings of the western slope have kept ever since as though it were a judgement, though it answered no dispute and settled no grazing; it was simply the thing he said, standing there, and it stuck to the place the way such things do.

The third, the youngest and the one Tormod had thought might outlast him if any of the three could, having been the strongest as a boy and the last to grey, died in the hundred and forty-second year, having lived to see his own grandchildren's children and having asked his father, near the end, propped up in the same bed he had been born in, whether it hurt, being left each time. Tormod sat with the old man's hand in his own hand, which the tellings note was by then the smoother of the two hands in that room by a very great margin, and told him no. It was the only lie the tellings ever charge against him, and they charge it gently, being of the opinion, most of them, that a man burying his third son has earned the right to one.

By the time the third son was in the ground, Tormod Liath had held Dùn ìseal longer than any holder before him whose length is remembered at all, and the hill had begun, without anyone deciding it should, to reckon its own age by how long Tormod had held it rather than by the year, the way a household with a very old grandmother will say "since before Granny's time" of anything far enough back, meaning nothing more precise than a long while.

## V. Of the Second Bank

It was in the year the hill's people count as his hundred and forty-third that Tormod set the second bank, the one that still stands outside the first and gives Dùn ìseal the ground it needed once its granaries had grown past what the old ditch could shelter. He set it the way his grandfather's grandfather Dòmhnall had set his boundary stones, walking it himself before he asked a spade of anyone else, pacing the line in the frost of two winters running until he was satisfied it took in the low ground by the spring without giving up the height that mattered for a watch-fire, and only then calling the households in to dig.

They dug through a spring and a summer, households that had never worked a spade together before finding, as households will, that the ones who talked least worked longest, and the bank went up doubling the hill's ground within it, so that Dùn ìseal from that year held not forty houses inside its earth but very near a hundred, with room, Tormod said when they asked him why so much more than they yet needed, for what would come after him, though he did not say, and none of them thought to ask, what he reckoned that to be.

## VI. Of the Grey

He went truly grey in the years the second bank was going up, not all at once as the king himself was said, in an older telling that had reached even that hill by then, to have gone grey in a single winter and no further, but slowly, the black going out of his hair the way colour goes out of cloth left too long in the weather, so slowly that no one household could have said which season it had happened in, only that a man they remembered dark was, some years on, a man they could not remember dark at all.

By his hundred and eighty-fifth year of holding he was grey through, brows and beard with the rest, and stooped a little at the shoulder where he had once stood straight, and the young of the hill, who had never known him any other way, took him for what he plainly appeared: the oldest man on Dùn ìseal, older, they said among themselves, than anyone could well remember, older than the second bank he had raised with his own hands, older, some of the very young said, than the hill itself. It did not trouble him to be thought so. He was, by the reckoning that mattered to the tellings that keep this, in fact the oldest living thing on that ground bar the oaks of the grove he had never once travelled north to see, and he wore the age the young gave him the way a man wears a coat cut generously, with room in it he did not need but no cause to complain of the fit.

## VII. Of the King at the Gate

He was an old man by every mark anyone on the hill could read, holding his hundred and ninety-third year, when the king came in at Dùn ìseal's gate one spring morning with no more than four of his household about him and no word sent ahead, as he did from time to time on no season anyone could predict, to see how a hill he had given into other hands was being kept.

Tormod came out from the hall to meet him bareheaded, as every holder of that hill had always come to meet him, and went down on one knee in the mud of the gateway the way his father Iain Dubh had gone down before him and his father's father before that, and the king put a hand under his arm, as he always did, to raise him before the knee had fully bent, an old habit of his that the hill's people had long since stopped remarking on. It was only when Tormod was upright again, an old grey man of a hundred and ninety-three years steadied by the arm of another, that the gathered household of the hill, watching from the gate and the bank above it, saw the two of them standing side by side in the one morning light: the king, dark-headed, unlined, no more than forty by the only measure any of them had ever been given to judge a man's years, and beside him the oldest man any of them had ever known, kneeling to him, and being raised by him, as a son might be raised by his father, though every soul on that hill knew, without anyone troubling to say it aloud, that it stood the other way about: that the dark-headed man had buried Tormod's father, and would in time bury Tormod, and would go on standing at gates being raised out of the mud by no one, while sons and grandsons and great-grandsons went grey and old and were carried to the cairn in their turn.

One of the household who watched from the bank that morning was a girl of nine or ten, a granddaughter of Tormod's second son and so already three generations down from the old man in the gateway, and she is remembered, in the one telling that troubles to name a witness at all, as having asked her mother, in a voice not lowered as much as it should have been, why the young man was letting the old one call him nothing more than what a stranger might be called at a gate. Her mother did not answer her there, in the crowd, but took her home and told her that evening, by the fire, what every household on that hill already knew and few of them said outright even to their own children until a child's own question forced the saying: that the young man was the king, that he did not age as other men aged, and that the old man he had raised out of the mud was the girl's own great-grandfather's father, who had held that hill longer than her mother's mother could remember any other holder of it.

None of the young who watched that morning had words ready for what they were looking at. It had been true of every holder before Tormod and would be true of every holder after him, and had been said, in one shape or another, at every gate on the island where the king had ever stood; but it is the tellings of Dùn ìseal in particular that keep the picture of that one spring morning, the old grey man on his feet again with the king's hand still under his arm a moment longer than it needed to be, because it was at Dùn ìseal, in the years just after, that the young first began to say aloud the thing their grandparents had only ever thought.

## VIII. Of the Name They Gave Themselves

By Tormod's hundred and eighty-first year, GE two thousand two hundred and one by the count the hill kept, the children of that generation had begun, without any elder telling them to, calling themselves by a name none of their grandparents had used for themselves: not hill-folk, which was true enough of where they lived but said nothing of what they were, and not Clann nan Dè, which belonged by then to a handful of families near the high valleys who traced their blood straight back to the grove and would not have thanked a Dùn ìseal fisherman's son for claiming it alongside them, but Dia-thìrich, the people of Dia-thìr, and the island itself, in the same breath, Dia-thìr and no other word.

No one now living, and no telling that reaches this far back with any confidence, can say whose mouth said it first, or whether it was said first at Dùn ìseal at all rather than carried there already made from the Red Hill or the ford or some hearth further off that kept no memory of having coined it. What is agreed is only that it did not arrive as a law or a naming ordered by any holder, king or otherwise; it arrived the way the wind-names had arrived a hundred and fifty years before, all at once and everywhere, so that within a generation of children the old words for what they were had gone out of use among the young without ever being forbidden, and the elders who still said hill-folk out of habit found themselves, more and more, being gently corrected by their own grandchildren.

Tormod heard it first, the tellings say, from a boy of perhaps seven, one of the households settled along the new ground the second bank had opened, who told him plainly, when the old man asked what clan he belonged to, that he was Dia-thìreach, and that everyone was, and seemed puzzled that so old a man should need telling. Tormod, who was by then further past a thousand years of holding than any of his own three dead sons had lived altogether, said only that it was a good enough name for a good enough island, and let the boy go back to his play; and that, so far as any telling of that hill preserves, is the whole of what the ninth of the Stone Kings ever said, for or against, on the matter of what his people had decided, of their own accord and without asking him, that they were.
