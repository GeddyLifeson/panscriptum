# Of the Great Gathering at the Ford

*GE 3,000*

*At the Red Hill this is kept as the old woman's own telling, carried down by her daughter's daughter to the reciters there, and by them to the reciters of the ford.*

## I. Of the Road Down

She was nine years old that summer, and she had never gone further from the Red Hill than the mill at Àth shean, where her mother traded cheese for grain twice in a year. So when her mother told her they would walk to the ford for the great gathering, and sell salt there for the length of three days, she thought at first that a great gathering must be a kind of market larger than the one at the mill, and nothing more.

They went down with six households of the lake shore, each with a cart or a laden pony, and the salt wrapped in sacking against the damp and packed so carefully that her mother would not let her sit near it, for fear a wriggling child might loosen a cord. The road ran fourteen miles from the Red Hill to the ford, easy walking for anyone grown, and they did it in a day, resting once at noon by a stream where the ponies drank and the women unwrapped bread and dried fish and ate standing, watching the road both ways out of habit rather than fear. It was on that road, past the second bend from the mill, that she first understood the size of what she was walking toward. Other households were on the road too, coming down from the hills to the north of the lake shore with pigs on a string and dogs that would not stop barking at the pigs, and further on a family from the west went by driving two carts heaped with hides and coils of worked leather, seal-skins among them, and one old man walking beside the second cart who nodded to her mother without a word, as people did to strangers they expected to meet again before the week was out.

By the time the ford came in sight, in the low light of the second evening, she had counted more strange faces on that one road than she had seen in her whole life at the Red Hill, and she gave up counting after that, because the ground below her, where the river widened and shoaled between two long green banks, was already dark with people and fires, further than she could see in the dusk, and more were still coming in on every road that led to it.

Her mother took her wrist then, and did not let go of it again for three days.

The ground the salt-traders used lay downstream of the ford, a wide flat shelf of turf between the water and the alder scrub, and it was there her mother's people made their fire that first night, among others of the lake shore and the pans of the south coast who knew each household by its salt before they knew it by its name. Upstream, where the river ran faster over a bed of clean gravel, was a second ground the girl did not understand until later, kept clearer than the rest and walked over by fewer feet, as though the grass there knew something the grass downstream did not. Beyond that again, on the far side where the bank rose in a low shoulder before the trees began, lay a third ground given over to the northern households and the households of the west, who set their pig-pens and their leather-stalls in orderly rows the salt-traders never quite managed among themselves.

By the second morning the three grounds had run together at their edges until nobody rightly knew any longer which bank was whose, and a child sent to fetch water might come back an hour later having made two new friends and lost the path to her own fire twice over. The girl was sent for water twice that morning and came back late both times, and her mother did not scold her for it, being too busy with her own trading to notice how late was late.

That first full day, before ever the king came down, was market enough on its own to fill a child's head for a year. Potters from the north-west had carried down whole stacks of grey ware nested one inside another on a single cart, and a man from further west than the girl had ever heard named traded three strings of amber, worn smooth by the sea and drilled through for the wearing, for a winter's worth of salt her mother would not have parted with for less. Wool went from hand to hand in raw fleeces and in cloth already woven, flint from the eastern quarry changed owners twice before noon, and somewhere among the fires a family of the salt shore sang the old four-line refrain of the pans, over and over, to nobody in particular, the way people sing a thing they have always sung rather than a thing meant to be listened to. Marriages were spoken of that day as freely as salt was, two households from opposite ends of the crowd sitting down together over a shared fire to weigh what a match between their children might be worth to each in a few years' time, in grazing or in grandchildren, and the girl heard more than one old woman remark, watching such a pair of households talk, that the ford had made more marriages than it had ever made kings.

It was toward evening of that first day that she heard the word said which she carried away more clearly than any other from the whole gathering, though she did not understand it then and was not sure she understood it fully even in her own old age. An old reciter of the hill, sitting with others of his kind at a fire near the upstream ground, said to those around him that this was the three thousandth year since the grove was lit, and that word of it had gone out along every road on the island that summer for that reason alone, so that people had come from coasts that had never troubled to send anyone to an ordinary gathering. The girl did not know what a grove had to do with a king, or what a thousand years was worth beside nine, and let the words go by her the way she let most talk of the reciters go by her; but she remembered, long after she had forgotten a great deal else that a child forgets, that the old men at that fire had spoken the number the way her own grandmother spoke the number of a bad winter, as a thing to be marked rather than a thing to be explained.

## II. Of the Man Who Came Like a Herdsman

It was on that second morning, with the sun not yet clear of the eastern trees, that a stillness went through the crowd on the upstream ground the way a wind goes through standing barley, bending it all one way before anyone has felt a breath of it themselves. The girl was standing with her mother at the edge of the salt-traders' ground, both of them for once not haggling over anything, and she saw the bending before she understood its cause: old men who had been sitting all morning got to their feet without being asked; a woman with an armful of wool let the wool fall and did not stoop for it; two boys wrestling in the mud stopped mid-throw and stood apart from each other, looking the same way.

He was coming down the hill road from the west with a handful of others, dressed as any herdsman dresses for a day's walking, in undyed wool with mud to the knee and a plain staff in his hand that he was using as a walking-stick and nothing more. The girl, craning past her mother's shoulder, was disappointed. She had heard the word king used all her nine years the way other children heard the word storm, as a thing that came from a distance and changed everything it touched, and she had built in her own mind a man taller than any man, crowned in something that caught the light. The man on the hill road was younger-looking than her own father, and no taller, and carried nothing that caught the light at all.

Then the crowd opened for him.

It opened the whole way down to the river, though nobody called out and nobody pushed, a channel opening through several thousand people before he reached it, the way standing water parts before the bow of a boat without any hand rowing the boat aside. The girl watched the faces as he passed rather than the man himself, because the faces told her more than he did: the old people, who she had thought nothing frightened, stepped back from that channel with their eyes down in a way she had never seen an old person step back from anything, and one old woman near her, whom she did not know, put both hands over her own mouth and said nothing at all, though her eyes were wet.

It was the faces that frightened the girl in the end, more than the man, and once she was frightened of him she found she could not look away from him for the rest of that day.

## III. Of the Hands Between His Hands

He went down through the channel the crowd had made and out into the middle of the ford, where the water came only to his knees though it ran fast there over clean stone, and he stood, and waited, with the herdsman's staff still in his hand as though he might yet have some flock to see to before the day was out.

The holder of the hill of the Stone Kings came out to him first, wading in from the upstream bank with his cloak gathered up over one arm, and put both his hands between the king's two hands, the way a man puts his hands into water he means to drink from cupped, and the king closed his own hands over them a moment and let them go, and spoke a few words to him the girl was too far off to hear. The holder waded back with the front of his clothes wet to the chest where the current had caught him despite the gathering of his cloak, and nobody on the bank laughed at him for it, though a boy near the girl started to and was stopped by his own mother's hand on his shoulder before the laugh was fully out of him.

After the hill's holder came the holder of the Red Hill, a young man the girl's own mother knew by sight and called out to once, softly, before catching herself, for such a day called for no shouting across the crowd. He was a smaller man than the hill's holder, narrow through the shoulder, and he went into the water more carefully, as though he did not trust the current the way the other had, and when he came out from the king's hands he looked, the girl thought, smaller still, though she could not afterward say what had made him look so.

Then came the holder of the salt shore, an older woman with grey plaited into her hair, whom the girl's mother did know well, having traded salt with her own people for two summers running, and after her the holder who spoke for the northern plain, and after him the holder who spoke for the households of the west; and each one waded out in turn, put hands between the king's hands, and waded back soaked to the chest or higher, and stood afterward among their own people a little apart from everyone else, the way a person stands apart who has just come from somewhere the others have not been.

Five went out and five came back, and the girl counted them on her own fingers without meaning to, the way she counted anything that happened five times running, and thought when the fifth had waded back that the day's strangeness must surely be finished.

Her grandmother, who had come up beside her by then, told her the names of the five as they went, in the low running voice old people use for things they have watched happen many times before: the hill's own holder that year, a grave, careful man the list would keep no separate count of once his turn was done; the Red Hill's young holder, new enough to his own hall that half the crowd still called him by his father's name before catching themselves; the woman of the salt shore, whom the girl's mother traded with every summer and had never once, in all that trading, mentioned held anything grander than a stretch of clay pans; and the two who spoke, in their turn, for the households of the northern plain and of the west, neither known to the girl by more than the word holder. Her grandmother named none of the five by any longer telling, and the girl understood even then, young as she was, that these were people whose whole worth to the reciters lay in the one wading and no more, the way a stone's worth to a wall lies in its standing and not in where it was quarried.

It was not the day's strangeness, either, that was finished.

For a little while after the fifth holder had waded back to the bank, nothing more happened, and the crowd, which had gone still for the wading, began to find its voice again in low murmurs and the shuffling of feet, the way a gathering does when it senses more is coming and cannot say what. Someone near the girl asked whether that was the whole of it, and someone else answered, in the flat certain tone people use when they are guessing and do not want it known, that it could hardly be the whole of it, for the king himself had so far done no more than stand there and receive what was brought to him, and a gathering did not walk days from every coast of the island only to watch five strangers get their clothes wet. The girl's mother, close enough by then to hear this exchange, said nothing to it either way, but drew her daughter in against her side and kept both hands on her shoulders, the way she held her at the edge of deep water, or at a cattle-fair when the beasts were being driven through, as though she too expected more to come and wished to be sure of where her own child stood when it came.

## IV. Of the Rings in the River

They came down from the upstream ground where the grass grew clean and little walked-over, and the girl had thought until that morning that the upstream ground was kept clear for some purpose of the reciters', a place for their fires and their reckoning apart from the crowd. She understood only when they began to come down, more of them than she could easily count, and more behind those, that the upstream ground had been kept clear all along for this, and for nothing else.

They were not all of an age, and that was the second thing that struck her, once the first shock of their numbers had passed. Some among them looked old, bent a little at the shoulder and slow on their feet, and she took these at first for the eldest of the family, until her grandmother, standing near her by then with the rest of the household, told her low that it ran backward: that the oldest of the king's children looked the youngest of any of them, and the ones with grey in their beards and stiffness in their knees were commonly the youngest born, grandchildren and great-grandchildren counted in mere centuries rather than in the thousands the eldest carried. The girl could not fit this into her head all at once, and did not try very hard; she only watched.

They went into the river in rings around him, the way water rings out from a stone dropped into it, the nearest ring made up of faces she thought at first must be his brothers and sisters, for they stood closest and moved with the most ease, though her grandmother said afterward that these were his children, the ones born earliest, in the first centuries of his reign, and that one bent, dry-faced old man among them, who took his place at the king's left hand as though the place belonged to him by long custom, was in fact his eldest living son, older by the reckoning of years than any grandfather the girl had ever heard named, though he stood in the water no straighter than any old man stands. She learned afterward, from a woman of the ford who knew the lines by heart, that this was Somhairle, keeper of the lines among his own people in the high valleys, two thousand nine hundred and sixty-two years old that summer and looking, by every account she ever heard after, every year of it.

Beyond that first ring stood a second, younger to look at and yet, her grandmother said, coming after in the order of birth rather than before, and among these a tall dark-haired man whom people on the bank pointed out to each other by name, Murchadh, who held the northern plain for his father and had eleven sons of his own scattered among the rings further out; he stood a head taller than most around him and looked, the girl thought, no older than her own uncle, though the woman who knew the lines said he had passed his twelve hundredth year that same season. Further out again, near the edge of the innermost crowd, was a young woman who laughed at a word said to her by the man beside her, no more than three hundred years old and looking every one of those centuries a girl of twenty, whom the same woman named Eithne, and said she held ground below one of the northern outposts and had a temper her father was said to find diverting rather than troublesome.

Ring beyond ring they went into the water, until the girl lost count of the rings themselves and could only see that the whole width of the ford between the two banks was full of them, standing thigh-deep and waist-deep according to where the river bed dipped, and that the water was piling up visibly behind their legs where so many bodies broke its running, so that the ford above them ran higher and slower than it had run all that summer, and below them ran lower and quicker, as though the whole river had been made to answer for a moment to the people standing in it rather than the other way about.

Somewhere in the middle of that long standing, a boy of her own age pushed in beside her at the bank's edge, a stranger to her, from one of the households of the west by his dress, and asked her, in the plain way children ask each other things at a gathering where every face is new, whether all of that in the water was truly one family. She said she did not know, though her grandmother had just told her so, because it did not feel to her like a thing that could be answered yes or no in a single word; and the boy, considering the rings a while longer himself, said only that his own father had eight brothers and sisters and he had thought that a great many until that morning, and after that the two of them stood together and watched without talking further, the way children will share a silence when a thing is too large for either of them to explain to the other.

The reciters on the near bank began then to say the names.

They said them in order, the eldest first, working outward ring by ring the way the family had gone into the water, and the girl heard names go by all that afternoon that meant nothing to her, sons and daughters and grandchildren and the grandchildren of grandchildren, some given a single word of what they held or had done and most given nothing but the bare name and the name of the parent before it. The sun crossed the whole sky while the reciters spoke, and still they were not through the rings nearest the far bank when the light began to go gold and then to go grey, and word came along the bank at last, passed mouth to mouth the way word travelled in that crowd, that the reciters had reckoned some four hundred of his blood standing in the water that day, and that they did not expect, when they began the telling again the next gathering, to be able to say the number had not grown since.

Watching him where he stood at the centre of it, unmoving while ring after ring of his own children and grandchildren stood in the cold water around him, the girl came slowly to a thought she never afterward heard put better by anyone older than she was that day: that he could not know all their names himself, any more than she could have named every pebble on the shore of the lake at home, and that this did not trouble him half so much as it seemed to trouble the reciters, who kept glancing toward him as though checking their work against some standard only he could set and would not.

Once, while the naming went on, she saw him turn his head and look a long while at a young woman standing in one of the outer rings, near the far bank, a woman no different to the girl's eye from a dozen others near her, dark-haired and quiet-faced and dressed as plainly as anyone in the water that day. He looked at her the way a man looks at a face he has met before and cannot place, turning it over, and the girl, young as she was, understood that look better than she understood anything else that happened at the ford that summer, for she had worn it herself more than once trying to place a face from the mill at Àth shean among a crowd of strangers. The woman did not seem to notice him looking, or if she did, gave no sign of it, and after a while he turned back to the reciters and the look was gone from his face as though it had never crossed it.

## V. Of Those Who Did Not Come

It was some while into that afternoon, with the names still being said and the light not yet failing, that the girl heard her grandmother say to another woman of the Red Hill, in the flat voice people use for a fact rather than a complaint, that no one had come that year from east of the vein.

The other woman said she had noticed the same, and that it was the second gathering running that the east had sent no one, and the two of them turned the matter over a little longer, wondering aloud whether the households there had trouble with their own harvest, or a grudge with some family of the northern plain, or simply no salt worth carrying so far when they had none of their own to trade for it. Neither of them arrived at an answer, and neither seemed troubled by the lack of one; and within the hour the girl heard the same two women laughing together over a joke a trader from the south had made about his own mule, the matter of the east's absence set down as easily as it had been picked up, the way a stone is set down that a walker has turned over once in passing and found nothing under.

The girl herself gave the matter no more thought than her grandmother had, being nine, and being far more taken up with the rings in the river and the names still being said than with anyone who had chosen to stay away from them. It was only in her own old age, telling this same gathering to her own daughter's daughter at the Red Hill, that she found herself dwelling on that hour longer than the rest of the day's telling seemed to want, turning over what it might mean that a whole coast of the island had gone unremarked at the one gathering every other coast walked days to reach; and even then she came to no answer either, and told it to her granddaughter only as it had come to her, a fact set beside the rest of the facts, with the day fine and the crowd content and nobody at the ford, that year, troubled enough by an absence to spoil an afternoon over it.

She asked her grandmother once, years and years after, whether anyone had ever gone east of the vein to ask why its people kept away, and her grandmother, by then old and stooped herself, said only that she had never heard of anyone troubling to, for the households there owed nothing to the ford and the ford asked nothing of them, and a debt that runs neither way is rarely chased down by either side of it. It was as much of an answer as the girl ever had, and she gave it to her own granddaughter in turn exactly so, no fuller than she had received it.

## VI. Of the Warm Water

The light was nearly gone from the sky, and the reciters' voices had gone hoarse with the saying of names, when the king spoke a few words to those nearest him in the water, too low for the bank to hear, and the rings began at last to break apart and wade back to the shore, ring by ring in something like the order they had gone in, though by then the light was too poor for the girl to be sure of it. She watched the crowd of them come up out of the river and scatter along both banks to their own fires, laughing now some of them, and calling to children left behind on the grass, ordinary people again once they were out of the water, mothers wringing river-water from their children's clothes and grandfathers complaining of the cold in their old joints, until it was hard to believe, watching them settle to their suppers, that they had stood, not long before, in rings four hundred strong around a man who had buried wives by the score and did not age.

When the ford was empty at last of everyone but himself, he stood there alone a while in the dusk, in the middle of the river where the current still ran a little high from so many legs having broken it all day, and did not come out at once, the way a man lingers in a doorway after everyone else in the room has already gone to bed.

The girl's mother had let go of her wrist by then, caught up in some last trade of salt for wool with a woman of the northern plain before the light failed entirely, and the girl had wandered, without meaning to wander far, to the very edge of the downstream ground, where the bank sloped low enough that the water came almost to the toes of her shoes. She was the nearest person to him on that whole stretch of bank, closer by twenty paces than anyone else left out in the dusk, and she stood there and watched him standing in the river the way she might have watched a heron stand in shallow water at evening, still, waiting on nothing she could name.

Behind her the fires were being built up for the night, and somewhere among them she could hear the salt-song being sung again, the same four lines she had heard sung all that first day, and further off a man laughing at something his own child had said, and under all of it the ordinary evening noise of several thousand people settling to their suppers within call of a river; and she remembered afterward how strange it had seemed to her, even at nine, that so much ordinary noise could go on so close behind her while the one man in front of her stood as still in the water as if the whole gathering had already gone home and left him there alone on purpose.

The water going past her own feet, she found when she crouched to trail her fingers in it, was warm, warm as the bath water her mother heated over the fire on a winter evening, though the day had not been especially hot and the river above the ford ran cold enough at that season to numb a foot left in it too long. Downstream of him, where the current carried past the place he stood, a thin mist rose off the water in the last of the light, the way steam rises off a spring on a cold morning, and hung there over the ford a little while before the evening air took it.

Her feet never went in past the ankles. She was nine years old, and her mother would have called her back before her knees were wet, and besides, some part of her, young as she was, understood without being told that the warmth in that water was not a thing meant for wading in. She crouched instead at the very edge of it, in the last light, with her whole hand under the surface where it ran warmest, and stayed a long while after he had at last waded out on the far side and gone up into the dark among his own fires, watching the mist thin and vanish over water that was already, by the time her mother came looking for her, beginning to run cold again.

To the end of her own long life she told it exactly so: that the river that evening had been warm as a bath round the king standing in it, and that downstream of him, in the last of the light, it had smoked like a spring.
