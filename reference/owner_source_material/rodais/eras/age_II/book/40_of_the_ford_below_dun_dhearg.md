# Of the Ford below Dùn dhearg

*GE 4,603*

*It is the tellers of the east themselves who give this best, for they had cause to remember it, and the telling that follows keeps closest to the account of a lad who stood in it, sixteen years old and the youngest spear of his household, whose own name the east never troubled to keep.*

## I. Of the Camp under Artair mac Fhearghail

They came down to the river in the last days of an Lùnastal, the whole strength of the country east of the vein, household by household, until the fires along the east bank ran for the better part of a mile and a boy standing at one end of them could not see the far end for smoke. Artair mac Fhearghail held Dùn dhearg that year, an old fort's holder grown grey in a country that had never sent a name to be recited at any ford, and it was under his word, more than any other's, that the households had come down at last, though he himself said afterward that no one word of his had done it: the thing had been three generations gathering, like water behind a bank nobody built on purpose, and he had only stood where the bank happened to break.

The lad's household came with the rest, twenty-some strong counting the old women who would not be left behind and the boys too young yet to carry a spear who came anyway to mind the fires. His father had cut turf on the second bank at Dùn dhearg the year it went up, and had told him since he could walk that no household east of the vein owed the hill at Dùn ìseal so much as a good morning, let alone a season's labour or a daughter in marriage; and the lad had grown up on that telling the way he had grown up on his mother's porridge, without ever once asking where the grain in it had come from.

Word had run down from the west all that summer, the way water finds its own low ground without anyone digging it a channel: that the king's own kin, greedy for the shares that would fall open when the old man went at last into the ground like other men, had begun to count each other's herds and each other's daughters with an eye that liked what it counted less each year; that the king held the hill at Dùn ìseal alone now, giving it to none of his blood, and sat on it the way a man sits on a coal he does not mean to hand to his neighbour's fire; that he had sent to Dùn dhearg once, some seasons back, bidding the households come in and be counted at last, and been sent an answer that owed him nothing and never would. Artair mac Fhearghail had carried that answer himself, as a young man, and he told it again at the fires the night the host came down, so that even those too young to remember the sending of it should know exactly what had been said and to whom.

"We told him we would keep our own bank," the old holder said, turning a coal over with a stick so that the good side of it faced up, "and that a hill five days' walk west of us was no more to us than a story is to a man with his own field to plough. That was the answer. It has not changed since, and I see no cause in twenty years to change it now."

Someone asked, from further back at the fire, whether it was true the king did not age, and whether that made him something other than a man that a host of ordinary spears might reasonably hope to bring down.

"He is flesh," Artair said, and did not raise his voice to say it, which was more frightening, the lad thought afterward, than if he had shouted it. "Whatever else he is, under the grey hair and the years, he is flesh, and flesh comes to an end somewhere. I have not lived sixty years east of the vein to tell my own grandchildren that a man cannot be met."

They had been told the king was coming east at last with his kin, meaning to hold the ford against whatever the east chose to do about it, or to be met at it and answered plainly one way or the other after three generations of silence; and the host had come down to the river in the dark before the first morning meaning to hold it first, on the strength of numbers alone, three thousand spears and more against however many the old man's kin thought fit to bring.

His mother had not come down with the rest of the household, being past the age for it and no fonder of rivers than her son's father was fond of words, but she had stood at the door of their house the morning they left and told him, plainly enough that he still had the whole of it by heart years after, that a fight three generations in the gathering was not a thing one lad's caution or one lad's daring would settle either way, and that his business in it was to come home again, and that she would hear no telling of the ford from him that did not end with him standing at that same door to give it. His father had said nothing to that, being a man who left the giving of instructions to his wife and the keeping of them to himself, but he had put his own hand once, briefly, on the back of the lad's neck before they took the road east to the river, which was as much as the lad had ever known him to say on any subject at all.

The lad slept badly that night, if he slept at all, between his father's back and the noise of a country's worth of neighbours settling their gear along a riverbank none of them had camped on before, and he remembers, of that whole night, mostly the cold that came up off the gravel through the ground and the sound, endless and low, of the river below the camp, which none of them yet knew to listen to.

## II. Of the Man Alone at the Ford

In the grey light before sunrise the lad woke to find the mist lifting off the water in ropes, and the west bank, which the host had gone to sleep expecting to see thick with the king's kin and their households and the spears of the hills mustered against them, empty.

There was one man on it.

He sat on a flat stone at the head of the ford with a spear laid across his knees, and there was no one else with him: no household, no fire, no line of spears drawn up at his back. The lad looked up and down the far bank for the rest of them, the way a man looks twice at a gate he expects to find shut and finds standing open, and found nothing but turf and the track going up from the ford toward the vein and toward Dùn ìseal beyond it, empty in the thinning mist.

Word went along the fires quicker than any man could carry it on his own two feet, the way such word always does: that the king had come ahead of his kin with a handful of his own household, and had sent that handful back up the road again in the night; that his kin, for all their coming in with tents and stewards two summers past, had not come to the ford at all, each house waiting on the others, each afraid that while its own spears stood at a river its cousins' spears would be settling accounts on its own hills behind its back. They had already begun, it was said, to quarrel over what the old man would leave, before he had left anything.

Nobody in the host of Dùn dhearg believed it at first. Then, all down the bank at once, as such things happen, everybody did, and a shout went up along the whole mile of fires that startled birds off the river a long way downstream.

The man on the stone stood.

The lad had heard all his life, at hearths from Caol shean to the double bank, how the king was said to be a big man, and found, looking across the water at him in the grey of that morning, that the king was not big as he had pictured him, and was bigger. He came down to the water's edge without hurrying and stood there with the spear held loose in one hand, and the grey of his hair was the only grey thing on that whole far bank of green turf and dark river-stone; and the lad, who had a spear of his own in his fist and three thousand spears at his back, felt something come across the water to him that he could only afterward liken to the warmth off a kiln a long way from the fire, and was afraid of it, and could not have said why he was afraid, since the man had not moved to threaten anyone and stood, if anything, unnaturally still.

Artair mac Fhearghail came down the bank to where the lad stood with his father, and looked a long while across the river before he spoke.

"He has left his kin behind him and come to the water alone," the old holder said, half to himself. "Either that is a great courage, or he does not believe we will come at him at all. We had best show him which of the two he has misjudged."

## III. Of the First Day

The first rush went into the ford at sunrise, before the mist was fully off the water. Two hundred went in together, or near it, with a roar that the lad felt in his chest before he heard it as words, and the shallow water broke white round their knees as they crossed; and the king went into the river to meet them without any haste that the lad could see, wading out from his stone until the water was at his thighs, and the front of the rush reached him about the middle of the ford, where the gravel drops and the current runs strongest.

The lad was young yet and had been kept back with the boys who minded the fires, so he watched the whole of it from the bank, and he said afterward, more than once, that he could not rightly tell what he had seen, only what came after it. The king did not fight the way the lad had watched men fight at a boundary quarrel or a cattle-raid, one man squared against another and then a third coming up behind the first. He fought, the lad said, the way a river fights a bank: one continuous taking-away of whatever came against him, no single blow answered by a single blow, so that spears went into the place where he had stood and found him already somewhere else, and men went down round him in a ring in the shallow water, and the ring did not close because there was no man left near enough to close it. The current below him ran red before the rush had been in the water the length of time it takes to say a short prayer, and the rush broke on him the way a wave breaks on a rock too old to be moved by any one tide, and what was left of it came back across the ford in worse order than it had gone in.

The king waded back to the west bank and sat down again on his stone, and did not seem, from that distance, so much as out of breath.

A cousin of the lad's mother, a big quiet man who had carried a spear at every boundary dispute the eastern country had seen in twenty years and never once come home from one with so much as a scratch to show for it, had gone in with the first rush and had not come out of the river at all, neither on his own two legs nor carried by anyone else's, and it was some while before anyone at the fires could say for certain into which of the several places a man might vanish in a press like that he had gone. His wife asked after him at every fire down the bank before noon, and was told, at each one, only that no one had seen him fall, which was not the same as being told he had not, and by evening she had stopped asking and sat instead with her children at her own fire, watching the water.

They came at him again before midday, in smaller lines this time, testing the water above and below the main crossing where the channel ran deep enough to swim a man off his feet, hoping to come at him from the side while the ford held his eye; and he was there before each line reached the bank, wherever there was, so that a household that had planned to cross unseen a bowshot upstream found itself, instead, wading back through its own dead not a quarter of an hour after it had gone in dry-shod. By early afternoon they had begun throwing spears from the bank rather than closing with him at all, a whole day's work of the fletchers spent inside two hours, and the lad heard men say, at the fires that dusk, that some of those throws had struck him true and did not seem, from what could be seen at that distance, to matter to him one way or the other.

At dusk the host drew back from the water to its own fires, as a beaten thing draws back to lick whatever can be licked, and counted its own losses, and did not care for the count. The king walked back to his stone in the last of the light and sat, and the lad, who had gone down near the water's edge with two other boys to fill a skin, saw him kneel at the river's lip and wash his hands in it before he went back to sitting, the way any man washes his hands after a day's hard work, and found that stranger than anything else he had watched that whole day.

## IV. Of the Second Day

By the second morning the dead had begun to dam the ford.

They lay so thick at the shallows below the crossing, where the current dropped them, that the water backed up behind them and spread wide over the gravel on either side, slow and red and warm where the day before it had run cold and clear; and the men of the east, coming down to the water that second morning, found they must climb over their own dead to reach any part of the river shallow enough to cross at all, which no household among them had ever had to do at any ford before, salt or fresh.

The lad went into the water on the second day, being older by one day and one night than he had been the first, and thought himself no longer a boy to be kept at the fires. He remembered afterward very little of it in order: the cold of the river round his legs and then, further out, a warmth in it he had not looked for, as though he waded into the outflow of a spring rather than the same current that had run cold past his own feet the evening before; a grey-browed face above him, closer than he had ever thought to stand to it, neither angry nor gentle, only still, the way a deep pool is still; and a hand taking the spear out of his two hands the way a mother takes a knife from a child too small yet to be trusted with the edge of it, without violence, almost without haste, as if the taking cost the hand nothing at all. After that he was on the east bank again, face down in trampled mud with the noise of the river in his ears, and it was some while before he understood that he was not among the dead damming it, only close to being.

He lay there the better part of that day, coming to himself in pieces, and heard the rushes go into the water without him, one after another, from before noon until the light began to fail, and heard them come back each time in smaller numbers than they had gone in with.

That second night the host of Dùn dhearg met at its fires, all of it that could still stand, and there was less shouting in that meeting than there had been the night before, and more silence. Men who had spoken loudest of holding the ford two nights before sat now with their eyes on the ground between their own feet, and one, a holder of grazing along the northern edge of that country who had lost two sons to the river in as many days, said outright that he did not care any longer what the king's kin did or did not do with their shares, and that he would take what was left of his own household home to bury its dead in its own ground rather than add a third son to the river's count.

Nobody answered him with anger, that being a grief no man at those fires could fairly grudge another. Artair mac Fhearghail let him finish, and let the silence that came after him sit a while before he spoke at all, which the lad thought afterward had been its own kind of answer.

Some others said, plainly, that the thing should be let go: that a river ford was no prize worth the whole strength of the east country, and that they should take what remained of their spears home to their own hills before there were none left to take home at all. It was Artair mac Fhearghail, sitting with his own spear laid across his knees the way the man on the far bank had laid his, who answered it at last with the only counsel anyone in that gathering had to set against it.

"No one man can hold a ford forever," the old holder said, and his voice did not rise for this any more than it had risen the morning before. "Whatever he is, under the years and the grey hair, he told me himself, before any of us came to this water, that he is flesh, and flesh comes to an end somewhere, the same as the sea comes to an end of a cliff, given water enough and time enough. We have given him two days of the one. Let every spear the east has go into that river together on the third day, and go on going in, and not draw back for anything short of his falling, and we will come, between us, to the end of him, because there is no other counsel here that does not end in going home with nothing, which I for one will not carry back to my own hearth while I have legs to stand at a ford on."

Nobody spoke against it after that, though a good few, the lad among them, said nothing for it either, only sat with their own thinking. It was, as Artair had said, the only counsel anyone had, and a host with nothing else to hold onto took it the way a drowning man takes whatever piece of wood comes past him, without asking first whether it will bear his weight.

## V. Of the Third Day

On the third day every spear the east country had went into the river together, at first light, before the mist was off the water, and did not come out of it again by twos and threes as they had the two days before, but stayed in, wave folding over wave the way the tide folds over a rock at the turn, however far back the rock throws each wave that reaches it.

The lad went in with the rest, being neither dead nor so wounded that his father's household would let him stay dry-shod while every other household sent its whole strength; and he found the water above his waist that morning where the dead of the two days before had dammed it higher than any ford on that river had ever stood, red through and through and warm as a bath left too long on the fire, and it stank in a way he never after smelled without the whole of that day coming back to him whole. He was far back in the press and saw the king himself only in glimpses, over the shoulders and between the spears of the men ahead of him: a grey head standing in the middle of a river with the whole strength of a country closing on it from three sides at once, and the strength breaking round that one head and closing again behind it the way a rising tide breaks round a sea-rock and closes behind, over and over, the whole day long, while the sun went from behind them to overhead to behind the west bank.

By the afternoon there was a light on the water where the king stood, low and close along the river's surface, of no colour the lad had a word for, dark and shining together the way no true fire is, so that where it touched the current the current gave off a kind of steam though the day was warm and windless; and men near enough to be within it, coming back afterward to the bank too spent to go in again, said among themselves, low, that they had seen in it every colour there was and none of them, and would say no more than that however they were pressed, then or after.

The king's kin did not come. Whatever else was true of that day, that was true of it entire: no household from Dùn ìseal, no spear of the hills, no rider on the western track. Twice that afternoon a cry went up along the host that dust had been seen on the road down from the vein, and twice men turned to look, some with hope of relief for the old man and some with dread of it, and both times the dust proved to be nothing but wind lifting the dry summer's dirt off a track that no one at all was riding down. The east had the ford and the river and the whole strength of the man in it entirely to answer for, and by the second false cry no one troubled any longer to turn and look.

At sunset he was still standing, in the middle of the ford, the host round him on every side and the dead round him higher than his own knees, and the river going over the dam of them in a low red fall where the shallows had been. He was not beaten. The lad, looking across at him in the last light with what strength he had left in his own legs, was as sure of that as he was sure of anything that whole day: that not one man of the whole east country had beaten him, and that no one man among them, not Artair mac Fhearghail himself with sixty years' grievance behind his arm, could have. It was only that there was no end to the men coming at him, and there was, somewhere in him however deep it was hidden, an end; and between those two facts, and nothing else, the whole of that third day turned.

## VI. Of the Fall

He fell in the dark of the third night, and no man afterward could say which spear had done it, or from which household it had come, and no household of the whole east country would let it be said, then or later, that the blow had been its own. It is told that when he went down the light went out of the river all at once, the way a coal goes out when a bucket of water is thrown square onto it, and that the water, which had run warm through three days no matter how cold the nights between them, ran cold from that hour, cold enough that men standing in it felt it turn under their own legs and cried out at the change before they understood what the cry was for.

The host stood a long while in the dark water after that, not knowing, for what felt the longest stretch of the whole three days, what it was that it had done, or what a man was meant to do next having done it; and some began to weep where they stood, waist-deep in a cold river in the dark, and could not afterward have said why they wept, since it was the very thing the whole east country had crossed three days of blood to bring about. Artair mac Fhearghail himself, who had spoken the counsel that sent them in on the third day and would carry that all his life whichever way the telling of it went, stood longest of any of them at the place where the king had gone down, saying nothing, until two of his own household came into the water after him and led him out by the arms as a man leads out an old horse that has stood too long in a flooded field and no longer trusts its own legs to find the bank.

They drew him out of the river at last onto the west bank, to the flat stone where he had sat the first grey morning of it, and laid him there. He lay on his back with his eyes open to the last of the stars going out overhead, and his face, when they brought a torch near enough to see it by, was the face of a young man, unlined, with nothing in it that had not been there, by every telling anyone could set against it, in the grove itself at the very beginning of things. Word went afterward, from those closest to Dùn ìseal, that the mason's own line, which keeps the years of its dead written beside their names, gave his as four thousand six hundred and twenty-six; that he had been king since the grove and had never once given it up in all that span; and that no man now living, east of the vein or west of it, had taken it from him with his own single hand, however many hands together had done, between them, what no one of them alone could have.

Before the dawn a saying went along the whole east bank, fire to fire, the way such sayings do when a thing has happened too large for any one teller to carry alone:

*Cha do leag aon duine e; leag an sluagh uile e.*
No one man brought him down; the whole host brought him down.

## VII. Of the Fire on the East Bank

The lad had waded out of the river some while before that saying reached his own fire, and sat by it wrapped in a dead man's cloak because his own had gone into the water with him on the second day and never come out again, too spent to feel the cold he must have been sitting in, and too spent, for a long while, to speak at all.

The men about that fire had been three days at a ford that had cost the east country more of its own blood than any raid or any famine any of them could tell of, and they wanted, as men will after such a thing, to have some word of it from someone who had been close enough to bring one back; and since the lad was known to have gone into the river both on the second day and the third, and to have come out of it alive both times, they asked him, when he had got his breath and a little of the fire's warmth into him, what the king had been like, close to.

The lad did not answer at once. He turned the question over the way a man turns a coin he is not sure he has been given the right change in, weighing what there was to say against what there was no way of saying, three days and a whole country's dead against a boy of sixteen sitting at one fire among a mile of them.

"He was warm," the lad said at last, and that was all he said, and he looked into the fire rather than at any of the men who had asked him, and none of them pressed him further for more, that night or, so far as the east country's own telling goes, on any night after it.
