# Of Eilidh the Grey and the Boy Who Went

*GE 2,391 – 2,402*

> *An gille a dh'fhalbh, cha tàinig e dhachaigh na aon fhear.*
> The boy who went did not come home the same man.
> — the Red Hill's own telling, of a fosterling it never named

## I. Of Eilidh's Two Sons

Eilidh Ghlas had held the hill a year when her elder son was born to her a second time in the telling, though he was the elder by birth from the first: for a mother of the king's own blood counts a child twice, once for the bearing and once for the year the world takes notice of him, and the ford did not take notice of Calum Ciar until his mother stood at the bank in the first sharing of her own holding and he stood a pace behind her right shoulder, dark-haired and watchful, and someone said, low, that here was the next one. He had not chosen it, and his mother had not pointed him out; a boy standing where an heir stands is heir enough for a ford to see, and after that no one at Dùn ìseal thought to look for another when they spoke of who would hold the hill when Eilidh was gone from it, however many hundred years that might be.

He was close with his mother in the way of a first child who remembers, or believes he remembers, a time before any other claimed her hand. Calum Ciar rode with her on the counting days and lifted the grain-skins himself beside her; and Eilidh, who did the same herself through every wet season rather than leave the weighing to a steward, thought this a good likeness and said so to no one but her own held silence. Of her younger son there is less to be told, and what is told belongs less to him than to the household that raised him, which is the way of the Red Hill's own list when it comes to speak of him at all.

He was born in the fourth year of his mother's holding, in a wet spring when the river at Àth shean ran high enough to float the ford-stones, and no reciter troubles to give the day, for no reciter of Dùn ìseal ever gave him a day for anything, first or last. He was smaller than his brother had been, and quieter, and grew, in the few years the hill's own telling allows him before it sends him from it, into a boy who watched more than he spoke and who followed the dogs about the yard more readily than he followed the men to the bank. Eilidh loved him, it is told, exactly as she loved his brother, for she had held enough seasons of counting by then to know that a hand which gives one child more takes something from the other that no gift repays. But she watched him, in the years he was old enough to be watched, with a plainer eye than she watched Calum Ciar, the way a holder watches a field that will not be sown this year and studies it all the same.

It was Eilidh's own mother, dead these many years before, who had first spoken of fosterage to her as a girl, in the valleys under the hill, long before either of them thought Eilidh would ever hold anything: that among the households of the near valleys, when a family had more than one son and land enough for only one to inherit, the younger was sometimes sent to kin at another hearth to be raised, so that he might grow up loved and useful in a house that was not waiting for him to die or go, and so that the house he left might have one less mouth counted against its portion and one less claim laid against its bank when the holder's time came. It was a custom of ordinary households, not of the hill, and Eilidh had turned it over in her mind through all the years of her own holding without doing anything about it, the way a woman turns over a thought she is not yet ready to act on, until the year her younger son was six years old and stood in the yard one evening watching his brother ride out with the men to see the boundary stones renewed, and did not ask to go, and did not seem to mind that he had not been asked.

She watched him from the hall door a long while that evening. It is told that she said to her steward, who stood by her, that a house with only one door out of it was no house for a second son, and that the steward, not understanding her, asked which door she meant, and she did not answer him.

She sent, in the spring of the following year, word to the Red Hill.

There had been peace at the ford for longer than either hill now living could number by its own holding: the meeting first made between Raghnall Ceannfhionn and a nameless holder of the west had held through every holder after, salt and flint and wool changing hands where it had first changed hands, marriages agreed there that neither hill's own custom would have made alone. But peace between two hills that owe fealty to the same king and nothing to one another is a thing kept fresh each generation by an act, not by the mere absence of a quarrel, and it had occurred to Eilidh, turning the old custom of the valleys over in her mind, that no act keeps a peace so well as a child sent to be raised inside it. She did not put this to her steward, or to anyone at Dùn ìseal, as a matter of the kingdom; she put it, when she put it to anyone, as a mother's plain wish that her younger son should learn a trade that was not waiting for an inheritance to be idle in, and the Red Hill's salt and its boats and its far sight of the western sea were as good a trade as any a boy of the king's blood had ever been given. Both things were true. The hill's own reciters, in the years after, chose to keep the second and let the first go, as reciters will when a mother's plain wish makes a better telling than a holder's policy, though no one now living can say which she weighed heavier the evening she sent the word west.

Calum Ciar, when he heard of it, asked his mother only whether he too would be sent somewhere, and was told no, that the hill was his and would stay his, and he was satisfied, or seemed to be, and never again in the hearing of the hill's own telling spoke of his brother's going before it happened or after. It is told, though not by the hill's own reciters, that on the morning his brother left he stood at the gate a long time after the party was out of sight down the valley road, and that when a groom asked him if he would come in out of the wind he said he was only counting how many days it would be fifty miles there and fifty back, which is a strange thing for a man of a hundred and fifty, who looked a youth of twenty, to trouble himself with unless he means, by counting, to make a thing feel nearer than it is.

Eilidh herself did not stand at the gate. She walked with the party as far as Àth shean, a morning's distance, and there, where the road turned west and her own road turned back to the hill, she knelt to her younger son's height the way Rìonach would kneel to it again four days later, and told him three things, which the hill's own reciters have kept whole, though nothing else of that parting: that he was not sent away for any wrong of his, that the Red Hill would be as much his home as this one had been for as long as he needed it to be, and that she meant to see him grown, and did not doubt the years the king's blood was given would hold long enough for it. None of the household walking behind her ever said she had wept at the parting, and she did not look back down the road once she had turned her own horse toward the hill, though the steward riding beside her said afterward, to no one who ever troubled to repeat it further than his own hearth, that she was silent the whole ride home in a way he had not known her to be silent before or after.

## II. Of the Road West

The boy went with two of his mother's household, a man called Donnchadh who had walked the salt road before and a woman called Rìonach who had kin at Àth shean and would turn off there, and he went on his own feet, for Eilidh had said that a boy fostered ought to arrive on the legs that would carry him the rest of his life at the Red Hill and not on a horse he would have to send home again. It was fifty miles and more from Dùn ìseal to the Red Hill by the salt road, west by south along the shoulder of the near valleys and down through country he had never seen, and it took the three of them the better part of a week's walking, sleeping the first two nights at households of the near valleys that Donnchadh knew by name and the rest under whatever roof or bank offered shelter, for the salt road in those years had shelters built along it at a day's walk apart, low stone huts with a hearth-place and nothing else, raised by no one household but kept swept by whoever passed last.

He carried the shape of that week with him more than its particular days: the valleys giving way by evening stages to country that ran flatter and wetter, reed beds where herons stood so still he took them for dead trees until one moved; a night at a shelter where rain came through a gap in the roof onto Rìonach's sleeping place and she only laughed and moved her blanket, saying a roof that never let rain through had never had a family under it; a day when Donnchadh, for no reason the boy could see, stopped on the road and looked a long while at a low green mound off to the south of it, and would not go nearer, and would not say why, only that it was older than the road and older than the salt trade and best left to whatever kept it. He asked Rìonach about it that evening and she said only that there were places on the island the Old Ones had left, and that a wise traveller let them lie the way he let a wasp's nest lie, out of no fear of any particular sting, only a plain sense that some things were not made for a man's curiosity to test.

Rìonach turned off for Àth shean on the fourth day, and it is told that she knelt to the boy's height before she went and told him that a household that took him in for a season, or a decade, or the rest of his growing, was not the household he had come from, and that this was a kind of doubling rather than a loss, the way a river that forks becomes two whole rivers, neither one lessened by the other; and that he would find, if he let himself, that he had two homes rather than half of one. He did not understand her then, or said he did not, though he remembered the saying well enough to repeat it, unprompted, many years and many miles from that road.

The land changed again on the fifth day: the reed country gave way to a wider sky and a smell the boy had never smelled before, which Donnchadh told him was the sea, though they were still some miles from any shore he could see, and told him also that what he was smelling under the sea was salt, and that where they were going the salt came from a lake and never from the sea at all, which the boy found so strange a thing to be told that he made Donnchadh say it twice.

They came down to the western lake shore on the sixth evening, with the light going long and gold across water that lay flat as a held breath, and a hill rising low and red-earthed on its far side with smoke standing straight up from it in the still air, and Donnchadh stopped where the road first showed it and said that was the Red Hill, and that its line had held the lake shore before ever the king came down from the grove, and that the boy would do well to remember, walking in, that this was a hill with its own long life and its own long pride and no cause whatever to feel itself the lesser of the hill he had left.

## III. Of the Household of Lachlann Ruadh

Lachlann Ruadh held the Red Hill in those years, a broad, red-bearded man not yet forty, who had come to the holding young by the death of an uncle with no children of his own and had held it since with a hand that Eilidh's own steward, hearing of him later, called plain in the same word he used of Eilidh, though the two had never met. He met the boy at the gate of the hill himself rather than sending a steward to do it, with his daughter Peigi at his side, a dark, quick-eyed girl some years the elder, who looked the fosterling over with the frank, unhurried attention of a child assessing a new dog brought into a yard she considered her own, and said, before her father could say anything, that he was thin for a king's son and she supposed they would have to feed him.

Lachlann laughed at that, a big sudden laugh, the sound, the boy soon learned, of the whole household settling, and said that Peigi was to be told he was no king's son and no king's grandson either, but a boy of the ford's people come to learn a trade, the same as she would tell any fosterling from any hearth, and that if she called him otherwise even once more he would hear of it. Peigi said nothing to that, which the boy learned soon enough was her way of agreeing to nothing and losing no ground by it, and took his one bundle from him without asking and carried it in herself.

The household he found within the gate was smaller than his mother's hall and louder, a red-earthed yard with byres on two sides and a hall low and long, full, in the evenings, of more voices at once than he had ever heard indoors. Lachlann kept no steward apart from himself for the small business of the hill, weighed the salt tallies with his own hand at the season's end the way Eilidh weighed the grain, and ate at the same board as his household rather than above it, a plain oak table scarred along one edge where, Peigi told him with some pride, her grandfather's grandmother had once split it testing whether it would take an axe blow without cracking through, and it had not cracked through, and the mark was kept.

They gave him work before they gave him a name for himself, which suited him, for he had never much wanted the one he had. He carried water the first weeks, and mucked the byres alongside boys his own age who did not know or greatly care whose son he was, and slept in the loft above the hall with three other fosterlings and two of the household's own younger sons, in a row of straw that Peigi ruled over, by common consent of the loft, as absolutely as her father ruled the hill below. She was not gentle with him, in those first months, in any way that a reciter would trouble to soften in the telling: she corrected his Red Hill speech when it slipped into the hill-talk of Dùn ìseal, laughed without much mercy the first time he fell into the lake wading for reeds, and once, when he cried in the loft at night in the second week and thought the others asleep, said nothing at the time but the next morning set herself beside him at the water-carrying without being asked and stayed there the whole of that day and most days after, which was, in its way, the kinder of the two responses and the one he remembered longer.

By the second year he had stopped thinking of the loft as a strange bed and started thinking of his own hall at Dùn ìseal, when word of it reached him, as the place his mother was rather than the place he lived; and it is told, though only by the Red Hill's own reciters and in a single line that the hill of Dùn ìseal has never troubled to dispute or confirm, that Lachlann Ruadh came to think of him, within a few years, less as a hostage of peace than as a son the sea had lent him, and treated him accordingly, teaching him himself the reading of weather off the lake and the working of the salt-rakes, skills he might as easily have left to a steward and did not.

Word travelled between the two hills that season and the next along the salt road itself, carried in the mouths of the carriers who walked it, for no household of either hill troubled to send a letter where a spoken word would do as well and travel as far. The boy learned, in this way and no other, that his brother had been given the judging of the near valleys' boundary stones that year, a small trust but the first of its kind, and that Calum Ciar had settled a dispute between two herders over a shifted marker with a plainness their mother was said to have approved of; and Calum Ciar, in the same way and by the same road, learned that his brother had grown a hand's breadth since the spring and had been let out on the lake in a boat for the first time and had come back wet through and pleased with himself. Neither brother sent more than that. The Red Hill's telling is careful to say this was no coldness between them; it was only that two brothers, the one a boy and the other long grown though he looked a youth, living fifty miles apart had, with each season, less to say that a carrier's memory could hold whole, and more that belonged only to the hearth where each now slept.

Eilidh herself came once, in the third year of the fostering, on no business but the seeing of him that she had promised at the ford, and stayed four days at the Red Hill as Lachlann's guest rather than as any holder visiting another's ground, sitting at the same scarred board the household ate from and asking after the tallies and the weather no differently than she asked after her son. It is told that when she left, the boy walked with her as far as the lake's western point, the furthest edge of ground the household called its own, and that she told him there, out of Lachlann's hearing, that she did not intend to come again for some years, out of no want of missing him, only a plain knowledge that a boy who watched the road too often for his mother's coming would never quite finish becoming the Red Hill's own; and he understood her, this time, without needing Rìonach's river to explain it to him, and did not watch the road after that, or not so that anyone at the Red Hill ever caught him at it.

## IV. Of the Lake and the Name Not Kept

The salt of the Red Hill did not come, as the boy had first thought when Donnchadh told him so on the road, from the sea at all, though the sea lay close enough to smell on any west wind; it came from the shallow western reach of the lake, where the water stood warm and still in the summer months and drew down in a dry year until its margin turned white with a crust that the household raked into baskets and carried up to dry on stone platforms above the shore. It was slower work than sea-salt and finer for it, prized on the ford's own salt road above the coarser grey stuff of the tidal pans, and the raking of it was a skill the Red Hill kept jealously among its own households and taught to no fosterling until it judged him ready, for a rake dragged wrong through the crust broke it into the mud beneath and lost a season's crust to nothing.

He was eight years old, in the third summer of his fostering, when Lachlann himself put a rake into his hands for the first time and stood behind him on the drying platform to show him the turn of the wrist that lifted the crust clean without breaking it into the black mud under it, a turn Lachlann said had come down in his own family longer than the Red Hill's own list could count, mother's hand to daughter's hand and, where there was no daughter, father's hand to whatever child stood nearest and willing. The boy broke the first three strokes of crust into the mud as any beginner did, and Lachlann said nothing but only turned the rake a little in his own grip to show the angle again, and by the fourth stroke he had it, a full clean sheet of white lifted whole onto the rake's flat and held there, and Peigi, who had come down to watch with no pretence that she had come for any other reason, said that was better than she had managed her first year and she would not have believed it of him.

It is a small thing to be given the first time a boy rakes salt without breaking it, and no reciter of Dùn ìseal has ever thought it worth the keeping; but the Red Hill kept it, in its own way, the way the Red Hill kept most things that mattered to it and let the hill of the Stone Kings keep the rest. The peace of the ford held, those years, without a word said of it at either hall; the salt road ran as it had always run; and if any at Dùn ìseal wondered, in the wet seasons when word came west and east along the road with the salt-carriers, whether the boy sent away at six was thriving where he had been sent, the answer that came back, carried in a salt-carrier's mouth rather than in any letter, was only that he was well, and growing, and had taken to the lake.

The Red Hill's own list, in those years, began a habit it would keep the rest of Lachlann Ruadh's holding and after: when the reciters spoke of him, weighing his years against his father's and his father's father's, they had taken to adding, more often than not, a second name after the first, so that he was Lachlann Ruadh and, in near the same breath, Lachlann an Altraim, Lachlann of the fostering, until in time the second name outran the first in the mouths of those who had never known him by any other, and the list, kept by rote from reciter to reciter, began to give the one where it might once have given the other. No one at the Red Hill could afterward have said the day the habit started, only that it had, sometime in those years the boy grew from six to eight and learned the turn of a salt-rake on the lake shore under Lachlann's own hand, and that once started it was never afterward unsaid.

Of the boy's own name in those same years, the hill's own telling, both the hill of the Stone Kings and the Red Hill after it, keeps nothing at all. His mother's name for him was spoken daily by the household that raised him, kept whole in every hearing; it is only that neither list, in the long custom that grew up around the two of them, ever troubled to set it down, the Red Hill because he was not yet its own and the hill of Dùn ìseal because he was, by then, no longer entirely theirs to claim. So he passed into both tellings, and out of them, as he had come, without one, called only the fosterling by the hearth that kept him and, more rarely as the years went on, the boy who went by the hearth that had sent him.
