# Of Murchadh in the North

*GE 2,601 – 2,999*

> *Cha bhi cnoc aig neach ach fhad 's a their an Rìgh e.*
> No one holds a hill longer than the King says he does.
> — said in the northern plain, of every holder since Murchadh

## I. Of the Spring That Never Freezes

Murchadh was the ninth of the king's sons, and the one the king sent north to the plain with nothing more than his own name to hold it by, and he was eight hundred and ninety-six years old the spring he rode out of Dùn ìseal with sixty households at his back and a strip of the northern plain in his keeping that no one before him had troubled to settle beyond its edges. He had his mother's black hair still, and would keep it long after the households he led north had buried three generations of their own greying, for he was born after the grove like his father's other sons, though already, in that ninth century of his life, the first grey had begun to show at his temples, faint as frost on a north slope, so that the older men riding with him took it for a kind of promise, that whatever else he was he would not outlast them by so many lifetimes that they need never learn the shape of his death.

The ground he was given had one thing on it worth the naming, a spring on a low rise above the plain that ran clear and full through every winter the households there could remember, never once skinned with ice though the standing pools about it froze hard enough to bear a man's weight from Samhain to the first thaw. Murchadh had heard of it from a herdsman before he had ever seen it, and when he came to it at last, in the fifteenth year of his holding, he stood over it a long while without speaking, watching the water break the crust of frost at its lip and run on regardless, and it is told that he said only, to the men beside him, that a household built round a thing that did not freeze need never again reckon its water against its wood, and that this was worth more to men in that country than any wall.

He raised his own hall there that same year, timber and turf as every hall on the island still was raised, and called the place for its size before it had earned the name, Baile Mòr mhòr, the great big town, which the households about him took at first for a joke against the six poor roofs then standing and kept afterward without irony once the joke had come true under them. By the time Murchadh's own hair had gone properly grey, in the fourth century of his holding, Baile Mòr mhòr stood eighty houses about the spring, byres and workshops and a granary raised on stones against the damp, the largest place built in that generation anywhere north of An Cnoc Ruadh, and the households of the plain came to it at every gathering-tide the way lesser streams come down into a river, bringing wool and hides and the grey flint of Cnoc àrsaidh to trade against salt carried up from the shore.

He did not call himself a king there, nor one of Rìghrean na Cloiche, nor anything the households of the south would have known the word for. He called himself, when anyone asked, a man holding ground his father had given him, and left it at that, and the plain took to calling him the same, Murchadh a chumas am Blàr, Murchadh who holds the plain, until the longer telling wore down to the shorter one and he was simply Murchadh Mòr, the name his own great size and his father's habit of naming things large had between them settled on him before he was two hundred years into the holding.

## II. Of the Rough Ground and the Boar-Thickets

The plain did not fill all at once, and what filled it, in the years after Baile Mòr mhòr stood entire, filled it the way water finds the low ground first and the high ground after, household following household along whatever line of easy walking or standing water led them on.

North-east of Murchadh's spring the ground rose rough and stony, fit for little but sheep, and it was sheep the households there kept from the first year they broke ground, calling the place Baile gharbh for the roughness under it, and sending their wool south along the coast road to Cruinneachadh an Àtha every year rather than north to Murchadh's own granary, for the coast road was the shorter way and the ford took wool as readily as the plain did. West of the spring, along the coast where the old thickets still stood thick with thorn and rowan, the boars ran heavier and meaner than anywhere else the island's herders knew, and the households that settled there, at a place they called Baile fhiadhaich for the wildness of it, hung the tusks of the biggest boars over their doors the way other households hung a fox's brush, and ate boar at every feast the way the southern shore ate fish, until it was said, half in mockery and half in plain fact, that a man from Baile fhiadhaich could be known by his gait before he opened his mouth, from a lifetime of walking ground a boar might break from at any step.

Further round the coast, on a harbour the old maps of that country did not trouble to name, households took up an anchorage beside a stone landing built by hands none of them could put a name to, older than any wall standing near it, and used the landing as it stood without mending a stone of it, out of a caution common enough among the island's settlers wherever Seann-Dhaoine' work still stood, that a thing built by hands so long silent was better left as it was found than corrected by hands that did not understand it. They called the place Cuan shean, the old bay, for the landing rather than for themselves, and it grew slowly at first and then, once the fish proved good off that coast, faster than either Baile gharbh or Baile fhiadhaich, until within two centuries it had its own small fleet and its own small quarrel with Murchadh's granary over which of them the coast road properly served.

Murchadh rode the round of these three settlements once in every decade of his early holding, less often as the decades lengthened into centuries and the ride came to feel shorter to a man for whom a decade was no longer than a season had once been to the men riding beside him. He judged what needed judging, a boundary dispute at Baile gharbh over a well two households claimed by the same spring, a quarrel at Baile fhiadhaich over whose dogs had put up whose boar, and settled each the way his father settled such things at the ford, by hearing both sides through and giving the ground to whichever household had been longer on it, without troubling to write the judgment down anywhere but in the memory of the households who had heard it given.

## III. Of Eithne and of the Sons

In the hundred and second year of Murchadh's holding, in the north, word came south to Dùn ìseal that the king had another daughter, born to a wife of the near valleys whom the reciters afterward remembered chiefly for a quickness of tongue that her daughter kept whole. They named her Eithne, and she was born in the year the households of Dùn ìseal count as two thousand seven hundred and three of the grove, a hundred years after Murchadh had ridden north with his sixty households, so that by the time she was grown enough to be spoken of as anything but a child she was already a stranger to the plain her half-brother held, and knew him, when she did come to know him, chiefly by his size and by a certain slowness of speech that she took, in her own quickness, for stupidity, and was some centuries in unlearning.

Murchadh himself, in that same span of years, had begun a household of his own sons, eleven of them born to two wives over the better part of three centuries, the first in the hundred and nineteenth year of his holding and the last so long after the first that the eldest of the eleven had grey in his own beard before the youngest had his first tooth. He gave each of them, as they came of an age to want it, a corner of the plain to hold under him rather than a share of his own hall, for he had learned, watching his father give and take back the whole island by the same rule, that a son set to holding a corner of ground learns the weight of it faster than a son kept idle at his father's own hearth, and every one of the eleven, in his turn, learned it. The eldest, a broad, slow-spoken man the plain came to call Aonghas an Spuir for a spur of high ground he held above the river, held it so well and so long that his own son after him held it in turn without any word from Murchadh needed to confirm it, and it was that steady holding, more than any of the louder ones, that Murchadh, grown old himself in the holding, came to think the truest mark of a household settled for good.

Eithne, for her part, grew into a woman as quick as her mother had been and quicker than most of her father's blood troubled to be, and when she went out from Dùn ìseal at last, in her hundred and forty-fourth year, she rode west to the ground below Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, a broken watch-tower Seann-Dhaoine had raised and long since let fall, where a scatter of households had lately settled and asked, having heard she was of the king's blood and quick with a judgment, that she stay among them a season and help them lay out their fields before the ploughing quarrels set in that every new settlement seemed heir to. She stayed the season, and then a decade, and then simply stayed, taking no stone from the ruin above the houses though it stood the nearest quarry to hand, out of the same caution the households of Cuan shean had shown their own old landing, and the place grew up round her the way Baile Mòr mhòr had grown up round Murchadh's spring, its own households naming it, within that first year and out of no more than hope for what it might become, Cathair mhòr, the great city, though it was then no more than a dozen roofs below a broken tower. It was hers in all but the naming even once the naming was done, and she held it, in her own right and without asking her half-brother's leave, for longer than either of them then reckoned that either of them had left to hold anything.

## IV. Of Dùn chrom and the Bank That Faced Two Ways

Midway between Murchadh's plain and the hills where the vein ran, on rising ground that looked one way toward An Cnoc Ruadh and the other toward the passes, households raised in the two hundredth year of Murchadh's holding a banked fort they called Dùn chrom, the bent fort, for the shape the ditch took following the ground rather than any straighter plan a surveyor might have set on it. It was built, from the first spade of earth turned, by two kinds of household at once, herders come up out of the plain who wanted the bank to face south against no threat any of them could name but old habit, and herders come down from the hills of the vein who wanted it to face north against the cold winds off the passes rather than against any enemy at all, and the two parties, neither willing to give way to the other and neither troubling, in that easy generation, to make a quarrel of it, simply raised the bank both ways, a long crooked earthwork that curved to answer both wants at once and satisfied neither party fully, so that the fort stood from its first year with a name that fit it in more ways than the households who gave it ever meant.

It stood so, its bank facing both ways and troubling no one, only a little while, until the second year of its standing, when the holder its households had by then settled on, a stone-faced man named Ruadhan Beag, fell into a quarrel with the holder of Dùn thais, the older bank a day's walk to the south, over which way a bank ought properly to face when the ground it stood on gave a household the choice, and neither man, once the quarrel was well begun, could remember afterward which of them had started it or over what actual grazing or water it had first been raised, only that it had grown, over two winters of hard words carried back and forth by herders neither man had sent as messengers but who carried the words all the same, into something large enough that both sent to Murchadh at Baile Mòr mhòr to settle it before it grew larger yet.

Murchadh rode to Dùn chrom himself, in his own one thousand and ninety-seventh year, a season he remembered afterward, by his own account to his sons, chiefly for the cold of it, and stood on the bank between the two holders and asked each in turn to show him what harm the other's bank had ever done him, and neither could show him any harm at all, only the discomfort of a ditch that answered a want its own household no longer entirely remembered wanting. He told them, at the end of a long morning's hearing, that a bank built to face two ways had stood its first winters without falling to either enemy the two builders had once feared, and that this seemed to him a better argument for the bank than either holder had yet made against it, and he left the ditch as it stood and rode home, and the quarrel, having been heard rather than settled, wore thin over the following year the way most such quarrels did on that plain. Neither side yielded it. It simply grew too old to feed, once a season had passed without blood in it, and neither holder could find the heart any longer to keep it warm.

## V. Of Beathan at the Slab

It was in the two hundred and third year of Murchadh's holding, while the quarrel of the banks was still fresh enough in the telling that herders carried it as news rather than as an old tale, that word reached the north of a death far to the south that touched Murchadh's own house not at all and yet was carried up the plain, from household to household, with a weight the quarrel of the banks never had.

Beathan, son of Somhairle, who had sat his nights among the herdsmen at the slab since before Murchadh was born and had grown old in the sitting the way none of the king's other blood then living had yet grown old, died that winter at Tobar dhìreach, in his own two thousand two hundred and thirteenth year, the oldest death any household on the island could then remember among the king's kin, and word of it went out from Na Glinn Àrda the way a stone dropped in still water sends its rings, further than any messenger could ever have travelled on his own legs. Murchadh had met him only twice in his own long life, both times briefly, at gatherings neither man had come to for the other's sake, and knew him chiefly by the Line he kept whole in his own head and by a certain slowness of speech the households of Na Glinn Àrda shared and the plain's own quicker talkers found strange in a man of the king's blood.

What the plain took from the death, more than the man himself, was the number, two thousand two hundred and thirteen years, said aloud at every hearth that winter the way a weight is said aloud when it is finally lifted and known, and the households of Baile Mòr mhòr, hearing it, looked at Murchadh, then not yet eleven hundred, and did their own reckoning under their breath, and some among the oldest of them began, that winter, to speak for the first time of what it might mean for a household to outlive not only its own holder but the holder's holder too, a thing none of them had yet seen done on the plain and all of them, without saying so aloud, understood they eventually would.

## VI. Of the Holy Hill, the Red Well and the Marsh Houses

Further south and east of Murchadh's own ground, in the years the plain came to call the settled years, three more places rose that the reciters afterward set beside Baile Mòr mhòr, Baile gharbh, Baile fhiadhaich, Cuan shean and Dùn chrom in the long telling of how the north filled.

On a hill whose spring the households that came to it found already good and whose name, Cnoc naomh, the holy hill, they found already given, by whom or for what reason none of them could learn from any teller then living, a settlement grew that kept the name and never troubled the why of it, taking the hill's own holiness, whatever its cause, as a fact of the ground the way they took its water and its grazing, without asking more of it than it gave. On the southern cape, where the keepers of a beacon fire had for generations kept watch over water thick with wrecking rocks, households built at last round a well whose water ran faintly reddish from the rock it rose through, and called the place Tobar dhearg for the well's own colour, and it grew, in time, into the greatest place on that whole coast, its beacon burning nightly for boats that had learned, by then, to look for it. And between Tobar dhearg and the older Seann Skell to its west, on ground too low and wet for any sensible household to choose first, a settlement rose whose first houses stood on driven piles over standing marsh water, moved afterward, once the piles proved the ground beneath them firmer than it looked, onto solid footing a spear's cast inland, and kept, even after the moving, the name the marsh had first given it, Muileann naomh.

None of these three troubled Murchadh's own holding directly, lying as they did beyond the edge of the plain he had been given, but the plain's own tellers folded them into the same telling all the same, for it was in those years, the reciters afterward agreed, that the whole northern half of the island passed from a scatter of households each alone on its own ground into something that could be spoken of, at last, as settled, place answering place along roads that had grown, without any single hand laying them out, from nothing more than the paths households wore between one hearth and the next.

## VII. Of the Valleys Given and Taken Back

In the two hundred and ninety-ninth year of Murchadh's holding, word came north from Dùn ìseal, this time carried by two riders sent for the purpose rather than by herders passing news from one household to the next. The message they carried, given to Murchadh in his own hall at Baile Mòr mhòr before the fire had properly warmed the men who bore it, was that the king was taking back the western valleys of the plain, the poorer, wetter ground toward the coast that Murchadh had held longest of all his father's gifts, and giving them instead to a young grandson of An Cnoc Ruadh's own line, a man Murchadh had met perhaps three times in his life and could not, that evening, entirely call to mind the face of.

He did not argue it. He asked the riders only whether his father had given a reason, and they told him, as plainly as they had been told to tell it, that the king had said no more than that the valleys had grown crowded enough to want a nearer hand than a hand at Baile Mòr mhòr could give them, and that this was reason enough, from that hand, for anyone on the island to accept without further asking. Murchadh sat with it a long while after the riders had eaten and gone to their beds, turning it the way his own father was said to turn most things, and told his eldest son, the broad quiet man who held the spur above the river, that he had held those valleys for the better part of three hundred years and felt, losing them, less the loss of the ground itself than the loss of the particular quiet they had given him, a corner of his holding he had never had to think hard about because it had never once given him trouble, and that this, he supposed, was as fair a payment as any holder on the island ever got for three hundred years of a thing going well.

His son asked him whether he would send word south asking the reason plainer, or asking to keep even a corner of the valleys for his own line's sake, and Murchadh told him no, that his father gave and took back by a rule none of his children had ever yet seen written down or heard spoken plainly, and that the rule, so far as twelve hundred years of watching it worked on his own household and on every household he had ever heard tell of, came to this: that a man held what he was given for exactly as long as the giving served, and no longer, and that arguing the rule had never once, in any telling Murchadh knew, changed what the king in the end did. The valleys passed to An Cnoc Ruadh's grandson before the season turned, and the plain, within a year, had stopped speaking of it at all, the way a wound stops being spoken of once the scar has set, and Murchadh's own household held what remained to it, the spring, the granary, the eleven sons each settled on his own corner, without any further word from the south for the better part of a century.

## VIII. Of the Summons

It was in his own one thousand two hundred and ninety-fourth year, so near the close of the settled years that the plain had by then almost forgotten there had ever been an unsettled time, that Murchadh received the second summons of his life to leave his own hall and ride south, the first having been the summons that sent him north to begin with, near four hundred years before.

The riders who brought it this time were of the king's own household, sent for the purpose, rather than herders carrying word between one household and another the way the quarrel of the banks had travelled. The word they carried was short and gave no more reason than the taking of the valleys had given: that the king would hold a gathering at the ford of the salt and the wool, in the coming year, greater than any gathering the ford had yet seen, and that every holder of every share of the island, near or far, was to come to it and bring what of his own blood cared to stand with him. Murchadh asked the riders, as he had asked the riders who brought the valleys' taking, whether his father had said why now and not some other year, and got, this time, no answer at all beyond a shrug that told him the riders knew no more of the why than he did.

He sent word that year to each of his eleven sons on their own corners of the plain, to Baile gharbh and Baile fhiadhaich and Cuan shean, to the holder now settled at Dùn chrom in Ruadhan Beag's place, and south to Eithne below Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, that the king had called a gathering and that whoever of the north's blood meant to be counted in it should be ready to ride before the spring turned. Most answered that they would come. A few, the households furthest north whom five hundred years of settling had made, by slow degrees, more the plain's own people than the king's blood at all, sent back only that they would send what they could and stayed where they were, and Murchadh, hearing it, found in himself neither the will nor, he told his eldest son, quite the right to press them further, for a household that had held its own corner of ground for five generations without once needing the king's hand to settle anything on it had earned, he thought, the right to decide for itself how far south its own feet still owed the walking. He rode for the ford himself that spring with sixty of his own household behind him, the same number, though none of the same men, that had ridden north with him at the holding's beginning, and did not yet know, riding, how many of his own blood he would find waiting there before him, nor how far south the ford truly lay from a life spent, by then, four centuries in the same one plain and close on thirteen hundred years in the world.
