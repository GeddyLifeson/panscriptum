# Of the Old Names

*GE 591 – 671*

*Part Two: Salt and the Handful*

*At Seann Skell the boat-menders still say a household came down the river before any of them had a boat to mend, and would not so much as lean a ladder on the old stones to reach their own roof.*

## I. Of the Word Seann

In the five hundred and ninety-first year of the hill, word went down every valley the king's blood had settled that a son had been born to Somhairle in the high pastures, a boy got on Cairistìona and named Beathan, and that Somhairle meant to keep him there among Clann na Ceiste rather than bring him down to the hill at all. It was carried by no messenger sent for the purpose, only by the ordinary traffic of drovers and salt-carriers who passed word along with everything else they carried, and it reached Iain mac Fhearchair's household on the middle reaches of the Abhainn naomh the same week as the shearing, so that the two things sat together afterward in the memory of that year: the wool piled white on the green, and the news that the long-lived line had another child in it who would, if the pattern held, still be a boy to look at when Iain's own grandchildren were old.

Iain's household had been settled on the river a generation, in a bend where the water ran slow enough for cattle to ford it without swimming, and they knew the coast only at second hand, from traders who came up with strings of shell and salted fish and went back down again with wool and hides, speaking of a shore two days further on where a people neither the king's nor any other's had once lived thick along every inlet and left their houses standing when they went. It was in Iain's father's day, or his father's father's, that the hill-folk settling those upper reaches had begun to call the old places by their old names with the plain word for *old* set in front, rather than trouble to make new names of their own or learn what the places had once been called in full: Seann Dunn and Seann Chwen for old places on coasts far off, Seann Skell for the harbour town, whose true name, if the Seann-Dhaoine who built it had ever spoken one aloud to a hill man, had not come down to anyone still living to speak it back. No one had decided this the way a judgement was decided on the hill. It had simply grown, the way a path grows across a field where enough feet have already gone, and by the time Iain was a grown man with a household of his own it was no more a choice to say *Seann Skell* than it was a choice to say *river* for the water running past his door.

"We keep their names and touch nothing else of theirs," Iain told his daughter Marsaili more than once, when she was young enough still to ask him why, "because a name costs nothing to keep and a great deal to make, and because I have never yet heard of any good coming to a man who took up a thing the Seann-Dhaoine left lying." He said this the way he said most things, without much weight behind it, a rule handed down the way a shape for cutting turf is handed down, useful and unexamined; and Marsaili, born the ninth year after Beathan and growing up on the same bend of river her father had grown up on, took the rule in with her milk and thought no more about it than she thought about the water's own habit of running downhill.

She grew up hearing the coast talked of at the fire the way distant, half-believed places are always talked of by people who have never gone there: the harbour with its old mole running out into the tide, long enough, the traders said, to shelter a dozen boats, though no hill boat had ever tied up along it in living memory; the low round houses set back from the water with roofs of turf grown so old and so thick they seemed part of the ground; and, standing apart from the houses nearer the harbour mouth, a tower of dry stone, squat and windowless but for a slit here and there, that no trader who had ever gone down that road claimed to have set foot inside, or wished to. Iain himself had seen it once, as a young man carrying wool down with his own father, and would say only that he had stood a while looking at it and had felt no wish at all to go nearer, a feeling he could put no better word to than the feeling a man gets standing at the edge of another household's grief, uninvited and better off staying that way.

Marsaili's grandmother, who had come up the river herself as a girl from a household further south and had once, as a young woman, gone the whole way down to trade at the harbour before any hill family lived within two days of it, told a different kind of tale of the coast, less awed than practical: that the Seann-Dhaoine's houses, whatever queerness clung to the tower, were built no differently in their bones from a hill house, stone and turf and a hearth in the middle, only rounder and lower and set closer to their neighbours than hill custom liked; that the smell of the place, salt and weed and the char of driftwood fires, was the whole of what she remembered as strange about it; and that she had stood once at the harbour mouth and counted eleven boats out on the water at once, drawn each toward a different reach of the tide with no more order to them than a scatter of gulls, and thought, young as she was, that she had never in her life seen so many hands at one work with no one among them giving orders. Marsaili turned that telling over more than the tower's, though she could not have said, even at the time, why one stayed with her longer than the other; only that it seemed to promise something the fireside talk of towers and silence did not: a shore that had once held ordinary lives as well as mystery, boats out at their ordinary work, a people who had risen in the morning to mend nets rather than to be spoken of forever after in a hushed voice by strangers who had never known them.

## II. Of the Coming to the Harbour

Marsaili was eight-and-twenty, married four years to Cailean mac Ghilleonain and mother already to two children with a third on the way, when her father called the household together on an evening in the dark half of the year and told them, without any great preamble, that he meant to move it down to the coast before another winter turned.

The river bend had grown crowded in his lifetime, three households now grazing ground that had fed one comfortably in his father's day, and word had come up, as word always came, that the harbour at Seann Skell stood as empty of hill-folk as it had ever stood, a river-mouth with good landing and no one living close enough to use it. "The Seann-Dhaoine are gone from it entirely," he said, "and have been gone longer than any of us has lived, and I would rather my grandchildren grew up on a shore with fish in easy reach than go on quarrelling over this bend with my brother's sons for another forty years." No one argued the sense of it strongly enough to change his mind, though Marsaili's mother wept the first night over the grave of her own mother, which she would be leaving behind unvisited, and Cailean said little, being a man who followed where his wife's father led and had long since made his peace with it.

They went down in the early spring of the six hundred and twenty-eighth year, eleven souls and what beasts and goods a scatter of ox-carts could carry, following the Abhainn naomh two days further than any of them had gone before, until the river widened between low banks and the air changed, carrying salt under the green smell of the water the way a distant fire carries smoke before it can be seen. They came out at last above the pool where the river met the tide and looked down on the harbour their traders had spoken of for three generations without any of them having stood where Marsaili now stood: houses roofless and grown over in green, the old mole running out from the shingle in its long low line of unmortared stone, its landward end still dry but its far foot already under the first of the flood tide, exactly as the traders had always said it would be; and set apart from the rest, nearer the harbour's mouth than any house had been built, the tower, grey and blind-walled, with gulls going in and out of the slits in its side as freely as if no one had ever built it and no one had ever left it.

Their first camp that night was pitched on the higher ground behind the old houses, not among them, though the roofless walls would have made a readier shelter than the carts and hides they slept under; and it was Iain who set the rule before anyone else could set it differently, walking the ground himself in the last of the light and marking with a line of stones where a household might build and where it must not, keeping every new wall a stone's throw and more from the nearest standing Seann-Dhaoine wall, and the whole camp further still from the tower.

"We will use what is already given to any hand that walks the shore," he told them, meaning the mole, the harbour, the fish that had gone uncaught in it for however many years it had stood empty, "and we will raise nothing of our own against what is theirs, and we will not go into that tower, not to shelter from rain, not to store grain, not for any reason a man can think of on a dry night that will not look a great deal smaller to him on a wet one." The hill kept no law in those years, only the king's own judgements given one at a time on his own hill, and no king's word had ever reached this shore at all. What held here was only Iain's rule for his own household, given the weight an old man's rule carries when no one troubles to test it, and it held, that first season, without a single soul asking why more than once.

They built their first houses that summer within sight of the old ones but never touching them, using timber carried down with the carts and turf cut fresh from the higher ground, and mended the landward stretch of the mole with stones gathered from the shore rather than lifted from the mole, a distinction Iain was careful to draw and careful that his sons and his sons-in-law drew after him: a man might lay a new stone beside an old one, he said, but he would not take an old stone up to make it new, for the taking felt to him a different thing entirely from the laying, though he could not have said in words exactly where the difference lay.

## III. Of the Storm and the Cracked Stone

Marsaili's third child, a daughter, was born that autumn in a house not yet a full season old, with the sound of the tide new enough in everyone's ears that her mother swore afterward the child had slept worse for it than either of her brothers had slept for anything in their own first winters; and it was in the deep of that same winter, in the first days of an Fhaoilleach with the year barely turned, that the first great storm out of the west found the harbour and put every soul in it to a test none of them had faced on the river.

It came in the night, as the worst of them do, a wind that rose from nothing in the space of an hour and drove the tide higher up the shingle than the eldest among them, who remembered storms enough from the river years, had ever seen water climb on open coast. Marsaili woke to hear the roof-tree groaning overhead and Cailean already up and bracing the door with his own shoulder against a wind that seemed to want the whole house and not merely the roof of it, and she gathered her children into the one dry corner and sat with them through the worst of it not knowing, until daylight came grey and reluctant over a sea still heaving, whether the mole her father had set such store by mending would stand the night at all.

It had not stood whole. The seaward third of it, the oldest stretch and the part no hill hand had touched, lay broken where the worst of the sea had found a weakness the tide alone had never shown, great unmortared stones thrown down and scattered along the shingle like a wall a giant had kicked apart in passing, and the harbour that had sheltered nothing more than a handful of coracles through the autumn now lay open at its mouth to whatever the sea chose to send in.

There was argument that morning, the first real argument the household had had since the leaving of the river, for two of Iain's sons-in-law held that the broken stones, lying now scattered and half buried in shingle rather than set in their old order, were no longer truly the Seann-Dhaoine's wall at all but only stone, ready stone, lying where any hand might lift it and set it again to better purpose than the sea had left it; and Iain, old now and walking the wreck of the mole with a stick he had not needed on the river, heard them out and said no.

"They are broken," he told them, "but they are not ours for the breaking, and a thing does not become mine because the sea has done to it what I would not do myself. If the mole is to stand again it will stand on new stone laid by our own hands beside what is left of the old, the way it stood before the storm, and not built up out of what the storm knocked down." Cailean, who had held his tongue through the whole of the argument as he generally did, said only, when it was over, that he thought the old man right, though he could no more have said why than Iain himself could, only that it seemed to him a shore a man had only lately come to was no place to start testing which of another people's rules could be broken and which could not, when the people themselves were no longer there to say.

They mended the mole that spring with fresh-quarried stone from the higher ground, a harder labour by far than reusing what lay ready to hand on the shingle, and the work went slower for it, so that it was two summers before the harbour stood sheltered end to end again; but no household on that shore, in all the years Marsaili afterward kept count of, ever again argued seriously for lifting a Seann-Dhaoine stone to lay it elsewhere, whatever state the sea or the years had left it in.

The quarrying taught them things the river had never had cause to teach: which of the higher slopes gave stone that split clean along a true face and which gave stone that shattered wherever it pleased, which carts could be trusted down the steep track to the shingle loaded and which would want a second pair of hands at the tail-rope on the last pitch. Marsaili, carrying her own weight in stone down that track through the whole of the second summer with her youngest strapped across her back, came to know the harbour's ground better in those two years of hauling than she ever came to know the river's, and said once, laughing, to Cailean, that she had left her father's country to escape one kind of labour and found the sea had simply given her another; and Cailean, who laughed rarely, laughed at that, and answered that at least the sea's labour ended somewhere a man could see it end, in a mended wall standing plain against the tide, whereas the river's quarrels over grazing ground had never in his lifetime ended at all, only paused between one dispute and the next.

## IV. Of the Boats and the Burial

It was in the six hundred and fortieth year, with the mole whole again and three more households come down from the river to join the first, that Cailean and two of Iain's grandsons built the first boats the harbour had held since whatever craft the Seann-Dhaoine had once kept there, small round-sided hide boats of a kind the hill had always used on lake and river, never before trusted to open tide; and it was Marsaili, not any of the men who had built them, who insisted on being in the first that went out from the mended stone, on a morning calm enough that even Iain, watching from the shingle with the caution of a man who had buried nothing yet to the sea and meant to keep it that way as long as he could manage, allowed that the risk was a small one.

She remembered, all the years she lived after, less of the fishing, ordinary enough once the strangeness of open water under her had worn off in the first hour, than of looking back at the harbour from out past the mole's broken and mended length and seeing, for the first time from that angle, how the shape of the old landing had been made, centuries before any hill hand cut a single new stone for it, by people who had understood the set of the tide and the run of the current well enough that the household's own hurried mending had done little more than follow, stone for stone, a plan already laid down and only needing to be honoured rather than improved upon. She said as much to her father that evening, and Iain, who had spent forty years believing a stone left better than touched, only nodded and said that he had thought something of the kind more than once, standing on the mole at low water, and had never found a way of saying it that did not sound, even to his own ear, more like the Seann-Dhaoine's due than a hill man's.

The boats went out through that whole summer and the years after it, and the harbour that had stood empty across every hill man's lifetime before Iain's began, slowly, to keep the ordinary marks of a shore that was lived on: nets drying, a smokehouse raised for the first catch too large to eat fresh, children set to mind the coracles at low tide the way children on the river had once been set to mind cattle at the ford. It was in the six hundred and sixtieth year, with Iain himself eleven years dead and Marsaili holding the household in his place, that the sea took its first life from among them in earnest, a young man of Cailean's own house drowned in a squall that came up faster than any boat then on that shore could outrun, and it fell to Marsaili to decide, with no custom of the river to guide her and none of the Seann-Dhaoine left to ask, how a household newly of the sea ought to bury one the sea had taken.

She chose, in the end, neither the river's cairn, which felt to her a landsman's answer to a thing the sea had done, nor any borrowing from what little could be guessed of the Seann-Dhaoine's own way with their dead, of which nothing certain had ever been carried up from that shore but rumour and the strokes on a midden stone two days' walk to the south; she chose instead to bury him on the higher ground behind the houses, within sound of the water but above its reach, with stones set at his head and feet in the plain hill fashion her own mother's grave on the river had worn, and told the household afterward, when one of the younger ones asked why they had not tried the sea itself, that a household new to a shore had no business inventing rites for it before it had lived there long enough to know what the shore would teach, and that the plain way, at least, wronged no one living or dead.

She held the harbour a further eleven years after that burial, long enough to see four more households come down the river to join the first four, long enough to see her own grandchildren born within sound of the tide who had never seen the river bend their grandfather had left, and long enough to have word reach her, once, of the king himself, riding the coast road in the company of no more than three men and stopping a night, not at her hearth, which never had that honour, but at a household two harbours further south that afterward told and retold the little he had said of Seann Skell: that he had heard the hill was settling an empty shore, and thought it a good thing, and would send no one to tell them how to hold it. It was the whole of what reached her, third-hand and thinner with every telling, and she made no more of it than the household two harbours south had made, save to say, when it was repeated to her at her own fire, that it sounded like a man content to let a shore be filled the way it wanted filling, and that she had never once, in forty-three years upon it, felt the want of any other kind of king.

In her last winter she took to sitting on the shingle of an evening with the mended mole running out dark before her into water gone the colour of the sky, and it was there her eldest daughter asked her once whether she missed the river, meaning the question kindly, as one asks an old woman a question already half answered by the asking of it. Marsaili said that she did not, and that she had come in the end to think of the harbour less as a thing the household had taken up empty than as a thing that had been waiting, stone by patient stone, for hands willing to use it without ever quite believing it was wholly theirs to use. She died in the six hundred and seventy-first year, the first of Iain's line to be buried within sight of the sea rather than beside the river her father had led them away from, and her household laid her, by her own wish given plainly enough beforehand that no one thought to argue it, on the same rise above the shingle where the young drowned man lay, within sound of water that had never, in all her years upon it, told her whether the Seann-Dhaoine had built their harbour for a people not their own to use, or for no reason a hill mind could ever hope to reach.
