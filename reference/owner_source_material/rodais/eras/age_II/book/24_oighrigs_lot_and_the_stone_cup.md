# Of Oighrig's Lot and the Stone Cup

*GE 2,403 – 2,408*

> *Chan e mo làmh-sa a th' ann, ach làmh gach teallaich.*
> It is not my hand; it is the hand of every hearth.
>
> — Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill, at the grinding of the cup, as the hearths of Tobar dhìreach keep it

## I. Of the Turns that Wore Thin

*This is told at Tobar dhìreach by whoever sits the night watch, and has been told so long that the telling has worn as smooth as the slab itself.*

For as long as anyone at Tobar dhìreach could reckon, the slab had been watched by households in their turn, one family a night, drawn from a list that the eldest of the hill kept in her head and nowhere else. It had begun, so the old ones said, in the dark of a Samhain so far back that even the list-keepers before this one could not put a year to it: a scatter of families who lived near the crack in the ground had taken, without any word said over it, to sitting by the stain in the stone a household at a time, watching for they knew not what. No one had ever seen the slab do anything. It lay grey and split-veined in its clearing, cold to the hand in every season, with the one dark stain at its lower edge that no rain had ever washed paler, and generation on generation had sat by it through the short nights and the long ones and gone home in the morning with nothing to tell.

By the year the families met to change the custom, the list had grown past what any one head could hold true. Thirty-one households stood on it, scattered from the near slopes to a full day's walk east, and the eldest who kept it, a woman named Beathag who had held the list eleven years and outlived two husbands doing it, had begun to lose the order of it the way a rope frays first at the place it is handled most. A household from the far end of the valley had come two nights running the summer before, thinking its turn was owed, and found no one else at the slab either night, the family before them having gone home a day early on the strength of a different memory of the order entirely. Nothing had come of the gap. Nothing ever came of any night at the slab, watched or not. But it sat wrong with the hill-folk that a thing so old should be let go untended even by accident, and it was Beathag herself, in the year of her own eldest son's death, who said at the Samhain gathering that she would keep no more lists, and that the hill had better find another way.

They met at the slab itself to talk it over, as such things were talked over on the hill, standing in the clearing in the last mild days before the dark half of the year, thirty-odd households or their heads, with children running at the clearing's edge and old men leaning on staffs and a wind coming down off the high valleys that smelled already of the winter behind it. Some wanted the list kept by two heads instead of one, so that memory might check memory. Some wanted no watch at all, arguing, not for the first time and not to be the last, that a stone which had done nothing in a thousand years of watching was unlikely to begin now for want of a night's neglect. It was an old man near the front, whom the telling does not name, who said at last that if the trouble was that no head could hold thirty-one households fairly in order, then perhaps no head should try, and that the thing should be given to chance instead, the way a household chose which child herded the goats on a wet morning: by lot, one keeper at a time, until that keeper's own life gave the place up.

The hill settled such matters by silence, as it always had. The silence held long enough that the old man took it for agreement, and no one afterward said he had read it wrong. Before the light went that day it was settled between them, without any of them quite deciding when the deciding had happened, that a single keeper would be chosen from among all the households by lot, would keep the slab alone or with whatever kin wished to sit by her, and would go on keeping it until death or infirmity called for another lot to be drawn.

Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill stood near the back of the gathering that day with her shawl pulled over her head against the first spits of rain, three-and-thirty years old and thinking, so far as the telling lets her think anything at all in that hour, of nothing more than whether her own turn at the slab fell that week or the next. Her household was among the thirty-one. She had sat the night watch herself perhaps a dozen times since she was old enough to be trusted not to fall asleep against the stone, and had seen, on every one of those nights, exactly what every watcher before her had seen: a slab, a stain, a silence, and morning.

## II. Of the Lot

The lots were drawn a fortnight later, at the next full dark, because a fortnight seemed to the hill-folk long enough to be fair and not so long that anyone would forget the agreement had been made. A leather bag was found, old and much mended, that had once held a household's spindle-whorls and now held one white pebble marked with a single scratch and thirty small dark stones that were not. Beathag herself, freed at last of the list she had kept eleven years, held the bag closed in both hands until every household had sent forward the one member the lot would fall on, by whatever rule of age or standing each house kept for such things; Oighrig's father, a herder of the near slopes named Dòmhnall who owned no more of the hill than any other man of that name and was kin to none of the old kings, sent his daughter forward because she was neither his eldest nor his youngest and it had fallen to her by a rule of his own that he never explained to anyone, least of all to her.

It rained that night, a fine soft rain that put no one off standing in the clearing, and the slab shone black with it under the last light. Oighrig stood in the ring of thirty-one with her hood down around her shoulders because the hood caught the rain worse than her hair did, and watched each in turn go forward, put a hand into the bag Beathag held, and draw out a stone. She had counted, without meaning to, that she stood eleventh from the front, and had spent most of the wait working out from the rate the others drew and were disappointed that there was no way to know, from a stone already drawn, whether the white one had gone before her turn or not.

When her turn came she put her hand into the bag the way she had put it into every dark bag of grain or wool her whole life, without ceremony, and felt the stones knock cold against her knuckles, more of them than she had expected for how few households remained undrawn, and closed her fingers on the first one they found. She brought it out already knowing, from the shape of it under her thumb before she ever opened her hand, that it was not like the others; the mark ran deep enough to catch a nail.

It is told that she stood a moment with her fist closed and the rain running off her knuckles, and that the two women nearest her, who had already seen the white edge of it between her fingers, said nothing to hurry her, out of a courtesy the telling does not explain and does not need to. When she opened her hand at last, Beathag saw it first of anyone, and let the empty bag fall to her side, and said only, "That's it, then," in a voice with no more weight in it than if the matter had been who carried water that week.

No one cheered, for it was not a thing to cheer, and no one wept, for it was not yet a thing to weep over either. An old woman near her touched Oighrig's shoulder once, the way a hand is laid on a shoulder at a grave rather than a wedding, and stepped back into the ring. Oighrig looked at the pebble a long while in the failing light, turning it so the scratch caught what was left of the day, and then at the slab itself, grey and rain-black in the clearing where it had lain since before her grandmother's grandmother watched it, and put the stone into her belt, where the hill's own telling says she carried it the rest of her life.

She walked home behind her father that night without either of them saying much, and it was her father, not she, who broke the silence somewhere along the track, saying only that he had not looked to send a daughter of his to a stone for the rest of her days when he sent her forward that evening, and had not looked, either, to be asked by every stranger on the salt road for the next forty years whether he was kin to the old king because he shared a name with the man's long-dead father. It was the first either of them had said aloud of a thing that would follow her the whole of her keeping: that hill-folk who did not know the near slopes heard "nic Dhòmhnaill" and thought at once of Dòmhnall Clachach, second of the Stone Kings, who held the north those very years and was, by every account the reciters kept, no kin at all to a herder of Tobar dhìreach's low pastures. Oighrig herself had to say it plainly more times than she troubled to count, to travellers who came up the valley expecting to find the old king's blood keeping the slab and found instead a herder's daughter with wet feet and no patience for the question: her father was Dòmhnall the herder, no more and no less, and the name had never carried further than that.

## III. Of the House with the Door to the Slab

The families raised her a house before the year was out, on the clearing's edge where the ground rose just enough to keep it dry, of the same turf and stone as any hill house save that its door was set to face the slab and no other way, so that whoever slept in it woke each morning looking straight along the floor at the stone she had drawn for. It was, so far as anyone on the hill could say, the first building on all of Dia-thìr raised for no reason but to stand near the vein, and it stood so plainly for that one purpose that even children who had never been told so understood it at a glance, the way a child knows a byre from a house without being told which beasts live in which.

Oighrig moved into it in the first hard frost of that winter, and found the sitting harder alone than it had ever been in turn with her own household beside her. The nights of a shared watch had passed in low talk and the passing back and forth of a skin of buttermilk and the small complaints of people who would be gone home by morning regardless of what the night gave them. A keeper's nights, she found, ran differently. She sat them mostly by herself those first months, for though her own kin and any others were welcome to sit with her and often did in the early evenings, none of them slept there, and by full dark she was alone with the slab and whatever wind came down off the high valleys, and no list to check the next night against, only the one long unbroken watch that had no end she could see from where she sat.

She was not unhappy in it, the telling is careful to say, though it does not say she was happy either. She had time, that first winter, to notice things about the slab that thirty-one households taking turns for a thousand years had somehow not troubled to set down in any telling she had ever heard: that the stain at its lower edge was darkest in wet weather and paled, though it never wholly vanished, in a dry summer; that the stone held the day's warmth an hour or two longer than the ground around it in autumn and lost it faster in spring, as though its temper ran contrary to the world's; that a hare had made its form under the slab's overhanging lip and raised two litters there in the years Oighrig watched, undisturbed by whatever the stone was or was not. None of this, she told the few who asked her directly, amounted to an answer to anything. She had sat by the slab longer now than any single household of the old rota ever had at a stretch, and had seen, she said plainly to a traveller who pressed her on it one spring evening, no more of what the stone was for than the traveller himself had seen, standing in the clearing a single afternoon.

What she had begun to see, by the second year of her keeping, was a different trouble altogether, and one nearer her own hand to mend. The handful of coal that each household of the near valleys burned in its own custom, the small ration of the grey vein's stuff that a family measured for itself against the cold, was measured by nothing more exact than the cup of whatever hand did the measuring that year, a mother's palm or a grandfather's or a growing son's, so that one household's handful ran near double another's without either house meaning to cheat its neighbour of anything. It had never much mattered while the custom was young and the vein plentiful and no keeper stood over the counting of it. It mattered more, Oighrig came slowly to think, sitting alone by the stone through the second dark half of her keeping, now that the slab had a single keeper whose word the hill was beginning, without quite meaning to, to ask after on other matters too.

## IV. Of the Cup Ground to Her Hand

She went to Niocol Snaidhear in the spring of her third year, a man of the near slopes some ten years older than herself who had worked stone since he was a boy old enough to hold a hammer without dropping it on his own foot, grinding querns and hearthstones and, once, a trough for a household too grand for a wooden one. He kept a low shed at the edge of his own ground with a bench worn white at one end from decades of the same motion, and Oighrig found him there of a wet afternoon with a half-finished quern between his knees and stone dust in the creases of his hands that no washing ever wholly got out.

She did not, by her own account afterward, come to him with much of a notion of what she wanted, only a trouble she had turned over enough nights by the slab to have worn its shape smooth. She set her own right hand on the bench before him, cupped as if to catch water poured from a height, and told him she wanted a cup ground of grey stone that would hold exactly what that hand held and no grain more, so that whatever household measured its handful against it would measure the same as every other household on the hill, and none would ever again burn double its neighbour's share by the accident of whose palm did the measuring.

Niocol looked at the hand a long while before he answered, turning it a little under the shed's poor light, and said at last that a hand was not a thing to copy in stone the way a quern's face was copied from another quern; a hand curled differently full than empty, and differently cold than warm, and he would need, he thought, more sittings of her than she would likely have patience for before he was done. She told him she had nothing but patience, now, and came back to the shed eleven times that summer and into the following one, each time setting her hand to the bench in the same attitude, cupped, fingers a little curled, while he worked a lump of the grey stone down with chisel and rubbing-stone and, in the last weeks, nothing but a wetted thumb and infinite slow patience of his own.

Two cups cracked in the grinding before the third one held. The first split along a vein no eye had seen in the raw stone, in the last week of shaping, and Niocol swept the pieces into a corner of the shed without a word Oighrig heard him say about it, and began again from a fresh block the following morning as though the loss cost him nothing, though she noted, and said later that she had noted, that his hands shook a little at the first cuts of the new stone in a way they had not shaken at the last cuts of the old one. The second held longer, near to finished, before a blow struck a shade too hard opened a hair-crack across its base that would have let the coal run out one grain at a time, worse than no measure at all; that one he did not sweep away but kept on the bench through the whole of the third cup's grinding, turning to it now and then as a man turns to a mistake he means to learn the whole shape of before he lets it go.

The third cup he fitted to her hand again and again through that second summer, grinding a hair's width here, a hair's width there, until it sat in the hollow of her palm the way, so the telling says, an egg sits in a nest that has been shaped around it and no other. It was smaller than either of the two that had cracked, and darker in the stone, veined faintly white through its base, and cold in the hand even on a warm day the way all the vein's stone ran cold, and it held, when she filled it once with water to be sure, exactly what her own two hands together could not quite hold cupped, and no more.

It is told, though the telling itself allows that no one but Oighrig could have known whether she truly said it or the words only settled onto her afterward the way words settle onto anyone whose life the hill comes to measure by, that she stood in the shed with the finished cup in her hand and said to Niocol, who had by then spent the better part of two years of his working life on the shape of her one hand, that it was not her own hand the stone held after all, but the hand of every hearth on the near slopes, hers no more than any woman's who would ever fill it. Niocol, whom the telling gives no word of his own in reply, is said only to have gone back to his bench and begun, without being asked, on a second cup, meaning it from the first stroke to be a copy and not an original, for he had already understood, before she ever said as much to him plainly, that one keeper's cup in one keeper's house would settle nothing for households that could not walk to Tobar dhìreach and back inside a winter's day.

## V. Of the Copies

Word of the cup went out along the near valleys faster than any single teller could account for having carried it, the way word does travel when it answers a trouble every hearth already knew it had without having words for it. Within the year, stone-workers at three other places had heard enough of the shape and the reason to ask for cups of their own ground against it: a woman at Àth gharbh, where the ford crossed the salt road and half the near valley's flint passed through on its way south; an old man at Achadh dhìreach, whose own hearthstone still bore, though few remembered why, a long-healed crack where a fire had once run colour under the king's own hand; and a household at Cnoc chaol, a full day's walk east of the slab, on ground poor enough that its people had less coal to measure than most and cared more than most, for that reason, that the measuring be exact.

Oighrig walked to each in turn over the space of a year, carrying the first cup wrapped in wool in a satchel she would let no one else carry for her, not out of any grandness about it but because she had grown, in the two years of its making, into the habit of having it always at her own hand and found she trusted no other hand to keep it safe on the road. At Àth gharbh the stone-worker ground her copy against Oighrig's own in three sittings, quicker than Niocol had managed the first, for she had his cup before her the whole time to measure against rather than a hand alone; at Achadh dhìreach the old man took longer, being slower now in his hands than he once was, but got there in the end with his grandson holding the light for him through the last evenings; at Cnoc chaol the household set two of its own young people to the grinding turn and turn about, and finished theirs inside a single season, being, as Oighrig said afterward with something that in a less careful woman might have been taken for pride, the quickest hands of the four though the last to begin.

Each copy she filled with water against the first before she would call it done, pouring from one to the other on a flat stone in the open air where any of the household could watch and see for themselves that the levels matched to a hair, and where they did not match she said so plainly and sent the worker back to the wheel, twice at Achadh dhìreach and once, to the grandson's very open disappointment, at Cnoc chaol. No cup left her hand called finished until it held water level with the first, and none of the three stone-workers, whatever else the telling says of them, is recorded arguing the point with her once she had shown them the difference with their own water and their own eyes.

## VI. Of the Meeting of the Cups

The four cups were brought together at last in the last mild days before the dark half of the year, on a stretch of open moor between Àth gharbh and Tobar dhìreach where a spring rose clear and cold out of a bed of grey stone and ran a little way before losing itself again in the heather. It had no name before that day that any telling keeps. Oighrig had chosen it, so far as she chose anything about the meeting rather than let the four keepers of the cups choose it between them, because it lay roughly equal walking from all four places and because the spring gave water enough and to spare for whatever filling and comparing the day required, and because, she said to more than one who asked her why there and not somewhere nearer the slab, a measure that was to belong to no one house had no business being fixed on any one house's ground.

They came in the cold clear morning of a day the hill-folk would go on keeping, in one way or another, for as long as the custom of the coal lasted: the woman from Àth gharbh with her cup in a bag of oiled hide against the damp; the old man from Achadh dhìreach carried in a cart the last mile by his grandson, his own legs no longer equal to the walk though his hands had lost none of their sureness; the two young people from Cnoc chaol, who had walked through the night to be there by first light and showed it in the set of their shoulders; and Oighrig herself, with the first cup, the one that had cost Niocol two broken stones and the better part of two years, wrapped as always in its wool.

They set all four on a flat stone at the spring's edge, side by side in the low light, and filled each in turn from the same water, and passed each cup along the line of four for every hand there to weigh in turn, a courtesy none of them had been asked to keep but that all of them kept without discussion, as though the measure itself required to be handled by every hand it would ever serve before it could be trusted to serve any one of them alone. The water stood level in all four to the eye of everyone who looked, and to the more careful eye of Niocol, who had walked up from his own ground to see the meeting though none of the day's work was left for him to do, and who said only, when asked, that he could find no fault in any of the four and would say so to anyone who doubted him.

It is that place the hill has called, from that morning on, the meeting of the cups, though nothing marks it beyond the flat stone and the spring that still rises there for whoever comes to fill a fifth cup against the first four, should one ever need making, or a broken one ever need replacing against the whole line of them. The families of the near valleys and the far, from the slopes above Tobar dhìreach to the poor ground at Cnoc chaol, took the four cups home with them that evening each to its own place, and from that year the hill-folk measured the coal of their handfuls by one measure and not by the accident of whichever hand did the measuring, a thing so small in the telling of it and so large in the daily life of every hearth that touched it that the hill's own reciters, when they came at last to set the year down, gave it no more than a line: that in the years of Oighrig's keeping, the hills came to measure with one measure, and had not measured otherwise since.
