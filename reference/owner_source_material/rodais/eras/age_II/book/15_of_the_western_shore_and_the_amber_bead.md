# Of the Western Shore and the Amber Bead

*GE 1,274 – 1,440*

*Part Three: The Far Shores and the Name*

*At the western Seann Skell the amber-drillers still say a woman carried the finest stone any of them ever found the whole length of the island on foot, and gave it away before she had gone a mile back the way she had come.*

## I. Of the Bay of Hawks

In the twelve hundred and seventy-fourth year of the hill, three households that had grown too many mouths for the grazing above Baile ghorm took their cattle and their looms and their children and went west, over the spine of the island and down through country no hill family had settled before, until the ground fell away under them and they stood looking at open water for the first time in their lives. They built where a stream came down to a curve of grey sand under a headland thick with gorse, in the round manner of the hill, turf-roofed and set back a little from the tide, and they fished from the shore with lines and baskets and did not put out boats, for none of them had ever built one and none of them trusted the water enough to learn. The bay took its name, in the way places took names on that coast, from what a man first saw standing on the headland looking down at it: a pair of hawks working the updraught off the cliff face, hanging so long on one spot of air that the man who told of it afterward swore they had not moved a feather the whole time he watched.

It was the first hill household on that shore, though not, the old wrack-gatherers of the strand would tell you, the first household of any kind, for the low grassed-over shapes further round the headland had held roofs once, and the middens under them, when a spade turned one up, gave back shell and bone gone the colour of the ground itself. The three households let those middens lie. They had come down out of hill country too poor to bring more than they carried on their backs, and a heap of old bones was no poorer a neighbour than the wind. No one among them could say who had raised those roofs or why the roofs had come down, and no one asked twice, for the second asking got no more answer than the first and only put a chill on the fire it was asked beside.

The crossing itself was told for two generations after as its own small history, though it took the three households no more than nine days on foot with their beasts loaded and their children carried the last of it. They had gone by the flint road as far as it ran west, then off it along a drover's track that ran out into heather with no path left in it at all, over a spine of hill where the wind never once let up, day or night, and down at last through a birch wood into ground softer underfoot than any of them had walked since leaving the valley they were born in. The eldest of the three households, a woman near her ninetieth year named Beathag, walked the whole of it on her own two feet and would take no one's arm, and said, when they came out at last above the bay and saw the water, that she had not believed until that moment there was so much of it in the world, more than any river or loch she had ever heard sung of. She lived eleven years more on that shore and was the first hill-folk buried within sound of that particular tide.

Flòraidh was born into the second of those three households, eleven years after the crossing, in a winter when the sea froze white with salt-rime along the tideline and her mother said afterward that she had come squalling into a world already half turned to stone. She was a middle child among five, neither the eldest who would hold the house nor the youngest who would be petted for it, and she grew up the way middle children on a poor shore grow up, finding for herself the one thing in the house nobody else wanted and making it hers.

## II. Of the Amber and the Wrack

The thing nobody else wanted was the wrack line after a westerly gale, when every other child on the bay went straight for driftwood and the gulls' eggs in the gorse and left the weed itself, black and stinking and thigh-deep in places, for whoever cared to turn it over. Flòraidh turned it over from the time she could walk without falling, at first for nothing more than the shells and the drowned crabs a smaller child could carry home for the pot, and then, the summer she was nine, for a lump of something the colour of cold honey that her fingers found half buried in a raft of bladderwrack and would not let go of even when the tide came back for it.

Her mother knew the stuff at once, though she had never in her own life found a piece worth keeping. The old people of the bay called it sun-stone or sea-stone, and said, without much conviction, that it wept out of trees that had stood somewhere in the world before the world was as it now was; it warmed in the hand faster than any true stone should, and when rubbed hard against wool it would lift a hair or a scrap of down off the ground and hold it there, dancing, for longer than any child who tried it could sit still and watch. It washed up seldom and only after the worst weather, thrown loose from some bed the sea kept far out and gave up nothing about, and a lump the size of a thumb-joint was talked of on the bay for a season.

Flòraidh's find that ninth summer was the size of a hen's egg, the largest anyone on that shore had seen brought in whole, and her mother would not let her keep it raw. There was an old man three houses along, blind in one eye from a spark struck off a flint, who had learned from a trader out of the eastern hills how a hole could be worked through a piece of sea-stone with a flint point and wetted sand and more patience than most men owned, so that a cord could be run through it and the thing worn rather than merely kept in a pouch to be taken out and admired. He worked at Flòraidh's egg-sized lump through the better part of a winter, a little each evening by the fire, turning the drill between his palms while she sat and watched and learned, without being taught in any way she could have named afterward, the weight of hand that kept a flint point true and the weight that snapped a good stone in two for nothing. By the time the hole ran clean through, front to back, no wider than a strand of wool, she was old enough to hold the drill herself on the next piece, and old enough, too, to understand that she had been given a craft rather than a favour.

She grew up the length of that shore known for it: the girl who did not mind the stink of the wrack, who could tell by the smell of the weed alone, before her hand had touched a thing in it, whether a gale had brought anything worth the walk. Traders who came down the flint road with baskets of pots and strings of shell for the coast learned to ask for her by name rather than knock at any door at random, and went away with beads worked to a finish no other hand on that bay gave them, paying in salt or wool or, once, in a knife of southern flint that Flòraidh's father wore at his belt the rest of his life and was buried with.

Her elder sister, who would hold the house after their mother, thought the whole business beneath a grown woman's time, and said so at every hearth-telling until the saying wore smooth from repeating; her two younger brothers thought it a fine excuse to spend a morning at the tideline instead of at the byre, and were sent home more than once with nothing to show for it but wet feet and their sister's amusement, for the wrack did not give itself up to a boy hunting driftwood with half his mind on his supper. Only her father never asked why she troubled with it, holding, as he held of most things, that a craft which fed itself and asked nothing of the byre was a craft worth leaving be. He was a quiet man who had come west already grown, in the first crossing, and remembered the hill valleys of his own boyhood well enough to tell of them but never troubled to wish himself back among them; it was he who first walked the strand with her after the worst gales, saying little, carrying whatever she found until her own arms could not hold more, and it was from him, more than from the blind drill-master, that she learned the patience the work wanted.

There came a summer, the year she turned sixteen, when no gale worth the name touched that coast from Bealltainn to Samhain, and the wrack lay thin and ordinary the whole season through. Flòraidh took to walking further than she had ever walked before, past the headland and along strands no household of the bay had troubled to search, on the reasoning, which she could not have argued for aloud, that a sea too idle to throw anything ashore near home might yet have thrown something further off where no one thought to look. She found little enough for her trouble that year, a few small pieces no better than what any child brought in, but she came to know the whole coast within two days' walk of the bay by its own name, before and after that, by the shape of its rocks and the run of its currents, in a way that would matter to her later more than she then knew.

## III. Of the Bead and the Road

The finest piece she ever drew from the wrack came to her in the thirty-first year of the hill households on that shore, out of a storm that took the roof off two houses and left the whole strand for half a mile heaped with weed a man could have got lost in. It was larger than the egg of her ninth summer by half again, the colour of a full winter sun through cloud, with a cloudiness deep inside it like smoke caught the instant before it thins, and no crack anywhere in it that her eye, or the old drill-master's, now three years dead, could have found. She worked the hole through it herself over four evenings, slower than she had ever worked a stone before, and when it was done she strung it on nothing, for a while, only kept it wrapped in wool in the box where she kept her mother's few beads of shell, and did not know, or would not say, what she meant to do with it.

Her mother knew. There had been talk on the bay for some years, carried up the flint road and back down again by traders, of a meeting each summer at a ford far to the east, between the hill and the holders of a red rise of ground called only the Red Hill, where salt from the southern pans was traded for wool and for everything else besides, and where, it was said, the king himself came and walked among the stalls, though what he did there nobody on that shore had ever heard told plainly, only that no one who had stood near him at it came away with quite the same telling as the man beside him.

"Carry it there," her mother said, turning the bead once in the firelight before she gave it back. "A stone that size, off this shore, should be seen where the whole of the hill can see it, and not sit in a box behind my loom until one of your own children breaks it playing."

The trading party that would carry her east was five years in the gathering, for no household bound so far went empty-handed and the bay's own wool and worked stone took that long to grow to a load worth the road; Flòraidh kept the bead wrapped through every one of those years rather than send it ahead with a smaller party she trusted less. She was five-and-twenty by the time the party was ready, and had never in all that waiting gone further from the bay than the flint road's first bend. She went with a trader's party that left the western Seann Skell at the turn of summer, six households' worth of wool and hides and worked stone loaded on eleven pack-beasts, and walked the length of the island in the company of people she had known a month, sleeping under whatever roof would have a trader's party or, twice, under no roof at all with the stars thick enough overhead that she lay awake past exhaustion only to look at them. The amber travelled wrapped in the same wool it had always known, in a pouch she kept against her body under her clothes rather than trust to any pack, and touched it through the cloth most nights before she slept, the way another woman might have touched a charm.

The road took them up through hill country greener and better watered than her own coast, past herds larger than the whole bay could have grazed in a lifetime and byres built four-square in a manner her father's people had never troubled to raise on the poor ground of the west. At a crossing where a swollen burn had to be forded waist-deep, the party lost a full pack of hides to the current before a young man travelling with them, a boat-mender's son bound the same way to sell his father's work, went in after it and came out grinning and blue with cold, holding the pack over his head and the hides inside it dry. Flòraidh thought little enough of him at the time beyond the ordinary gratitude of a woman whose own pack had ridden the same beast, though she would remember, years after, that he had laughed at the cold rather than cursed it, and that this had seemed to her, without her quite deciding why, worth remembering.

Twice the party fell in with other travellers bound the same way, once a family of Red Hill kin driving cattle down for the same gathering, and once a lone drover who said nothing of himself at all and left before dawn each morning ahead of everyone else, so that Flòraidh never learned so much as which coast he called home. By the third week the country had opened out flatter and busier than anything her eye had known, smoke rising from more hearths in a single valley than her whole bay held households, and on the last evening before the ford itself, from a rise in the road, she saw the fires of the gathering laid out below her like a second sky come down to rest on the ground, more lights together than she had ever in her life seen in one place.

## IV. Of the Ford and the Man Who Bought Nothing

She came down to the ford on the fourth day of the gathering, footsore and burned dark by three weeks of open road, into a noise and a press of people greater than anything the bay had ever shown her: pens of sheep bleating against the lowing of penned cattle, the knock of a flint-knapper's hammerstone somewhere behind a line of tents, salt stacked in baskets taller than a child, and everywhere the particular high chatter of people haggling in a dozen accents of the same tongue. She had thought, walking the last mile of the road, that she would know the king the moment she saw him, by some mark or blaze she could not have named; instead she stood a full morning at the edge of the trading ground before anyone told her that the tall man in plain wool, moving slowly along the line of stalls with his hands loose at his sides, was the one she had come to see.

He bought nothing. She watched him the better part of an hour, at first from a distance and then, drawn closer than she had meant to go, from near enough to have touched his sleeve. At a stall of southern pots he turned one over in his hands, weighed it, set it down exactly where it had stood. At a rack of worked flint he stopped longer, running a thumb along an edge without testing its keenness the way any other man on the ground would have. He asked the price of nothing, offered nothing, and yet the stallholders straightened when he came near and settled again, slower, when he had gone on, as though the ground had held its breath under him and only now let it go. His face gave his years away no more than a hearthstone gives the years of the fires it has carried, smooth and unlined as a man of five and twenty summers, though he had the stillness of someone who had stood in that exact spot, or one very like it, more times than he cared to count, and found each time something worth the standing. No torque, no dyed cloth marked him, nothing a poor household could not have put on its own back for a feast day, and yet the crowd gave way from him a full arm's length on either side without any of them seeming to notice they had moved, the way water gives way round a rock in a burn without the burn troubling to ask why.

A stallholder near her, an old salt-woman down from the pans with a face gone the colour and grain of driftwood, caught Flòraidh staring and leaned to say, low, that the king had walked that same ground every gathering since the ford was first agreed, and had never once, in all the tellings she knew, been seen to buy so much as a bannock off any stall on it. "Some say he has no need of what we sell," the woman said, "and some say he buys everything already, in some coin none of us would know how to weigh, and needs nothing more from us than the standing and the looking. I have sold salt on this ground thirty summers and I could not tell you which of the two is truer, and I have stopped asking."

Flòraidh had meant to lay the bead quietly in the old ground at the meeting-place, among the other small offerings that travellers left there without much ceremony, a scrap of ribbon or a worked bone tucked under a stone, and go home again with a tale to tell rather than a gift given by hand. Her nerve failed her for that and gave her something else instead. When the king passed within an arm's length of her, she found the pouch already open at her breast and the bead already out and held up on her flat palm before she had decided to do either.

He stopped. He looked at the bead a long moment, and then at her, in a way that made her feel, for the length of that look, entirely known, though she had never in her life stood before him. His hands did not come up to take it.

"Where does the sun-stone wash," he asked her, "on your shore?"

"After a westerly," she said, her voice steadier than she had thought it would be. "Only after a westerly, and only the worst of them."

He nodded, as though she had confirmed something rather than told him anything new, and looked once more at the bead without touching it. "Keep it," he said. "Lay it in the ground here yourself, with your own hand, where you meant to. A thing carried the length of the island should be laid by the one who carried it, not passed off to another hand at the last step of the road."

She did as she was told, kneeling at the old ground among the ribbons and the worked bones while he stood by and watched, and when she rose he was already three strides off among the stalls again, buying nothing, looking at everything, and no one near enough to her afterward could tell her with any certainty whether he had smiled before he turned.

She stayed three days more at the ford after that, sleeping in the trader's tent among the wool bales and walking the trading ground each morning half hoping and half dreading to see him again, though she never did; he was, the salt-woman told her, as likely to be seen at the far end of the ground haggling in spirit if not in coin over a beast he had no wish to own as he was to vanish entirely for the length of a day and be found again at dusk sitting alone by the river with his boots off, and no one on the ground troubled him either way. On the second of those three days the boat-mender's son sought her out where she sat mending a strap, and asked, plainly enough that she laughed before she answered, whether a woman who had spoken with the king himself had any words left over for an ordinary man; she found, turning the question over afterward on the long road home, that she had rather more than she had expected.

## V. Of the Years the Bay Was Told Of

She walked home by the same road she had come, in a trader's party bound the other way, and told the tale of the ford so many times over the following winter that her younger brother swore he could have told it in her voice without her in the room, down to the very words of the salt-woman she had spoken to only the once. The boat-mender's son followed her west the next summer with two of his father's finished boats loaded on a cart drawn by a beast he had traded three years' labour for, and asked her father for her outright, standing in the yard with his boots still crusted from the road, in a manner the bay found so forward that it was talked of nearly as much as the bead had been. She married him that autumn.

They raised three children on that shore over the years that followed, in a house built nearer the water than her mother's had been, for her husband held, as boat-menders will, that a house too far back from the tide was a house always walking to its work. None of the three ever brought home a stone from the wrack worth a telling of its own, though all three learned the drill at her knee exactly as she had learned it at the blind man's, turning the bow between their small palms under her eye until she judged the weight of their hand true; and her eldest daughter, in her own old age long after this telling closes, would teach the same craft in turn to a granddaughter of Flòraidh's who never once heard her grandmother's own voice. Flòraidh herself gathered the wrack every westerly gale of her life until her knees would no longer take the wet stones, and after that walked the strand anyway, some evenings, with a stick, looking rather than gathering, for she said the looking had always been half the pleasure and she saw no cause to give it up before the walking failed her too.

She died in her sixty-eighth year, in a mild autumn with the wrack lying quiet and unbroken along the whole strand, her husband four years dead before her and her children grown with households of their own up and down that coast. She was buried, at her own asking, facing the water rather than the headland, so that whatever came in on the tide would come in past her; and for a full year after, it is told, no household on that bay let a westerly gale pass without someone walking down at first light to see what it had brought, half in the old habit of the work and half, though none of them put it in so many words, in case it had brought something meant for her.

It is told on that shore, too, of other things of those years, carried back to it the way all news of the wider island came, by traders and by the boat-menders who took after her husband's trade and travelled further with it than he ever had.

The households at the harbour of the round houses, near enough the Red Hill to pay it salt, had begun in Flòraidh's own middle years to pay it and to be counted among the Red Hill's households, as every household within a hard day's walk of a hill holding had by then come to do without any word from Dùn ìseal telling them to. No one could afterward point to the year it started or the household that started it; it was said only that one autumn the custom was new and the next it was old, the way a path across a field is new one season and worn the next without anyone marking the day the first foot crossed it.

Cattle were walked up the flint road itself, the length of it, into the north-west, to a green rise called Cnoc ghorm where until then the people had lived off seal and fish and deer and had never kept a beast on a tether in their lives. It took three summers of driving small herds up by stages, resting them at every burn the road crossed, before a herd stood there large enough to be called a herd rather than a curiosity; and the first calves born on that rise were watched over by the whole settlement in turn through their first winter, the way a stranger's child new to a household is watched, given the warmest corner of every byre and the first cut of every store, until they had proved themselves, calving in their turn and standing the cold as well as any beast bred to it, no stranger thing after all.

And in the north-west, on a rise above a firth wide enough to lose a fleet in, a long-house was raised the length of forty paces, byre and hall under the one roof, greater by half again than any roof the age had yet put up, timbered with wood carried down from the flint road's own hills and roofed with turf cut a whole season in advance and dried under cover so it would not sag with its own wet weight. Word of its raising travelled the flint road and down the coast within the year, and for a season it was spoken of on Flòraidh's own shore as the boat-menders spoke of a hull built beyond the ordinary run of hulls, admired by people who would never see it and never quite believed by all of them either.

It stood four winters before a spark from an untended hearth-fire took the thatch of the byre end in the small hours of a still night, and though every soul under that roof got out alive, the hall end burned with it before the byre's own beasts, driven out bawling into the dark, could be turned back to help beat down the flame. The household that had raised it raised it again on the same footing before the following winter closed in, for the ground, they said, had proved itself good ground, whatever the fire had proved about the roof; and it stood after that the way most things on that coast came, in the end, to stand: mended, and carrying its scorch mark where anyone who cared to look might see it, waiting, though none of that household would have said so aloud, for whatever the next spark might prove of it too.
