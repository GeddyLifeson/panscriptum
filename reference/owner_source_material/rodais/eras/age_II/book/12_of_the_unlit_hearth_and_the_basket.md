# Of the Unlit Hearth and the Basket

*GE 902 – 1,070*

*At Achadh dhìreach the hearths still measure an gual dathach by the hand Anna nic Coinnich taught them, though most who measure it now could not say whose hand it was.*

## I. Of the Hearth Never Lit

Before there was a household at Achadh dhìreach that anyone now living could name, there was a hearth, and the hearth was never lit.

It stood a little back from where the stream bent under the first rise of ground, laid with the same care any hearth was laid in those years, stones set close and true and a clay lip built up round the fire-bed to hold the ash from spreading. Dry wood lay ready beside it, split fine for a first catching flame, and beside the wood, wrapped in a scrap of hide gone stiff with age, a single sliver of an gual dathach, no bigger than a child's thumb. Whoever laid that hearth had meant to light it. The wood was cut for lighting and the coal was set out for lighting and neither had been touched by fire in all the years anyone afterward dug down to look at them, so that the hearth-stones, when at last a spade turned up their edge in the nine hundred and second year of the king, came up out of the ground as clean of soot as the day they were laid.

No one at Achadh dhìreach in that year could say who had laid it, or why they had gone away before the striking, or where they had gone. The valley folk who found it were three brothers new to the ground, clearing a strip for barley their grandfather had marked out and never lived to break, and they stood over the buried hearth a long while before any of them would put a hand to the coal, for a coal so old and so untouched seemed to them a thing that had waited on purpose, and a man does not care to be the one who wakes what has chosen, for whatever reason of its own, to go on sleeping.

They left it dark. It is told that the eldest of the three, a man named Griogal, said only that the hearth had been left as it was for a reason a hearth-builder would have known and he did not, and that he would sooner leave a mystery standing than put a flame to one, and his brothers, who had less patience for mysteries generally, agreed with him in this one thing because neither of them wanted, any more than he did, to be the hand that lit a fire someone else had refused. They laid a flat stone over the coal and the wood, and built their own hearth a stone's throw off, in ground that had never held one before, and told their children, as their children told theirs, to leave the old stones be.

By the nine hundred and fifty-sixth year the buried hearth lay three yards down, covered by every season's turf and every year's midden and the slow gathering of a valley floor that never stopped rising under the feet of the people who lived on it, and only the oldest households still troubled to point out, to a child digging where a child ought not to dig, the exact spot where the ground held its secret. It became, in the way of such things, less a fact remembered than a habit kept: no one broke new ground there, though no one any longer troubled to ask why not, and the valley folk who farmed the good soil all round it did so without once feeling the want of the strip they left alone.

## II. Of the Measure Nowhere Written

The custom that Achadh dhìreach came at last to keep with an gual dathach did not begin at Achadh dhìreach, and no one there, when it reached them, could have said where it had begun, only that it had reached every hearth in Na Glinn Àrda within the space of a very few years, the way a plain sense sometimes travels faster than any single teller can be found to have carried it.

It is told that at a drovers' ford some days off, where beasts were rested on the road to the hill and travellers slept a night or two among strangers before going on, a household new to the ground had burnt more of the coal in one sitting than its neighbours thought wise, warming a sick child through a hard night, and had been watched doing it by three others who came the following winter to see for themselves what a fire fed too generously with an gual dathach would do. Nothing came of the watching that year, nor the year after; but the households of that ford, and then the households near them, and then households further off than any of them had ever traded with directly, took to measuring what they put on a fire by the width of a hand cupped and no wider, a full hand and no more, and kept to it without any one of them being able to say who had first said it aloud or when.

By the thousand and eleventh year, or near enough that no two households at Achadh dhìreach agreed on the count, a full hand had become the whole of the law that any of them knew regarding the coal, and it was law in the plain sense that a stranger travelling from the ford country to Na Glinn Àrda and back again would have found the same measure kept at every hearth along the road, though no king had proclaimed it and no holder of any hill had ever ridden out to enforce it. It sat alongside the old buried hearth in the valley's own reckoning of itself, two things neither explained nor questioned: a hearth once laid and never lit, and a measure everywhere kept and nowhere written, and it was the particular cast of that valley's mind, by the time Anna nic Coinnich was old enough to carry water to her mother's fire, to hold the two as somehow of a piece, as if the unlit hearth had been the first hand's warning and the measured hand the whole valley's late answer to it, though no one then living, and likely no one who had ever lived at Achadh dhìreach, could have said whether the two things touched each other at all outside a child's tidy way of making sense of what she was given.

Anna was born in the thousandth year of the king into a household two fields off from the buried hearth, the youngest of four, and grew up knowing the width of her own cupped hand the way she knew the sound of the stream under the first rise: a fact of the valley so plain it needed no more remarking than the shape of the hills. Her mother measured coal onto the fire from a clay jar kept high on a shelf, out of the smaller children's reach, and said the same words each time without seeming to notice she said them, *làn na glaice, 's gun tuilleadh*, a full hand and no more, until Anna herself said them without noticing, first to her own younger cousins and later, when she had a household of her own, to children who were hers.

## III. Of Achadh dhìreach

She married at nineteen a man named Aonghas, a byreward from the next valley over, and came to keep her own hearth within sight of the old buried one, close enough that on quiet evenings she could stand at her own door and see the flat stone the valley still pointed out to children, greyed now with a lifetime's weather and half sunk into ground that had risen round it a further hand's depth in her own lifetime alone. She raised four children of her own there and buried one, a boy of six years taken by a fever the winter she was thirty, and came in time to be one of the older women of that stretch of the valley, the kind whose hearth other households' children were sent to when their own mothers had no time to mind them, and whose word on the raising of children, and increasingly on other matters besides, carried further than her own household's fence.

The years between were ordinary years, and the valley's own telling of them kept little of their particulars beyond a bare handful: a dry summer that thinned the barley but did not fail it outright; a boundary quarrel between two households over a strip of grazing that Anna's own father, before his death, had helped settle by walking the ground with both parties and marking new stones where the old ones had shifted; a stranger through from the coast one autumn with salt to trade and news of a boat hollowed whole from a single oak, which the valley folk heard with the mild interest they gave most things that happened somewhere else. Achadh dhìreach in those years held some dozen households strung along the stream, no larger a place than it had ever been and in no hurry, so far as anyone there could tell, to become larger, its people keeping cattle and a little grain and the long patience of a valley that had never once, in any teller's memory, been visited by anything worse than weather.

Two households nearer the stream's bend than Anna's own belonged to a man named Eanraig Ruadh and his wife Beathag, who had come to that stretch of ground a generation before Anna's own birth and were, by the time this telling reaches them, well into the years a valley calls old without troubling to count them exactly. Eanraig had buried two wives before Beathag and outlived three of his own children, and had the name, among the younger households, of a man who kept his own counsel and his own hearth hotter than most, being of the kind, not uncommon at any age, who felt the cold in his bones before his neighbours felt it in theirs and would sooner burn a little more than shiver through a long evening. Careless he was not thought. His beasts were well kept, his roof well thatched, and his grandchildren, of whom there were by then a good many scattered among the valley's other households, were spoken of kindly wherever they went, a man careful, by every neighbour's account, in every particular save one. It was only the fire he kept generous, and it was a generosity the valley had grown used to seeing without remarking on it further than to say, now and again, that Eanraig liked his hearth warm.

## IV. Of the Basket

The summer of the year one thousand and sixty-six never properly came, the valley said afterward: a cold wet season with a north wind in it that killed two of Eanraig's own lambs before their first shearing and set every household along the stream burning peat in the middle of an t-Iuchar as though it were the wolf-month. Eanraig Ruadh, an old man past any count he still troubled to keep, felt that cold in joints that had troubled him little in warmer winters, and said to Beathag, more than once in the hearing of a grandchild who later told it, that a man his age had earned the right to sit warm through whatever was left to him, and that he did not propose to spend his last winters shivering for the sake of a measure some ford household three valleys off had thought up before either of them were born.

It is not told, and Anna herself, questioned on it by her own grandchildren once she had grown old, would never say she knew, whether Eanraig meant from the first to break the measure outright or only to stretch it a little further each night than the night before, the way a habit is broken most often, by inches too small for the breaker to notice breaking anything at all. What is told is that on a night early in an t-Iuchar, with the cold still hard on the valley and a wind come round to the north that found every gap in a poorly chinked wall, Eanraig set on his fire, in one going, very nearly the whole of a basket his eldest living son had carried up from the trading he alone regularly made along the ford road, a basket meant, by that son's own account given afterward, to see the household through the better part of the coming year measured out by handfuls and not spent in a single night by a man who had stopped, in his old age, quite believing that the measure applied to him.

Anna was wakened past midnight by a light in the sky the wrong colour for any fire she had ever seen a house burn, a light that stood up over the roofs of the nearer houses in a colour she groped afterward, telling it to her own children, for words to fit and never quite found, saying only that it ran shot through with something beneath the ordinary red-gold of a burning roof, a colour that seemed, she said, to come from further down in the fire than fire usually burned from. She was out of her own house and across two fields before Aonghas had his boots properly laced, and she was not the first there, for households nearer Eanraig's had wakened before her at the sound alone, a sound she never afterward tried to describe with any confidence, only that it was louder, and went on longer, than a roof-fire's ordinary crack and settle.

The house was past saving by the time enough hands had gathered to try. It had gone up whole, roof and walls together, in the time it takes a household to wake and dress and cross two fields, and what the valley folk stood back from, in the end, was a column of flame past any fighting with water carried in pails, the colour of nothing any of them had a word ready for, standing straight up in a windless patch of air though the wind blew hard everywhere else that night, and giving off a heat that drove them back a good ten paces further than an ordinary house-fire would have made them stand. Eanraig Ruadh did not come out of it, nor Beathag, nor a grandchild of six years named Ualan who had been left to sleep the night under their roof while his own mother tended a sick sister two houses off, and the valley folk who stood in the cold that night, watching a fire none of them had ever seen the like of and none of them could get near enough to fight, told each other afterward, in whatever words came to each of them, that they had known, standing there, that they were watching something answer a measure that had been broken, though not one of them, questioned closely by whoever came after to ask it of them, could say what they meant by the word *answer* beyond the plain fact of the fire's colour and its heat and the three lives it took with it.

The house burned to its stones before the light came up grey over the wet fields, and when the ash had cooled enough for hands to go into it, what the valley folk found at the heart of the ruin was the hearthstone itself split clean through from one edge to the other, a crack a finger's width across running the whole length of it, and the crack, where the ash had been swept back from it, ran with a colour none of the hands that first uncovered it wanted, at first, to put a name to, a slow seep of something neither quite liquid nor quite the ordinary black of a burnt-out fire, the colour, Anna said afterward more than once, of the light that had stood over the roof before any of them reached the house.

## V. Of the King's Hand

Word went up to the hill the following morning by the fastest rider the valley could put on the road, and it is not told, because the valley folk who sent him did not themselves know, what word he carried beyond the plain fact of three dead and a hearthstone that would not stop running colour a full day after the fire that cracked it had gone entirely cold. The king came down himself, and came quickly, by whatever road he judged quickest, with no more than two men riding behind him, and stood at Achadh dhìreach before the second evening was out, looking, to the valley folk gathered at a careful distance from the ruin, exactly as he had always looked to every valley he had ever come down to: a big man, plainly dressed, with a warmth about him that those nearest him felt on their faces like a hearth's own heat though the day was cold and he stood some way off from any fire, and no more years on him, so far as the oldest among them could judge, than he had carried the last time any of their grandparents had seen him, which for some of those standing there was longer ago than their own lifetimes reached.

He walked into the ruin alone, past the ring of ash and fallen roof-timber the valley folk had not yet found the heart to clear, and stood over the split hearthstone a long while without speaking, while behind him the whole of Achadh dhìreach that could walk that far stood in the cold and watched him do it. Anna, near the front of that watching, close enough to see plainly what the others further back could only half make out, saw him crouch at last and put his open hand flat on the stone where the crack ran widest, over the seep of colour that had not stopped running since the fire went out, and hold it there.

She said afterward, to whoever asked her in the years that followed, that she had expected some manner of struggle in it, a wrestling of some kind between a man's hand and whatever force had cracked a stone and burned a house to its footings in a single night, and that what she saw instead was stranger for being so plain: the king knelt with his hand on the stone the way any man might kneel to warm cold fingers at an ordinary fire, unhurried, his face giving away nothing she could afterward describe as effort, and the colour, under his open palm, went out of the crack the way colour goes out of a coal left to burn down past its last spark, fading first at the edges nearest his fingers and then, by degrees too slow for any single watcher to say exactly when the last of it went, along the whole visible length of the crack, until what was left under his hand, when at last he stood, was a stone greyed through and dead as any hearthstone that had never held more than an ordinary fire.

He turned to face the valley folk then, and it is this part of the telling that Achadh dhìreach kept whole, word for word as near as any single teller could manage it, long after the particulars of the fire itself had softened in the retelling. He did not ask, standing there, who among them had broken the measure, though there were those in that crowd, Anna among them, who believed he already knew it as plainly as if Eanraig himself stood before him to confess it, and he did not speak, either, of the three dead beyond a look toward the ruin that every witness afterward called grave without any of them agreeing on quite what in his face had made it look so.

"The full hand is a good custom," he said, and his voice, Anna said, carried no more weight in it than a man's voice carries saying any plain thing to a gathering of his own people, no raising, no anger she could point to, only a flatness that some of those who heard it took afterward for a kind of tiredness and others took for something nearer to grief. "Keep it. I will not make it a law, for I have made none, and I do not mean to begin with this one. But keep it, all the same, for your own sakes and not for mine, and teach it to whoever is too young yet to have learned why."

He said no more than that, and did not stay the night, though he sent his two men back some days later with grain enough to see the valley's poorer households through a year shortened by the loss of Eanraig's stored barley along with everything else the fire had taken, and he did not come again to Achadh dhìreach for a good many years after, so far as any teller there afterward troubled to remember, there being, in the plain course of things, no further occasion that called him.

The valley did not build a new house on the ground where Eanraig Ruadh's had stood. What they did, in the following spring, once the ground had settled and the last of the ash had been carried off, was raise a house a little to one side of the old footings, close enough that the greyed and cracked hearthstone still lay within an easy carrying, and set that same stone, whole as the king had left it, as the new house's own hearth rather than break it up for footing-stone or leave it where it lay to be grassed over the way the buried hearth of a hundred and sixty years before had been grassed over in its turn. It was Eanraig's eldest living son who raised that house and who chose, against some counsel that thought it an ill-omened stone to build a fire on, to keep it rather than quarry a fresh one, saying only that a stone the king himself had greyed with his own hand was as safe a hearth as any household in the valley was likely to keep, and safer, by his own reckoning, than one that had never been tested at all; and it was on that same stone, mended nowhere and cracked still, though the crack never widened nor ran with any colour again in all the years anyone at Achadh dhìreach afterward kept a fire on it, that his own children and his children's children, down a good many generations, laid their own fires and measured their own coal by the same full hand the whole valley had kept since before any of them were born.

Anna nic Coinnich lived a further fourteen years after that summer, long enough to see grandchildren of her own grown to households and to sit, in her last years, the same kind of hearth other households' children were sent to when their mothers had no time to mind them, as she herself had once kept for the valley in her middle years. She was asked, more than once by grandchildren too young to have any memory of the fire themselves, why the full hand mattered so much when a household's own fire seemed, to a child's eye, small trouble whichever way it was measured, and she answered them each time, so far as the valley's own telling of her preserves it, the same way and in the same few words, holding out one open palm, cupped, for the child to see the shape of it.

"Làn na glaice," she would say, "'s gun tuilleadh." A full hand, and no more. And when the child, as children mostly did, asked her why a hand and not some other measure, she would only close the hand slowly, as if weighing what it held though it held nothing at all, and tell them that a hand was the one measure every household owned alike, rich or poor, old or young, and that a custom kept because everyone owned the means to keep it was worth more, in her long experience of the valley's ordinary years, than any law a distant hill might have troubled to write down and send a rider to enforce.
