# Of Eòghann at the River

*VE 4,196 – 4,274*

## I. Of Eòghann's Keeping

Five hundred and fifty years had gone by since Cormac stood at the hearth of Tobar dhìreach with his ruined hand raised for the valley to see, and in all that span the bag had grown no lighter. Three coals lay in it still, cold and ordinary as the day the mason first tipped them out of his tunic: the silver-sheen, the one that would wake, if it woke at all, to no more than the snap of a hand, and the rainbow, which held every colour the eye could give a name to and answered to none of them. Keeper had followed keeper in that time, more of them than the recital troubled any longer to number separately, each carrying the three dark coals down to whichever water or fire or ground seemed to that keeper's own eye most likely, and each handing them on unlit to the next. The valley had long ago stopped remarking on the waiting the way it had stopped remarking, generations before that, on the waiting for the black coal and the red-brown. A thing five centuries unanswered ceases to feel like a question at all, and becomes instead a kind of furniture the household keeps because it has always been there, dusted at intervals and otherwise let stand.

Eòghann was born into that furniture at Tobar dhìreach, the second child of the household that held it, some five hundred and twenty-five years after Cormac's misstrike. He was not raised to think the keeping strange, any more than a child raised beside a hearth thinks the hearth strange; the bag hung on its peg above the fire-pot from his earliest memory, and he had counted the three coals into his own small palm long before anyone asked it of him in earnest, turning them over the way another child turns over river stones, for the plain unremarkable pleasure of a smooth thing in the hand. His elder sister was the one looked to carry the keeping forward, by every custom the valley held to, and Eòghann grew up free of it in a way few before him had been free, watching the bag rather than answering for it, until his sister died of a fever in her nineteenth year and the keeping came to him instead, whether he had been raised to it or not.

He was twenty that year, and he took the bag down from its peg and untied the thong with his own hands for the first time on the same evening his father was carried out to be buried beside the slab, and he said afterward, when he was old enough to be asked such things and patient enough to answer them, that the two griefs had come so close together in his mind that for a long while he could not think of the coals at all without also thinking of his father's hands, which had counted them out the same way for thirty years before his own.

He was not a man of Cormac's temper. Where Cormac had struck at a thing that would not go his way, Eòghann turned from it and walked, and the valley came in time to know him by his walking the way it had known Cormac by his scar: a keeper who thought best with his feet moving, who would set out at first light with the bag over one shoulder and be gone three days, four, a week, and come back saying little of where he had gone or what he had tried there, only that it had not answered. His mother, who lived to see him grown into that habit and shook her head over it more than once, used to say that her son had been given a question too large for any one valley to hold him while he carried it, and that a body restless in that way was better let walk than penned to a hearth to grow sour on its own waiting, as she had watched other keepers before him grow sour, in households not her own, on years of counting the same dark coals into the same idle palm.

He married, in his twenty-fourth year, a woman of the lake-shore line named Dearbhail, who kept his fire-pot fed and his supper warm through absences that grew, as the years went on, neither shorter nor less frequent, and who used to say of him, laughing to her own kin about it, that she had married a man who would sooner walk to the edge of the island and back than sit still with a question he could not answer. She did not come to Tobar dhìreach ignorant of what she was marrying into; her own grandmother had kept a fire-pot for a lesser household two valleys off, and Dearbhail had grown up knowing, in the plain way a hill child knows which crops fail on which ground, that a keeper's household ran on a different clock than other households did, its comings and goings set less by season and herd than by whatever the bag's silence had lately driven its keeper to attempt. She never once, in all the years the valley afterward remembered of her, asked Eòghann to give the keeping over to some other line, though it was hers by no blood of her own and she might, by the custom of in-marrying wives elsewhere in the hill country, have been forgiven for wishing a quieter husband; she said only, when she was asked, that a man who walked was easier kept than a man who sat and brooded, and that she would rather feed a keeper his supper cold three nights in ten than feed a bitter one his supper hot every night of the year.

He tried, in his first years of keeping, most of what the recital told him earlier keepers had tried, and a good many things it did not trouble to tell, for every keeper before him had added some private trial of his own to the bag's long history and not all of them had thought their failures worth passing on. He carried the silver coal to the Abhainn ghorm below Tobar dhìreach, where his own great-grandfather's generation was said to have first carried it, in case the water nearest the hearth had simply never been given its fair turn against the water of strangers' valleys. Two other rivers he tried besides, north and east, in the same summer, and a mountain tarn that was no river at all but held, by the telling, water enough to be worth the asking. None of it answered him. Over those years he grew into the particular patience of a man who does not expect an answer and goes on asking anyway, less from hope than from the plain fact that the bag was his to carry and a thing carried wants somewhere to be carried to.

It was old custom in the valley, older than Eòghann's grandfather could say with any certainty, to carry the dark coals down to the northern shore from time to time and throw them into the sea, in memory of the gold that had answered there and out of the stubborn belief, held longer in some households than in others, that water was water and the sea might yet be asked again what the small rivers had refused. Eòghann did not put much store in it himself; he had heard his father call it an old man's habit kept past its use, water thrown after water, and had heard his father do it all the same, twice in Eòghann's own boyhood, because a thing done by every keeper before him was not a thing a keeper lightly let drop, whatever he privately thought of it. Eòghann kept the custom too, in his turn, the way a man keeps a saying he no longer quite believes because his father said it and his father's father before him, and in his twenty-ninth year, with nothing else newly occurring to him and the bag's three coals lying as dark against his palm as they had lain the day they came to him, he took them down to the shore once more.

## II. Of the Northern Shore

It was two days' walk north from Tobar dhìreach to the coast, over the shoulder of the massif and down through birch and open moor into country that grew rockier and windier the nearer it came to salt water, and Eòghann went it alone, as he went most of his errands with the bag, telling Dearbhail only that he would be back inside the week and that she was not to trouble the fire-pot on his account, for it burned as well without him standing over it as with. He slept the first night in the lee of a boulder with a small fire of heather roots, as every keeper before him had slept on that same road, and came down to the shore on the evening of the second day with the wind already rising and a grey swell running in long unbroken lines from somewhere far out past the edge of what any teller on the island had ever claimed to have seen.

He knew the place. It was the same stretch of shingle his father had brought him to as a boy, a low curve of grey stone below a line of dunes, with nothing on it but the wrack the tide had left and a scatter of driftwood too salt-soaked to burn well, and a scatter, further up the beach, of the low stone hearths the seal-hunters of the near bothies built and abandoned season by season, cold now and half filled with blown sand. He had stood on that same shingle as a boy of nine or ten, holding his father's hand while the older man went through the same plain motions Eòghann went through now, and he remembered, standing there again with his own bag open, that he had thought nothing of it then beyond the ordinary wonder a child brings to any errand of his father's, salt spray and grey water and the strangeness of a coast after a childhood spent inland among hills. It did not feel strange to him now. It felt, if anything, like a duty discharged rather than a wonder attempted, the way a man goes through the motions of a rite whose meaning has worn thin with the keeping of it and finds that the going-through has become a kind of comfort in its own right, whatever it does or does not accomplish.

He stood there in the last of the light with the bag open on his palm, the silver coal and the untried one and the rainbow lying together the way they had lain together on every such evening for five hundred years, and threw them, one after the other, out past the breaking water into the grey swell beyond it.

He did not know, standing there, quite what he expected to see. No keeper's telling that had come down to him described the sea giving back anything more than the plain fact of its own indifference, a coal sinking, or washing back in on the next wave to be found again among the wrack, cold and wet and no different for the wetting; and that was what he found now, wading out to his knees in the falling light to gather them back before the tide could carry them beyond finding, three coals as dark in his palm as they had been dark in the bag, the salt water running off them the same as it ran off any stone on that shore. He stood a while longer than he needed to, watching the swell come in, less because he thought a longer watching would change anything than because a man who has walked two days to be disappointed is in no great hurry to turn round and walk two days back to say so.

The wind had backed round to the north-east by the time he set out for home the next morning, carrying rain in from somewhere further out than the eye could follow, and he walked the first day of the return in a steady wet that soaked through his cloak by noon and left him, by the time he found shelter for the night under an overhang he remembered from his boyhood travels with his father, colder and wearier than the walking alone accounted for. He did not think, that first night, in any words he could afterward have set down, about the coals at all; he thought, as a tired man thinks, of the fire he did not have and the dry clothes he did not have and the distance still between him and his own hearth, and slept badly, and woke stiff, and went on.

The second day the rain eased into the kind of fine wetting mist the hill country called no more than a greening day, not worth a cloak's trouble, and Eòghann walked through it with the bag closed again over his shoulder and his mind, so far as he afterward could say, on nothing more than the ground in front of his own feet, one stride and then the next, the way a man's mind goes when a body has been walking longer than it wants to and has stopped troubling itself with anything beyond the next mile. He came down off the moor by the middle of the afternoon into country he knew well, the birch scrub thinning into the upper reaches of the high valleys, the shoulder of the massif rising grey and wet-shouldered ahead of him, and somewhere in the last hour of that day's walking, with the light already going and his own thirst grown sharper than he had troubled to notice until then, he heard, before he saw it, the particular voice of moving water that meant the Abhainn ghorm, running full and brown-clear with the two days' rain, in the cut below the last rise before Tobar dhìreach.

## III. Of the Abhainn Ghorm

He came down the bank at the place where the path always crossed it, a shallow reach where the water ran clear and quick over grey stone and a man could kneel at the edge without wetting more than his boots, and he knelt there now without much thought behind the kneeling, the way a thirsty man kneels at the nearest water without weighing whether it is the water he means to make a study of, and cupped both hands and drank.

It was cold, colder than he had thought it would be so far downhill from the height, and it ran clean and sharp against a throat gone dry with two days of salt wind and rain, and he drank a second time before he sat back on his heels with the water still on his chin, and it was only then, sitting back with his hands still wet and the last of the day's light lying flat and grey along the surface of the river, that some part of him not given to remarking on such things noticed, the way a man notices a change in weather before he has troubled to look at the sky, that the water sat differently in his mouth than the water he had waded through two nights before, and he said it aloud, to no one, the way a tired man will say a thought aloud simply because there is no one nearer than the water to hear it and correct him.

"*Tha blas eile air an uisce seo seach uisce na mara,*" he said. This water tastes different from the sea water.

It was not, when he turned it over afterward at the recital and in the long years after that, a thought he could claim any cleverness for. He had not walked two days home turning the silver's long failure over in his mind and arrived, footsore, at some conclusion worth the saying; he had knelt, and drunk, and been thirsty, and the words had come out of him the plain unplanned way a true thing comes out of a tired man, before he had troubled to think whether it was worth saying or to whom. But he sat back on his heels a while after he had said it, with the bag still over his shoulder and the coals inside it as cold and unregarded as they had been all the way down from the shore, and something in the saying stayed with him longer than the words had taken to say, the way a thing said aloud sometimes settles in a man's mind more firmly for having been given a voice than it would have settled left silent.

He did not reason his way to what he did next, not by any account he ever afterward gave of it. He untied the bag's thong there on the bank, with the light nearly gone and the water running loud over the stones in front of him, and took out the three coals, and without weighing the sea's long failure against this one river's long failure, without any thought that this crossing had been tried before by his own great-grandfather's generation and had answered no better than the rest, he threw them, one after another, into the current in front of him, the way he had thrown them into the sea two evenings before, save that this time he did not wade in after them, being too tired, or too little minded to trouble, to go looking for what the river might do with them before it had done it.

The silver-sheen coal took the current a hand's breadth below the surface and did not sink further, held for a moment turning in the flow the way a leaf turns caught on a stone, and then it caught.

It came up from beneath the water rather than from its own surface, a thread of pale fire spreading out along the coal's underside first, silver-white, the colour of moonlight on moving water, and then whole, a flame standing in the current and burning there, under the surface, fed by nothing the eye could account for and quenched by nothing the river could bring against it, the water running over it and through it without troubling it any more than the wind troubles a hearth-fire built in a sheltered lee. It lit the riverbed beneath it, the grey stones there showing pale and trembling through the moving water, and it lit, faintly, the underside of the current's own surface, so that a man kneeling on the bank, as Eòghann now knelt, saw not only the flame but its light thrown up and scattered across the whole width of the river in front of him, broken and re-made a thousand times over by every ripple that passed above it.

He knelt there a long while, by his own telling of it after, watching a fire burn under water that had never in all the tellings he knew burned under anything but open air, and did not at first think to look for the other two coals at all, the untried one and the rainbow, which the current had rolled on past him into the dark; he found them at first light the next morning, wading the shallows below the crossing, lodged cold and dark against a bar of gravel a stone's throw downstream, and carried them home in the bag as dark as they had gone into the water. The silver burned on where it had caught, through that whole night and, so far as anyone at Tobar dhìreach was ever after able to say, through every night since, a low pale light under the current at the shallow crossing below the height, that any traveller might kneel at the bank and see for himself on a dark evening when the water ran clear.

## IV. Of the Recital

He came into Tobar dhìreach some while after full dark, wet through, empty-handed but for the bag which now held only what the river had left him, and found Dearbhail still awake by the fire-pot, who took one look at him standing in the doorway, soaked and hollow-eyed and saying nothing, and thought at first that some harm had come to him on the road before she thought to ask about the coals at all. He told her the whole of it standing there in his wet clothes before he would sit to the fire, the two days at the shore and the nothing they had brought him, the walk home in the rain, the kneeling at the crossing, the taste of the water and the words that had come out of him unbidden, and the fire that had stood up under the current and gone on standing there while he knelt on the bank and watched it, and Dearbhail, who had heard him come home disappointed from that same errand more times than either of them troubled any longer to count, said nothing at all for a long moment, and then only that he had better sit down before he told the rest of it, for he was shaking, though whether from the cold of the road or from what he had seen at the crossing he could not himself have said with any certainty.

At the next telling of the Line, on the night of the lightning, Eòghann stood at the hearth of Tobar dhìreach with the bag counted out before him as every keeper before him had counted it, one coal gone from three to two, and spoke the new line into the recital before the gathered valley: that the sea had taken the gold-sheen coal, and the height the white-flecked, and the earth the black slowly in the dark, and a man's own blood the red-brown, and now the river had taken the fifth, not thrown to it in trial as every coal before it had first been thrown, but given to it, he said, by a man who had done no more than kneel at its edge, thirsty, and tell it the truth of what he tasted. The valley took to calling it *gual airgid*, the silver coal, and some among them, turning the telling over at hearth after hearth that winter the way every new wonder was turned over before it settled into a thing everyone knew, said that of the five coals now answered, this was the only one that had asked nothing of the man who woke it beyond the plain truth he would have told anyway, to no one, at the edge of a river he had crossed a hundred times before without once expecting it to answer him.

There were those at the recital that first winter who pressed him afterward, at his own hearth and at others, to say again exactly what he had tasted in the water that made it different, hoping perhaps to learn some sign by which another man might know a river worth kneeling at before he troubled to throw a coal into it, and Eòghann, patient with them as he was patient with most things, told it the same way each time he was asked and owned, each time, that he could not have set the taste into words that meant anything beyond the plain fact of its being different, no more than a man could say in words what told him one face from another he had known all his life. It was not a taste he had gone looking for, he said, and he did not believe, however often he was pressed on it, that a man could go looking for it and find it any sooner than he himself had, kneeling tired and thirsty at a crossing he had waded through a hundred times without once thinking to drink from it slowly. Some among the valley's older hearths took that for a kind of answer on its own, that the coals had never once been woken by a man who set out meaning to wake them, and said so at their own firesides that winter, though none of them said it at Eòghann's hearth, judging it unkind to tell a keeper, however gently, that his own long walking might have counted for less in the matter than his own thirst.

Travellers came to the crossing below Tobar dhìreach in the years that followed, more than had ever troubled to pass that way before, kneeling at the bank in the last light of an evening to see for themselves the pale fire standing under the current, and some of them, the recital afterward kept, knelt and drank from the same water Eòghann had drunk from, though none of them, so far as any teller ever carried word of it back to the hearth, found the water tasting to them any different from water anywhere else on the island. The fire coal and the rainbow lay in the bag as dark as they had lain in it for five hundred years and more, the one that would wake, if it woke at all, to no more than the snap of a hand, and the one that held every colour and answered to none; and Eòghann kept the bag some thirty years further, walking still, though less far and less often as the years wore on him, and in that long stretch of his own keeping tried the fire coal against flint more than once, without ever striking from it more than the ordinary spark any stone gives a blade, and let it be, at the last, telling Dearbhail that some things wanted a hand he did not have and would come, if they came at all, to a keeper not yet born.

He died in his six-and-sixtieth year at his own hearth, with the fire-pot burning near him as it had burned near Cormac and near every keeper of the line before either of them, and Dearbhail, who outlived him by some years at Tobar dhìreach, told it afterward that his last words, before the fever took his sense from him, were of the crossing, not the coals, asking whether the water still ran clear there and whether the light could still be seen under it on a dark evening, as though the one answer his long keeping had ever been given mattered to him, at the end, less as a wonder accomplished than as a place he wished, one last time, to be told was still there. The bag passed from his grave to a younger kinsman of the line, as it had passed from Cormac's, carrying two coals still dark and a keeping not yet finished; and the crossing below Tobar dhìreach kept its pale fire under the current through every season that followed, so that a traveller coming down off the height on a clear night might see it a long way off, a low still light lying under moving water, and take it, before ever he was told otherwise, for no more than the moon caught and held a moment on the stones.
