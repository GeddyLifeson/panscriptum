# Of the Three Wells and the Slab

*VE 72 – 226*

## I. Of the Black Well and the Young Well

By the time Conall's son took up the watch of the well-cut, the fire in the vein was no longer a wonder to the valley. It was a fact of the place, the way the ridge above it was a fact, and the children who grew up within a day's walk of Tobar dhìreach had never known a world where it was otherwise. They knew the two flat stones set across the mouth of the cut, and the low red glow that lay between them if you looked down at the right hour of the evening, and they knew, because they were told it before they could well understand why, that the fire had come down out of a storm and into their great-grandfather's well, and that no other well on the island could say the like.

It was this last claim that other wells began, in those years, to dispute.

There was a household two valleys east that kept its hearth from a black seam of its own, cut into stone so dark it had never wanted for a name beyond the black well, and there was a household further east again, beyond the black well, whose well was young in the way that a spring is young, having been sunk only a father's lifetime before Oidhche an Dealanaich, so that everyone still living remembered its cutting. Both had taken fire from the mason's line in the early years, as every hearth for three valleys round had taken it, freely given and never grudged; and both, as the generations turned, began to tell a different tale of where that fire had first been struck.

The loudest of these tellers was a man of the black well named Tadhg, big in the chest and bigger in the voice, who had grown up hearing his grandmother swear that the crack of the lightning on the night it fell had come from directly over their own valley and no other, and who had turned that swearing, by the time he was a man grown, into something very near a certainty. He had never doubted it a day in his life. He did not doubt it now, at six-and-forty, holding up before a gathering at the black well a stone the size of two fists, dark along one face in a way that might have been fire and might, as well, have been nothing but the stone's own colour.

"There," he said, and turned it so the firelight ran along the dark face of it. "There is the mark of it. My grandmother's mother saw the sky open over this valley, not that one, and here is the stone that took the strike, kept in my family since before any of you were born to doubt it."

No one at the black well doubted it, or said they did not, for it was Tadhg's stone and Tadhg's voice, and both were large enough in that small gathering to leave little room for another opinion. Word of it went out along the paths the fire-bearers walked, as word of anything strange or proud will go, and by the following year there were those three valleys over, at hearths that had never seen the stone and never would, repeating that the black well had the truer claim, on the strength of a story told about a stone none of them had held.

The mason's own household heard it and did nothing.

This was remarked on, in its turn, almost as much as the claim had been. There were those at Tobar dhìreach who wanted to send someone east with the truth of the matter, since the truth of the matter was there for anyone to see: the capped shaft with its scorched lip, black to this day where the lightning had run down the rock, a mark no scattered stone could match for plainness. But the eldest of the household, Conall's son grown grey now at the work his father had left him, would not have it argued.

He had his own reasons, and gave them plainly enough to anyone who asked. He had seen, as a boy at his grandfather Conall's knee, how little a quarrel over a well had ever fed a hearth, and he held to the plain shape of the thing: theirs was the fire, and the fire had been walked out to a hundred houses that did not care two straws which valley the lightning had chosen, so long as it kept burning on their own floor. A dispute carried east would cost three days' walking and buy them, at the end of it, nothing warmer than an argument won. He would sooner spend the three days at the well-cut, teaching his own son the watch, than spend them proving a scorch on a stone to a man who had already made up his mind not to see it.

"Let Tadhg keep his stone," he said. "We keep the well. A man may say what he likes of a fire he did not carry home; it will not make the fire his, and it will not make ours less ours for his saying it." And when his own son pressed him, wanting to know why they should let a false thing stand unanswered, he said only that a fire spoken of by two households was a fire remembered by two households, and that this was no bad thing for a fire to be, however crookedly the remembering ran.

It was not long after that a second claim rose, quieter than Tadhg's and, in its way, harder to answer, for it came without a stone to hold up or a loud voice to carry it. At the young well an old woman began to tell, in the dark half of the year when such tellings are told, that her own mother had stood at the door of their house on Oidhche an Dealanaich and seen the light go down into their own well, newer sunk though it was, and that the mason had only carried fire from his cut because it lay nearer to hand for the carrying, and no other reason. She told it the way the old tell things they have turned over so long they no longer remember whether they were given it whole or worked it out themselves: softly, without heat, as a fact rather than a boast, which is why it travelled further than Tadhg's stone ever did, and outlasted it.

By then there were three tellings abroad in the high valleys, where there had once been one plain fact, and no way left for a stranger passing through to know which well the lightning had truly struck without walking to Tobar dhìreach and looking down at the scorch on the stone himself. Few did. It was easier, and pleasanter, to believe the well one's own grandmother had sworn by, and the valleys settled, without any of them quite deciding to, into three tellings held at once and never set against each other, the way three roads may run to the same market and each traveller swear his own the shortest.

The household at Tobar dhìreach let it stand so. They had the fire. Whatever else was said of it, at the black well or the young well or anywhere a telling might wander to, could not be carried home and put on a floor to warm a child's hands, and it was this, more than any argument, that the eldest man of the line held to when his son asked him, once more and for the last time, whether they ought not answer Tadhg. "Answer him with the fire," the old man said, "the way we have answered everyone else who ever came cold to our door," and after that the matter of the three wells was let alone at Tobar dhìreach, though it did not stop being told, east and south, for as long as there were hearths near enough to hear it.

## II. Of Muirne

The girl who would keep the vein through its last years was born at Tobar dhìreach some forty years after Conall died, a great-granddaughter of his line on the mother's side, in a household grown too large by then to count easily by the fathers alone. Her name was Muirne, and she was given, almost as soon as she could be trusted on her feet, to the woman whose task it had long been to see that the household's floor-fire never wanted for tending, so that she grew up as much at the hearth as in her own bed, and knew the sound a good fire makes settling for the night before she knew her letters, not that anyone in the valley had letters to know.

She was quick, and she was quiet in the particular way of a child who has been let sit still among grown talk from very young, listening more than she was listened to. By the time she was old enough to be sent to the well-cut on her own, an honour and a chore both, granted only to those the household trusted not to lean too far over the two flat stones, she had already spent half her short life near enough to fire to have opinions about it that older folk found, when she gave them, oddly sound. She said once, watching an aunt feed the floor-hearth with wet wood that hissed and sulked rather than caught, that the fire was not lazy, only hungry for the wrong thing, the way a person turns from bad bread and reaches for good, hungry all the while. The aunt, who had never thought of it so, laughed and told it about the valley as a child's saying, and it stuck to Muirne all her life, so that grown folk who ought to have known better than to ask a girl such things came to her, even young, wanting to know what a fire was hungry for, as though it were a question with an answer only she could give.

She grew, too, in a household that had by then forgotten what it was to be small. Where Neachdan had once fed a single hearth with his own hands and Conall had walked a coal to the nearest neighbour and thought it a long errand, the kin at Tobar dhìreach now numbered enough grown men and women that Muirne could go a full day without speaking to the same face twice, and the fire her forefathers had carried out to strangers was, by her childhood, burning in so many houses along the valley floor that no one troubled any longer to count them. She heard the old tellings at the hearth as every child of that household heard them, of the wedge and the storm and the burning well, and she heard them the way a child hears the lie of the land she was born on, already there before she thought to ask how it came to be.

She took up the true watch of the well-cut somewhat before she was grown, when the man before her, worn out at the work as men are worn by any long watching, began to leave more of the winter nights to her than he kept for himself. No one made a ceremony of it. She simply came to be the one who sat on the two flat stones through the dark half of the year, wrapped against the cold with her knees drawn up as every keeper before her had sat, looking down at the vein in its crack in the rock.

And it was Muirne, more than any who had watched before her, who first let herself see plainly what the fire was doing.

It had burned, by then, for the better part of a hundred and fifty years, Teine na Fèithe, down in the same broken seam the lightning had first found, fed by nothing but its own store since the night it was struck, for no one had ever thought to feed the vein the way a floor-hearth is fed; it was the floor-hearths that were fed from it, a hundred of them and more by then, walked out hearth to hearth across three valleys and the northern moor besides, each one a small child of that single fire. The vein only burned on, low and dark and steady in its crack as it had burned from the first, and no one who had not sat with it through many winters running would have marked the change in it from one year to the next. Muirne sat with it through many winters running.

She learned, in those years, the small offices of the watch as her forebears had learned them: to lay a broken branch across the mouth of the cut when the wind ran hard down the valley and threatened to worry the flame thin, though she never once fed the vein itself, for that was not the watch's work and had never been; to keep children back from the two flat stones with nothing sharper than a raised hand and a word, the way she herself had once been kept back; to sit still enough, on the coldest nights, that a fox might cross within a spear's throw of her and never know she was there. It was slow work, and lonely work for long stretches of it, and she came in time to prefer it to the crowded warmth of the floor-hearth, where a dozen voices talked over one another and no one voice could be heard clearly. At the well-cut there was only the fire and the wind and, eventually, her own thoughts, turned over slowly the way a knapper turns a flint nodule in his hand, and it was in that quiet, more than anywhere else, that she began to notice what she noticed.

She saw, where her father's father had seen only a fire that had never failed, that the glow between the two flat stones was a little lower each year than the year before. A man rarely marks his own child growing, seeing the same face at the same table every evening, and yet knows well enough, meeting a cousin's child after some years apart, how far the growing has gone; it was in that way, slow and sidelong, that Muirne came to know the vein was failing. She said nothing of it for a long while, for it was a hard thing to say to a household that had built its whole understanding of the world on a fire that did not go out, and she was young yet, and did not trust her own eye against the certainty of her elders. But she kept a private reckoning of it in her own mind, a habit of noticing she never spoke of to anyone, matching this winter's glow against her memory of the winter five years gone, and five years before that, until she could no longer doubt what she saw: that the vein, however slowly, was going the way of any fire that is not fed, and that there would come a night, though she could not have said which, when it would go the whole way and not come back.

## III. Of the Last Ember

The wet spring that finished it came when Muirne was near forty, in a year the whole valley remembered afterward for other reasons besides the vein, for the rain that spring stood in the low fields a hand's depth deep and rotted the seed in the ground, and there was hunger enough abroad that the fire's failing was, for once, not the worst thing spoken of at any hearth. But it was spoken of, and by Muirne first.

She had gone down to the well-cut on an evening when the rain had not let up in three days, gone the way she always went, without expecting anything different of the visit than she had found the evening before, and had found the glow between the two flat stones smaller than she had ever seen it and paler, a red gone the colour of an old bruise rather than the colour of living heat. She sat with it a long while in the wet, not moving, the way a person sits with someone they already know will not see the morning, and somewhere in that sitting, though she could not afterward say at what hour, the glow went out.

It did not go out all at once, with any sound or sign a teller could make much of. It simply, at some point in the dark and the rain, stopped being there, and when Muirne put her hand down into the cut, careful as she had been taught to be careful of a fire that might still bite, she felt only wet stone, cold under the rain the way any stone is cold, and knew before she climbed back up into the storm that the fire the mason had carried home out of the lightning, the fire that had burned in that one crack of rock since before her grandfather's grandfather was born, had burned itself out at last, quietly, with no one there to see the last of it go but her.

She told the household plainly, at first light, for there was no way to tell it that would make it smaller than it was. There was grief in the hearing of it, real grief, though every fire in three valleys still burned bright as it had always burned, fed from floor to floor and needing the vein no longer for its own life; the valley mourned that morning the mark itself, the sign of Oidhche an Dealanaich, gone from the world the way the night was already long gone, kept now only in the telling.

It was not many days before someone said what more than one had already been thinking, which was that the fire might be found again if the well were dug deeper, since a vein that had held fire once might hold fire again, further down where the coloured stone was said to run. It was a young man of the household who said it first, and others took it up quickly, for there is comfort in a task even a false comfort, and digging is a task any strong back can offer a valley grieving something it cannot otherwise mend. Muirne let them talk of it two full days before she spoke against it, and when she did, she spoke standing at the well-cut, with half the household gathered in the drizzle to hear her.

"The fire did not come to us out of the ground," she said. "It came out of the sky, into the ground, on one night only, and it is not for us to go looking after it down where it fell, the way a man goes looking for a knife he has dropped. What is down there now is stone the lightning never touched, and stone the lightning never touched will not answer a maul any better than it answered one before the mason ever struck this seam. We did not make the fire. We will not go digging to remake it."

Not everyone in the household believed her outright, that first morning, and there were those who thought, privately, that Muirne spoke more from fear of disturbing a holy thing than from any true knowledge of what lay under the broken rock. The young man who had raised the talk of digging said as much to her face, there in the drizzle, asking what harm a few strokes of the maul could do a stone already broken once by lightning. Muirne did not answer him with anger. She only asked him, in turn, whether he had ever known the vein to grow brighter for any care they gave it, in all the years of the watch, or whether it had only ever burned as it was struck and gone the way it was going now regardless of what hands were laid on it; and when he had no answer to that, the household took her silence for his and let the matter rest. Whether it was her word that stopped them, or only that the wet spring had left few backs strong enough to spare for a well no one could promise would give anything back, the digging was never begun, and within the season even the young man who had first proposed it had let it drop, the way most griefs are let drop once the worst of the winter that bore them has passed.

## IV. Of the Slab and the New Well

What the household did instead, in that same wet year, was what Muirne herself proposed once the talk of digging had died down: to leave the broken vein as it lay, and cover it.

They chose a stone for it from the same outcrop the mason had cut his own wedges from, long ago, a flat slab broader than a grown man lying full length, Leac na Fèithe that came after to be called Leac na Fèithe, and it took six of them the better part of two days to lever it up the slope and set it down across the mouth of the cut where the two smaller stones had lain since Neachdan's own time. Muirne stood by through all of it, past the years when her own back could have joined the lifting, but she watched each stone's fall as closely as she had once watched the fire, as though the setting of it mattered as much as the labour that set it.

When it was down, and the last gap along its edge packed with turf so that no rain would run in beneath it, she knelt and put her palm flat on the stone, in the drizzle, the way she had once put her hand down into the cut to feel for a living warmth that was no longer there. Leac na Fèithe gave back nothing but its own coldness, and the wet of the day, and she knelt there long enough that those waiting on her began to wonder whether she meant to speak. She did not. She only rose at last, wiped her hand on her skirt, and said that no water was ever to be drawn beside that stone again, not for beasts nor for washing nor for any use at all, and that this, from that day, was the whole of what the household owed the place: to leave it be, and remember it was there.

The black cut the mason had first sunk with wedge and oak maul, the well itself, gave the household no more water after that, though the water in it had not failed; Muirne simply would not have it drawn from so near the covered vein, fearing, though she never said so outright, that a bucket lowered too near Leac na Fèithe might one day come up holding something other than water. A new well was cut that same season, a stone's throw off across the yard, by men with strong backs and no memory yet worn thin enough to make the digging feel like anything more than an ordinary well-cutting, and it gave good water from the first day, cold and clean, the way any well on that ground gives water when it is cut with care. In time it was this well, and not the black cut under Leac na Fèithe, that the household drew from every day of its life thereafter, so that a stranger walking into the yard a generation on would have taken the new well for the old one, and never known, unless someone troubled to tell him, that the true well of Teine a' Chlachair lay a stone's throw off, sealed and silent under a slab no one drew water beside.

Word of the covering went out along the same paths the fire-bearers had walked for generations, though it travelled slower than the fire had once travelled, for a covering is a smaller thing to carry from house to house than a live coal in a clay pot. At the black well Tadhg's grandsons heard it and said little, for there was less glory to be had contesting a stone that gave off no light than there had been contesting a fire that had; and at the young well the telling of the light going down into their own cut went on being told regardless, since a story that had never needed the proof of a burning vein to be believed was not about to be troubled now that the vein no longer burned for anyone to compare it against. The three tellings held on, east and south, long after the one true well at Tobar dhìreach had nothing left to show but a flat stone and a household that would not draw water near it.

Muirne lived on many years after Leac na Fèithe was laid, long enough to see her own grandchildren old enough to be sent to the well-cut in their turn, though there was no fire in it now for them to watch, only the covered stone and the telling that went with it, which she made sure was told to each of them before they were old enough to forget it wrongly. She told it the same way every telling year after year, standing, as Conall had stood to give the rule of the stone, and by the time she was old her telling had become the one the household kept, unaltered, passed down after her the way the fire had once been passed hearth to hearth: a wonder found, and a wonder covered, and a well that gave water all the same.

In her last years she grew slow on her feet and could no longer walk out to Leac na Fèithe in the worst of the winter weather, and it fell to her grandchildren to go in her place and bring back word of the stone's condition, as though it were a sick relation that wanted visiting rather than a covered well that asked nothing of anyone. She would ask them, each time, whether the turf at the edge still held the rain out, and whether any fool had left a bucket standing near it, and was satisfied only when told the stone lay exactly as she had last seen it, unmoved and unvisited by anything but weather. It was, by then, less a duty to her than a comfort, the way an old woman finds comfort in hearing that a door she can no longer reach is still shut fast.

She died in the household's own house at Tobar dhìreach in the fullness of years the valley reckoned long for that country, and was buried, as the custom of the hill-folk was by then, in the ground near the house rather than carried anywhere else, with no coal and no fire laid beside her, for it was the vein's own fire she had kept, not any hearth's, and the vein's fire she had already given, whole and entire, back to the stone. What was left to the household after her was the telling, and Leac na Fèithe, and the new well giving water a stone's throw from the old, and for some years yet, until another keeper rose to take up the office Muirne had made her own, it was the youngest of her grandchildren who walked out to the covered stone in the dark half of the year, knelt, and laid a bare palm on the cold flat of it, the way she had taught them: for what the stone had once held, not for anything it gave back now, and because a place that has held fire is never quite the same, to those who remember it, as any other stone.
