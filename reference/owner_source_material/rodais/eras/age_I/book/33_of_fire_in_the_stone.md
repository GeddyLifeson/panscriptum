# Of Fire in the Stone

*VE 4,672 – 4,850*

## I. Of the Stone New in the Grass

Lughaidh was two and twenty the year he first drove his flock up the western slopes past the ford where the two burns met, following the ewes further than his father had ever troubled to follow them, on the reasoning, which was his own and which he never explained to anyone who did not first ask, that grass eaten short by one summer's flock was grass a man wanted to leave a season or two before he brought his beasts back to it. He was a patient shepherd rather than a clever one, the kind neighbours spoke of as steady before they spoke of him as anything else, and it was this steadiness, more than any restlessness of his own, that carried him that year to a fold of the western grass no one of his own hearth had grazed in living memory.

He came on the stone in the last of the day, when the light lay long and gold across the tussocks and every small rise threw a long shadow ahead of it. It stood alone on a low shoulder of ground where nothing else stood, no cairn near it, no path worn to it that he could see, a single upright slab taller than a man with his arm raised, grey where the lichen had not yet taken it and green-grey where it had, leaning not at all, so square to the ground that Lughaidh's first thought, looking at it across the darkening grass with his dog gone quiet at his heel, was that no weather he knew of stood a stone up that straight on its own.

He did not go to it that evening. He counted his ewes instead, the way a shepherd counts before anything else, and made his fire a good bowshot off, on ground he knew, and ate what he had carried and lay down with the dog against his side and did not sleep well, waking twice to look toward the shoulder of ground where the stone stood black now against a paler dark, telling himself both times that a stone was a stone and had never yet troubled a sheep or a man, and neither time quite believing himself enough to sleep sound on it.

In the morning, with the light coming up grey and then gold behind him and the stone standing plain and ordinary in it, he walked over.

It was new. That was the thing he could not afterward make anyone who had not seen it for themselves believe: new in the way of a thing lately made, the cut faces of it sharp-edged still, unrounded by rain, the grass at its foot not yet grown back over whatever digging had set it, a bare collar of turned earth no wider than a hand's span all round the base, greening at the crumbled edges and black and raw at the centre where no green had reached at all. Lughaidh had seen old stones before, the low grey teeth of them scattered here and there about the high ground the way old stones are scattered on any hill a people has walked long enough, softened at every corner, sunk a little into their own hollows, grown into the land until a man half forgot they had ever been raised. This one had begun no such forgetting. It stood the way a new-cut post stands in a fence line before its first winter, still telling, to anyone who looked close, exactly how lately a hand had set it there.

He put his own hand flat against the face of it, for no reason he could have given beyond the reason any man puts a hand to a strange thing, and found it warm from the morning sun and rough under his palm in a way that was not all weather; and looking closer, with his eyes now used to the light, he saw that the roughness ran in lines, a shape laid on the stone by some other hand than weather's, a single running line of signs cut into the upper face from one edge near to the other, in a script he did not know and had never seen, and that no one of his people cut or read. It ran level, and it stopped clean at both ends, as though whoever had cut it had known exactly how much they meant to say and said no more and no less.

He did not touch that line again once he had seen it plainly. But lower down the face, where the stone narrowed toward its foot and the full morning light now fell square upon it, he found a second cutting, shorter, four small clusters of signs set close together, cut narrower and shallower than the line above by what he judged, turning his head to catch the shadow in the grooves, a finer tool and another hand. He could no more read them than the line above. He stood a good while with his hand near the stone and not on it, looking from the narrow lower cutting to the broad line above and back, turning over in his own slow way the plain fact that here were two hands where he had at first looked for one, and that neither hand had left any other mark on the hillside, no footprint the grass had not already swallowed, no ash of a fire, no litter of chips such as a mason leaves at his work. The stone stood finished. Whoever had raised it had raised it and gone.

He brought his flock down off that ground within the week, going a longer way round than the ground asked, and keeping the stone in sight over his shoulder the better part of the descent, as a man keeps a strange dog in sight rather than turn his back on it first. That night, at his own hearth, with the fire built up higher than a shepherd's supper called for, he told what he had found to his father's household, and was made to tell over again the shape and the place of the lower cutting, how many clusters, how close, how deep; and his father, an old man by then and slower to wonder than his son, asked only whether the boy had left anything of his own behind him up there, a mark, a stone moved, anything the ground might afterward be made to answer for, and Lughaidh, a little stung that this should be the first question asked of him, said he had moved nothing at all, which was true, and remained true the whole length of his life after.

The tale went from that hearth to the next inside the week and further before the season turned, gathering as it went, the way any tale gathers: that the stone stood taller than a man with his arm raised became, by the third or fourth telling, taller than two men stood one on the other's shoulders; that Lughaidh had found it in the last light of evening became, in some mouths, that he had found it by a light of its own, which Lughaidh denied plainly and without heat whenever the telling reached back to him. And by the next winter every hearth that told of the stone told also what its four low words said, *Bha teine sa chloich*, there was fire in the stone, though no one of the valleys could read a sign, and no one could say whose mouth had first given the words to the marks. How that came to be known is a riddle, as the knowing of the lines in the Doire uaine hills had been; known it was, and said the same way in every mouth, as though a saying that fitted so well the felt shape of an old wonder needed no hearth's embroidering to carry.

What the line made of it, Lughaidh himself never troubled to learn in any close way, being a shepherd and not a keeper, and the keeping of stones not being work he had ever been asked to take up; but it came back to him, in the way things came back along that same slow road, that the fire the second saying spoke of was held by Clann na Ceiste to be their own fire, the mason's fire, though who had cut the words, and when, and by what right a hand not of the line's own reckoning had come to speak the line's own matter in stone, none of them could say any better than Lughaidh could say who had left the older, wider line above it. The line took the lower words for their own. The upper line they left as Lughaidh had left it, unread, and it stayed unread by every eye that came after his, the whole length of Lughaidh's own life and long past it, a single running mark on a stone none could account for standing above four words that everyone, in the end, agreed to.

Lughaidh kept his flock on those slopes and the others near them for the better part of fifty years after, growing old the way a steady man grows old, and he is remembered, where he is remembered at all beyond his own hearth, as the shepherd who found the stone new. He died in his seventieth year, at his own hearth, the same year, as it happened, that a young man of the line standing at that season's gathering said the four words aloud for the first time to a crowd that had come to hear the fathers named; and whether Lughaidh, failing by then in his last illness, ever heard that they had gone so far, no one troubled to say.

## II. Of the Second Stone

The second stone stood a good day's walk from the first, further into the hills where the ground rose broken and stony and the grass thinned to tussock and bare rock between, on ground no shepherd of Lughaidh's own valley grazed at all, being poor feed and further than any flock needed driving for it. It was found later than the first, by how much later no telling ever fixed with any certainty, though the line came to reckon it a matter of some years rather than many, and it was found, as such things are found on ground that few walk, by a traveller rather than a shepherd, a man of no particular note who left no name behind him in any telling and who is remembered only as having said, when he brought the word down to the nearer hearths, that he had near enough walked into the thing before he saw it, so little did it stand apart from the broken grey rock all about it.

This stone was shorter than the first and plainer, no line of unread script upon it at all, only the one cutting, three words only, in the same narrow, sure hand that had cut the four words low on the other stone, or in a hand so like it that no one who compared the two, then or after, could swear to any difference between them:

*Cuimhnich an teine.*

Remember the fire.

Who was bidden to remember, and what exactly they were bidden to remember beyond the plain word, no telling that came down from that hillside ever said, and the line at Tobar dhìreach, asked by more than one traveller over the years whether the second stone spoke to them any plainer than the first, gave the same answer each time: that the fire was theirs to remember whether the stone bade them or no, and that a command cut in rock to no one in particular was, for all that, a command they were glad enough to take as meant for them, there being no other hand on the island that kept a fire so old or minded so hard to be told to keep minding it.

It was some years after the second stone's finding that the four low words of the first stone were said, for what the tellers held to be the first time, standing, before a whole gathering rather than at a single hearth. The young man who said them was of the line, new enough to speaking before a crowd that his voice caught once in the saying and had to be steadied, and he said them as a thing come down to him, by the same slow road as everyone else, from a traveller who had had it from a traveller before him, back, though no one at that gathering could have traced the whole chain of it, to the first hearths that told of the stone, fifty years gone. The gathering took the words up the way the valleys took up any saying that fitted a felt thing, repeating it after him at the fire until it had passed into the ordinary stock of what was said at every gathering after, alongside the recital of the fathers and the old unanswered riddle of the coals.

## III. Of the Ring at the Second Stone

Mòrnait was born at Tobar dhìreach into Clann na Ceiste, though not into the narrow line of fathers that kept the bag from father to son, her own household being one of the many that had gathered, over the generations since Oscar's jest first took, about the line's own hearth without ever holding the coals themselves. She grew up on the Telling as every child of the valleys grew up on it, and on the newer sayings besides, the four words and the three, said so often at so many gatherings by then that a child of her own age could not have told anyone which she had learned first or from which mouth, the sayings having become, by the time she was old enough to say them back, simply part of what was known, the way the shapes of the hills were known, without a first telling anyone troubled to remember.

She was a woman of no particular office among the line, keeping no bag and reciting no fathers, but she had, from a girl, the kind of restlessness that finds its own work where none is asked of it, and what she found, some years into her own married life, was the stone of the three words, Colbh Cnoc chiar, and the poor, broken ground about it, which she took to visiting at first out of no more than the wish to see for herself a thing so much spoken of and so seldom looked at, the valley's own folk being no readier to walk a hard day into stony hills than the shepherds before them had been.

The walk was no small undertaking for a woman with a household of her own to keep, a full day out over broken ground that gave a walker nothing easy underfoot the whole way, rising steadily from the last of the good grass into country where the path, such path as there was, picked between slabs of bare grey rock split by frost into edges sharp enough to cut a soft boot-sole, and where the wind, coming off the high ground with nothing to break it, found a traveller from every side at once rather than the one side a valley wind came from. She went in fair weather when she could choose her day and in weather no fairer than most when she could not, carrying the coal wrapped in its damp moss inside a second wrapping of oiled hide against the wet, and food enough for the two days the walk there and back and the sitting between them asked of her, and she came home each time, her own husband said afterward, looking as though the ground itself had had something to say to her on the way that she had not yet finished turning over.

Asked once, by a niece of her own household who thought the whole business a strange use of a grown woman's days, what she meant to prove by it, Mòrnait is remembered to have said that she meant to prove nothing, proving being the line's own long business at Tobar dhìreach and none of hers; and asked a second time, more gently, by an old woman of a neighbouring hearth who had herself walked the long road once and found nothing at the far end of it but a stone and cold rock, what she hoped to find there that the older woman had not, Mòrnait said only that she went to leave something rather than to find anything, a fire, where a stone had asked for one to be remembered, and that whether the stone or anyone behind it ever marked her leaving it was not, in the end, greatly her business either. What she did, once she had made the walk a second time and a third, she never afterward explained in any way that satisfied everyone who later asked her why, saying only, when pressed, that a fire kept meant more than a fire spoken of, and that a stone bidding her remember seemed to her, however the stone had meant it, ill remembered by words alone. She carried fire up from the pot at Tobar dhìreach, the coal wrapped close in damp moss the whole hard day of the walking so that it lived to the far end of it, and she built, on the bare stony ground about the foot of the second stone, a ring of fire-cracked stones gathered from the broken rock all round, and in the ring she laid and lit a fire the size of any ordinary hearth-fire, no bigger, and sat by it until it had burned down to nothing, and then she went home.

She did this again the next year, and the year after, at first alone and then, as word of it went about the valleys the way word of anything went about them, with others who came to sit by the fire with her a night or a day before going their own ways home, so that by the tenth year of her keeping it the ring at the stone had grown a worn path to it where before there had been none, and the ground within the ring had taken the hard-trodden, ash-blackened look of ground that has held fire and feet upon it many times over, no different in the end from the ground about any hearth kept a working lifetime, save that no house stood by it and no one lived there and the fire, once built and burned down, was left to go out on its own rather than banked to keep till morning.

Nothing came of it beyond the keeping, no wonder shown, no answer given, the fire she built behaving exactly as any fire behaves that is fed dry wood and left to burn, no coal of the vein ever brought to that hillside to be tried against the stone's own bidding, for Mòrnait held, and said so to anyone who asked whether she meant the fire as some trial of the riddle, that the stone had asked only to be remembered and not to be questioned, there being questioning enough already given to the two dark coals by better keepers than herself over more years than she had been alive to count. She built her fires instead the way a woman keeps any old duty that has come to feel, over years of keeping it, less like a duty than like her own: because the ground asked for it, and because a stone that had stood so long bidding anyone at all to remember deserved, she thought, at the least, to see that someone had.

The ring was kept the whole of her own life after, walked to every year she was able, her steps growing stiffer with the walking as the years told on her the way years tell on any woman, until in her own sixty-fifth year she made the walk for what turned out to be the last time, her own household by then grown used to letting her go alone and expecting her back within the fortnight as she had always come back before. The fire she lit as she had always lit it, and sat by it as she had always sat by it, and it is told, by the few who came that year to sit an evening with her before going on their own ways, that she said, watching the flame go down toward the last of its light, that she did not know what the stone remembered, only that she had kept it as well as she knew how, and that whoever came after her would have to find their own way of keeping it if they meant to keep it at all.

She died that same year, at her own hearth at Tobar dhìreach, some weeks after her return from the last walk, of no illness anyone could name beyond the plain wearing-out of a long life, and her own daughter, and after her the daughter's own children, took up the walk to the stone in her stead, less often than Mòrnait herself had kept it, yet often enough that the ring did not go cold in any one man's lifetime after hers, the ash of each new fire laid down over the ash of the last until the ground within the stones held, in its blackened depth, more years of keeping than any single teller could any longer count from the top of it.

Of an evening, in the years after Mòrnait, a traveller crossing that stony ground toward dusk might still come on the ring at the second stone with a fire newly lit and low in it, and a face or two beside it he did not know, keeping, without much to say for themselves beyond the plain fact of the keeping, a fire that answered no riddle and proved no coal and asked, of anyone who sat by it, nothing at all but to remember; and the stone stood over them as it had stood over Mòrnait and over the traveller who first found it before her, plain and grey and three words deep, giving no more account of itself in the last light of any one of those evenings than it had given in the first.
