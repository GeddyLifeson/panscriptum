# Of Cormac of the Misstrike

*VE 3,640 – 3,719*

## I. Of Cormac and the Four Dark Coals

It was going on eight hundred years since Brian had dug his hole beside Leac na Fèithe and found the black coal warm in the morning, and in all that time the line had answered nothing further. Four coals still lay dark in the leather bag: the red-brown, the silver, the one that would wake, if it woke at all, to no more than the snap of a hand, and the rainbow that held every colour and answered to none of them yet. Three had spoken, out of the sea and the height and the ground, each in its own slow year, and the households of the high valleys had grown so used to waiting on the fourth that they had stopped remarking on it, the way a family stops remarking on a debt so old it has become a kind of furniture.

Cormac was born into that waiting at Tobar dhìreach, the eleventh child and the fourth son of a household that had held the bag and Poit an Teine and the recital, father to father, since Neachdan's own well. He was not the eldest, and by the custom of the valley that ought to have kept him at the plough and the herding and let an older brother stand for the line at the hearth on Oidhche an Dealanaich; but his two elder brothers died within a season of each other in his fifteenth year, one of a fever that took half the valley's children that winter and one of a fall on the scree above the black well, and so it came to Cormac, whether he wanted it or not, to learn the recital word for word from his father and to be the one who, when his father died, took the leather bag down from its shelf and untied the thong and counted the four dark coals into his own palm, one by one, the way every keeper before him had counted them.

He was a big man, wide through the shoulders, with a temper that came on him early and that his mother had spent his childhood trying, without much success, to teach him to keep behind his teeth. He never raised his hand to man or beast, and no one in the valley ever called him unkind. But he was quick to anger at a thing that would not go the way he meant it to go, a gate that would not latch, a rope that slipped, a lamb that would not be caught, and he had been known, more than once, to put his fist through a hurdle he had spent the morning building rather than mend the one weak lashing that had let it sag. His wife, Aoibheann, of a household two valleys west, used to say that Cormac could split more stone in an afternoon of temper than another man could split in a week of patience, and that the trick of living with him was never to be standing near the stone.

He grew up on the recital the way every child of the line did, said standing at the hearth on Oidhche an Dealanaich, the fathers named back to Neachdan and Conall, the tale of the boy who walked to the grove and came back with a riddle and no answer, the sea that had taken the gold, the height that had taken the white-flecked coal, the grave beside Leac na Fèithe that had taken the black one slowly, in the dark, overnight. He had heard it so many times before he was grown that he could have said it in his sleep, and did, once, to Aoibheann's laughing report, waking the whole house with the line about Fearghas and the height. It was a story he loved the way a man loves a thing he has never once had to work at loving. What he did not love, what caught at him worse each year he held the bag, was the four dark coals themselves, lying cool and ordinary in his palm at every year's telling, giving nothing back.

He tried, as every keeper before him had tried, the things that were known to have failed: rain, wind, the sea a second time in case the sea had merely been careless the first, the coals together in one fire in case the spirits, once woken, might wake each other. Nothing answered. He tried things no one had thought to try before him, because he was not a patient man and patience was what the line had mostly brought to it until then: he buried the red-brown one a second time, deeper than Brian had buried the black, thinking perhaps the earth wanted more of it; he carried the silver one to three different rivers in one summer, thinking perhaps the Abhainn ghorm alone had been asked and had said no for reasons of its own. He came back from each of these journeys with the coal as dark as he had carried it out, and each time the temper in him grew a little worse before it settled, and Aoibheann learned to have the supper ready and to say nothing until he had eaten.

It was in his thirtieth year, some three years into his keeping, that he came to the idea of the grove. He had heard, the way every child of the line heard it, that Diarmad had gone north to a place where the wind talked and come back with the whole Telling and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich both; that Coimhdeach na Fine had stood there once, in the smoke of an oak-twig fire, and had not been seen since by any teller the line kept. It seemed to Cormac, turning it over through a wet spring while the lambs were born, that a place with that much of Coimhdeach na Fine's business behind it might hold some leaving of whatever the smoke-figure had been, in the ground there, that would answer a coal the way the earth at Tobar dhìreach had answered Brian's. He said so to Aoibheann one evening, expecting her to laugh at him, as she laughed at most of his notions before he had finished having them.

She did not laugh. She looked at him a long while across the fire, the way she looked at a beast she was deciding whether to trust with a lamb, and said only that if he meant to go he had better go once the hay was in, and that she would keep Poit an Teine fed and the bag's thong tied while he was gone, and that he was to come back whether the coal answered him or not, for she had buried two brothers-in-law already and had no wish to add a husband to the count on the strength of a story.

## II. Of the Month Under the Oaks

He set out on a grey morning at the turn of autumn, with the four dark coals wrapped each in its own scrap of hide inside the leather bag, and a second bag of oatcake and dried mutton over his other shoulder, and no company but his own, for he had asked no one to come and no one had offered, the valley holding by then that a man's business with the coal was his own to carry. It was three days' walk to the grove, north and a little west, over ground that grew wilder the further he went from the last hearths he knew: up out of the high valleys onto open moor, down again through birch scrub into a country of standing water and rushes where he lost the best part of a morning finding a way round a bog that had not been there, or that he had not heard tell of, the last time anyone from Tobar dhìreach had gone that way. He slept the first night under an overhang of rock with a small fire of heather roots, the second in the lee of a fallen birch with no fire at all, for the wood was too wet to catch and his temper was too short by then to spend an hour coaxing it. He came to the oaks on the evening of the third day, footsore and hungry and in a worse humour than he had left home in, and stood at the edge of them a while before he went in, the way every teller said Diarmad had stood, though he did not think of that until afterward.

The grove was as the tellers described it and larger than he had pictured it: old oaks, wide-spaced, their crowns meeting high overhead so that the light under them came green and broken, and a stillness in the air that owed nothing to the wind, which he could hear moving in the crowns without ever feeling it move at ground level. He found the fallen trunk the tellers spoke of, or one he took for it, grey and half sunk into the leaf-mould, and made his camp in its lee, and built a small fire there of oak-twigs as Diarmad was said to have built his, more out of habit than hope, for he did not truly expect Coimhdeach na Fine to come for the asking.

It did not come. He sat by the little fire the first evening with the four coals laid out on a flat stone in front of him, watching them, as if watching hard enough were a kind of work, and nothing answered him but the small sounds of the wood settling into night, an owl somewhere off to the west, the creak of a branch finding its own weight. He told himself, that first night, that a place needed time to notice a man in it, the way a dog needed time to notice a stranger was not going to hurt it, and that he would give it a week.

A week became a fortnight, and a fortnight a month. He kept the little fire burning most nights, fed on fallen wood, for the grove gave him that much without complaint, and he ate through his mutton and then through what he could find, the last of the hazelnuts, rowan berries, once a wood pigeon he brought down with a stone after two days of trying. He walked the whole grove end to end more times than he could afterward have counted, laying each of the four coals in turn at the foot of every oak he thought looked older or stranger than the rest, leaving them a night, coming back for them in the morning as dark as he had left them. Thinking the ground here, so near whatever Coimhdeach na Fine had been, might succeed where the ground at Tobar dhìreach had not, he tried burying them again as he had at home; it did not answer him any better. He tried speaking to the grove outright, once, standing in its centre in the grey hour before dawn, asking it plainly what it wanted of him, and felt so foolish afterward, alone under the oaks with his own voice still hanging in the air, that he did not try it again.

The temper that Aoibheann knew so well, and that three years of failed rivers and a second grave had already worn thin, did not improve on a diet of hazelnuts and disappointment. By the third week he had stopped laying the coals out gently at the feet of trees and begun setting them down hard, as a man sets down a thing he is angry at, and by the fourth he had begun to talk to himself in the grove the way another man might talk to a stubborn beast, short words, no patience in them. He knew, some clearer part of him knew, that the coals could not hear him and would not answer him faster for being cursed at, and he went on doing it anyway, because there was no one else in the grove to curse at and a man alone for a month with a failure grows less careful of himself than he would be watched.

It was near the end of the fourth week, with the hay long in at home for all he knew and Aoibheann's patience, he suspected, wearing thinner than his own, that he came to the end of whatever restraint the month had left him.

## III. Of the Misstrike

He had carried, among the little he brought from home, the same maul and stone wedge his father had used for splitting stone, more from habit of a travelling man than from any plan to use them, for there was no well to sink in the grove and no wall to raise. On that last morning, waking stiff and cold and hungry to a fire gone to ash overnight, he took the red-brown coal from its scrap of hide and looked at it a long while, turning it over in his fingers in the grey light, a plain dull stone the colour of old blood dried on wood, giving no more sign of a spirit in it than the pebbles under his boots.

It came to him, in the flat, stubborn way an idea comes to a tired and angry man, that he had never once tried to break the coal open. Every trial the line had made, his own and his father's and his father's father's back to Aodh, had been a trial of where the coal was put, the sea, the height, the ground, the hearth. No one, so far as the recital told it, had ever asked what might be found inside one, whether the spirit, if it was there at all, lay hidden under the surface the way the colour had lain hidden in the vein before the mason's wedge went in and struck it open. It was not a clever thought, and some later part of him, telling it afterward at the recital, allowed as much; it was the thought of a man who had run out of patience and still had a hammer in his hand.

He set the red-brown coal on a flat stone at the foot of the fallen trunk, the same stone he had watched it on every night of the month, took up the maul in his right hand, and struck.

The coal was harder than any stone he had split in his life, harder than the granite of the well his own grandfather's grandfather had sunk, and the maul came down on its curved, unyielding face and turned in his grip, glancing sideways off the surface instead of biting into it, the way an axe turns on a knot no one saw in the wood. He had swung with the whole of a month's temper behind it, not thinking to guard against a miss because he had not truly believed, in the part of him that still believed anything, that the coal could turn a blow struck that hard. The head of the maul came round and down across the back of his left hand, which had been steadying the coal on the stone, and laid it open from the base of the thumb to the first knuckle, deep, to the white of the bone.

He did not cry out. He said afterward that the pain came a breath behind the blow, the way it always does with a true wound, and that in the space of that breath, before the pain had caught him up, he watched his own blood run off his fingers and fall, three drops, four, onto the red-brown coal still sitting where he had set it.

Where the blood touched, the coal caught.

It came up slow at first, a thread of colour spreading out from where the first drop had fallen, the way a stain spreads in cloth, and then all at once, a flame standing up out of the stone, the exact colour of the coal, red-brown, deep, low, not bright the way the gold had burned on the sea or fierce the way the white-flecked coal had burned in the fall through open air, but warm, close, the colour of firelight seen through the grain of oak, or of blood held up to a lamp. No sound came with it, and no spark. It stood there on the flat stone, burning quietly in the green morning light of the grove, while Cormac knelt with his ruined hand still open and dripping in his lap and stared at it.

He said, afterward, that he had no thought in his head for a long while but the plain fact of it burning, and that the pain of his hand, which by every reckoning should have driven every other thought out of him, did not reach him at all in that first stretch of time; he knelt there in the leaf-mould with the blood running unheeded down his wrist and into his sleeve, watching the small red-brown flame stand up out of a coal that had lain dark in a bag on his shoulder, and in his father's before him, and in every father's before that, back thirty generations to Brian, and he did not move, and did not bind the hand, and did not think to.

The other three coals lay forgotten on the ground beside him. He did not touch them again that morning.

## IV. Of the Bound Hand

How long he knelt there he could not afterward say with any certainty, though he guessed, from the light, that it was the better part of the morning. The flame burned steadily the whole while, fed by nothing he could see, taking neither larger nor smaller as the hours went, and it was only when it began at last to sink, guttering low the way any fire sinks when it has burned through what feeds it, that Cormac came back to himself enough to notice his own hand, stiff and swollen and crusted black with dried blood to the elbow, and to understand, with something between shame and wonder, that he had knelt beside a fire lit from his own body for the better part of a day and never once thought to stop the bleeding.

He bound it then, tearing a strip from his shirt and winding it tight over the wound, wincing for the first time since the blow had fallen, as though the pain had been waiting politely for his attention and came on all at once the moment he gave it. By the time he had the binding knotted the flame on the stone had gone out entirely, leaving the coal lying dark again where it had lain, save that its surface, when he picked it up at last with his good hand, felt warm still, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire on it has died.

He gathered the other three coals, and the red-brown one now gone quiet, back into the bag, and broke his camp that same afternoon, though the grove had given him nothing but the one answer and he might, by the letter of what he had set out to prove, have stayed and tried the rest of the summer against the oaks as he had tried the first month. He did not. Telling it after, he said that something in him understood, kneeling there with his hand torn open and the small flame standing over his own blood, that the grove had answered nothing; that whatever had spoken had spoken out of him, out of the wound and not the wood, and that there was no more sense in staying under the oaks to ask the same question of his other hand than there would have been in staying under the roof of any house merely because a good thing had once happened inside it.

The walk home took him four days instead of three, for the hand slowed him and would not let him use it, and twice he had to stop and rebind it when the old binding worked loose and the wound, still raw, began again to weep. He came down into the high valleys on a cold evening with the bag over his shoulder and his left arm held stiff against his chest, and Aoibheann, who had been watching the northern track for the better part of a week without telling anyone she was watching it, saw him coming from a long way off by his walk before she could see his face, and went out to meet him at a run.

He was thirty that year. Standing in the yard before he would go in to the fire, he told her the whole of it: the month, the hazelnuts, the last morning, the blow, the blood, the flame that had burned quietly over his own hand for the better part of a day while he knelt and watched it and forgot, in his wonder, to save the hand at all; and she looked at the binding, already soaked through again, and said nothing about the coal at all, only that he was a great fool and that she would look at that hand properly before he said one more word to anyone.

The scar it left him ran the width of his hand, thick and pale, the bone beneath it never quite straight again, so that from that day he could never again close that fist all the way and had, more than once, to ask another man to draw a stubborn peg for him. He wore it, once the shame of the misstrike had worn off him, the way another man wears a mark he is secretly proud of, and let it be seen, and the valley, which had laughed at Diarmad's question near three thousand years before and long since stopped laughing at anything the line brought home from the grove, did not laugh at Cormac either. What they called him, from that year to the end of his life, was Cormac of the Misstrike, and he answered to it without complaint, saying once, when a nephew asked whether the name shamed him, that a man ought to be glad of any name he is given for having done a thing no one else in the world had ever done, whatever the manner of the doing.

At the recital that was said the next night of the lightning, he stood at the hearth at Tobar dhìreach with the bag counted out before him as every keeper counted it, three cold, one gone quiet after its burning, and held up the scarred hand for the whole gathered valley to see before he spoke the new line into the telling: that the sea had taken the gold, the sky had taken the white-flecked coal, the earth had taken the black slowly in the dark, and now a fourth had answered, quick, out of a man's own blood, the colour of firelight seen through oak. The kindred took to calling it *gual donn-ruadh*, and, more often, simply the coal of life, for it seemed to them, turning the word over at hearth after hearth that winter, the rightest name a coal could have that had wanted nothing of sea or sky or ground, and had wanted only a living man, and the hurt of him.

Cormac kept his keeping some forty years more, the temper in him gentling, Aoibheann said, a little each year, though never so far gentled that she stopped keeping the supper back until he had eaten it in peace. He tried the silver in three more rivers before he gave it over to a younger cousin to try in his stead, and lived to see that trial fail as his own had failed, and to laugh about it, which the valley took as proof enough that the misstrike had done him some good beyond the scarring of one hand. He died in his two-and-seventieth year, at his own hearth, with Poit an Teine burning near him as it had burned near every keeper of the line before him, and Aoibheann, who outlived him by six years, told it afterward that his last clear word, before the fever took his sense from him, was a complaint that his hand ached in the damp, the old scar aching the way old scars do, long after the wound is a story told and not a wound felt.

They buried him near Leac na Fèithe, as Brian had been buried, and the leather bag with its three remaining coals, silver and the one that had never yet been tried by fire and the rainbow that answered to none, passed to a younger kinsman of the line, whose name the recital keeps but does not give to strangers, as it does not give any name between one finder and the next. For some years after, at the hearths above Tobar dhìreach, children of the valley were shown the trick of it, an old scar on an old man's hand held up in the firelight and the story told again, of the coal that would not break and the blood that lit it anyway, until it had passed, the way every wonder passes in a valley that has already had three thousand years of them, into a thing everyone knew and no one any longer marvelled at, save on the one night of the year when the recital brought it round again, and the reciter's hand, whoever then held the office, was raised at the telling of it, empty of any scar, in memory of the one that had once been shown there.
