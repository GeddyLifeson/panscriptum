# Of the Stakes in the Mud and the Watcher in the Sea

*VE 2,937 – 3,146*

## I. Of the Stakes in the Mud

The north-western point ran out low and black into the sea, a spine of rock worn smooth by a tide that came and went twice in a day and left, at its lowest ebb, a wide shelf of mud the colour of a bruise. The households that fished that stretch of coast knew the shelf the way a man knows the floor of his own house in the dark, by feel and by long habit rather than by looking, for it was seldom worth looking at: grey mud, grey stone, grey water going out to a grey sky, and nothing in any of it to hold the eye. They went out on it at the low tides to gather cockle and razor-shell, and to see to their own stakes, the short hazel posts driven into the firmer ground above the mud-line where the coracles were tied of an evening against a rising tide that would otherwise have carried them off to wherever the sea carried things it was given.

It was a boy minding his family's stakes, in the year the households later reckoned as some four generations back from Criomthann's own time, who found the others.

They lay further out than any household drove its own, in a line so straight that no eye could mistake it for anything the tide had arranged by accident: a row of posts, most of them broken off at the mud's surface and a few standing a hand's height proud of it, and caught about the base of one, worked down into the silt until only a corner of it showed, a scrap of hide gone black and stiff as old leather left in a byre. The boy dug it free with his fingers and brought it home, and his father turned it over by the fire that evening and could make nothing of it beyond what his own eyes told him: that it had been stitched once, along one edge, with a neat close seam no different in kind from the seams every boatman on that coast sewed into his own hide boats, though finer than any hand on that shore now living could match.

Whose posts they had been, and what boat or boats they had once held against what tide, no one on that coast could say, then or after. They were not new work when the boy found them; the wood was grey through and through, and the hide had the look of a thing the sea had been working at for longer than any single life could account for. Some of the elders said the posts were old kin's work, driven by grandfathers of grandfathers no one living had known to speak to. Others said, in the low voice a man uses for a thing he does not care to say loudly, that the stakes were not kin's work at all, that no household on that coast had ever driven a mooring so far out into water no coracle of theirs had cause to sit in, and that whoever had rowed there had rowed from somewhere else and gone back to it, or not gone back at all. The mud gave up nothing further to settle it either way. It kept the stakes, half of them still standing after all those generations, and it kept its silence about the rest, and the households of that shore learned, the way they learned most things the mud and the sea would not answer, to let the not-knowing stand rather than fill it with a telling that pretended to more than it had.

They called it only the stakes, after that, when they had cause to speak of it at all, which was seldom; a landmark for the low tide, no more remarked than the black spine of rock itself. Children were shown it once, usually by an older cousin rather than a parent, with a shrug rather than a story, and grew up knowing it the way they knew the shape of the point, without ever quite wondering at it the way a stranger might have wondered. It sat in the mud below the north-western point for as long as anyone's telling ran back, half swallowed, patient, asking nothing and answering nothing, until the year Uilebheist na Mara was first seen from that same stretch of water.

## II. Of the Watcher

The crew that saw it were four men out after mackerel in the grey hour before a summer dawn, working a net between two coracles lashed gunwale to gunwale the way that shore fished a big shoal, with the net paid out between them and the fish already knocking against the hide in numbers enough to make the morning's work a good one. The sea that morning lay flatter than any of the four had known it lie in a lifetime of going out on it, flat and pewter-coloured under a sky not yet decided whether it meant to clear, and it was into that flatness, off to the north where the water ran deep and no boat of that coast had reason to go, that the eldest of the four, a man near fifty who had fished that water since before any of the others were born, happened to look up from the net.

What he saw, he said afterward, more times than he ever wanted to say it again, was a back. Not a wave, not a rock, for there was no rock on any chart the coast kept in its head for that stretch of open water, and not a whale either, though it was the shape his mind reached for first and fastest, because a man's mind reaches always for the shape it already knows. It was too long for a whale and it broke the water wrong, rising without the roll a whale's back gives as it comes up to breathe, rising instead the way a low island might rise if an island could rise, smooth and dark and glistening where the light caught it, and it held above the surface for longer than any breathing thing he had ever watched hold, long enough that he had time to call out to the others and have all three turn and see it too before it went down again, without spray, without sound, the water closing over it as quietly as it had opened.

None of the four could afterward agree on how long it had shown itself, whether the span of ten breaths or the span of a hundred, which the tellers of that coast came in time to take as its own kind of proof, on the reasoning that a true wonder unmakes a man's count of things the way an ordinary sight never does. What they agreed on, all four, without needing to put their heads together over it first, was the size of the thing: bigger, by every man's reckoning, than the biggest whale any of them had brought a story home of, bigger than anything that had business being in water that shallow so close to shore, though none of them could afterward say with any confidence how far out it had lain, for distance on a flat morning sea is a hard thing for even a practised eye to judge.

They rowed home without finishing the net, which no man on that coast did for any lesser cause, and they told what they had seen before the fish they did bring in were so much as gutted, and within the day it had gone from that one household to every hearth within a morning's walk, growing, as such things grow, a little surer and a little larger in every mouth that carried it onward. By the second telling the thing had a name it kept from then on, though none of the four who saw it first ever claimed to have coined it: Uilebheist na Mara, *Uilebheist na Mara*, as though the old women who first fixed the name to it had reasoned, in the space of a single evening's talk, that a thing so patient in its rising and its going under again could only be watching, and had been watching, likely, for longer than any of them had been alive to know it.

Not every hearth believed the four had seen what they said they had seen. There were fishermen on that coast, in that first year and in every year that followed it, who held the whole telling to be nothing more than four tired men and a trick of flat light on deep water, and said so, sometimes to the four men's faces, in the blunt way one fisherman tells another he thinks him a fool without quite using the word. But the four did not change their account under any pressing, not that year and not in all the years after, and one of them, the youngest, carried the telling of it word for word into his own old age and told it to his grandchildren exactly as he had first told it to his own father, without adding to it or taking from it, which the coast came in time to count for more than any argument the doubters brought against it. Believed or doubted alike, the water off the north-western point went, from that year forward, by a name it had never needed before: Uilebheist na Mara's water.

## III. Of the Beacon

Criomthann was born on that shore some five-and-twenty years after the four men saw what they saw, into a household of net-menders and boatmen no different in its trade from any other on that stretch of coast, though different in one particular that shaped him before he had any way of knowing it had: his mother's mother had been kin, at some remove no one troubled any longer to trace exactly, to the household that had once sent a great hull and five men west over the open sea and got back only a dying boy and a telling that was, by the time Criomthann was old enough to hear any of it, kept low and close and spoken of, when it was spoken of at all, at a covered fire rather than an open one. He grew up knowing the shape of that older grief without ever being told the whole of it plainly, the way a child on that coast came to know most things that mattered, by the silences round a subject rather than by anyone sitting him down to explain it, and it left him, from very young, with a caution about open water that some of his cousins mistook for want of nerve and none of them, in the end, ever managed to shame out of him.

He was not, by his own account and by every account his neighbours gave of him afterward, a bold man in any way that coast measured boldness. Never once, in all his years on that shore, did he build boats bigger than the water called for, or express the smallest wish to see what lay past the edge of sight to the west. What he had instead, and had in a measure none of his kin quite matched, was a steadiness that showed itself first in small things: a net mended so that it held a season longer than any other man's, a boy pulled from a rip current by Criomthann's own hand while three grown men still stood arguing on the shingle about the best way to go after him, a hearth kept banked through a winter storm that put out every other fire in the household's row of huts. By the time he was a young man grown, the shore had come to trust him with the kind of trust that is given seldom and taken back almost never, the trust given to a man who does the plain necessary thing before anyone has finished discussing whether it needs doing.

He was a score of years old, married a year and with his first child not yet walking, when the second of the great losses came to that shore, though it did not yet come from the water Uilebheist na Mara had been seen in. A boat working close in below the point, in weather no worse than any boat on that coast went out in twice a week, went over in a swell that rose from nowhere any of the watchers on shore could afterward account for, and two men drowned within sight of their own hearth smoke, in water shallow enough that a third man, hauled out half-drowned himself, stood afterward and could point to the very patch of sea where his companions had gone under. It was not laid to Uilebheist na Mara, not by anyone, for the water was the wrong water and the drowning had the ordinary shape of any drowning that coast had known before the four men's telling ever began; but it happened in a year when Uilebheist na Mara's telling was still young enough on that shore to colour everything, and Criomthann, standing on the shingle with the rest of the household watching the third man dragged in, found himself thinking, not for the first time and not for the last, that the sea gave a man very little warning of the exact moment it meant to take something from him, and that a warning given late was no better than no warning at all.

He said as much to his wife that night, by their own hearth, with the child asleep between them, and she asked him what warning a man could give the sea that the sea would trouble itself to answer to. He had no answer for her then. It was another seven years before he had one, and when it came it came, by his own telling of it afterward, less as a thought arrived at than as a thing he found himself already doing, the way a man sometimes finds his hands at work on a task before his mind has finished deciding to begin it.

What he began, on the last headland of that coast, the one that stood highest and looked furthest out over the water where the four men had seen the back rise, was a fire.

It was not, at first, meant as anything more than what any fire on any headland might have been meant as: a mark for boats working late to steer home by, no different in kind from a dozen such fires kept on a dozen headlands the length of the island, wherever a household wanted its boats to find the shore in failing light. Criomthann fed it himself, most evenings, climbing the last steep pitch of the headland with an armful of driftwood and gorse after his own day's work was done, and for the first season or two no one on that coast thought it any more remarkable than a careful man's habit. It was only in the second year, when he began to keep it burning through the daylight hours as well, in fair weather when no boat needed a mark to find its way home by, that his own kin began asking him plainly what the fire was for.

He told them, when he told them at all, in few words, and the words changed from telling to telling while the sense of them held steady: that a man who watched water long enough came to feel, in his own body before his mind had properly reasoned it out, where the water changed its temper, and that the stretch off that headland, ever since the four men's telling, had felt to him like water that wanted watching rather than water that wanted trusting. He never once claimed to have seen Uilebheist na Mara himself, in all the years the fire burned, and when pressed on it he always said the same thing: that he had not needed to see it to believe four men who had no reason between them to agree on a lie, and that a fire cost him little enough to keep, set against what it might, one day, be found to have saved.

The word he settled on to go with the fire, the word he began calling down to any boat that worked too far out into that stretch of water, came to him worn smooth over several seasons, the way a saying is worn smooth by repeated use, until only the truest, shortest form of it was left standing. By the time he had said it enough evenings running that his own children could say it back to him in their sleep, it had settled into the few short words every household on that coast came in time to know as well as they knew Aithris a' Chruthachaidh itself:

> *Tionndaidh air ais. Chan ann dhutsa an t-uisce seo.*
> Turn back. This water is not for you.

He called it down from the headland to any boat he judged had strayed too far into Uilebheist na Mara's water, in a voice pitched to carry over wind and surf both, a voice his own sons said they could still hear plainly enough asleep in their own beds a full night's memory away. Most boats that heard it turned without argument, for a warning shouted from a headland by a man known the length of that coast for steadiness rather than fancy was not a warning easily laughed off, whatever a man might privately think of Sea-Watchers and old women's tellings. A few did not turn, in those first years, and came to no harm for the not turning, which some on that coast took as proof the whole fear was overblown and others took as no proof of anything at all, since a warning given need not be answered by disaster every time it goes unheeded in order to be a true warning.

The fire burned on. Criomthann tended it himself for close on forty years, through every season and every weather that coast gave him, adding wood and gorse and, in the leanest winters, peat cut specially for the purpose from a bank his own household kept aside and touched for nothing else, so that the fire on the last headland was never once let go dark in the whole span of his tending it. He was three-and-sixty, and had been a widower nine years by then, his own wife carried off by a fever that took half the young children of that shore with her in the one bad season, when his own strength began, at last, to fail the climb; and it was his eldest son, and after him his son's son, who kept the fire fed from the year Criomthann's legs would no longer carry the wood up the last steep pitch, so that the flame he had first lit for no reason he could fully put into words burned on above the water long past the reach of his own life.

He died at four-and-sixty, in his own bed, with three generations of his own kin about him, having outlived, by then, every one of the four men whose telling had first sent him up that headland with an armful of driftwood in his arms. His own asking was to be buried on the lower slope of the headland itself, apart from the household's other dead, within sound, his sons said afterward, of the fire he had kept; and the fire went on being kept above him, generation folding into generation the way fire on that coast was always kept, fed by hands that had never seen Uilebheist na Mara and never once doubted that there was good cause to feed it.

## IV. Of the Widows of Ros ruadh

It was Criomthann's grandson who was tending the fire, some years into his own turn at the keeping of it, when the season came that the whole of that coast afterward reckoned the worst it had known.

It began, as the worst seasons on that coast most often began, in weather that gave no warning at all of what it meant to take. A calm settled over the water in early summer of the kind fishermen prayed for and seldom got in full measure, day after day of flat grey-green sea and a wind too light to trouble a coracle's balance, the sort of run of weather that filled a household's stores for a whole winter if a crew worked it hard enough while it lasted. The boats of Ros ruadh, a fishing settlement some way down the coast from the headland where the fire burned, close enough that its own boats had heard Criomthann's call turned back more than once in his lifetime and his grandson's after him, went out harder that season than any season in living memory, for a calm like that one was not a thing a fishing household let go to waste.

The first boat did not come back from an evening's work that any of the watchers on shore could afterward point to as different, in any particular they could name, from a hundred evenings before it. It had gone out with three men aboard, working water well inside what any household on that coast called safe, in sight of the shore for most of the day, and it simply did not come home with the tide, and no wreckage of it, then or ever after, washed up on any shingle the length of that coast for anyone to find. A second boat went the same way eleven days later, from a different household and a different stretch of that same calm water, with two men aboard this time, and again no wreckage, again no storm any watcher on shore had marked, again only the tide coming in as it always came in and the boat that should have ridden it home simply not among the boats it brought.

By the time the seventh boat failed to return, in a season that had by then gone on calm for longer than any fisherman on that coast could remember calm holding, there was no household left at Ros ruadh untouched by it, for seven boats lost from one settlement in one season, out of the handful that settlement kept afloat at any time, meant there was scarcely a hearth in the place that had not lost a father, a son, a brother, or all three together, to water that showed no storm, gave up no bodies, and left no boat's timber behind to say what had taken it.

The widows of that season were the ones who first said, plainly and at every hearth that would let them say it, what half the coast had until then only thought and not spoken: that Uilebheist na Mara had taken them. It was not the four men's telling, grown old and half-doubted by then in some mouths, that gave the widows their certainty; it was the plain fact, turned over at every hearth in Ros ruadh through that whole terrible summer, that seven boats crewed by careful men working water they knew like their own hands had gone under, one after another, in a season with no storm in it to blame, in water that lay within a morning's row of the stretch a headland fire had been kept burning against for two generations by then. What else, the widows asked, and asked in a way that was less a question than a naming, could take seven boats from calm water and leave nothing behind to say how, if not the very thing the fire on the headland had been lit to warn against in the first place.

It was the first time on that whole coast that a loss had been laid at Uilebheist na Mara's door by name rather than only feared in its shadow, and once said it was not unsaid again. The tellers who carried the telling onward in the years after gave the widows themselves the naming of it, and said, more than one of them, that a fear kept general and nameless for two generations had needed only one bad season, and the plain grief of women who had buried nothing because there was nothing left to bury, to become a fear with a name fixed to it for good.

The fire on the headland burned harder that year than in any year since Criomthann first lit it, fed, the tellers say, not only by his grandson's hands but by kin from three households besides, men and women who climbed the headland of an evening with what wood they could spare, as though a fire fed by more hands might somehow answer for what seven boats' worth of careful seamanship had not been able to answer for. It went on being fed, year on year after that season, long past any living memory of the seven who had been lost in it, banked by night and built up by day, tended by hands that had themselves been born after the worst of that summer and grown up knowing the fire only as a thing that had always burned there and always would, until its ash, heaped and shifted and heaped again on the same patch of ground for lifetime after lifetime, stood a man's own height deep on the headland where it was kept, deep enough that a boy sent up to feed it could stand in the hollow of it and be seen from below only from the shoulders up.

Of the seven boats themselves nothing further was ever told. No wreck came ashore in any season after to close the account, no drowned man's body was ever found on any shingle to be carried home and buried where the rest of Ros ruadh's dead were buried, and the coast, having no better answer to give and having by then a name it trusted to stand for whatever the true answer was, let the not-knowing settle the way it had let the stakes in the mud settle two generations before, without pretending afterward to know more of it than it did. What Ros ruadh kept, in place of graves it had no bodies to fill, was the empty places on its own shingle where seven boats had once been drawn up of an evening among all the rest, spaces the households marked for years after by habit rather than by any need, walking round them at the tide's edge the way a man walks round a grave he does not mean to stand on, until the shingle itself, shifted by enough winters' tides, no longer held even the memory of where the seven boats had lain, and only the fire on the headland, and the telling the widows had first given a name to, remained to say that they had ever gone out from that shore at all.
