# Of the Counting of the Fathers

*VE 890 – 950*

## I. Of the Telling at the Hearth

He came down into Tobar dhìreach on the evening of the third day with mud to the knee and his feet gone past aching into a kind of numbness he no longer noticed, and the household that had watched him walk north with a stone in his shoe of a question came out to watch him walk south with something in his face none of them had a name for. His mother reached him first, and old as her eyes had gone she saw before she asked that he had not found what he went for, and something else besides, and she put both hands to his face the way she had done when he was small and fevered, to feel whether the heat in him was sickness or only walking.

"What did you find," Fionnghuala said, the words shaped less to draw an answer out of him than to steady the hand that held his face.

"I found what there was to find," Diarmad said, and said no more of it that night, even to her. He sat by the fire eating what was put in front of him without tasting it, gual bogha-froise still wrapped against his chest under his cloak where it had ridden the whole way, until the household had gone one by one to their own sleep and only his mother sat up with him in the low light, waiting the way she had always waited, without pressing.

It was two nights more before he told it, and when he told it he told it standing, the way a man stands to say a thing he means the house to keep. He had thought, on the road home, that he would tell it the way it had been told to him, but he found before he was three sentences in that no mouth could carry the whole of what had come into him that night in the grove, the sparks that did not fall and the sea with no land in it and the grass-people blinking up out of the heather, not in an evening, not in a hundred evenings; so he told instead the bones of it, the making of the island out of drops of fire let fall from a broken coal held over grey water, the forgotten gift buried in the ground and dug up and thrown away by a valley of grass-people who did not know what they held, and the smoke shape that had stood across a small fire from him in a grove three days north and told him all of it in a single long night without once sitting down. He told them Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich last, because it was the only part of it he had by heart word for word, having said it over to himself the whole road south until the strange tongue and the plain tongue had worn a single path through him: the sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit; merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

There was a silence after it of the kind a house keeps after a death, and then someone at the back, one of the young men of Tadhg's line by marriage who had never once in his life let a chance for laughter go by unused, said that Diarmad had walked three days into an empty grove and talked to the wind, and the wind, being an accommodating sort of listener, had told him whatever he most wanted to hear; and the laughter that followed was not unkind, exactly, it was the same laughter the valley had always kept ready for Diarmad's question, worn smooth from years of use, the laughter of people glad to have their world handed back to them the shape it had always been. Diarmad did not argue it. He had expected the laughter, had walked three days home turning over how he would meet it, and he only said that he had told them what he had been given to tell, and that they might keep it or let it go as they pleased, and sat down again by the fire, and let the talk move on to other things the way talk always does when it has worn out the one thing it came for.

It was his wife who would not let it go, though not, at first, in his favour. Niamh had married him four years before, a strong-handed woman of a hearth two valleys east who had heard of Diarmad's question long before she ever met the man who carried it, and had thought, in the way of a young woman weighing a match, that a man known the length of the high valleys for a single stubborn foolishness might at least be a man of some substance under the foolishness, which had proved true enough in the small ways a marriage tests it, in patience and in temper and in the work of two hands against a hard year. But she had not married a man who talked with the smoke of the dead, and when he told her, alone, what he had not told the whole hearth, the telling in full this time, sparks and grass-people and the shame he had felt for a valley of grass-people he had never met, she heard him out to the end and then said, plainly, that three days alone on an empty moor with too little food and too much cold in his blood could put a great many things into a man's head that had no business being in it, and that she loved him, and that she did not believe a word of it.

He did not press her either. He had learned, in twenty years of asking one question to a valley that had no answer for it, that pressing only wore a thing smoother without changing its shape underneath, and he let her keep her unbelief the way he let the young men of Tadhg's line keep their laughter, and went on saying Aithris a' Chruthachaidh at the hearth, a little more of it each time as he found the words for pieces of it he had not had words for the first night, until there were nights when even Niamh sat and listened without leaving the fire, arms folded, her face giving nothing away, and other nights when she did not.

## II. Of the Fathers' Names

What changed the house was a second thing Diarmad set himself to beside Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, in the second year after his return, a thing that asked for no belief at all, only memory. He began going hearth to hearth among the kin, the near kin and every household further out besides, from the high valleys to the northern moor, any that could claim so much as a grandmother married into the line, and at each one he asked the same plain question he had once asked about the coal, save that this one had an answer if only enough mouths could be found to hold it between them: who was your father, and who was his, and how far back could the house say the names before the names ran out into "an old man" and "before my grandmother's time" and silence.

It was slow work and unglamorous, nothing like a night in a gale with a god's smoke across the fire from him, and for that very reason it won him a hearing Aithris a' Chruthachaidh itself had not. A man who talks to spirits in a grove is easy enough to laugh at over a fire in company; a man who sits at your own hearth of an evening and asks patiently after your grandfather's grandfather, and writes nothing down because there is no writing to be had, only carries the names away whole in his own head the way a fire-bearer carries an ember in a pot of moss, is doing a kind of work every house in the valleys understood and most of them, once they thought about it, wanted done. Old women who had never once troubled themselves over Diarmad's question about the coal turned out to have kept, unasked, four and five names of fathers back, the way a house keeps a worn tool it has stopped using but cannot bring itself to throw out, and were glad enough of someone willing to take the names off their hands and carry the weight of remembering them onward.

He worked at it three years, off and on, between the ordinary work of a household that still had to be fed and warmed whatever a man's head was full of, and at the end of it he could stand at his own hearth and say, without a pause anywhere in it, the whole of the line back to Neachdan who broke the vein and Conall who carried it burning to the first hearth beyond his own, father and father and father, thirty-six names in all, and the few deeds that were remembered with them, Conall's watch over the burning vein and Leac na Fèithe laid over it in the days of his sons' sons, on down to his own father's father's name and his own; and where a house's memory ran out before it reached him, he said so plainly, rather than filling the gap with a name invented to make the line look longer than it was, which his mother told him, when he asked her once whether he should, was the one thing that would make the whole of it worth nothing at all.

He fixed the saying of it, in the end, as a thing said once a year, standing, on the darkest night, rather than told loosely by the fire on whatever evening someone thought to ask for it, the night every household in the high valleys already kept in its own smaller way as the night Teine a' Chlachair first came into the world; and it was on that night, in the year that made five years since his walk to the grove, that he stood at his own hearth with the whole household gathered and every kin-house that could reach them in one evening's walk besides, and said the fathers' names from Neachdan to himself without a stumble, and after him, because he asked it of her before he began and she had not said no, Niamh stood and said Aithris a' Chruthachaidh after him. She had not come to believe it; she said afterward only that a house ought to hear the whole of what its head carried, belief or none, and that a wife who would not say a hard thing for her husband when he asked it of her once in a marriage was no wife worth the name. The recital had no name that night beyond "the fathers' names" or "the Line," said plainly, and the name it bears, Sloinneadh a' Chlachair, came to it only slowly, as names come; but the shape of it was set that night and never afterward broken: the fathers named standing, on Oidhche an Dealanaich, in the order they had lived, with no name added that a house could not answer for.

## III. Of the Trials That Failed

Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich did not sit idle in him all those years, though it did no better for the waiting. He had come home from the grove with six coals still dark in the leather bag at his hearth, the black and the fire-red and the snowflake white and the silver and the red-brown, none of them tried even once against their own element the way the rainbow had been tried against sky and earth and buried ground alone in the grove, and it seemed to him, turning the words over through those same years he spent gathering names, that a riddle which spoke of three spirits and their merging could hardly be answered by one man trying one coal alone, and that the line owed it more than his own two hands could give while those two hands were also busy carrying names from hearth to hearth.

So when the kin gathered for the standing of the Line, in the years after that first telling of it, they tried the coals as well, together, the whole bag of seven emptied out onto the flat stone before the fire while half the valley watched. They put four of the dark ones into a driving rain one autumn, laid out bare on the hillside through a night of it, on the thought that rain was as much the sky's water as the sea's, and found them in the morning only wet and cold and themselves, no different for the soaking than any stone left out in weather. They carried the bag up onto the ridge above Tobar dhìreach on a night the wind came hard and steady off the moor, thinking that if earth and sea each had a spirit to answer to, the sky's own spirit might answer to nothing gentler than its own true weather, and held the coals up bare-handed into the gale until their arms ached with the cold of it, and the wind went through their fingers and around the coals and past them into the dark, indifferent as wind always is to what it passes over. All seven went into the one fire together another year, on the reasoning that if merging was the word of Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, perhaps it meant only that the coals themselves must be merged, thrown into one flame rather than kept apart in the bag or tried alone one at a time; they burned down black and ordinary together exactly as each had burned alone at every hearth since the mason first fed his own fire from the vein, no brighter for the company. And because word of Oisean's boat and Scàire's skerries and the salt taste of the wider sea had reached even so far inland as Tobar dhìreach by then, Diarmad sent to the south shore for a skin of true sea-water, carried the long way home on a man's back and poured over the coals in a stone basin at the hearth itself, the nearest any of them could come, that far from any coast, to trying the sea without walking to it; and the sea-water did to the coals exactly what the rain had done, wetted them and left them cold, and was tipped out afterward onto the ground the way any spent water is tipped, and soaked away into the same earth that had already refused the black coal its own trial twice over.

None of it worked, and none of it surprised him, by the third or fourth failure, though he did not stop trying for that. He came, in those years, to a kind of patience with Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich that was not quite belief that the answer was near and not quite despair that it would never come, a patience his mother, before she died in the second year after the first standing of the Line, told him was the only sensible way to hold a question too large for one lifetime to finish: to carry it the way a man carries a full pot on a long road, minding it without staring at it every step, and letting it crush none of the ordinary days under it. Fionnghuala died in her own bed with Poit an Teine within reach of her hand, as she had asked, and was buried the way the valley buried its own, and Diarmad took up the keeping of the pot himself, that year, alongside the keeping of the names and the keeping of Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, three weights on the one back, and carried all three without once, in any hearth's telling of him afterward, being heard to complain of the load.

## IV. Of the Telling's Going Out

Aithris a' Chruthachaidh itself, meanwhile, had begun to do what no argument of Diarmad's could do for it: it travelled. A fire-bearer who had heard it three times at Tobar dhìreach on his rounds carried it, unasked, to a hearth on the northern moor where he stopped each winter to trade, and told it there in his own halting way, missing pieces and mixing others, so that the version that reached the moor had the sparks becoming stars but lost the grass-people entirely and gained, from somewhere in the fire-bearer's own head, a line about the god's laughter that Diarmad, hearing it come back to him years later from a traveller who had it from the moor in turn, did not recognise as his own telling at all until the laughter itself gave it away. He did not correct it. He had learned by then that a telling, once it left his own mouth, belonged as much to the mouth that carried it onward as to the one that first shaped it, the way a coal passed hand to hand at a fire-begging keeps the shape of the pot that last held it more than the shape of the hearth it came from, and that a thing told at every hearth from the high valleys to the northern moor could not, and perhaps should not, come back sounding exactly as it went out.

It went east as well, slower, because the eastern farms traded less with Tobar dhìreach than the moor did and had their own tellings already, of deer and dogs and the salmon runs, that made room grudgingly for a new one. But it went, carried by traders who came up for salt and hides and went home with more than they had bargained for, and by the thirtieth year after Diarmad's walk to the grove there was hardly an eastern hearth that had not heard some shape of it, sparks or grass-people or Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich itself said wrong in a dozen small ways and right in the one way that mattered, that the coal held a gift no one had yet found how to open. Diarmad heard, once, from a trader newly down from the east, a version of his own riddle so garbled that the sea and the sky had traded places in it and the earth had dropped out entirely, replaced by a line about fire answering to fire that made no sense at all set against what he himself had heard in the grove; and he sat a long while after the trader left, turning that over, not troubled by the wrongness of it so much as struck by how far a true thing could travel and still be carried, however badly, by people who had never doubted for a moment that it was worth the carrying.

He grew old carrying all of it, the names and the pot and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich and the slow spread of Aithris a' Chruthachaidh east and west of him, and the household watched him go from the man who had walked north at seven-and-twenty with one question and come home with a longer one, to a man they no longer laughed at even the young men of Tadhg's line, though few enough of them, in the end, said outright that they believed him. Niamh believed him at last in the tenth year after his return, though she never told him so directly; she told their daughter instead, one evening when she thought Diarmad out of hearing, that a woman could live a long while beside a strange thing without believing it and then find, on some ordinary evening with no sign to mark it, that she had come to believe it without ever once deciding to, the way a path comes to lie worn into a hillside without any single footstep being the one that made it a path; and Diarmad, who had in fact been within hearing the whole time, said nothing of it to her that night or ever after, and kept what he had overheard the way he kept the coals, quietly, and did not try to open it further.

## V. Of Diarmad's Death and the Daughter Who Kept It

He died in his seventieth year, an old man by the reckoning of the high valleys though no age at all beside Coimhdeach na Fine's long night, sitting up in his own bed with Poit an Teine near enough to touch and the leather bag of six dark coals and one that would not stay any colour long enough to be named laid on the floor beside him, and the last thing the household heard him say, to Niamh, who sat with him through that last night as she had sat up with him through so many nights of trying and failing before it, was that he was sure now the answer was near, nearer than it had ever been in all the years of the trying, though he could not, when she asked him plainly what he meant by it, say what had brought the nearness home to him beyond a feeling he had no better word for than the feeling a man gets on a long road when the smoke of a known hearth first shows over a rise he has not yet crested. He was not able to say whether he meant Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich would be answered in his son's time or his son's son's, or only that a question carried long enough and honestly enough could not, by its own weight and the weight of everyone who had come to carry it alongside him, go forever unanswered; and Niamh, who had spent forty years learning not to press him for more than he had to give, did not press him for that either, and let him die with the nearness unexplained, the way she had once let the whole of Aithris a' Chruthachaidh itself sit unbelieved in her for ten years before it settled.

It fell, in the end, to his daughter to keep Aithris a' Chruthachaidh whole, where his sons had not. Deirbhile was thirty-seven when her father died, old enough by then to have heard Aithris a' Chruthachaidh said at the hearth every darkest night of her own remembered life and young enough still to have the whole of it by heart in a way her older brothers, busy with the herding and the ordinary work of the household, had never troubled to make theirs; and where her father had told it a little differently each time, finding new words for pieces that had never quite settled into words the first time, Deirbhile set herself, almost from the week of his burial, to saying it the same way every time, word for word, the sparks and the grass-people and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich in the exact order and the exact phrases her father had used in the last years of his telling it, on the reasoning she gave her own children when they asked her why she was so strict about it: that a telling which changed with every mouth that carried it would, given enough mouths and enough years, end up saying nothing at all that the first mouth had meant, the way a coal passed round a whole valley of hearths comes home, if it comes home at all, cold and unrecognisable; and that if her father's question was ever to be answered by anyone, that someone would need the words as he had first been given them, not as forty years of retelling had worn them.

She taught it the same exacting way she kept it, sitting each of her own children down in turn once they were old enough to hold a long telling in their heads without losing the thread of it, and making them say it back to her a section at a time until they had it as clean as she did, and she taught it, besides her own children, to any of the wider kin who would sit still for the whole of it and promise to keep it as strictly as she asked; and by the sixtieth year after Diarmad's walk to the grove, there was no longer any single mouth in the high valleys that alone held Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, but a scattering of them, cousins and cousins of cousins, each one able to say it clean from the first spark to the last plain line of Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, so that the thing Diarmad had carried home alone on his own two feet from an empty grove now walked the valleys on a dozen pairs of legs at once and could not, any longer, be lost by the death of any single one of them.

## VI. Of the Telling at an Eastern Hearth

It was said, that year, at a hearth three days' walk east of Tobar dhìreach, by a woman who had never met Deirbhile and had it instead from her own mother, who had it from an aunt who had sat once, as a girl, at Deirbhile's own knee and taken the whole of it down clean; three mouths removed from the keeper who had fixed it, and further still from the mouth of Diarmad himself, and further again from the grey shape that had first given it, word for word, across a small fire in a grove no one at that eastern hearth had ever seen or would ever see. The woman said it standing, on the darkest night of that year, to a household that kept no fire-pot and no bag of coloured coals and no claim at all on Sloinneadh a' Chlachair beyond the bare fact of being kin to kin to kin who had once married into it three generations back, and she said it the way Deirbhile had taught her mother to say it, the sparks that did not fall, the grass-people blinking up out of the heather, the sky and the earth and the sea each carrying their own spirit, and the words she gave last, because they were the words she herself had been given last and had never once heard explained: merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

A child at that hearth, hearing it for what he could not have known was the first time in his own short life though it would not be the last, asked the plain question every child who ever heard it seemed, sooner or later, bound to ask, the same question that had once sent a boy of seven at Tobar dhìreach out to badger every fire-bearer and elder and traveller within a day's walk of his own hearth: what did it mean, to merge a spirit. The woman who had told it did not know, no more than her mother had known, no more than the aunt three mouths back had known, no more, in the honest end of it, than Diarmad himself had known on the last night of his life with the nearness of the answer sitting unexplained in him like a smoke he could smell but not yet see the fire of; and she told the child, as her own mother had told her when she asked the same thing at the same age, that it was a question for An Clachair's own line to answer, if any line on the whole island ever answered it, and that a household three days east of Tobar dhìreach had only to keep the words themselves whole and hand them on, the way a fire is handed on in a pot of moss from a hearth that lit it to a hearth that did not, asking of the coal only that it stay lit long enough to be carried, and trusting that whoever received it at the far end would know, better than the bearer ever could, what to do with the fire once it arrived.
