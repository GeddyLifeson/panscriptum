# Of the Gatherings and the Rows of the Dead

*VE 4,951 – 5,169*

## I. Of the Crowd at the Column

The saying at Calbh Cnoc ghorm had been a thing spoken to whoever stood near enough to hear it said, in the years after the column was first raised: a shepherd passing, a household come to leave an offering at the older stones nearby, a handful of children sent to fetch water from the spring below the hill who lingered to hear an old man say the words over because the words pleased them, whether or not the sense of them had yet come home. By the time Seathan's snap had been told a few winters over, that had stopped being true. Word of gual teine's waking in Seathan's hand had gone out from Tobar dhìreach faster and further than any telling before it, carried by kin visiting kin and by traders who had no kin in the matter at all but liked a good tale on the road, and it reached Achadh a' Chalbh already grown in the telling, so that some who heard it there believed the fire had come down a second time out of a clear sky rather than out of a tired man's own hand on his own doorstone. Whichever way they had heard it, they came to the column to hear the saying said properly, by someone who could be trusted to have it right, and they did not come one or two at a time any longer.

They came in tens, and then, on the nights the reciters chose for the telling, in what the households of Achadh a' Chalbh afterward judged to be some hundreds, walking in from every quarter of the hill country and camping about the stone for a night or two before they walked home again. No one had built anything at Calbh Cnoc ghorm before that; the column had stood alone on its rise with the grass growing up to its foot and nothing near it but the older markers that no one now living could say the meaning of. The first shelters went up in the year the crowd first overran the grass around the stone and trampled it to mud in a wet spring, low walls of turf and stone roofed with branches and hide, built by whoever had walked furthest and meant to stay the longest, and taken down again when the telling was over because no one yet thought of the column as a place a person lived. They were built again the next year the crowd came, on the same ground, because it was easier to raise a wall where a wall had stood before than to choose new ground and clear it; and within a lifetime there were shelters enough about the rise, rebuilt so many times over on the footings of the ones before them that a household coming for the first time could see, in the worn hollows and the packed earth between the walls, that other households had stood there before it and would stand there after.

The telling changed with the crowd, though no one who told it would have said they had changed anything. Where a single household's fire had once heard the recital from whichever elder of the line was nearest, the crowd at Achadh a' Chalbh wanted a voice that could be heard past the first ring of listeners, and got, in time, a manner of telling suited to it: slower, with a pause held after each father's name long enough for those at the back to catch it passed forward from mouth to mouth, and a way of standing on the rise above the column, with the crowd below it, that no single hearth had ever needed. Clann na Ceiste sent one of its own to Achadh a' Chalbh for the great tellings now, in the years after Seathan, a duty that fell in turn to whichever of the line could be spared from Tobar dhìreach and had the voice and the patience for it; the recital says only that it fell first to a nephew of Seathan's, a man the reciters keep among the names they do not give to strangers, and that he stood on the rise at the telling that named gual teine among the six already lit and told it, as Seathan had told it at his own hearth, without dressing the matter up beyond the bare truth of a man breaking coal-dust with his fingers on a warm evening.

He was a young man for the charge, by his own later account, and had stood the whole day before the telling watching the shelters fill and the cook-fires start up across the trampled ground, sick with a fear that had nothing to do with the words themselves, which he had known since boyhood, and everything to do with the number of faces he would have to say them to. An old woman of Achadh a' Chalbh, who had walked to every telling at the column since she was a girl and meant, she said, to walk to this one though her knees no longer thanked her for it, found him sitting apart from the fires with his hands pressed together between his knees, and asked him plainly whether he meant to lose his supper before he had given the hill country its telling. He told her he had never spoken the recital to more than a household's worth of listeners in his life, and that Seathan's telling belonged, by rights, to Seathan's own kin at Seathan's own hearth, not to a hillside of strangers who would carry it home and change it in the carrying. She said, and the recital keeps her words as it keeps few from outside the line, that a story kept in one hearth was safe from changing and safe from nothing else, and that a story given to a hillside might come back altered in a hundred mouths but would not, this way, die in the one mouth that first held it. He stood on the rise the next evening and gave the telling, and did not lose his supper, and the crowd, by every account that came down from it, wept a little at that telling as the old women of the line had wept at Tobar dhìreach, and went home in the days after and told it themselves at their own hearths with such changes, large and small, as every telling gathers in the mouths of those who were not there to hear it first.

By the middle years of that century the gathering at Achadh a' Chalbh had become, without anyone deciding that it should, a thing the hill country reckoned its year by nearly as much as it reckoned by the seasons: a night when households that never otherwise met walked toward the same rise from every quarter of the country, slept a night or two in walls that had stood, in one shape or another, longer than any one of them could remember building them, and heard a name spoken that every hearth in the hill country now knew before it left home. Some among the older households said the column had grown too crowded for its own good, and that a saying was worn thin by so much handling; more said nothing against it at all, and came again the next time it was told, and brought their children so that the children too could say they had stood under the stone and heard it.

## II. Of the Rainbow Passed Hand to Hand

In one of those years, at the great telling at the column, the keeper of the bag who had come down from Tobar dhìreach to say the Line did a thing no keeper had done before him at any gathering: when the fathers had been named and the six lightings told, and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich said, he untied the thong of the bag and took out gual bogha-froise and gave it into the hand of the woman standing nearest him in the front of the crowd, and told her to look at it and pass it on.

He had not told anyone at Tobar dhìreach that he meant to. He said afterward that he had not known he meant to until the coal was in his hand, and that it had come to him, standing on the rise over so many faces, that the coal had been kept in a bag on a peg in one house for longer than anyone could count, and tried by the hands of that house and no other, and that there were hundreds of hands on that hillside that had never touched it, and that it did not seem to him a thing any keeper could be sure of, that the coal would not answer some hand that had never been let hold it.

So it went round. It went from the woman in the front to her husband and from him to a boy beside him, and on through the crowd in the dark by the light of the fires, hand to hand, each person holding it a little while and turning it so that the firelight ran the colours across its face, gold and green and a blue that no sky wore, and passing it on. Some held it a long while and some hardly a breath. Some said something over it, low, and some said nothing. An old man who had walked four days to be there held it against his forehead with his eyes shut, and would not say afterward what he had asked of it. A girl of five, lifted up by her father to take it, put it in her mouth before anyone could stop her, and took it out again, and said it tasted of nothing, and was laughed at, not unkindly, by everyone near enough to hear.

It took the better part of the night to go round the whole crowd. The keeper stood on the rise the whole while and did not sit down, watching for it in the dark, following it from fire to fire by the stir it made where it passed; and near dawn it came back to him, carried up the rise by the same woman who had first taken it, who had gone round the crowd behind it to see that it was not lost. He took it from her and looked at it. It was as dark as it had been when he untied the thong, and as cold.

He put it back in the bag. He said, when he stood to speak again in the grey light, that the coal had been held that night by more hands than had held it in all the years since the mason, and had answered none of them, and that he did not know whether to be sorry for it or glad; for it seemed to him that a coal which would not answer to a whole hillside of hands asking it together, each in its own way, was not a coal that wanted asking at all, in the way the line had always asked, but something else, which he could not name. The crowd went home in the morning and told it at their own hearths, how they had held the seventh coal in their own hands and it had done nothing; and it was told for a long while after, by those who had been there, as the one night of their lives on which they had held the mason's vein, and it did not seem to any of them, telling it, that the coal's silence made the holding any less.

## III. Of the Burial Ground No One Had Made

Two days' walk and more to the south-east of the column, near the southern shore, on rising ground above a burial hill the hill country called Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, the dead of that country had for longer than any telling could account for been laid in the ground without ceremony that any teller thought worth keeping. No one living could say who had first chosen that hill over any other for the purpose, or why; the graves nearest the crown of it were older than the oldest names that could still be read there, and no name at all could be read there, for no hand on the island yet cut a name into stone for the dead any more than it cut one for the living. The thorn grew thick over the older mounds, and the households nearest the hill let it grow, holding that ground once given to the dead was not ground to be cleared again for any other use, though none of them could have said which ancestor had first laid down that rule, or whether it had ever been laid down in words at all rather than simply done, once, by someone whose reasons died with him, and kept ever after because no one saw cause to do otherwise.

The dead of the hill country went into that ground in the plainest way the households knew: wrapped in a cloak or a hide, laid on their side with the knees drawn up as though asleep, and covered over without marker or word beyond whatever was said at the graveside by whoever had loved them, which the reciters at Achadh a' Chalbh never kept and never claimed to keep, holding that a hearth's own grief belonged to the hearth and not to the column's telling. A few of the dead went into the ground with something of their own beside them, a knife worn smooth with handling, a comb, a string of shells from the western shore that had come to the hill country by trade a generation back and further; most went with nothing at all but the cloak they were wrapped in, for the hill country did not hold, as a rule, that the dead had need of what the living still had use for.

It was into this ground, some six score years after Seathan's snap, that a household from a hearth two valleys from Tuam Cnoc dhìreach carried a child too small to have been given more than a season's worth of a name among its own kin, and laid it in a grave dug at the western edge of the ground where the older mounds gave out into open turf.

## IV. Of Aoife's Child

Aoife had been married four years to a man of that hearth when the child was born, her first living child after two that had not lived past their first winter, and she had carried this one, by her own later telling of it, with a fear she had not let herself speak aloud to her husband or to her own mother, having buried enough already that she had stopped trusting a full nine months to mean what it seemed to mean. The child lived through its birth and through the first hard days after it, and lived through its first winter besides, thin and quiet but breathing steadily enough that Aoife's mother, who had raised six of her own and buried three, said at the turn of the year that this one, at least, she judged would live to walk. Aoife let herself believe it that winter, for the first time since the first of the two she had already lost, and she sewed, in the last of the cold months, a small coat cut to the child's own growing rather than handed down from an older cousin as its clothes had been until then, a thing she had not let herself do for either of the children before it.

It did not live to walk. The child sickened in the spring, in the way small children of that country sickened and sometimes did not recover from, without anyone being able to say afterward what the sickness had been or why it had taken this child and spared another, two houses along the same valley, that had shown the same fever in the same week; and it died in Aoife's own arms on a morning near the end of that spring, quietly, without the crying-out that had marked the deaths of the two before it, so that she said afterward she had known, before she was willing to know it, that this death was not going to be like the others.

She kept, from the leather pouch her own grandmother had left her, a sliver of an gual dathach no larger than a fingernail, black-veined and streaked through with the same gold and white and red-brown a hundred hearths in the hill country kept a piece of by then, broken long ago from some larger fragment traded down from household to household until no one troubled any longer to ask which of the seven colours it belonged to or whether it belonged to any. She had kept it as a child keeps a bright stone, without much thought of what it was, turning it over on quiet evenings the way every household with such a piece turned its own over; and on the morning the child died she took it from the pouch, without asking her husband and without, by her own account, quite deciding to do it in any way she could afterward explain, and closed it into the small cold hand before the wrapping was done, so that it went into the ground with the child rather than staying in her own keeping.

Her husband's mother said, when she heard of it, that a hearth ought not to give away a piece of an gual dathach to the dead when the living still had use for it, and there were words between the two households over the matter that neither side afterward cared to keep in the telling. Aoife did not take the sliver back, and would not be moved to, saying only, when she was pressed on it, that the child had held nothing else of its own in the whole short span of its life, no toy worn to its shape by its own hand, no coat cut to its own growing but the one small coat she had sewn it that last winter and that had gone into the ground with it besides, and that she would not begrudge it the one small dark thing that had been, for the little while it lived, given wholly to it and taken back from no one. The household buried the child at the western edge of Tuam Cnoc dhìreach with the sliver closed in its hand as she had closed it, and those who lowered it into the ground saw, before the last of the cloth was drawn over, a dark stain already spreading through the wrapping the shape and size of a small closed fist, though what had stained it, the coal or the child's own body or the two together, none of those standing at the grave could rightly say.

No other grave in all that ground, before that spring or after it through the whole span the recital covers, is remembered to have held any piece of the vein. Aoife's own mother said, in the years that followed, that she doubted any other household in that country could have done what Aoife did even if it had wished to, for want of a piece to give rather than want of the grief to give it with; An gual dathach had by then gone thin in its scattering, worn down and lost and handed on so many times over since the mason first filled his tunic with it that whole valleys held none of it at all, and a household that had kept even a sliver this long, and had not already burned it or lost it or traded it away, was rarer by that century than it had been in Colla's day. Aoife kept nothing else of the coal after that morning. She had given the one piece her house still held, and did not, so far as the telling knows, ever trouble to ask after another.

She went back, in the years after, to the western edge of Tuam Cnoc dhìreach more often than her husband's kin thought a woman ought to trouble herself with a grave already closed and grieved, walking the day's distance alone when she could spare it and sitting a while by the small mound with nothing in her hands, and she said, to the one or two who ever asked her outright what she went there to do, that she went to see whether the turf had settled evenly and to pull the thorn back before it could reach the child, which was, by that country's own reckoning, no more than any mother owed a grave, however far the walking. It was on one of these walks, in a year the recital does not trouble to fix, that she first noticed the new graves beginning to keep to a line along the western edge where before they had been scattered as the ground allowed, and remarked on it to no one, having by then grown used to watching that particular stretch of turf more closely than most.

## V. Of the Rows That Bent

The graves at Tuam Cnoc dhìreach had for longer than living memory been laid without much order beyond the plain custom of the ground: a family buried its dead near its own earlier dead where it could, and elsewhere where it could not, and no one had troubled to lay one grave square with another by any rule a stranger walking the hill could have named. That began, in the years after Aoife's child was buried at the western edge, to change in a way no single household could claim to have started and every household, once it was pointed out to them, admitted they had somehow fallen into. The newer graves, dug further out from the old crowded crown of the hill where the thorn grew thickest, began to be set in something like a line, each new grave a body's length from the last and facing the same way, heads toward the great mountains that stood blue and unreachable on the northern skyline from every part of that ground; and the line, once two or three graves had been laid to it, drew the next grave to itself the way a worn path draws the next foot that crosses that ground, until a household bringing its dead to Tuam Cnoc dhìreach a score of years after looked for the row before it looked for empty turf, and laid its own dead into the row's own line without being told to.

The rows held straight where the ground let them. Where an older grave stood in a row's path, a low unmarked swelling in the turf that no one now could put a name to but that every household knew, by long custom, not to open or to build across, the new row bent around it rather than through it, and straightened again on the far side, so that a stranger walking the ground while the thorn still allowed the rows to be seen whole could read in the bending of them the shape of every old grave that the newer dead had been laid to honour without a name to honour it by. The bending was not planned by anyone; the recital is plain on that, and every household questioned afterward agreed on it. A grave was dug in the row where the row ran, and if an old swelling stood in the row's way the diggers went round it because no one among them, not the most impious of the young men who sometimes grumbled at the extra work of the going-round, would put a spade to ground that had held a body long enough for its memory to have gone out of the world entirely. One young man of a hearth near the hill, digging his own grandfather's grave in those years and finding an old swelling square in the line he had meant to keep, said aloud to the two kinsmen working beside him that they might as well level the thing and be done with the awkward turning it cost them; and his uncle, who had dug more graves at Tuam Cnoc dhìreach than any other man then living, put down his own spade and told him plainly that a man who could not say whose grave he opened had no business opening it, grandfather's grave or no, and that the going-round cost an afternoon's awkward digging and no more, against a debt to some stranger's bones that no living man could reckon in years and had therefore no business trying to pay off with a spade. The young man dug the grave round the swelling, as every grave before his had gone round it, and did not raise the question again.

The rows grew, year on year through that century, out from the crowded crown of the hill toward the open turf at the edges where Aoife's child lay, each new row a little further out and a little more given to bending than the one before it, until the whole western face of Tuam Cnoc dhìreach showed, to anyone who stood back far enough to see the whole of it, no longer straight lines but a slow curved weaving, row folding round row folding round the swellings that no living hand had made and no living memory could explain.

Clann na Ceiste, through those same years, did not let the rainbow rest. The bag that had passed from Seathan's grave to his son passed down the line of fathers, and one of them, whose name the recital keeps from strangers, carried the one coal left in it, gual bogha-froise, north to Doire ghlas, meaning to try it in the grove where Coimhdeach na Fine had first given Diarmad Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich and where no keeper of the line, so far as the recital knew, had thought before to bring it. He walked the whole way north on his own feet, a journey of three days from Tobar dhìreach by the tracks that then ran, telling those who asked him along the road only that he went to the grove because every other ground the line had tried had failed the coal and the grove alone, of all the places the recital named, had once answered a question with more than silence. He found the oaks much as the tellings described them, tall and old with the wind loud in their crowns, and he built a small fire in the lee of a fallen trunk much as Diarmad was said to have done, and sat with gual bogha-froise turning in his hand through the evening and on into the dark, watching the firelight run the colours across its face and waiting, by his own later account, for anything at all: a smoke that stood still, a voice, even a wind that blew wrong. Nothing came. He held the coal to the coals of his own small fire until the fire burned down around it, and it lay black and cold among the ash in the grey of the morning, no likelier to catch than it had been the evening before, and the grove gave him, in a whole night's waiting, nothing that any teller afterward thought worth calling an answer.

He carried it home again, and told, at the next great telling at Achadh a' Chalbh, that the grove itself had answered him nothing, so that the crowd who had begun to hope, on hearing where he had gone, that Coimhdeach na Fine might speak again as it had spoken to Diarmad, went home disappointed of that hope along with every other. The line tried the coal again after him, at the sea and at the river and once, by one keeper's own idea, buried a night in the earth as the black coal had once been buried; and it answered none of them, through all the years the recital covers in this telling, any more than it had answered Seathan or Caitir before them.

Aoife lived on at her own hearth some forty years past her child's burial, raising two children born after it who lived to grow, and telling, to those of her own kin who asked her, the plain account of the sliver and the small stained cloth, growing no fonder with age of the versions that went about the valley with more grief dressed into them than her own telling ever gave it. She died in her seventieth year, and was carried to Tuam Cnoc dhìreach and laid in the row nearest the western edge where her child already lay, the row bending a little as it reached her grave to keep clear of the old swelling that had stood there, unnamed and unopened, since before either of them was born.
