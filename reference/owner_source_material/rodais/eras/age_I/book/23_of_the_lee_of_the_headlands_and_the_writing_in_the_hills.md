# Of the Lee of the Headlands and the Writing in the Hills

*VE 3,147 – 3,303*

## I. Of the Boats That Kept the Land Close

For two generations after Criomthann's fire the boats of the north-west still went out by the old marks, keeping the headland on the left hand and the open water on the right, the way their fathers had gone before Uilebheist na Mara had a name to be feared by. It was not fear alone that turned them in the end, or none of the tellers of that coast ever said so outright; it was the slow arithmetic of empty boat-places on the shingle, which any fisherman can do without being taught it, and which comes, in time, to the same answer as fear does.

The change was not a single season's work. A crew that had always worked the open water off the northern point began, without much said about it, to work the water in the lee of the headland instead, where the swell broke early and a boat could see the whole of its ground at once; and the next season two more crews did the same, and by the time a boy born the year the fire was first lit had grown old enough to take an oar himself, there was hardly a hide boat left on that coast that put out past the last headland while the light held. The change carried its own cost, which the shore hearths counted as carefully as they counted the empty places: seal thinned where the old grounds had always given seal, at Ros ruadh and along the outer skerries, and the middens that had grown for generations at those grounds grew slower, and in some years did not grow at all. But in the sheltered water south of the headlands, and along the strait where the tide ran quiet between the mainland and the near skerries, the middens of Bothan a' Chaolais rose faster than they had ever risen, shell on shell and bone on bone, until the oldest of the bothy folk said that a man might build a wall as long as a house out of what a single winter left there.

It was the strait folk themselves who first said it plainly, at a gathering that had no more purpose than the mending of nets and the telling over of who had lost whom that year. An old steersman of Bothan a' Chaolais, whose father had fished the outer grounds all his life and never once seen what his son now feared without having seen it either, put it the way it was afterward remembered: that the sea had not grown smaller, only that the island's hand had drawn back from the part of it a man could no longer trust, the way a hand draws back from a fire that has burned it once, without waiting to be burned a second time to learn the lesson again. No one at that gathering disputed him, and no one thanked him for saying it either, for there is little thanks going in a fishing camp for a man who says aloud what everyone has already done without discussing it.

The strait was not without its own dangers, for no water on Dia-thìr was without danger, but its dangers were the old, known kind: a squall coming down off the mainland heights faster than a boat could run from it, a rip where two tides met at the narrows, a rock that showed at low water and did not show at high. Against these a fisherman could set his knowledge and his oar and win more often than he lost. Against the other danger, the one that lay off the open coast and showed itself perhaps once in a generation and was never once proved false by any wreck brought home to argue with, there was nothing to set but distance, and distance the strait gave freely. So the boats came in from the open water, and the seal bones thinned at the old grounds and thickened at the new, and a whole coast learned, within the span of two lifetimes, to call a stretch of open sea its neighbour's water and not its own, though no one on it had ever seen the neighbour, and no one who had seen it had lived long enough to be believed twice.

## II. Of Eimhir and the High Pastures

South of that coast, and a long day's walk inland from it, the land climbed into a country of thin soil and thinner grazing that the hill folk called, among themselves, the green grove country, for the oaks that had once stood there thick enough to darken a hillside, though by the time this telling opens the old grove itself had gone back to scrub and bracken and only the name remembered what it had been. It was harder country than the shore, and harder again than the broad valley where Clann a' Chlachair kept its fire and its recital twelve miles to the north, and the households that worked it were fewer and kept closer to their beasts than to each other, each family holding its own stretch of high pasture through the summer months and coming down only for the worst of the winter.

Into one such household, some three hundred years after the black coal was warmed in the ground at Tobar dhìreach, though the hill folk reckoned their years less carefully than the valley did and would have said only that it was long since, a girl was born who was given the name Eimhir, and who grew, as most children of that country grew, first at her mother's hearth and then, as soon as her legs were long enough to keep pace with a flock on a slope, out on the high ground with the sheep and the cold and the wide grey sky. She was not a remarkable child by any account that survives her, quick enough with a crook and a whistle, slow to anger and slower to forget a slight, more comfortable, by most tellings, in the company of beasts than of people, which is no rare thing among those raised to that work. She knew the recital, as every hill child knew it, in the broken, half-understood way it reached households that stood twelve miles from the hearth where it was said whole, and she could recite the count of the coals — gold, white, black, and the four still unanswered — without being entirely certain, if pressed, which coal was which, or why the count mattered so much to the valley folk who came up now and then to trade wool for salt and talk of nothing else.

What she knew better than the recital was the ground itself: every fold of it, every seep and outcrop and stand of gorse for three valleys in either direction, learned the way any herder learns a country, through the soles of the feet more than through the eyes, over years of walking it in every weather the sky could send. By the time she had seen twenty summers she could have walked the whole of her family's grazing blind, and often did, in the grey light before dawn when the flock needed moving before the day's heat brought the flies up from the bog.

It was in the course of that walking, and no more than that, that she came, on a morning in the season when the bracken was just turning, upon the face of rock that would give this telling of the hills its name.

## III. Of the Cutting on the Stone

A ewe had strayed in the night, as a ewe will, up a narrow defile where the family did not usually take the flock, being poor grazing and awkward footing both; and Eimhir went after it before the light was properly up, more from habit than from any great worry for one animal among many, following the beast's own track through wet bracken into a fold of the hill she did not remember walking before, though she had thought she knew every fold within a day's reach.

The ewe she found easily enough, caught by a bramble and glad enough to be caught by a person instead. It was on her way back down, with the animal on a rope and the light coming up grey and slow behind the eastern ridge, that she stopped, for no better reason at first than that something in the corner of her sight did not sit the way stone usually sits.

The rock face rose sheer on her left hand, a grey scarp perhaps twice her own height, weathered the way every outcrop in that country was weathered, lichened in patches of orange and pale green. But across the lichen, where the surface had been struck away to bare stone beneath, ran lines: not the crack and tumble of frost-split rock, which she had seen a thousand times and would have known blind, but lines cut with intent, straight and angled and joined to one another in shapes that repeated, here and there, close enough to be the same shape said twice, the way a word is the same word said twice in a telling. They ran the height of the rock and along it for longer than her own arm span, more of them the further along the face she walked, the ewe forgotten on its rope behind her.

She had seen carved stone before. Every household in that country marked its boundary posts, and Clann a' Chlachair itself, she had been told, kept the count of the coals by mouth at Tobar dhìreach and said it standing, though she had never gone to hear it. But those marks she knew, plain notches and simple figures a person could learn in an afternoon. What ran across this rock face was neither plain nor simple, line after line of it, in a hand — if a hand it was, and not some older weather she did not have the word for — that she did not know at all, and had never heard any teller in her own hearth's memory claim to know either.

She stood a long while before she touched it. When she did, running two fingers along one cut line from where it began to where it stopped, she found the stone at the bottom of the groove smoother than the weathered face around it, as though wind and rain had not yet had the years to roughen what the tool had first laid bare; and yet the lichen at the edges of some of the cuts was old lichen, thick and long-established, telling her without words that whatever hand had made these marks had not made them yesterday, nor in her grandmother's day either.

And there was this, which she noticed last and remembered longest: in three places along the face, a passage of the cutting had itself been cut across, the clean lines broken by a second working, rougher than the first, done — she would have sworn it, standing there, though she could not have said how she knew — with the very same kind of tool, as if whatever hand had written the lines, or another hand that knew the same trade, had come back afterward, deliberately, to strike part of the writing out.

She left the ewe to find its own way home, which it did, being a sensible enough animal on level ground, and went down off that hill faster than she had come up it.

## IV. Of the Word Going Down

She told her mother first, at the family's own hearth, in the plain, halting way a person tells a thing they themselves do not yet believe, and her mother, who had lived sixty years in that country and buried a husband and three of six children in it, did not laugh at her, which Eimhir noted and was grateful for long after she had forgotten most of what was actually said that morning. Instead her mother sent word, by the boy who carried salt between the hill households and the valley, to the two or three elders of that country whose opinion still carried weight beyond their own hearths, and within the week a small company of them had climbed the defile behind Eimhir to see the thing for themselves.

They were longer at it than she had been, and quieter. One of them, a bent man named for a grandfather three generations dead whose own name this telling does not keep, put his palm flat against the stone the way a person tests a beast for fever, and held it there a good while before he took it away again and said nothing at all, which unsettled the company more than any word he might have offered. Another, a woman who had outlived two husbands and kept, besides her flock, a longer memory of the hill country's own small tellings than anyone else living in it, walked the length of the writing twice, once each direction, and then said, in the flat voice of someone stating a fact rather than offering an opinion, that she did not know the hand, and did not know anyone, living or lately dead, who had ever claimed to know it, and that in her long experience a thing no one in a small country claimed to know was, more often than not, a thing that country would come, in its own time and by its own means, to know anyway.

She was right, though not one of the company standing at the rock face that morning could have said how she knew to say it, or how the knowing would come. It did not come as a telling handed down from someone who had read the marks, for no one on that hill, then or after, ever claimed to read them; the lines stayed as unreadable to the last hill-born child of that generation as they had been to Eimhir on the morning she first laid two fingers against them. It came the way certain things come to a small country that lives close together and talks at its hearths through the long winter evenings with little else to talk of: slowly, by no one path that could afterward be traced, gathering itself out of dreams two or three people swore, independently and without having spoken to one another first, that they had dreamed in nearly the same shape; out of a half-remembered scrap some old woman recalled her own grandmother saying, without recalling where her grandmother had had it from; out of the plain, wordless dread that settled, before any of it could be put into a sentence, over anyone who stood alone at that rock face after dark. By the time two winters had passed, there was not a hearth in that country that could not have told a stranger, if a stranger had ever come asking, what the cutting on the stone was held to say: that something had once walked those hills that did not breathe as a living thing breathes, and that it fell upon whoever lit a fire where it could see the light of it burning.

No one could say why breath and fire, of all things, should be bound together in the telling, and the hill folk, being practical people more given to watching their flocks than to turning over the whys of their own fears, mostly left the asking alone. It was enough, for most of that country, that the fear had come, and had come to more than one hearth at once, and agreed with itself too closely, dreamer to dreamer, to be set aside as one old woman's fancy.

## V. Of the Cutting-Over

What none of the company at the rock face could account for at all, that first morning or in the years after, was the second working: the passages of writing struck across and spoiled by a hand using, as near as anyone with an eye for stonework could judge, the very tool that had cut the writing in the first place. Two thoughts went about the hearths concerning it, and neither ever won out fully over the other.

The first was that whoever had written the warning had come back to unwrite parts of it, out of some purpose no hill teller could guess at: to hide a name, perhaps, or a place, or some part of the telling too dangerous even for a warning to carry whole. The second, favoured by the woman who had walked the stone twice that first morning and by most who thought as she did, was harder and colder, and it was this: that the very thing the warning spoke of had done the defacing itself, offended, in whatever way such a thing could be offended, at having been written down and named where the light could fall on it. A hand that could scar stone the way another hand had first cut it was not a hand a sensible person went looking for after dark.

Eimhir herself, in the many years she lived after that morning, was asked more than once which of the two she held to, and by every account that survives her she never answered plainly either way, saying only, when pressed, that she had touched the smoothed groove of the first cutting and the rougher groove of the second with the same two fingers on the same morning, and that her fingers had told her nothing at all about which hand she should fear, only that both hands had been in earnest.

## VI. Of the Years Between

The finding of the writing did not empty the hills, nor did it stop the flocks going up to the summer grazing, for a country that lived by its beasts could not afford a fear that kept it from its work, and the hill folk, whatever else is told of them, were not a people who let fear idle their hands for long. What it did, slowly, over the better part of two decades, was change the shape of the days: fewer households sent a child alone to the far pastures after the finding than had sent one before it, and fewer still let a fire burn low and unwatched through the small hours on a night when the wind came off the high ground with nothing in it a person could put a name to. The defile where Eimhir had found the ewe was given a wide berth by common unspoken consent, though sheep still strayed into it now and again, and were fetched, though never after dark and never by a child sent alone.

Eimhir herself went up past that rock face more times than she ever troubled to count, for a herder's country does not rearrange itself around one bad morning, and in time the going grew almost ordinary to her, the way any fear grows ordinary that a person must live alongside rather than flee. She married into a neighbouring household two valleys over. Of her husband this telling keeps only that he was a quiet man, steadier with beasts than with words, and that he outlived her by eleven years. She raised children on the same slopes she had herself been raised on, and taught them, as she had been taught, to read the weather and the ground and the temper of a flock before they were old enough to read anything else. What she taught them of the writing on the stone she kept plain and short, by every account: that it was there, that no one read it, that the hills had their own sense of what it meant, and that a fire built where the ridge could see it was a fire built carelessly, whatever else a person believed or did not believe about why.

Twice in those years word came up from that country of a thing seen on a ridge at dusk by travellers with beasts to drive home and the light failing on them before they were ready for it: an upright figure, unhurried, walking the crest against the last grey of the sky, with no breath standing in cloud above it though the night was cold enough to hang breath from every mouth and muzzle below. Both times the ones who saw it lived to tell it, and both times the telling agreed on the one particular that the hill folk came to hold above all others: that neither party had had a fire lit or a light shown on the path, and that this, and nothing else they could name, was why they had come home to tell it at all.

## VII. Of the First Covered Hearth

It was in the sixtieth year of Eimhir's own life, by the hill country's rough count, that a household two ridges to the east of her own — new to that ground, having come up from a lowland valley to take a grazing left empty by a family that had died out — dug its cooking fire down into a pit for the first time and roofed it over with a flat slab lifted on two low stones, so that no flame showed above the ground at all, only a slot of red light and a thread of smoke escaping sideways into the dark. They had heard the telling of the writing from their new neighbours within a season of arriving, as any newcomer to that country heard it soon or late, and had chosen, being cautious people in a country new to them, to give the fear more credit than a life's habit, and dig accordingly.

They were mocked for it a little, in the way any first departure from custom draws a little mockery before it is judged either foolish or wise, and one or two of the older households said openly that a family which had not lived in that country long enough to lose anyone to it had no business teaching the country its own fears back to it. But no one stopped them digging, and within the year two more households on the eastern side had done the same, more from watching the first family's fire burn on, unremarked and untroubled, through a whole hard winter, than from any argument put to them in words.

Eimhir watched the pit dug from a little way off, on the day the eastern family first cut it, and said nothing to anyone about it that this telling keeps, though her daughter, many years after, remembered her mother standing longer at the sight than the digging alone seemed to call for.

## VIII. Of Eimhir's Own Hearth

She did not dig her own fire down, that year or in any year that followed it, though she lived to see a fourth household and then a fifth take up the covered hearth after the first three, each a little more readily than the one before, the way any new custom gathers speed once enough hands have tried it and not been burned for the trying. Her own hearth stayed as her mother's had been and her mother's mother's before that, a ring of stones on the open floor of the house, the fire built up each evening and banked each night under its own ash, giving its light up through the smoke-hole in the roof for any ridge in that country to see, had anything been walking the ridges to see it.

Her daughter, grown and married with children of her own, asked her once, late in her life, why she had never dug the fire in, when half the hearths of that country had done it by then and the other half were talking of doing it before the next hard winter. Eimhir was an old woman by that time, by the standard the hill country kept for old, her hands slower on a crook than they had once been and her eyes gone milky at the edges, but her mind, by every account, no less her own than it had been on the morning she followed a strayed ewe up a defile she did not remember walking before.

She gave no answer at once, but sat, when the question was put to her, on the low stool she had used at her own hearth for the better part of forty years, with the fire built up before her the way she had always built it, open to the room and open, through the smoke-hole above, to whatever sky lay over the roof that night; and she looked at it a long while, the way a person looks at a thing they have looked at every evening of their life without, perhaps, ever once looking at it quite so closely, before she said what she said, which her daughter kept, and which is the last of Eimhir's own words this telling has to give: that she had touched the writing on the stone with her own two fingers before any hearth in that country had thought to cover itself against it, and that whatever the writing meant, and whatever walked the ridges when it chose to walk them, she was not going to spend the years she had left hiding her fire from the sky that had watched every fire her family had ever kept, on the chance that the sky had turned against her only now, in her own old age, after keeping faith with her the whole of her life before it.

She said no more than that, her daughter's telling has it, and went on looking at the fire, and the fire went on burning the way it always had, open to the room and to the roof and to whatever, if anything, watched from beyond it.

## IX. Of the Years After

She died two years on from that evening, some sixty years after the finding of the writing, of no illness or misfortune the country could put a name to beyond the plain wearing-out of a long life honestly spent; and she was buried, as the hill folk buried their own, on a slope above her family's grazing, with no marker but a stone set on end and no telling but the one her children and her children's children carried on afterward at their own hearths.

Her own fire went out with her, banked for the last time by her daughter's hand and never built again in that house on the open floor; for her daughter, though she had defended her mother's choice to every questioner who asked it of her in Eimhir's own lifetime, dug the family's hearth into the ground within the year of the burial, and said, when asked why she had not done it while her mother lived, only that a daughter does not take a fire from a living mother's floor, whatever she herself might choose to do with her own.

By the time forty years more had passed over that country, the covered hearth had spread from household to household and ridge to ridge until an open fire, built up on the bare floor the way Eimhir had kept hers to the last, was a rare enough sight in the Doire uaine hills that a child born into a household that still kept one was asked about it by every other child at every gathering, the way a child is asked about any household custom that has become, elsewhere, a thing of the grandparents' time. It had not yet become the whole country's law, nor would it, this telling must own, for some years longer than either Eimhir or her daughter lived to see; there were households still, scattered along the country's quieter edges, that kept the old open floor through habit or stubbornness or a plain want of belief in a fear no living soul could any longer claim to have seen with their own eyes. But the hills had begun, by the century's turning, to keep their light low and covered more often than they kept it open, and to watch their ridgelines at dusk the way they had once watched only the weather; and what had begun on one morning with a strayed ewe and two fingers laid against a cut stone had become, by slow and uneven degrees, the shape of how a whole country lived after dark.
