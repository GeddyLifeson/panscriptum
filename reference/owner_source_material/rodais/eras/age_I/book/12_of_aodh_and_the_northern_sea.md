# Of Aodh and the Northern Sea

*VE 1,331 – 1,442*

## I. Of the Boy Who Had Not Seen the Sea

Aodh was born at Tobar dhìreach a good many fathers after Diarmad had first brought Aithris a' Chruthachaidh down out of the grove, into a hearth that had by then told the same three lines so many times over that the youngest child at it could say them before it could walk a field alone: the sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit; merge the spirits, and they become almighty. He learned the words the way every child of that line learned them, from his mother's mouth before he had teeth enough to say them back whole, and he grew up knowing the sky for the wind that came down off the moor and the earth for the black ground his own feet stood on and the wool his own hands helped card, and knowing the sea, when he thought of it at all, for a word.

He was nine years old before it troubled him that a word was all it was. A traveller up from the eastern farms, resting a night at the hearth on his way to trade salt for hides, said something in passing of a grey water without a far shore to it, and Aodh, sitting close by the fire with the leather bag of the seven pieces in his lap as every child of the line was let hold it once the elders judged the small hands careful enough, asked the man plainly whether he had seen it himself. The traveller laughed and said he had, more times than he could count, from a headland a day's walk past his own farm, and that it went on flat and grey to the very edge of the sky and gave nothing back to a man's eye but more grey water. Aodh turned that over the whole of the next day, and the day after asked his mother how far off the sea might be found, and she told him a day's walk north, more or less, past the last hearth any of their kin kept, and that she had never gone to see it, having had no cause.

He had cause enough of his own by then, though he could not yet have said what it was. Every recital of Aithris a' Chruthachaidh that he had sat through since he was old enough to sit still for one ended the same way it had ended since Diarmad first brought it down out of the grove: that gual òir held the sea, and had never yet been carried to the sea itself, only wetted in Diarmad's day with sea-water fetched home in skins, because no one of the line, in all the generations since, had carried it the day's walk north to try. The old women who kept the recital word for word said this the way they said every other part of it, as a fact long settled and long past troubling anyone new, the way a household says a well has always run dry in a hard summer and always filled again after; and it was some while before Aodh understood that what the old women called long settled, no one living had actually gone and done.

He asked why, the way Diarmad had once asked why the stones had colours, and got much the answer that first Diarmad had got: that the sea was a day's walk and more for a household with beasts to tend and a hearth to keep, and that a coal thrown once and sunk was a coal thrown, and there were six pieces left in the bag that answered to nothing anyone had found yet, and the line had waited five hundred years already and could wait a season or two longer. It did not strike him, at nine, as a good answer, though he had not yet the years to say so to anyone who would listen; and the thought sat in him after that the way the grove had once sat in Diarmad, waiting on nothing but a body old enough and free enough to go and do it.

## II. Of the Growing of the Question

The recital raised him the way a trade raises another boy, said standing at every gathering of the fathers' names, at Oidhche an Dealanaich when the whole kin turned out under the dark of the year to say the Line from Neachdan down, and at the lesser tellings besides, when a traveller asked to hear it or a child was born and the elders judged the new soul owed the whole of the story before it was a season old. Water was carried and peat was cut and the shape of every field his household worked came to him the way it comes to any child of a hearth, the black soil that gave the barley and the wet ground that did not; and under all of it he carried, the older he grew the more rather than the less, the one plain fact that troubled him: that he had heard the sea named at every recital of his whole life and had still, at fifteen, at twenty, never once stood before it.

His own mother had little patience left for the trouble by the time he was grown, having heard it out of him since he was nine and judged it, by twenty, a habit more than a hope; but his mother's aunt Muirgheal, who kept Poit an Teine at the hearth and had Aithris a' Chruthachaidh word for word as Deirbhile's teaching had fixed it, had a longer patience for it than anyone else at Tobar dhìreach, a woman of few words who could say Cunntas nan Athraichean back further than most cared to follow; and it was Muirgheal who did not laugh when Aodh at five-and-twenty said aloud, at last, what he had carried quiet since he was nine, that he meant to walk north and try gual òir against the sea himself before another generation let it lie.

"Your grandfather's grandfather said the same," Muirgheal told him, "and never went. It is easier to mean it than to walk it."

"Then I will walk it," Aodh said, "and find out which I am."

She did not answer that at once, and when she did it was not to forbid him, only to say that a thing waited five hundred years bore waiting a little longer yet, until the barley was in and the beasts settled for the dark months, so that a household could spare the hands his going would cost it; and Aodh, who had waited sixteen years already since the trouble first took him, found he could wait one more season for the sake of an old woman's asking. He worked the harvest that year with more will in him than any harvest before it, the way a man works faster at a thing he means to leave behind him clean, and when the last of the barley was under thatch and the beasts driven in from the high pasture, he told Muirgheal he would go with the first clear spell of weather, and she nodded and said nothing more against it.

## III. Of the Walk North

He went in the season the leaves were down off what few birches grew that high, when the moor above the valley stood the colour of a bruise under a sky the colour of ash, and he carried the leather bag with all seven pieces in it against his chest under his cloak, in the same fold of hide Diarmad had once carried an gual dathach alone. The night before he left he had asked Muirgheal whether he ought to carry the gold piece alone and leave the other six safe at the hearth, and she had told him no, that Aithris a' Chruthachaidh said the seven were cast at the mason's own fire together and ought, in her own reckoning if in no other's, to go together wherever one of them went, so that whatever answered would answer among its own. He did not ask her how she came by that reckoning, and she did not offer to say more of it, only wrapped the bag in a second fold of hide against the weather and pressed it into his hands the way a mother presses bread on a son going to a fair, as though the wrapping were half the gift.

The moor rose steadily under him the whole first hour, heather giving way in patches to bare black peat where the wind had scoured it, and he walked it alone, for no one else of the hearth had cause to go and he had not asked anyone to come; this was a thing he meant to do with his own two feet and no other's, the way Diarmad had gone alone into the grove eighteen fathers before him. The wind came harder as the ground climbed, the last cold breath of the season before the true dark months shut the moor entirely, and twice he stopped in the lee of a rock to eat a little of the bread he carried and to feel, through the two folds of hide, that the bag still sat close and whole against him.

He crossed no hearth he knew the whole first day, for the last of his own kin's fields lay behind him before noon, and the ground past them belonged to no household he had ever heard named, bare moor cut here and there by a burn running fast and brown off the high ground, cold enough at the fording that his feet ached the length of the far bank before feeling came back into them. Once, on the highest stretch, he startled a flock of small dun birds up out of the heather at his very feet, so sudden and so many together that his heart went hard in his chest before he understood what had frightened him; and once he passed the bleached ribs of some beast long dead, sheep or deer he could not tell by then, picked clean and half sunk into the peat, and gave it no more than a glance, being in no mind that day to read a sign into every bone the moor happened to show him.

It was past noon of that first day, with the moor's highest ground behind him and the land beginning at last to fall away north, that he smelled it before he saw it: a coldness in the air different from the moor's own cold, carrying something in it he had no word ready for, salt and weed and a wideness that seemed, even before his eyes found anything to confirm it, to have already got into his breathing. He came over the last rise with that smell thick in his throat and stood a long moment looking down at grey water running flat to the edge of the world, exactly as the trader long ago had said it would, and found that sixteen years of imagining it had prepared him for nothing at all. It moved. That was the thing no telling at any hearth had ever thought to say plainly, that the sea did not lie still the way a loch lay still on a calm day. It heaved from edge to edge, slow and grey and endless, a live thing breathing under a skin too wide for any eye to take in whole.

He stood there long enough that the cold began to work into his feet before he remembered to go on, and came down the last slope to a shingle shore where the land gave out at last into the water proper, waves running up the stones in long grey folds and drawing back with a sound like a held breath let go, over and over, without ever once resting between one and the next.

## IV. Of the Casting

He did not throw the coals that first evening, though the light still held enough for it. A day and more of walking lay behind him now, and something in him, once he stood at last on the stones with the bag warm against his chest, wanted the doing of it done properly and not rushed at the tail of a long walk with his legs still shaking under him from the climb; so he found a hollow above the tideline out of the worst of the wind, ate what bread was left him, and lay through a cold night listening to the sea work at the shingle below him, a sound he had never once heard in his life until that day and thought, lying awake under it, that he might never afterward be able to sleep easily without.

In the morning, with the light come up grey and even over water that ran the same colour as the sky above it, he went down to the water's edge and opened the bag.

The seven pieces lay in his palm the way they had always lain in the leather bag at the hearth, small and dull and unremarkable to any eye that did not know what they were, none of them showing, in the flat morning light, more than the plain black of any coal broken from any vein. He held each in turn a moment before he threw it, the way a man says a word over a thing before he lets it go, though he had no fixed form of words for the doing and made none up on the spot, only stood at the water's edge with the cold coming up through his boots and let each piece go into the swell as it rose to meet his hand.

The first sank without a mark of any kind, gone under the grey water as plainly as a common stone would have gone, and the second went the same way, and the third and fourth after it, each swallowed into the sea's own colour before the ripple of its falling had even smoothed away. He had known, standing there, that six of the seven were not the sea's to answer and had told himself as much on the whole walk north, so that he felt less each time than he had feared he might, only a small settling disappointment, familiar by the fourth piece, that this one too had been only a coal. The fifth went the same way. The sixth.

The seventh he held longest of all, though he had meant to hold none of them longer than another, for his hand seemed, of its own accord, unwilling to let it go; and when at last he did throw it, no differently than he had thrown the six before it, out into a swell rising grey and slow toward the shingle, it did not sink.

It took fire on the water, a small bright gold flame standing up out of the swell where the coal had struck, no bigger than a hand's span and burning steady in a wind that would have snuffed any ordinary flame in a breath; and it rode the rise and fall of that one swell the whole way in toward the shore, burning gold on grey water under a grey sky, until a breaker further out gathered itself and rolled over where the small flame stood and put it under in a single fold of white water, and it did not come up again.

Aodh stood at the water's edge with the empty bag hanging loose in his hand and watched the place where the flame had been, though there was nothing left to watch, the sea already smoothing back into the same grey heave it had shown him the evening before, giving no more sign that a coal had burned gold on its face than it gave of the six that had simply gone under. He stood there long enough that the cold reached past his boots into his legs before he remembered himself, and when at last he turned from the water he found he was weeping, though he could not afterward have said, when the old women at the hearth asked him, whether it was for the five hundred years the answer had waited or for the small span of a morning that a fire had burned and then had not.

## V. Of the Telling at the Hearth

He walked home slower than he had walked out, for the weather had turned against him and the ground was heavier with rain underfoot, so that a day and more going became the better part of two days coming back; and he came into the valley at last gaunt and salt-stained and empty-handed but for the leather bag, which he had folded and carried home though there was nothing left in it that answered to anything.

Muirgheal was the first to hear it from him, sitting by the fire she had kept since before he was born, and she made him say it twice over, once plain and once again slower, before she would let herself believe the whole of it: that six had sunk as any coal sinks, and the seventh, the gold, had taken fire on the swell and burned there, riding the water, until the sea had put it under. When he had said it the second time she sat a long while without speaking, her hands still in her lap, and when at last she did speak it was to the fire rather than to him, in the voice of a woman working out a sum she had carried unfinished a very long time.

"Nearly five hundred years," she said, "since the grove." And then, to Aodh, "Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich is true, then. Each answers to its own."

Word of it went out from Tobar dhìreach faster than Aodh himself had walked home, carried by whoever first heard it told and could not sit still on the hearing, so that by the second evening households a half-day's walk off in every direction had sent someone to ask whether it was true, and by the third Poit an Teine's own door stood open near the whole of the daylight to let in one more household wanting to hear it from Aodh's own mouth rather than a neighbour's telling of it. He said it over so many times in those first days that the words wore into the same shape each time, the six that sank and the one that burned, until he no longer needed to search for how to say it and it came out of him as easily as the fathers' names came out of the old women at a recital; and he noticed, saying it the tenth time and the twentieth, that no one who heard it laughed, not once, not even the youngest children who had laughed at every other wonder a grown man ever told them.

She wept that night at the hearth before the whole gathered kin, an old woman who had buried a husband and two children of her own without more than a dry eye shown for either, weeping now over a fire lit gold on a grey sea a day's walk from where she sat; and when she was asked why, by those too young to have carried the weight of it themselves, she said only that she had said Aithris a' Chruthachaidh word for word since she was a girl, and had never once let herself believe, in all her years of saying it back word for word, that she would live to hear it proved even once. The recital that night ran longer than any within living memory, for the fathers' names were said as always down from Neachdan, and then, for the first time since the mason's own well, a new line was added after them, plain and short: that in that year gual òir was thrown into the northern sea by Aodh of the line, and took fire there, and burned gold until the water put it under. It is from that night that the piece was first called, at every hearth Aithris a' Chruthachaidh reached after, *gual òir*, gual òir, a name it has kept at every recital since.

## VI. Of the Six That Would Not Answer

Aodh did not let the matter rest with the one answer given him. He went back to the northern shore twice more in the years after, though the bag now held only six pieces and no eighth chance for the gold, and threw each of the six in turn from a different height and a different ground each time he went, from the hilltop above the shingle where the wind stood hardest, from the black peat of the high moor dug open to the bare ground beneath, from a shallow pool of standing rainwater he found once in a hollow and thought, for want of any better guess, might stand close enough to the sea's own element to serve. Nothing answered him. The coals lay in whatever ground or water he set them in the same dull black they had always shown, and he came home from each of those later walks with nothing to tell the hearth but that the six were still six, and the gold still one, and the answer, whatever shape it would take for the rest, was not going to be hurried by a man's own wanting it hurried.

He did not stop trying for that. In the years he had left he carried the six pieces to every high ground and every water within a hard day's walk of Tobar dhìreach that he could reach and return from before the season turned against him, and tried each against ground and water and once, in a hard frost, against the bare air, held up on an open palm the way Diarmad was said to have held an gual dathach up to the sky in the grove; and each time he came home to say, to whoever still troubled to ask him, that the ground had not answered and the water had not answered and the air had not answered, only the sea, only once, and that he did not yet know what any of the rest were waiting on. Muirgheal did not live to hear the last of these failed tryings. She died an old woman by then even by the reckoning of a household that buried most of its own well short of her years, and the recital that told her death added, after the fathers' names, only that she had lived to hear the sea answer and had wept for it, and that she was carried out and laid as her own mother had been laid before her, with the fire kept burning at the hearth the whole night through as it had never once been let go out since the mason first carried it home.

Aodh himself did not live to see any of the six answer either. He kept the trying up until his own strength for the walking gave out under him, and died in his own bed at Tobar dhìreach at eight-and-sixty years, a long life by the reckoning of that hearth though not the longest the line had known, with the leather bag of six unanswered pieces still under his own hand where he had kept it close through all his last years the way another man might keep a tool he had not yet finished the work he meant it for. The recital that told his death said what it had said of every keeper of the line before him, the fathers' names down from Neachdan and then his own name added after them; and it said, besides, what no recital before his had ever had cause to say of any man of that line, that in his one lifetime the sea had been answered where it had waited five hundred years unanswered, and that six waited yet, and that whoever came after him would have to carry them further than he had, or find some ground he had not thought to try.

## VII. Of the New Pieces from the Stream-Bank

It fell, in the years just after Aodh was laid in the ground, to a boy of his own house too young to remember him clearly, out following a strayed goat along the bank of the stream below Tobar dhìreach where the water had cut a fresh notch into the earth after a hard season of rain, to find the black vein showing bare in the cut bank exactly as the mason's own crack had once shown bare in the rock of the well, a seam no wider than two fingers laid together, dark and dull in the wet earth. He did not know, at first look, what he had found, only that it was not the ordinary black of the peat around it, and he carried a broken piece of it home in his fist the way any child carries home a thing that has caught its eye, meaning to ask someone older what it was.

The elders of the hearth knew it at once for what it was, and more than one of them wept a second time that autumn, more than forty years after Muirgheal had wept at the gold, to see a bare showing of the vein found again after so many generations of the line had known it only as seven old pieces kept close in a leather bag and never renewed. They went out themselves to the stream-bank in the following days and cut what fresh pieces the notch would give them, careful of the bank's own crumbling the way the mason had once been careful of his well, and brought the new coal home to lie beside the old seven in the bag the children still played with, so that for the first time since Neachdan's own day the line held more pieces of the vein than the seven it had counted and recounted for five hundred years, with no more certainty than the mason himself had ever had of what any of the new ones might answer to, or when, or in whose lifetime the answer would come.

They said the recital that year with the new pieces passed hand to hand around the gathered kin before the fire was banked, an old custom half remembered and half made new on the spot, so that every soul at the hearth might hold, once at least, a piece of the vein not yet tried against anything at all; and it is told that the youngest child there, no older than Aodh himself had been the year the trader first spoke to him of the sea, held one of the fresh pieces up before the fire the way every child of that line had done since Diarmad first turned an gual dathach in his own small hand, and asked, in a voice too young yet to know how old the question was, what it was for.
