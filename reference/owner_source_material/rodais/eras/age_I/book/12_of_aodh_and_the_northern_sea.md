# Of Aodh and the Northern Sea

*VE 1,331 – 1,442*

## I. Of the Line After Diarmad

Four hundred years is a long while to carry one question, and the Mason's line carried it the whole of that time without once setting it down. Deirbhile's grandchildren tried the coals as her father had tried them, in rain and in wind and in the one fire together, and their grandchildren tried them again in ways their grandparents had not thought of, burying the black one a hand's depth in the garden plot one spring on the reasoning that a seed wants covering before it grows, and hanging the snowflake coal from a branch in a gale on the reasoning that a bird's nest is built high for a purpose. Nothing came of any of it. The coals stayed coals, the fire-red no redder for a night in the ash, the silver no brighter for a morning in the dew, and each failure was folded into the Telling the way every failure before it had been folded in, taken for proof that the riddle was still worth the trying rather than for shame, for a thing tried and found wanting so many times over so many lives could hardly be a thing anyone had simply misunderstood at the first asking.

The Line itself grew in the telling of it, father added to father each dark-of-the-year night, Sloinneadh a' Chlachair said standing at Tobar dhìreach with more names in it every generation than the one before, until a child born into that household could stand at ten years old and hear a hundred names go by before the recital reached her own grandfather, and understand, without anyone troubling to explain it to her, that she had been born into the middle of a labour far too long for any one life to see the start or the end of. The fire-pot passed from hand to hand as the coals did, kept always by a woman of the line, and the leather bag that held what was left of the vein's broken pieces grew thin in those centuries and was mended and thinned again, for pieces were lost the way small things are always lost from a household that moves them often, dropped in a dark corner, carried off by a child too young to know better, given away once or twice in the early years before the rule against it hardened into something no one any longer questioned. What did not thin was the sea. It sat in the riddle the whole time, the third of the three spirits, and no one at Tobar dhìreach had ever gone to look at it, for the high valleys where the fire had first been given away lay inland of every coast by more days' walking than most households of that reach ever had cause to spend on any errand that was not feeding themselves.

It was into that household, in the year the count of fathers had grown so long that no one troubled any more to say how many lives it spanned back to Neachdan, that Aodh was born, the only son to live past his first winter of a woman named Ròis and a man remembered afterward chiefly as Aodh's father, for the deeds that would be told of the son left little enough room in the telling for the father's own. He was a big child from the first, slow to walk and slower to talk, and there were those among the kin in his early years who wondered aloud, though never where his mother could hear it, whether so quiet a boy had it in him to carry a line so heavy with names and coals and an unanswered riddle; and Muirgheal, who was by then the oldest woman living at Tobar dhìreach and had kept the fire-pot longer than anyone else then breathing could remember it being kept by another hand, told them, the one time the doubt was said within her own hearing, that a slow tongue and a slow foot were no more against a man's worth than a fast one was for it, and that she had buried enough quick, clever children in her own long life to have stopped setting much store by cleverness as a sign of anything at all.

## II. Of the Boy Who Had Never Seen the Sea

Aodh heard the Telling first the way every child of that household heard it, sitting cross-legged at the edge of the standing crowd on the darkest night of the year with the cold coming up through the ground into his small bones and his eyes fixed on whichever elder was speaking that year, and he heard, along with the sparks that did not fall and the grass-people blinking up out of the heather, the three spirits named as plainly as his own name was named to him: the sky, the earth and the sea, each with its own coal to answer to, and none of the three yet answered. He asked, as every child of that household seemed bound sooner or later to ask, what the sea looked like, and was told, as every child before him had been told, that the sea lay a long day's walk north of the valley, grey and cold and wider than any eye could take in at once, and that no one now living at Tobar dhìreach had gone to stand at its edge, for a household that lived by herding and by the barley of the high fields had never yet had reason enough to spend a day's walking and a day's walking back on a thing it could hear told at the hearth just as well as seen.

That answer sat easily enough in most children who heard it, a fact taken in alongside a hundred other facts about a world too large for any one life to walk the whole of, and forgotten by most of them within the season. In Aodh it lodged and stayed. He was seven, by his mother's counting, the same age Diarmad had been when he first asked why the stones had colours in them, though no one at Tobar dhìreach troubled to remark on that likeness at the time, having heard by then generations of children ask generations of questions about the coals and the riddle without any one of those questions coming to more than the last. He took to asking, whenever a traveller came through the valley from the north with salt or with word of the moor households, whether they had seen the sea themselves and what it had looked like to them, and he listened to the answers the way a man listens who means to build a true thing out of what he is told rather than only hear it and let it pass, storing up a grey that was sometimes flat as a held breath and sometimes torn white the whole width of the horizon, a smell some travellers could not describe at all and others called sharp enough to catch in the back of the throat, a sound like a held note that never quite finished. By the time he was a man grown the sea he carried in his own head was stitched together out of forty travellers' half-remembered tellings, none of them whole, and he had never once stood where any of them had stood to see it with his own eyes.

He was twenty-five, big-boned and slow-spoken as he had been as a child, keeping by then the greater part of the household's herding with two younger cousins under him, when the thought that had sat in him since he was seven finally moved him to more than asking. It came to him, he told his mother once he had already half decided it, the way a slow river finally cuts through a bank it has been working at for years, with no one standing on either side able to name the day the bank gave: that a man who meant to understand a riddle naming the sea as one of three spirits owed it, at the least, to go and stand where the sea was, and see with his own eyes what forty travellers' mouths had only ever handed him secondhand. His mother did not try to stop him, though she told him plainly that she did not expect the standing and the looking to answer anything the coals themselves had not answered in four hundred years of trying, and Muirgheal, old by then past the counting of it in any way but her own bent back and her own slowed hands, told him only that a man ought to see the thing he meant to spend his life questioning before he spent too much more of it questioning it blind, which was, though she did not put it in so many words, as close to a blessing as the old woman had it in her to give.

In the last days of one summer he walked north from Tobar dhìreach, taking nothing with him beyond what a day and a night on the moor asked for, and came, on the second day, over a low rise of gorse and stunted thorn to a wind that smelled of nothing he had ever smelled at any hearth in his life, salt and wet stone and a colder thing still under both, and then to the sea, grey that day under a grey sky, running away from him in a long unbroken swell to a horizon he could not have told, looking at it, from the sky it ran under. A long while he stood at the top of the last rise before the shingle without going down to it, the way a man stands at the door of a house he has heard of his whole life before he lets himself cross the threshold, and when he did go down at last and stood on the wet stones with the swell running up almost to his feet and drawing back again, he found that no traveller's telling, however carefully he had stored it, had come anywhere near the true size of the water, the way it took up the whole of the world in front of him and gave nothing at all back to the eye of what lay beyond it. Two days he stayed on that shore before he turned for home, sleeping in the lee of a rock the way his own forefather had once sheltered from a different weather in a different valley, and watched the water at every hour of the light, low tide and high, calm morning and one grey squall that came through in the second afternoon and left as quickly as it had come, and he came to understand, in those two days, that the sea was not a thing a man learned in an afternoon's standing, and that he had not gone north to learn it in an afternoon.

## III. Of the Casting of the Seven

He came home changed in a way his mother could see in him before he had said a word of what he had found, the way Fionnghuala had once seen it in Diarmad returning from the grove, and it was near a year before the change in him found its shape in words rather than in a longer silence at the hearth than had been his habit before. What he had brought back from the northern shore was a certainty rather than an answer, he told his mother at last, sitting with her by the fire-pot on an evening the rest of the household had gone early to their beds: a certainty that the sea the riddle named was the actual cold water he had stood in to the ankle on that shingle, and none of the sea of the Telling, heard and pictured and never touched, and that whatever the coal owed to its own element, it owed to that water and no other, the true wet weight of it and none of the idea of it carried four hundred years inland by mouths that had themselves mostly never seen it either. His mother asked him, plainly, what he meant to do about a certainty of that kind, and he told her, as plainly, that he meant to carry the coals north himself and try them against the true water, the way no one of the line before him had ever tried them, for rain and river-water carried home in a skin were a poor stand-in for the sea, whatever the reasoning that had once made them seem near enough to serve.

He set out again in the following autumn, at six-and-twenty, on the fourteenth day of an t-Sultain by the household's own counting, with the leather bag of seven coals wrapped in a fold of cloth against his chest under his cloak the way the rainbow alone had once ridden against Diarmad's chest on the road home from the grove, and Muirgheal, who could no longer walk the distance herself and had not tried to talk him out of going, stood at the edge of the valley to see him off and told him only that she had waited near sixty years to hear whether the sea would answer, and that she meant to go on breathing until she heard it one way or the other. He walked the moor in a single hard day, faster than he had walked it the first time, driven by a purpose the first journey had lacked, and came down onto the same shingle he had stood on the year before as the light was going, the sea running grey and heavy under a sky already clouding for weather, the wind off the water colder than it had been in high summer and carrying in it, for the first time since he had left the valley, the smell of the actual autumn gale that was building somewhere out past the edge of his sight.

Every part of him that had walked a hard day to reach that shingle wanted to throw the coals that first evening, and he did not. He had learned enough of the sea in his two earlier days on that same shore to know that a gale building offshore was no place to stand in the shallows with a task that wanted a steady hand and a clear eye, and he slept that night in the lee of the same rock he had sheltered by before, listening to the wind rise and the swell come in harder against the stones through the dark hours. He woke before first light to a morning that had blown itself out in the night and left behind it a sea still heaving from the gale's memory but no longer building under it, the swell long and grey and even, breaking white a little way out and running up the shingle in a hiss that drew back after it every few breaths like a live thing breathing.

He waited for full light before he went down to the water, for he wanted to see the colours plain when the moment came and not guess at them in a grey dawn, and when the sun had cleared enough of the eastern cloud to put a little brightness on the swell he unwrapped the bag from against his chest and knelt on the wet stones and took the coals out one by one to look at them in that light, the black as black as a wet crow, the fire-red with its colour moving in it the way flame moves seen through closed eyes, the snowflake dark and flecked all through with small white stars, the silver shining under its sheen the way the moon shines on running water, the red-brown deep and warm-looking as old blood, the rainbow shifting even in his still hand as oil shifts on water, and last the coal with the sheen of gold over its black, which glinted, held up against the risen sun on the swell behind it, very nearly the way the sea itself glinted a little further out where the light caught the tops of the running waves.

He waded in to his knees, the cold of the water going through him at once past anything he remembered from either of his earlier days on that shore, for a man standing still in autumn water learns its cold in a way a man merely watching from the shingle never does, and he held the black coal up a moment against the grey swell before he threw it, not because he expected it to answer any more than it had answered rain or river-water or a night in a gale, but because he had told himself, walking north, that he would give each of the seven its own moment against the water and not throw them all together as something to be done with quickly. The black struck the swell a little way out and sank without a mark on the water's face, gone from his sight before he had drawn his next breath, and he threw the fire-red after it, and the snowflake, and the silver, each one striking and sinking the same unmarked way, no brighter and no darker for the water than they had ever been for rain or wind or fire, until only the red-brown, the rainbow and the gold-sheened coal were left in his hand.

He threw the red-brown, and it went under exactly as the four before it had gone, and he stood a moment with the rainbow and the gold coal both, the rainbow that had already failed sky and earth and buried ground alone in the grove four hundred years before him, and the coal with the sea's own sheen on it that no one of the line had ever thought, until his own two days of standing on that shingle, to try against the water it already seemed to carry. He threw the rainbow first, watching it turn once in the light before it struck the swell and sank as unmarked as the rest, and then, with the last coal in his hand and the cold of the water past his knees now as a wave ran a little higher than the ones before it, he threw the gold-sheened coal out into the swell.

It struck the water and did not sink. Where it touched the face of the wave a flame stood up gold, bright as the risen sun caught full in a still pool, and it did not go out as the wave rose under it and carried it forward, but rode the swell whole and burning, sinking a little into the trough behind each wave and rising again unquenched on the back of the next, gold on grey water the whole length of the swell Aodh could follow with his eye, until a breaker further out than the rest curled over and broke white across it and the flame, and the coal with it, went under and did not come up again. Aodh stood in the water to his knees a long while after, the cold no longer reaching him at all, watching the place on the sea where the fire had gone under as though it might yet rise a second time, until the ordinary ache of standing too long in cold water brought him back to himself and he waded out, shaking, and sat down hard on the wet stones above the tideline, and put both hands over his face, not from grief and not, in any way he could afterward find words for, from simple joy either, but from the particular kind of weeping that comes on a man who has carried a question his whole life and watched it, in the space of a single breath, stop being a question.

## IV. Of the Telling at the Hearth

He waded back out once his hands had stopped shaking and groped in the cold shallows where the six other coals had gone under, and found, by feel more than sight, five of the six lying loose among the stones a little way out, wetted and cold and exactly the colours they had always been, the black no blacker and the silver no brighter for their brief drowning; the sixth, the snowflake, he did not find that morning however long he searched, and went home at last without it, telling himself that the sea that had kept one coal lit might as well keep another dark one besides, and that six recovered out of seven thrown was more than he had any right to have looked for.

He walked home slower than he had walked north, for the two days of standing and the one morning of throwing had taken more out of him than the walking itself, and came down into Tobar dhìreach on the evening of the third day to a household that had watched the northern rise for him since the morning before, having reckoned, by the count of days he had said he would be gone, that he was near due. Muirgheal was the first to reach him, as Fionnghuala had once been first to reach Diarmad, and she did not ask him what he had found before she had looked once, closely, into his face, for she had spent near sixty years listening to travellers describe a sea she had never seen herself and had learned, in that time, to read a face that had stood where she had not.

"It answered," was all he said to her there in the open, the rest of the household still coming up around them, and it was not until the fire had been built up and the whole hearth gathered close, near kin and the wider kin sent for from the nearer households the moment word of his return had run ahead of him, that he told it standing, the way Diarmad had once told the Telling itself standing, because a thing the house meant to keep was a thing said on its feet and not lounging by the fire as an ordinary evening's talk was said. He told the two days of watching the water before he ever threw a coal into it, and the black and the fire-red and the snowflake and the silver and the red-brown and the rainbow going under one after another with no mark on any of them, and then the coal with the sheen of gold striking the swell and standing up gold and burning and riding the waves whole, gold on grey water, until the breaker took it under for good.

There was no laughter after it, the way there had been laughter once for Diarmad, for four hundred years of the riddle sitting unanswered at every hearth in the valley had worn away whatever readiness for laughter a first telling might once have found; instead there was a silence, and out of the silence Muirgheal, who had not spoken since she first reached him on the rise, said, in a voice that shook a little more than her age alone accounted for, that it was near five hundred years since a boy no older than Aodh had walked north-west instead of north with one question in him, and that she had begun to think, in her own last years, that she would die not knowing whether the grove's smoke shape had told Diarmad the truth or only a riddle with no answer waiting anywhere at the far end of it; and now, she said, she knew, and she wept there in front of the whole gathered household without troubling to hide it, an old woman's weeping that no one at that hearth thought less of her for.

It was Aodh himself, once the weeping had run its course and the talk had turned to what the finding meant, who said aloud the thing that would be repeated at every telling of it afterward: that the riddle had not lied and had not been idle, that each of the three spirits truly did carry its own coal and would answer to nothing else, sky to nothing, earth to nothing, but the sea to the very water of the sea and no water carried home in a skin instead of it; and that if the sea's own coal had waited only for a man willing to stand where the sea itself was, then the other two spirits, the sky and the earth, might yet be answered the same way, by a coal carried at last to the true high air or the true deep ground and not merely wished at from a safe distance inland. The coal that had burned was given its name that same night, before the fire had burned low, gual òir, the gold coal, said aloud at Tobar dhìreach for what the household believed to be the first time any mouth had ever shaped it, though no one there could have said for certain that some traveller far along the coast, hearing a shape of the Telling worn thin by a dozen mouths, had not already guessed at some name near enough to it on his own account.

## V. Of the Six That Would Not Light

Aodh did not rest on the one lighting the way a lesser man might have let himself. He carried the six coals that remained him, the black, the fire-red, the snowflake he had after all found again on a second search of the shallows some days later, the silver, the red-brown and the rainbow, to every shore his own two legs could reach in the years that followed, the southern coast and the western, a stretch of coast far enough east that he slept three nights on the road each way to reach it, wading in to the knee at each of them and giving each coal its own moment against the water the way he had given them at the first shore, and none of it answered a second time. He climbed, in the years between the shore journeys, whatever high ground lay within a hard day's walk of Tobar dhìreach, holding the snowflake coal up bare-handed into whatever wind the day gave him on the reasoning that if the sea had wanted the true water of the sea, the sky might want the true wind of the highest places and not merely a gale felt from a ridge that was itself still some way short of the sky's own reach; and the wind went through his fingers exactly as it had gone through Diarmad's and every trying hand between them, and the snowflake coal stayed dark. He buried the black coal a second time, deeper than the garden plot his own grandmother's generation had tried, in ground he judged truer to the deep earth than any plot a household had already turned for planting, and dug it up a season later no warmer than the soil around it had been.

He was not, in those years of trying and failing at shore after shore and height after height, the bitter man such a long string of failures might have made of another, for he had the one answer already, the gold coal's own burning, to hold against every failure that came after it, and he told his own children, when they were old enough to ask why he kept walking to seas he had already proved could not answer twice, that a man who had watched one spirit answer had no more excuse for despairing of the other two than a man who had caught one fish in an empty-seeming river had for giving up the whole day's fishing. Muirgheal did not live to see any of those later journeys bear fruit, for she died in her own bed at Tobar dhìreach some eleven years after Aodh's return from the northern shore, old past any counting the household still troubled to keep exactly, with the fire-pot near enough her hand to touch as Fionnghuala had once asked for it, and the last words of hers that the household kept were that she had lived to hear the sea answer and wanted nothing further of her own remaining years but to be buried near enough the slab that she might go on, in whatever way the dead went on, listening for the day the other two answered as well.

Aodh went on walking to shores and up onto heights for the better part of thirty years more after her death, a big man grown grey and then white in the beard, slower on the road each year though never, by any telling that came down of him, less willing to set out on it, and he died at last in his own house at Tobar dhìreach in the sixty-eighth year of his life, the bag of six coals that had never answered a second call lying by his bed as the seven had once lain by Diarmad's, and the last clear words the household kept of him were not despair at the six that had failed him but a kind of settled patience near enough to Diarmad's own last nearness that the two tellings, said one after another at a later standing of the Line, sounded almost as though the same man had spoken them twice across four hundred years: that the sea had waited five hundred years for a man willing to stand in its own water, and that the sky and the earth could wait as long again if they had to, for a question answered once could not, after that, be believed unanswerable a second and a third time merely because the answering was slow.

It was some eight years after his burial, when the bag that had once held seven whole pieces of the vein had been thinned by four hundred years and more of trying and dropping and the ordinary wear of being carried on so many long roads that a woman of the line, going over what remained of it one evening by the fire-pot, found it holding barely enough of each dark colour to be sure, by feel alone in a poor light, which piece was which, that the household turned for the first time in its whole remembered history to the vein itself rather than to the leather bag alone. A young man out after strayed sheep in a wet spring found, where a stream above Tobar dhìreach had cut a fresh channel down through the shoulder of the hill in a hard winter's flood, a dark seam laid bare in the cut bank exactly like the seam Neachdan had first struck open with his wedge so many lives before, and brought home in his two cupped hands as much of it, broken small, as he judged the household might want; and from that spring onward it became the custom of the line, whenever the bag ran thin, to send someone to that same stream-bank for a fresh handful rather than let the vein's own gift run out through nothing worse than careless carrying, a handful taken perhaps once in a life and no oftener, for the vein gave freely but the line had learned, over four centuries of watching small things lost from a household that moved often, not to take more of any gift than the carrying of it truly asked for.
