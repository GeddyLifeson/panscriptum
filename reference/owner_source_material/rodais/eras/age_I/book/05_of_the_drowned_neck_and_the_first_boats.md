# Of the Drowned Neck and the First Boats

*VE 506 – 750*

## I. Of Gràinne and the Drowned Neck

There was a time, the north-shore tellers say, when a household could walk dry-shod to Eilean dhubh at any tide of the year, and a time after it when the same walk could be made only at the ebb of the lowest tides, and a time after that when it could not be made at all. No one household watched the whole of that change. Gràinne watched more of it than most, for she was born on the neck itself and she died on it, or near enough to it that the difference hardly mattered to those who buried her.

Her people had kept a hearth on the low neck since before her grandmother's grandmother, on a shelf of dry ground between two arms of shingle where the crossing to the island was narrowest and firmest underfoot. It was good ground for the purpose it was put to: near enough the mainland pastures to run beasts on both sides of the water, and near enough Eilean dhubh itself, where deer grazed thick and unhunted in her great-grandmother's day, that a household settled there ate better in a hard winter than a household settled further inland ever managed. The neck was never a rich place to live, being low and salt-scoured and bare of any shelter but what a household built for itself, but it was never a hungry one either, and Gràinne's people had no reason, through all the generations before her, to think of leaving it.

She was born in the four hundred and fifty-fifth year of the fathers' count, the youngest of a large household, and grew up doing what every child of the neck grew up doing: watching the tide. It was not a game to her, though it began as one. Her mother could read the run of a spring tide against the last one the way Conn's people, far south of her, read the colour of the light before dawn, and she taught Gràinne to read it the same way, walking the shingle with her at the top of every high water and setting a stone at the mark the sea had left, so that the next high tide could be measured against it. By the time Gràinne was a young woman she kept that watch herself, without her mother telling her to, because it had become a thing she did not know how to stop doing.

The stones told her what the household already half knew without wanting to say it aloud: that each year's highest tide came a little further up the shingle than the year before's, some years so little that only Gràinne's own eye could have caught it, and some years, after a wet season or a run of westerly gales, a stride's length further in one water than it had taken three ordinary years to gain. Her grandmother's telling held that in the grandmother's own girlhood a household could cross to Eilean dhubh on any day it pleased and get its feet no wetter than the mud always made them; by the time Gràinne was grown, the crossing could be made only within two hours either side of the lowest ebbs, and only then by those who knew which channels ran soft and which ran firm, for the neck no longer dried the same way in every place.

She married on the neck and buried a husband on the neck and raised children on it, and through all of that the sea kept its slow account against the shingle, a stride a year in the bad years and less in the good ones, and never once, in Gràinne's whole life, a stride backward. She would not leave. Her sons asked her to more than once, in the years when asking was still a thing that could be done gently, pointing out that the dry ground behind the shelf ran back further than the shelf itself and that a hearth moved a bow-shot inland would still be a hearth on the neck, still near enough the crossing and the deer and the shellfish beds her household had always worked. She heard them out each time and gave them the same answer each time: that she would move her hearth when the sea came into it and not a day before, for a hearth that ran from the water before the water had truly come for it was not a hearth kept by a people who meant to stay anywhere.

It came for it in her fifty-first year, in a spring of hard westerlies that piled water against the whole north coast for the better part of a month. Gràinne woke before dawn to a sound she had never before heard at that hearth, water moving under the floor of packed earth where her own feet had walked dry every morning of her life, and came out into a grey half-light to find the sea standing in the lowest corner of the house, black and cold and already a hand's depth deep, working its way toward the hearth-stones themselves with each small surge. She did not weep over it, or if she did the tellers do not say so. She raked the fire out into the clay pot her mother had raked fires into before her, and she carried it out past her own threshold for the last time, and she stood on the higher ground behind the shelf while her sons and their wives pulled what could be pulled from the flooding house, and she watched the water find the hearth-stones and put out, in a long hiss she said afterward she could hear over the wind, whatever of the old fire's ash had been left lying loose upon them.

They built the new hearth that same day, on the ground behind the old shelf that had stood dry through every tide Gràinne had ever measured, and she carried the coal to it herself out of the pot rather than let any of her sons do it, and lit it there before dark. She lived twenty-five years more at that second hearth, old even by the reckoning of that hard coast, and she kept the same watch on the tide out of the same habit as before, though by then it mattered less to her personally than it had, for the ground behind the shelf ran back far enough that no tide she lived to see came near it again. But she taught the watch to her grandchildren all the same, walking the shingle with them at every high water the way her own mother had walked it with her, and telling them, in words her household kept long after she was gone from it, that the sea did not forget an inch it had once taken, and that a household on that coast that stopped watching the water was a household asking to wake and find it already indoors.

The deer of Eilean dhubh, cut off from the mainland pastures once the neck stopped drying at all but the very lowest ebbs, grew smaller across the lifetimes that followed, penned as they were on ground too small to feed a herd that had once ranged the length of two islands as one. Hunters who crossed to the island by boat in Gràinne's granddaughters' time, when a boat had become the only sure way across, brought back carcasses lighter than their grandfathers' tellings had led them to expect, and said among themselves, without much wondering at it, that an island shrunk to an island fed its deer accordingly. Gràinne herself did not live to see that told at the hearth; she died at the second house, by her own fire, in the five hundred and thirty-first year of the fathers' count, and was buried on the higher ground she had moved to rather than on the shelf that had been her people's for longer than any teller could reckon, which by then lay under the sea at every tide that came.

## II. Of Oisean and the Hide Boat

On the far side of the island, on the south shore where the water ran calmer and the land shelved gently rather than drowning by inches, a young man named Oisean grew up watching the sea for a different reason than Gràinne's people watched it. He did not care what it took. He cared what it held.

Oisean was born some seventy years after Gràinne, to a household of seal-hunters who worked the rocks and skerries of the mainland shore the way their fathers had worked them, waiting for the grey seals to haul out within reach of a spear thrown from dry land, and going home hungry on the seasons when the seals chose to haul out somewhere a spear could not reach. Oisean had watched, since he was old enough to be taken along and told to sit still, the greater number of seals that hauled out on Eilean mhin, the low island that lay off the south coast in plain sight of his own hearth on a clear day, further out than a man could wade and further out, so every hunter of his household had always said, than any boat of that coast could carry a man and bring him back.

There were no boats of that coast, in truth, worth much more than a log a boy might straddle to cross a burn in flood. Oisean's people knew hide, and knew how to stretch it and how to grease it against wet, for every hearth on that shore kept its winter cloaks of sealskin and knew the working of the stuff as well as any household on the island. What Oisean did, in his twenty-third year, was a thing none of his own people had thought to do with that knowledge before him, though afterward it seemed to every one of them a thing that should have been done long since: he built a frame of bent hazel, light and springy, shaped roughly to the outline of his own body doubled, and over it he stretched and stitched hides greased with seal fat at every seam, working alone at first, ashamed to be watched failing at a thing he could not himself have said would work.

It took him the better part of a season to make a hull he trusted to hold, and longer than that to trust himself to sit in it on open water rather than the calm inlet where he had first floated it and found, with some surprise, that it did not sink under him. His household thought him strange for the trying and stranger still when the trying went on past the point where a sensible man would have given it up; but no one stopped him, for it was known on that coast, as on every coast of the island, that fire and shelter were owed to any kin who asked, and a young man's strangeness was his own business so long as it fed no one badly.

He crossed to Eilean mhin in the calm of an early summer morning, alone, with nothing but the paddle he had shaped and a coil of plaited hide should the boat need mending on the far side, and he was gone from his own shore long enough that his mother had begun, by the tellers' account, to walk the beach and watch for him with a dread she did not speak aloud. He came back before dark with three seals lashed across the frame behind him and the boat riding low enough in the water that he had shipped a hand's depth over the gunwale more than once on the crossing back, and he stood on his own shore soaked to the skin and laughing, by every telling that has come down of that evening, in a way his household had not seen him laugh before.

It was, so far as any teller of that coast or any other has ever claimed otherwise, the first time anyone on Dia-thìr crossed open water on purpose, meaning to reach a place he could see but could not walk to, and meaning to come back from it the same day. What Oisean had proved, standing dripping on his own shore with three dead seals and a boat that had not failed him, was a thing simpler than any of his household at first understood it to be: that the water between two pieces of dry land need not be a wall. It could be crossed, if a man built the right thing to cross it in and trusted the building enough to sit inside it once it was afloat.

He did not stop at the one crossing. Within two summers he had built a second boat, larger, with room behind the paddler for a season's worth of dried meat and skins, and he began to camp on Eilean mhin rather than crossing and returning within a single day, staying out among the seal rocks for a stretch of good weather at a time and coming home only when the meat ran low or the weather turned against him. The camp he kept there, a low shelter of driftwood and hide pitched in the lee of a rise above the landing beach, came to be known among his own kin and the kin near them simply as the bothies, and it was kept a season at a time rather than year-round, for no household yet trusted itself to winter on ground with no fresh water of its own and no shelter but what could be carried across in a boat still small enough to overset in a bad sea.

Others followed him across in the years after, first his own brothers and then young men of neighbouring households who had watched the boats being built and reasoned, as Oisean himself had reasoned, that a thing built by hands could be built again by other hands. By the time Oisean was himself an old man, the crossing to Eilean mhin in the calm months was no longer spoken of as a strange thing a strange young man had once done, but as a thing the shore households of that coast simply did, the way they gathered shellfish or dried fish on racks, and the seal-bothies on Eilean mhin stood, each summer, ready to be thatched again and lived in until the weather sent the boats home. Oisean himself crossed and camped there most summers of his life, and died at last at his own hearth on the mainland shore, in his sixty-ninth year, with his grandsons already building boats of their own beside his, each one, so the tellers say, a little surer in the making than his had been, for they had his to look at and he had had nothing at all.

## III. Of Scàire and the Grey Bank

Further along the coast and further north than Oisean ever crossed, the water ran harder and the weather came in worse, and it was there, on the north shore, that a woman named Scàire took the boat further than any of her own people had yet trusted it.

She was born some sixty years after Oisean, into a household that had already, by her girlhood, taken up the making of hide boats after the manner that had spread up the coast from the south, though the boats of the north shore were built heavier and lower than Oisean's had been, for the water off that coast ran seldom calm and seldom warm even at midsummer, and a boat built light for a still southern crossing would not have lived an hour in it. Scàire grew up in those boats rather than merely watching them made, taken out by her father and her uncles from the time she was old enough to sit still and bail, and by the time she was a grown woman she handled a boat in a following sea as surely as any man of her household, which was, on that coast, saying a good deal.

The seals of the north shore did not haul out on any single island a man could reach and camp on in a day, as Oisean's did, but on a scatter of low skerries off Eilean fhada, bare rock standing clear of the water only at the ebb and swallowed whole at the flood, where the grey seals came each autumn in their hundreds to pup on ground no fox or wolf could reach. It was autumn work by its nature, for the seals came with the first of the year's gales and were gone again before the worst of the winter storms set in, and it was gale work by its nature too, for a crew that waited for calm weather to make the crossing waited, on that coast, for weather that autumn seldom gave.

Scàire went out with her father's crew from her sixteenth year, in boats that shipped water over the bow with every third or fourth swell and had to be bailed continually by whoever was not paddling, and she learned, the way every hunter of that coast learned, to judge by the smell of the wind and the run of the cloud which days would let a crew out and back before the gale that was always somewhere behind the weather caught up with them. It was not judgement any teller could set down as a rule, only as a knowing that a body earned by being wrong about it once or twice early and surviving the wrongness. Some years the judgement failed more often than others. The households of the north shore kept, without troubling to set it down as anything more than a shared memory, a rough reckoning of how hard a year had been by how many of its boats the autumn gales had held ashore rather than let out to the skerries at all, and the year Scàire turned thirty-eight stood, in the memory of anyone then alive, as the hardest of that kind: five boats out of every six kept on the beach the whole autumn through, and the seal harvest that year thin enough that more than one household went into the winter short of oil for its lamps.

She herself was master of her own boat and crew by then, her father dead some years and his place taken by her without much argument from anyone, for no one on that coast doubted, by that time, that Scàire read a north-shore sea as well as any man alive on it. It was on one of the crossings of her middle years, out on the skerries themselves with the seal-taking done and the crew waiting on the tide before the run home, that she climbed the highest of the bare rocks to look for the change in the weather her father had taught her to watch for, and saw, lying along the whole northern edge of the sea where sky and water ought to have met in a plain line, a grey that did not lift.

It was not fog as she knew fog, which came and sat on the land in the small hours and burned off with the sun. This lay always in the same place, along the same stretch of horizon to the north and, she came to notice on crossings after, along much of the eastern water as well, never closer and never further, a wall of grey that the brightest day never burned through and the hardest gale never blew away. She asked the older hunters of her own crew whether they had marked it before, and found that they had, in a loose way, the way a man marks a hill he has looked at all his life without ever once asking its name; no one on that coast had gone out far enough, or often enough, or with reason enough to look hard at it, to have made more of it than a grey bank that sat, as it had always sat so far as any of them could tell her, on the sea to the north and east and nowhere else.

Scàire looked at it a long while that day, from the highest of the skerries with the wind coming cold off it and the seal-blood still on her hands, and she did not go toward it, for there was nothing in the going that any purpose of hers required, and the boats she and her people built were built for the work they had, not for a water no one had yet had reason to cross. She told of it at her own hearth that winter and it went from there, as such things went on that coast, hearth to hearth along the shore until most who worked the northern water had heard some form of the telling: that there was a grey on the sea to the north that never lifted, that it stood always where it stood and moved for no wind, and that no boat of that coast, so far as anyone yet knew, had gone into it and come back to say what lay beyond.

She herself went out to the skerries most autumns of the rest of her life, gale years and calm years both, and grew old on that hard water the way Oisean had grown old on the gentle water of the south, dying at last at her own hearth in her sixty-third year, remembered by her own kin less for the grey bank, which by then every household of that coast had heard of and settled into simply knowing, than for the thirty-eight boats she had brought home whole across a working life on a sea that forgave very little.

## IV. Of the Weirs on the Abhainn ìseal

While the two shores learned the water each in its own way, a quieter change was working itself out along the banks of the longest river on the island, where it ran wide and slow through the low ground before losing itself in the sea.

The Abhainn ìseal had never, within any telling that reached that far back, run in quite the same bed for two generations together, spreading itself across the flat ground in wet years and drawing into a narrower, deeper channel in dry ones, until sometime in the years after Gràinne's death it settled, as rivers will when the ground beneath them has finally been worked to its own true shape, into the wide bed that the households along it came to know as simply the river, without further telling being needed to say which one was meant. It was in the side channels of that newly settled bed, the shallower cuts where the current ran gentler and the fish gathered thickest at certain seasons, that the first weirs of hazel stakes were driven.

No single teller is remembered as the one who thought of it, and no single household claimed the making the way Neachdan's household claimed the mason's fire; it was a thing that seems to have occurred to more than one household along that stretch of river at much the same time, once the bed had settled enough that a stake driven one autumn could be trusted still to stand there the next. The stakes were driven close together across the mouth of a side channel at the season when the fish ran thickest, angled to hold against the current, with gaps left for a man to reach in and take what the current had crowded against the barrier; and when the season's run was done the stakes were left standing rather than pulled, for a household that had once found the right place for a weir saw no reason to find it again from nothing the following year.

They were renewed rather than rebuilt, generation on generation, the households of that stretch of river driving fresh stakes into the same holes the old ones had rotted out of, so that a weir kept by a household thirty years on from its first driving stood, to any eye that measured it against the riverbank, on almost exactly the ground it had first stood on, though not one stake of the original driving remained in it. It became, without anyone remarking on it as a change worth remarking, the way that stretch of the Abhainn ìseal was fished, and children of those households grew up wading the shallows at weir-mending time the way children elsewhere grew up herding, knowing which hole took a stake easily and which fought it, long before they knew why the holes were where they were.

The salmon came up that river in their great runs each year at much the same season the seals hauled out on Scàire's northern skerries, and it was in the seven hundredth year of the fathers' count, the weirs by then old and well proven, that the households along the river first kept what came to be remembered as a feast of it rather than simply a harvest: households from up and down that stretch gathering at the largest of the weirs when the run was at its thickest, sharing out what the barriers had caught between more hands than any one household's own, and eating, by every account that survived of it, better in that one week than most of them ate in the whole of any other season. No single voice planned it, the tellers say; it grew the way the weirs themselves had grown, out of neighbours finding it easier to work a good run together than to guard it apart.

By then the neck where Gràinne had watched the tide lay drowned at every water, and the seal-bothies of Eilean mhin stood thatched each summer as a matter of course, and boats of one build or another put out from half the coasts of the island in weather that would once have kept every hearth ashore. A traveller of that year, walking the bank of the Abhainn ìseal at the height of the salmon feast, might have seen at the largest of the weirs a boy no older than six or seven wading out with his father's hand steadying him, driving a fresh stake into a hole worn smooth by stakes his father and his father's father had driven there before him, into water that ran, as it had run for longer than either of them could reckon, exactly where the river had chosen, at last, to run.
