# Of the Outpost and the Three Rings

*VE 2,524 – 2,678*

## I. Of the Wall on the Spur

For as long as any hearth of the Seann-Dhaoine could tell of itself, its people had kept to the shore. They dressed stone at Morn, dried fish over peat smoke in the seven pits of the kilns, launched hulls down the ramps at Brenn, raised round houses over a stranded whale's ribs, and buried pits of clay-lined store against the lean months, but whatever they raised, they raised within a morning's walk of salt water, and no telling of that shore said why, only that it had always been so. In the year the spur was first climbed, that had already stopped being true a season or two, though none of the diggers who broke ground on it that spring could have said when the stopping began.

The spur stood a day's walk inland from the nearest kiln, a shoulder of higher ground thrown out from the southern lowland where the grass thinned to gorse and the wind lost the salt smell it carried everywhere nearer the water. A household that had fished three generations out of the same cove sent its two eldest sons up first, to look before anyone thought to build, and what they came back saying was that a man standing on the spur's crown could see the whole lowland laid out below him the way a gull sees it, and see, too, any thing that moved across that lowland long before it reached the cove. Whether that was reason enough to raise a wall so far from the water, no one now living could tell straight, but a wall went up all the same, through two summers of carrying stone on sledges dragged by hand up a slope steep enough that more than one load went over sideways and had to be gathered again from where it had rolled.

The mason who set the first course, a woman past the middle of her years and known on that shore for the evenness of her fitting rather than for her name, chose to dress the stones only on the face that would show outward, leaving the inner face rough as it came from the ground. Asked why, by a younger man who thought it a waste of good stone to leave any of it unworked, she said only that a wall need be smooth where an enemy's eye would run along it looking for a hold to climb by, and rough where a friend's hand would rest on it going up the inside steps, and that a stone dressed on both faces for no reason but tidiness was a stone whose labour had gone begging. The younger man had no answer to that beyond a shrug, and the wall rose the rest of its course the same way, smooth without, raw within, and stood so through every season it was kept.

The gate they set to face inland, away from the sea the whole people had always kept its face toward, and this choice more than any other is remembered to have been argued over at length before the first lintel stone was lifted into place. One old man, who had fished the same cove as the mason's own kin and had never in his life built anything higher than a smokehouse, said plainly that a gate looking away from the water was a gate built by people who had forgotten what the water had always been to them, and that no good would come of turning a whole household's door from the one direction the sea had never yet failed to feed it from. The mason answered him, not unkindly, that the sea had never yet come up the spur to trouble anyone standing on it, and that whatever a gate here needed watching against would not come rowing. The old man went home unconvinced and is not remembered to have climbed the spur again in the years he had left, though he sent his own grandsons up to help finish the carrying whenever the work asked for more hands than the spur's own people could spare.

Through that first winter Làrach an Dùin-fhaire held no more than a handful of families, enough to keep the wall's steps swept of snow and a watch kept from first light to last, and it is told that the watch in those early months was kept less for any thing seen approaching than for the keeping's own sake, as though the people who had built so far from the water needed some task to fill the silence a coastal hearth never knew, the silence of ground that gave back no tide sound and no gull cry, only wind moving through gorse and, some nights, nothing at all. A woman who kept that first winter's watch longer than anyone else of her household, because sleep came harder to her than to the rest, used to say afterward that the quiet up there had a weight to it unlike any quiet the shore ever gave, and that she had grown, before the winter was out, almost to prefer it to the sea's own noise, a preference she never fully explained even to those who asked her outright what she meant by it.

By the second summer the gate itself was hung, a heavy door of oak planks pegged edge to edge and swung on a socketed post that two strong men together could just move on its hinge, and the day it first swung shut on its own weight without needing a shoulder to help it home is remembered as a small feast day among the households that had given hands to the work, though no telling now living can say which household first thought to mark it so, or what exactly was eaten or said. What is remembered plainly is that the old fisherman who had argued against the inland gate came up that day unbidden, stood at the threshold a long while without going either in or out, and at last put his own hand flat against the swung door and said nothing at all, a silence the young folk who saw it took, rightly or wrongly, for a kind of blessing withheld and given both at once.

Cattle were driven up the spur that same summer for the first time, a slow business of coaxing beasts used to level grazing up a slope steeper than any they had climbed before, and more than one animal balked outright at the gate itself, planting its feet and lowing until a rope round its horns and three men's shoulders together got it through. Once inside, though, the beasts settled to the thin grass within the wall readily enough, and a boy set to herd them there through that first season used to say the cattle seemed, within a few days, no longer to miss the sea grass they had grazed all their lives, a settling he thought stranger, in his own way, than anything the people themselves had done in coming up.

The steps that ran up the wall's inner face on both sides of the gate were cut narrow and set close, each no deeper than half a foot, so that a watcher could go up or down them at a run in the dark without needing to look at his own feet to find the next one, and it was the boy who herded the cattle, more often than any grown watcher, who was sent up and down them fastest, carrying word between the gate and whoever stood the height at any hour, since a boy's legs tired less of the running than a grown man's did and a boy could be spared from other work more readily than a man who was needed, besides, at the wall itself or out among the beasts. He came, before that first year was out, to know every uneven tread in both flights of steps by the feel of it under a bare foot, and is remembered to have said once, when an older cousin asked him how he never once stumbled going up in full dark, that his feet had simply learned the wall the way a hand learns a rope it has hauled on every day for a year, without his ever once sitting down to learn it on purpose.

It was that same boy, in the second autumn of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire's keeping, who first raised the cry that brought every watcher on the wall to the gate side at a run, for what he saw, coming across the lowland from the west in the last low light of an evening, was a line of shapes he took at first for a herd of deer moving faster and more evenly together than any deer he had ever watched cross open ground. The watch was called and held through that whole night, torches kept burning along the inner steps though no one on the wall could say for certain, once full dark had come down, whether the shapes were still out there or had never been more than deer after all, moving as deer sometimes did in a hard year when a whole herd shifted ground together ahead of an early frost. By first light nothing showed on the lowland at all, no track a careful eye could find once a party went down to look, and the matter was let go without ever being settled either way, though the boy himself held to his first telling of it all his life, and Làrach an Dùin-fhaire's watch, from that autumn on, was never again let lapse to a single pair of eyes the way it had sometimes been let lapse before.

## II. Of the Cistern and the Three Rings

Some distance off, on a height nearer the south-eastern coast, a greater work was rising in those same years, and the two crews that raised it never once, in any telling that has come down whole, met at either site or knew in full what the other built, so that each held for a long while to its own account of what was going up on the far height, some saying it no more than a second watch-wall like their own, others saying it something larger they could not picture without having seen it. It was, in the end, larger by a wide measure: three walls, ringed one within the other round a hilltop broad enough, once the innermost ring was closed, to hold four hundred people at once with their beasts and their stores besides, a scale no single household on that coast had ever raised to before.

The crew that raised it worked under a woman the tellings give no name to beyond what her own people called her, the one who reads the ground, for it was said of her that she could stand on a rise and see, before a single stone was laid, where a wall would hold and where the same wall built a spear's length to either side would slide with the first hard winter's frost, a reading she never once explained beyond saying that the ground told her plainly enough if a body only stood still long enough to be told. Under her eye the outer ring went up first, broad and low, more a barrier to slow an approach than to stop one outright, and behind it the middle ring rose higher and thinner, and behind that again the innermost ring stood tallest of the three, tall enough that a man standing at its base and looking up could not see over it without stepping well back.

The cistern was cut into the bedrock at the hill's own crown before either ring was finished, a basin hollowed by hand and fire together, heated stone after heated stone cracked loose from the rock with water thrown on while it still glowed, a labour that is remembered to have taken the better part of two whole seasons and to have blistered more hands than the rest of the building together. The woman who read the ground stood over that digging as closely as she stood over the walls, and is remembered to have said, when a digger asked how deep the basin need go, that it should go deep enough to hold water through a summer no rain fell in, though none then living had seen such a summer, and that a cistern cut shallow to save a season's labour was a cistern that would fail exactly the year it was most needed.

It filled for the first time the spring the innermost ring was closed, a slow filling from a spring found seeping through a crack near the basin's foot that no one had marked until the digging opened it wider, and the day the water stood a hand's depth over the basin's floor a child of one of the building households was let climb down on a rope to fetch up the first drawn measure in a skin, more for the wonder of the thing than for any thirst the height's own people yet had. The woman who read the ground tasted that first measure herself before anyone else touched it, held it in her mouth a long moment, and said only that it tasted of stone, which everyone standing by took as high praise from her, whatever it might have meant from anyone else.

Cattle were driven inside the rings that same year, a longer and steeper climb than the spur had asked of its own beasts, and more animals were lost on the way up, over loose scree that gave without warning under a hoof, than the spur's herders had ever lost driving theirs. A woman who lost two of her own household's beasts that climb is remembered to have said afterward that whatever the three rings kept out, they charged dear enough for what they let in, a saying repeated on that height for years after by anyone minded to grumble at the work the rings' upkeep still asked of them.

The inner buildings went up more slowly than the walls, round houses set close along the innermost ring's inner face, roofed in turf cut from the hill's own flank so that from a distance, before smoke rose from any of them, the whole crown of the hill showed as one unbroken slope of green, the houses hidden in the ground's own shape the way a bird's nest is hidden in a hedge. A household that moved up from the shore to take one of the first of these houses is remembered to have found, its first winter within the rings, that the wind that reached them there, filtered three times over by wall after wall, was gentler by far than any wind the same household had known at the water's edge, a mercy none of them had thought to look for in coming so far from the sea, though it was thought a poor trade by more than one older voice among them, weighed against all that the sea itself was no longer at hand to give.

The woman who read the ground grew old within the rings she had raised, and in her last years she took to walking the innermost ring alone each morning before the rest of the height was awake, a slow circuit with a stick to lean on that had once needed no stick at all, and more than one of the younger builders who saw her at it took to walking a length of the wall with her, for she said little on those mornings, but to be shown, by nothing more than where she chose to stop and rest a hand flat on the stone, which stretches of the three rings she still judged sound and which she had begun, in her own private reckoning, to distrust. She never said outright that any stretch of wall she had herself set was failing, only that a wall, like a person, aged unevenly, some lengths of it good for a hundred winters and others, laid the very same season by the very same hands, good for a good deal less, and that a height meant to hold four hundred people needed someone always walking it who could tell the difference before the ground told it plainer, in a wet spring, by giving way.

She died within the innermost ring in a season the tellings give no exact count of years for beyond saying she was old, and her own people buried her, at her own asking made long before, not outside the rings among the dead of the households that had come up from the shore, but within the second ring's shelter, close under a stretch of wall she had named to more than one listener, in her last winter, as the soundest work of her whole life. A cairn was raised over her there small enough not to trouble the ground the cattle grazed, and it is told that for as long as the height was kept, no herder let a beast graze close against that particular cairn, though none of them could have said, pressed for a reason, what harm they thought a hoof might do to stones that had already stood so many winters undisturbed.

## III. Of the Kerbed Way

Between the spur and the height a stretch of lower ground ran, marsh in its wettest reaches and stony moor in its driest, ground that had never before carried more traffic than a herder's own two feet, and it was across this ground, in the years Làrach an Dùin-fhaire's wall stood finished and the rings' innermost course still rose course by course on the height beyond, that a track was laid the like of which the Seann-Dhaoine had never built away from the shore in all the generations the tellings hold.

It began, by most accounts, as no more than a line of trodden ground worn by the few who had reason to go between the two rising works, a path that grew muddier each season as more feet used it, until a household living along its worst stretch, tired of hauling loads through ankle-deep mire every time the rain held more than a day, laid a scatter of flat stones across the softest ground simply to keep its own feet dry, with no thought, at the laying, of any greater work to follow. Others saw the dry going those few stones gave and laid more beside them, each household adding what it could spare of its own labour to the stretch nearest its own door, so that the track grew the way a shoreline midden grows, one household's contribution at a time, with no single hand ever holding the whole plan of it from one end to the other.

It was only some years into this scattered laying that anyone thought to kerb the edges, setting upright stones along both sides of the trodden way to hold the paving in place against the ground's own slow spreading, and the first stretch so kerbed held so much better through a wet winter than the unkerbed stretches on either side of it that the practice spread along the whole track within a few more seasons, each household that had laid its own piece coming back to add the kerb its neighbours had shown the worth of. A cart, once the kerbing held firm enough over a long enough stretch, could be dragged its width without a wheel sinking to the axle, and it is told that the first load ever so dragged, of stone meant for the height's own innermost ring, went across in a single day where the same load, hauled the old way over open moor, had once taken the better part of three.

The paving itself was never of one stone throughout, for each household cut what its own nearest quarrying gave, so that a traveller walking the whole way in one going would have passed, in a single day's walk, over pale stone and dark, over slabs broad as a door and stretches of smaller stones fitted edge to edge like the pieced lids of a storage pit, the whole track a record, to anyone minded to read it so, of every household that had ever laid a hand to it, though none of those households, laying its own short stretch, is remembered to have thought of the work as anything grander than keeping its own feet dry between one season and the next.

A cart was driven the whole length of the track for the first time in the year the last of its middle stretches was kerbed, laden with dressed stone meant for the height's outer ring and drawn by two yoked oxen a household near the spur had raised from calves for no other purpose, and the driver of that first cart, a young man who had never before taken a load further than the short haul from his own quarrying to his own household's wall, is remembered to have walked the whole way at the oxen's heads rather than ride, watching the wheels for any give in the paving that might tip the load, and to have found, at the far end, that not one stone of it had shifted the whole way, a steadiness he called out to the height's own people before he had so much as unyoked the beasts, as proud of the road's holding as he might have been of a wall he had raised himself.

The last stretch to be laid ran up the final rise toward the height itself, steeper than any ground the track had yet crossed, and the crew that closed it set its final slab, by every account that has come down whole, tilted a little rather than level as the rest of the track had been laid, so that the paving's whole run, seen from below, seemed to lift the eye up the slope and on past the height itself toward the high country further inland, the central mountains standing pale and distant beyond the nearer ground the way they stood on every clear day from anywhere on that coast, though no teller of that laying ever said the tilt was meant to point there, only that the stone was set so and has lain so ever since, unmoved by any season that has passed over it, and that whoever set it last, whether by design or by nothing more than the ground's own lie under that final slab, is not remembered by any name the tellings have kept.

## IV. Of the Turning

What passed between the households of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire and the households of the three rings in the years both works stood finished, no telling that has come down whole says plainly, for the two never worked one crew and kept, even after the track joined them, more to their own hill each than to any single people shared between the two heights. But it was of both together, and of no other work the Seann-Dhaoine had raised before them, that the shore hearths first began to say the thing that afterward became a saying repeated the length of that coast: that a people who had always kept their faces to the sea had, in those years, without any single voice among them ever quite deciding it aloud, turned their faces from it instead, toward the ground behind them and the high country beyond that ground, a turning no shore mother ever fully explained to a child who asked why the family's own door, that had opened on the water three generations running, now opened instead on the path inland.

It was not that the sea was given up. The kilns at Morn still smoked through every drying season, the ramps at Brenn still launched their hulls, and the households of the two new heights sent down, every year, kin enough to keep a hand in the old work and bring back dried fish and traded shell for a hearth too far from the water to gather its own. But the gate that had once seemed, to the old fisherman who mistrusted it, a strange thing to build facing away from the only direction that had ever fed his people, no longer seemed strange to anyone climbing the spur by the year the last slab of the track was laid, and a child born within either ring of the height, or within the spur's own wall, grew to walking age never once having slept in a house that heard the tide.

The last stone was set into the kerbed way's final stretch on a still morning with no wind off the sea to carry any salt smell up that high, and the small crew that set it, having no more stone of its own to add and no further stretch left unpaved between the two heights, stood a while looking back the way the track had come, down across the lower moor toward the shore neither height could any longer see from where they stood, and then turned, without any word the tellings have kept, to look instead the other way, up the track's own tilted last slab and past it, toward the high pale country that had stood at the edge of every clear day's sight for as long as any of them had lived, and had never before that morning seemed a thing worth walking toward.
