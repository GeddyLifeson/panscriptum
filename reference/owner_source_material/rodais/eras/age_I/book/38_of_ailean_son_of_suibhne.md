# Of Ailean Son of Suibhne

*VE 5,300 – 5,324*

## I. Of a Child Born Slow to Speak

Suibhne was a boy of eighteen, born the same year Cairbre and the ten who had gone with him into the hills in the crossing season failed to come home, when he walked with the search that at last found their bones strung out along the cats' road at wide distances, as if the men had scattered from one another at the end rather than kept together. He carried nothing of the vein with him on that walk; there was no custom of it, and none of the households along the road had ever heard that fire of any kind would serve against what kept that road at that season. He came home from it saying little, by the account of those who knew him, and it was some years after, in his own house at Tobar dhìreach with a wife new to the keeping, that his son was born.

Ailean was a big child from the first week, heavier in the arm and the leg than any infant the women who helped at his birth could readily match against their own memory, and slow to close his hand round a finger held out to him and slower again to let it go once he had closed it, so that his mother said of him, half in complaint and half in something nearer pride, that he had been born holding on. He was slow with words as he grew, keeping to single ones while children a season younger than him at the neighbouring hearths were already stringing sentences, and there were those among his own kin who wondered aloud, in the way kin will wonder aloud over a child too young to answer for himself, whether the line had at last thrown up a son too plain to be trusted with the keeping; his mother put a stop to that talk wherever she heard it, saying that a slow tongue was no mark against a steady hand, and pointing to his hands themselves, broad already at six years old and thickening year on year after, as proof enough of what sort of man he would be.

He had, from as early as anyone could afterward call to mind, an interest in the fire-pot that went beyond a child's ordinary fascination with flame. Other children of that hearth had been drawn to the coloured pieces in the leather bag as toys, turning them over for their colours and their smoothness and losing interest once the colours proved to give nothing back to the hand; Ailean, from the age he could sit upright unsupported, would be found by the pot for whole mornings together, not reaching for it or troubling it, only sitting near enough to feel what heat came off it, watching the low steady flame the line had kept without a break since the mason first carried it home under his cloak. His father found him there so often that he came in time to leave the boy be, saying to his own wife that a child could do worse than keep company with the one thing in the house that had outlasted every one of their forefathers, and that if the boy meant to sit and stare at it until the staring had taught him some sense, there was no harm and might be some good in letting him.

He grew into a young man built along the same broad lines as the child had promised, tall by the reckoning of the high valleys and heavier through the shoulder than his father, with hands that the old women of the household said, more than once and not always meaning it kindly, were the mason's own hands come round again after five thousand years, hands shaped for a wedge and a maul rather than for the small close work the keeping of the coals more often asked. His speech never fully caught the pace of other men's; he answered slowly, and thought visibly before he did it, so that strangers meeting him at the recital sometimes took his slowness for dullness and were corrected only once they had put a question to him worth a real answer, for what he gave back, when he gave it, was seldom the thing a quicker man would have said first. Among his own kin this was known well enough not to need remark, and the reciters who taught him the Line of the Mason found him no slower a pupil for it than any other, only one who wanted the words said over more times before he would trust himself to say them back whole.

## II. Of the Rainbow That Would Not Light

Suibhne kept the bag of two coals from his own father's death, a fire coal grown ordinary through generations of handling since Seathan's day and a rainbow no keeper between Seathan's household and his own had ever once coaxed into flame; and he kept, as every keeper before him had kept, the custom of trying it, some years with more hope in the trying than others. He was not, by nature, a man given to running after a thing that had already refused him a dozen times, and there were long stretches in his own keeping, a decade together on occasion, when the rainbow sat untouched in the bag while he gave his mind to the herds and the hearth and the long slow business of a household growing round him; but the recital came once a year, and every year at the recital the reciters gave the tellings of the six lightings in their order, and every year, hearing his own forefathers named one after another, Suibhne felt the old itch return to try the seventh again himself before the next telling came round.

The height he tried first, in the second year of his keeping, climbing to Binnean a' Chlachair in a morning wind that near took him off the summit and dropping the rainbow from the height of his own arm over the same steep side Fearghas had dropped the snowflake from, watching it fall the whole way to the stones below without so much as a flicker in it. The river he tried the year after, kneeling at the same crossing Eòghann had knelt at, and threw it into the current where the silver still burned beneath the water in its low steady flame, so that for a breath the rainbow lay beside a coal that was already alight, dark against bright, before the current carried it on downstream to be fished out cold some way below the house. The sea he tried once, on a hard grey autumn when the household could spare him a week for the walk, wading to his knees in water cold enough to numb the foot and throwing it out past where the gold was said to have taken fire on the swell in Aodh's day, and watched it sink without a mark on it the same as every coal before it that had not been the gold.

He tried the earth twice in different years, once digging a hole no deeper than a man's knee and once, in the year he judged himself old enough to know better and dug it anyway, a hole as deep as the grave Brian had dug for the black coal three and a half thousand years before, laying the rainbow in it and covering it over and going back for it the following dawn to find it cold as the clay around it had been cold. By blood he would not try it; that trial he left alone all his life, saying to his own wife once, in a rare unguarded moment by the fire, that a man ought not go looking to open his own hand for a coal that had already shown him six times over that it wanted no single element and no single hurt, and that he had no wish to carry a scar for nothing the way Cormac had carried his for good cause. The small idle way that had served the fire coal he never once thought to try, for that way, by its nature, could not be gone looking for; a man does not set out with a purpose to stumble on a thing by accident, and Suibhne, trying the rainbow at all, was always trying it with a purpose, which was, though he never said so in these words, half the reason it never answered him.

He was four-and-forty years old, going grey at the temple and thicker through the middle than the man who had climbed Binnean a' Chlachair in his youth, when he took the rainbow north to Doire ghlas for the first and only time in his life, walking the three days alone with the coal wrapped in a fold of cloth inside his shirt against his chest, reasoning, as his son would reason after him though by a longer road, that the place where the Keeper had first spoken might hold some virtue the shore and the height and the river did not. No fire did he build in the grove, having nothing to ask that a fire would serve to ask it with; he only sat under the oaks a night and a day with the coal held loose in his open palm, watching it for any change of colour or warmth that his own eyes might have missed a hundred times before in duller light, and saw none, and walked home again three days further with it cold in the same fold of cloth it had travelled north in.

He told the household, on his return, that he did not think the rainbow meant to answer to him, and that he did not know, past a certain point, whether a man ought to go on asking a question that had by then been asked in every way a man's own life could think to ask it. He said this at his own hearth and not at the recital, for he had never been a man to trouble a crowd with his own private failures, and it was among the last things of any length that Ailean, then sixteen years old and sitting near the fire-pot as he had sat by it since infancy, ever heard his father say on the matter. Suibhne died two years after, in his forty-sixth year, of a fever that came on him hard in a wet spring and took him inside a fortnight, quick enough that there was no long dying to make a telling of; Ailean, eighteen years old and already the broadest man at that hearth though not yet its keeper by any word said over him, sat with his father through the worst of the last nights and heard nothing from him about the rainbow at all, only the ordinary things a dying man says to a son he trusts to carry on the herd and the house without him.

## III. Of Lìobhan and the Thought That Came

Ailean had married the year before his father died, taking Lìobhan of a household two valleys west that kept no coal of its own and had never, by her own telling, expected to marry into a keeping at all; she had known him three summers at the recital before either of them said anything of marriage, drawn, as she told her own daughter, less by his slow careful speech than by the way he sat with animals and small children alike, as a man sits who has never once in his life needed to raise his voice to be minded. She came to Tobar dhìreach a bride of fifteen, young even by the valley's own reckoning, and found herself within the year keeping house at the hearth of the fire-pot, learning from Suibhne's own widow, in the short time that remained to teach her, how the pot was fed and how the bag was kept and what was and was not said of its contents to a stranger at the door.

Their daughter was born the same year Suibhne died, in a wet autumn that the household afterward remembered chiefly for having buried one keeper and welcomed one child within the turn of a single season; they named her Gormshuil, for the colour of her eyes at birth, which Lìobhan's own mother swore had never once in all her years attending births looked so blue so soon. Ailean was eighteen that year, a father and, by his father's death, the keeper of the bag as well, though he did not think of himself in those words for some time after, going on much as he had gone on before, minding the herd and sitting his evenings by the pot with his daughter more often than not asleep against his shoulder.

He tried the rainbow himself, in the first years of his keeping, much as his father had tried it before him, though with less patience for the long solitary journeys his father had made of it; he threw it in the river once and dropped it from the height once and dug it into the earth once, in each case finding it as cold afterward as his father had always found it, and came away from each trial a little more short with himself for having expected, however faintly, some different end to it than the end six generations of keepers before him had already reached. It was Lìobhan, by her own account given years later to her own grandchildren, who first said aloud to him what he had by then half thought and not yet let himself say: that a coal made, by the Keeper's own telling, of every colour together might not be a coal that any single element, taken alone, would ever own as its own, any more than a man born of two households could be said to belong wholly to either one.

Ailean turned that thought over through the better part of a winter, by the fire and at the herding and on the long walks a shepherd takes alone with sheep for the only company, and it grew in him slowly, the way his own words had always grown in him slowly, until it had settled into something nearer a certainty than a guess: that the six coals had each answered a single spirit because each of them, sea and sky and earth and blood and river and the small fire in a man's own hand, was a thing wholly on its own; the seventh held all six spirits at once, and so could not be woken, he came to believe, by offering it to any one of them alone, any more than a man could satisfy six hungry guests by setting a single dish before whichever one happened to be nearest the door. If the rainbow held every spirit, he reasoned, then it wanted every spirit answered at once, brought together in the one place and the one hour, so that whatever bound the coal shut might hear all six speak to it together rather than one after another across a hundred separate years.

He said as much to Lìobhan on an evening in his three-and-twentieth year, turning the rainbow over in his own broad hand by the fire while Gormshuil, five years old by then, slept curled against her mother on the far side of the hearth, and Lìobhan, hearing him say it slowly and carefully as he said everything, told him only that it sounded to her like a thing worth the trying, since nothing else in six generations had been. He did not go to the recital that year with any announcement of what he meant to do; he told the reciters only, when his turn came to stand and give his portion of the Line, that he meant in the year ahead to light the six again himself, one by one, by each one's own old way, and carry them living to wherever the seventh might at last be answered, and the reciters, who had heard bolder claims than this from keepers before him come to nothing, said only that they would wait to hear how it went before they put any new line into the telling.

## IV. Of the Six Kindled Again

Ailean began with the gold, in the last warmth of that same year, walking the day's road north to the shore with a fresh piece of the vein's colour carried loose in his palm the whole way, as Aodh had carried the seven before him, and a clay pot slung at his hip with a lid of green turf cut that same morning to fit its mouth. The northern sea in that season ran grey and short-tempered, breaking hard on the shingle under a sky the colour of wet slate, and he stood a long while at the water's edge before he waded in; it was the plain cold of it that held him back, and no fear of what would happen, for no telling of Aodh's day had ever troubled to mention the cold. Then he threw the coal out past the breaking water with an underhand cast that sent it further than a smaller man could have managed, and watched it strike the swell and take, exactly as the telling gave it, a flame the colour of its own sheen that rode the heave of the sea bright gold until a wave rolled over it; only this time, where Aodh had let it burn out on the water and gone home with an empty hand, Ailean waded in after it to the waist, careless of the cold, and caught it up still burning between his cupped hands the way a man catches a moth without crushing it, and carried it, dripping and alight, back up the shingle to the waiting pot, where it went on burning under its lid of turf all the long walk home, fed each evening with driftwood he gathered as he went.

Before the winter closed the height he climbed to Binnean a' Chlachair, carrying the second pot on his back beside the first, and stood in a wind cold enough to make his own teeth ache exactly as it had made Fearghas's teeth ache, and let the snowflake coal fall from the height of his own arm as his forefather had let it fall, and watched it catch fire halfway down the steep side and burn white against the grey rock in the failing light. Going down after it took him longer than the wading had taken, the ground there being steeper and the light worse; he found it still burning where it had fetched up against a shelf of stone, and lifted it with a flat piece of slate into the second pot, and carried both fires down off the height together in the last of that day's light, the gold and the white swaying against each other in their pots on his shoulders with every step.

The black he woke at home, needing no journey for it, digging a hole beside the old slab as deep as a grave in the last week before the ground froze hard, and laying the third coal in it and covering it over as Brian had covered his five, and going back to it at first light the next morning to find it warm in his hand and glowing at its heart the dark red Brian's own telling had given it, so that he had three fires burning in three pots under his own roof before the shortest days of that year were out, and Lìobhan said afterward that the house that winter never once in her memory stood so bright of an evening, with three low flames instead of one keeping the dark back from the walls.

He woke the red-brown in the early spring, alone in the yard behind the house on a morning grey with the last of the frost, setting the fourth coal on a flat stone as Cormac had set his and striking it with a hammer meant for splitting kindling, and the hammer turned on the coal's hard face as it had turned on Cormac's and came down across the back of his own left hand, laying it open near to the bone; he stood a long moment watching his own blood run down over the coal on the stone before the pain of it reached him at all, and where the blood touched the coal a flame came up the colour of the coal, deep and warm as firelight seen through oak leaves, and Ailean lifted it into its pot with his good hand while the other bled freely down his sleeve, and only then went in to have Lìobhan bind the wound, by which time the bleeding had slowed of its own accord and the scar it left, when it healed, sat white and puckered across the back of his hand much as the old tellings said Cormac's own scar had sat across his.

He woke the silver at the river below the house on an evening in early summer, kneeling at the same crossing his own grandfather's grandfather's grandfather had knelt at and drinking from his cupped hands before he threw the fifth coal into the current, less from any need to test the water's taste, which every child of that hearth had known from infancy, than from a wish to do the thing whole, in the manner the telling gave it, rather than skip to the part that mattered; the coal took fire as it went under exactly as Eòghann's had taken fire, burning silver on the riverbed while the current ran over it and could not put it out, and Ailean lifted it out with a shallow wicker scoop he had made for the purpose and set it, wet and still alight, into the fifth pot.

The sixth he woke without meaning to, on a warm evening near midsummer, sitting on his own doorstone with the last fragment of the old fire coal crumbling to dust between his fingers as he turned over in his mind the whole year's work and what remained of it, thinking, as it happened, of nothing at all to do with fire; his fingers closed the way a hand closes on its own account when a man's mind has gone elsewhere, and snapped without his asking them to, and the dust on them caught and flared red-gold for the length of a breath before he beat it out against his coat from old habit though it left, as it had left Seathan, not a mark on him. He sat looking at his own open hand a good while by the last light, and then went in and told Lìobhan only that the sixth was done, and did not trouble to say how, for it seemed to him, once it was over, too small and too accidental a thing to make a telling of, though Lìobhan drew the whole of it out of him before the night was over all the same.

## V. Of the Road North

Six fires stood that summer under one roof for the first time since the making of the world, each in its own clay pot with its own lid of turf, fed morning and evening by a man who had by then, without quite noticing the change come over him, stopped sleeping a full night through, waking instead at odd hours from long habit to see that none of the six had failed. He kept them through the last weeks of that summer at Tobar dhìreach, going about the ordinary business of the herd and the house by day and tending the pots by evening, while Lìobhan watched him grow thinner and quieter across those weeks and said nothing against it, having by then no more doubt than he had that the year's work was not yet finished. Gormshuil, six years old that summer, was kept from the pots by her mother's word, though she was found more than once crouched at a safe distance watching the lids lifted and the low strange colours beneath them, gold and white and the black coal's low red glow, silver, and the deep red-brown of the fifth, with the plain wide-eyed attention of a child who knows well enough that no other house in her own memory has ever kept the like.

In the last week of an t-Iuchar he set out for the grove, carrying the six pots slung in pairs from a pole across his shoulders, three to a side, with the seventh coal, the rainbow, wrapped in its old fold of cloth inside his shirt against his chest where his father had once carried it the same way. Lìobhan walked with him as far as the last hearth before the moor, carrying Gormshuil on her hip, and said little at the parting beyond that he should mind his feet on the northern slopes, which were wet that year even in high summer, and that she would keep the house fire as she had always kept it until he came home to it. Three days he walked alone, the pole's weight settling into a rhythm across his shoulders that he found, by the second day, he no longer noticed except when he set it down, and slept each night in the lee of whatever rock or bank offered itself, tending the six fires by starlight before he lay down and again before first light, so that he came into each new day already a little short of sleep and grew no less short of it as the days went on.

He came down into Doire ghlas on the twenty-first day of an t-Iuchar, in the year the reciters give as five thousand three hundred and twenty-four, with the light already going long and gold through the oaks and no wind at all in the crowns of the trees, a stillness he had not looked for after everything the tellings said of the gale that had met Diarmad in that same grove four thousand years before. There he set the six pots down in a ring among the roots of the oldest oak he could find, and lifted their lids one after another, and stood back to watch six fires stand up together out of the leaf-litter in their six colours, gold and white and the black coal's deep red and the red-brown of his own blood and the river's silver and the clear fire-colour that had once been dust on his own hand, closer together than any six of them had stood since the day each was first found. Never in his life had he seen more than three of them burning under one roof at once, and he stood a long while looking at the ring of them before he set the rainbow down in the bare ground at its centre and sat down himself beside it to wait, for every telling that had come down to him said that this was the place the Keeper came, if it came at all.

It was there before the light had wholly gone, standing among the oaks the height of a tall man and made, as the old telling had it, of smoke that did not move with the smoke of his own six fires though that smoke blew past it steadily on whatever small air still stirred in the grove. Ailean looked at it a long moment before he found the words his father had never had cause to use and his own slow tongue had never once needed until that hour.

"You have brought them all," it said.

"All I could," said Ailean. "The last will not light."

It said nothing to that for a moment, and the six fires around him burned on without a sound in the stillness of the grove, gold and white and black-red and the river's silver and the dark warm red-brown and the last clear flame that had lived thirty years in a bag before it ever came to Ailean's own hand. Then it came forward into the ring, stooping low over the bare coal at its centre where Ailean had set it, and where it stooped the rainbow took fire.

Ailean said afterward, to the few he ever spoke of it to at all, that he could not have named the colour of that flame to save his life, for it would not hold still long enough to be named, running through every colour the six fires around it held and others besides that he had no word for; and the six fires themselves, gold and white and black and red-brown and silver and the clear fire-red, leaned in toward it all together where they stood in their ring, the way grass in six different places leans the same way in one wind, and for the first time since the making of the world all seven coals of the island burned together, each out of its own spirit and all of them now out of one flame besides.

"Stay with them," the Keeper said, "until they are cold."
