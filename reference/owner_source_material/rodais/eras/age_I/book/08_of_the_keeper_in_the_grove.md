# Of the Keeper in the Grove

*17 an Gearran, VE 890*

## I. Of the Fire Under the Oaks

Three days he had walked, north and west, out of the high valleys where every hearth knew his face and his question both, and into a country that grew stranger the further he went from Tobar dhìreach: the heath thinning, the birch giving way first to hazel and then, on the last day, to oak, standing thick and old in a hollow where the wind funnelled down off the moor above it and never seemed to rest. He had gual bogha-froise loose in his palm inside the fold of his cloak, as his mother had bidden him, though for most of his life he had carried it in a small bag at his belt, and his food was three days gone and his feet were three days blistered, and he came down into the grove at Doire ghlas in the last light with the gale already roaring in the crowns of the oaks over his head.

He had meant, that first evening, only to find shelter and go on looking in the morning, though he could not have said what he thought there was to find. A herder of the northern moor had told him once, in the way men tell a thing they do not quite believe themselves, that there was a grove far to the north-west where the wind talked if a man sat still enough to hear it, and Diarmad had carried that the way he carried every scrap anyone would give him, turned it over on the long road the way he turned the coal over in his hand, and come, in the end, because it was the last thing left that no one had yet laughed him out of trying.

He had been asking since he was seven years old, and he was seven-and-twenty now, and in that whole span he had learned exactly one thing worth the learning: that a question asked often enough stops sounding like a question to the people who hear it and starts sounding like a habit, the way a limp stops sounding like pain to those who have watched a man walk with it for twenty years. The fire-bearers who carried the flame from Tobar dhìreach out to the last farm before the moor only shrugged when he asked them, and said the fire was the fire, colour or none, and warmed a hand the same either way. Travellers who came up from the eastern farms with salt and hides to trade looked at him the way a man looks at a child who asks why the sky is up. The old women at his own hearth, who kept every other telling of the valley word for word back past memory, had patted his head when he was small and, later, when he was not small any longer and went on asking, stopped patting it and started laughing instead, not unkindly, the way a house laughs at the one habit of its own that it has stopped expecting to outgrow. Only his mother had never laughed. She had never had an answer for him either, but she had never once told him to stop asking, and it was the memory of her face, more than any hope of an answer, that had kept the question alive in him after every other mouth in the valley had worn it smooth with repeating.

The trunk of a fallen oak lay along the hollow's lowest ground, its roots torn up in some older storm into a wall of black earth and white wood taller than a man, and in its lee the wind dropped enough that a small fire might live if it was fed with care. He gathered what dry twigs the ground gave him, oak mostly, snapped short, and built them up the way his mother had taught him when he was small enough to sit at her knee and watch her hands do it before his own were old enough to be trusted with the work, a little tent of the driest first and the damper laid to catch after. No new fire was struck. He had carried an ember the whole way, in a clay pot packed with moss, the way the fire-bearers carried it to every hearth from the northern moor to the eastern farms, and he tipped it out now into the twigs and blew until they caught, and sat back on his heels with his hands out to the small heat of it while the gale went on tearing through the oaks above him as if the grove were a thing to be pulled apart.

He took gual bogha-froise out of its hide and held it up before the little flame, the way he had held it up before every fire he had ever sat beside since he was seven years old, turning it slowly so that the colours ran across it and would not settle into any one of them, gold sliding into red and red into a blue he had no better word for, and none of it staying long enough to be named. It burned black-flamed like any other coal when it was thrown into a fire whole, the way the whole seven had burned black the first night the mason ever fed his own fire from the vein; every hearth in the high valleys knew that story, and every hearth had heard, sooner or later, that Diarmad of Tobar dhìreach still asked why the colour went out of it. He thought of none of it now, sitting there warm at last after three cold days on the road, his mind gone as empty as a man's mind goes when the walking is done and the fire is caught; and it was in that thoughtless moment, with the coal turning in his fingers and the small fire streaming sideways in the wind that came down through the roots, that he looked up and found someone standing across the fire from him.

It was the height of a tall man, and it was made of grey smoke, such as a peat fire gives when it is banked low and burns slow through a wet night, and it stood in the middle of the gale and did not stir. Diarmad's own little fire streamed flat along the ground under the wind's hand, the flame laid nearly sideways, the smoke of it torn away as fast as it rose; and the figure across from him stood in that same wind as still as a standing stone, its grey unmoving, no fold of it lifting, no edge of it fraying into the air the way smoke frays into air everywhere else in the world. The oaks roared. The dead leaves went past between them in streams, low along the ground, and not one passed through where the figure stood; they went round it, the way water goes round a rock in a stream, and came together again on the far side.

He did not run. The tellers make much of that afterward, when the tale is given at the hearth in the dark of the year: that a man alone at night, far from any house he knew, with a shape out of nowhere standing in his own fire's light in a wind that should have scattered it to nothing, did not run. He had asked his question too long and been laughed at too often to let a wonder pass him by unquestioned, whatever it cost him to stay and ask it; or perhaps, and this is also told, he was too weary from the road to find the strength for fear. He sat where he was, with the coal still warm in his closed hand, and looked at it a long while before he found his voice, and when he found it, it did not shake as much as he had thought it would.

"What are you?" he said.

"I am Coimhdeach na Fine," it said. Its voice was quiet and very clear, and the wind did not carry it away, though it carried away everything else in that grove that had a voice to carry, the crack of the fire, the roar of the oaks, his own breath when he let it out. "The keeper of the kin. I watch over the people of this island for the one who made them."

"Are you a god?"

"No."

"A ghost, then."

"Neither. I was made out of the smoke of the first coal he woke. I have watched your line since the mason." It was still a moment, the grey of it neither settling nor stirring, and then it said, "You have a question. Ask it."

Diarmad held up the coal in his hand, so that what little light his fire still threw caught the edge of it and the colours moved across Coimhdeach na Fine's stillness the way they moved across everything else he had ever held it up to. "Why are there colours in it?" he said. His voice had steadied now, and there was something in it that had not been there when he asked the boatmen and the fire-bearers and the old women at every hearth from his own valley to the last farm before the moor, something that had learned, over twenty years of being laughed at, not to hope for an answer and to ask anyway. "What are they for?"

## II. Of the Telling

Then Coimhdeach na Fine told him.

It did not tell it the way a teller tells a tale at a hearth, with the shape of a beginning and an end laid out for the listener to walk between. It came to him instead the way a fire comes up in a room full of smoke, all at once and everywhere, so that afterward he could never say in what order he had learned it, only that by the time the grove went quiet around him again he knew it whole, the way a man knows his own name.

He saw sparks flying wide from a coal broken open in a god's hand, and the sparks did not fall. A cold came over him in that seeing, though his own small fire still burned at his knee, the cold of the dark before there was any world to be warm in, and he watched the sparks hang and catch and become, one by one, the very stars that hung now over the roaring oaks above his head, so that when he looked up in the middle of the telling he found himself looking at the same fire twice, once remembered and once real. The weight of hills pressed up out of a black coal by two thumbs was in his own hands as he felt it, though his hands held nothing but gual bogha-froise, no bigger than an egg. A grey sea opened before him with no land in it anywhere, and a last coal held up over the water and let burn, and drops of fire falling from it one at a time into the grey, each drop hissing and hardening where it struck, until a rock stood in the sea, and then a hill, and then mountains he knew the shape of from every ridge he had ever walked, though he had never once thought before to ask how they had come to stand there.

He knew, without being told it in any order he could afterward have set down, that there was more folded into the coal in his own closed hand than the black weight of the ground alone: a colour for the sea and a colour for the air, a colour for the running water and a colour for the blood in a living thing, and last of all the one he held, that took every colour and kept none, holding whatever the other six were only once they were together. He did not see each in its turn the way a teller lays out a telling for a child, one thing and then the next; he felt them the way a man feels the separate aches of a body he has driven too hard on a long road, all at once, none of them waiting politely for the others to be named first. The names came to him afterward, in the days and years he had left to turn them over; that night in the grove he had only the feeling of a great deal folded very small, the way a whole winter's warmth is folded small into one banked coal under its blanket of ash.

He saw people come up out of the heather like grass after rain, blade after blade, too many to count and each its own, blinking in a light they had never yet opened their eyes to, the soil still on their bare feet. He had heard old women call the folk of the island the green grass of the island all his life and thought it only a manner of speaking, the kind of thing said at a hearth because it had always been said; now he saw it, or something that was not seeing and was not less than seeing, and understood for the first time that it had once been meant whole: that the people of Dia-thìr had grown up out of the island itself as grass grows, root and blade together, and that this was the oldest ground of the saying every hearth on the island knew without asking where it came from: *we did not come; we were here*.

And then, somewhere in the telling, though he could not have said where the joy of it turned, he felt something close and hot behind his own eyes that was not the fire's heat, and knew it for shame before he understood what he was ashamed of. He was seeing a hillside torn open by a flood, long before the mason, long before any fire burned on the island at all, and a child of the old godfolk standing in the raw bank with her cloak filled with stones that shone where no stone should shine, gold and silver and white-flecked and red-brown and the black that took a polish like still water at night; and he watched, helpless in the seeing the way a man is helpless in a bad dream, while that whole valley turned the stones over in the sun and struck them together and broke a tooth on one and cut a shape into another and threw it down in disgust, and called them, in the end, the droppings of the mountain, fit for children to play with and no one else, and let the heather grow back over them until there was no one left alive on the island who remembered they had ever been found. It was his own blood in that valley, though bloodlines meant little enough in days before any line was counted; it was his people, whatever people meant so long before there were hearths to keep the count of a people at all, and they had held the whole of what he had spent his own life since he was seven years old asking after, held it in their two hands in the sun, and let it fall back into the ground unasked.

"Was he angry?" Diarmad said, when he could make himself say anything at all. His voice came out smaller than he meant it to, a boy's question more than a man's. "The one who made it. Was he angry, that they let it lie?"

The grey shape across the fire did not answer at once, and when it did there was something in the stillness of its voice that might, in a living face, have been the ghost of a smile. "He was not," it said. "He watched it from his father's hall, and he laughed, as a father laughs who sees his child set aside the finest gift of the feast to play with the wrapping instead. A gift that is not opened is still given. He could wait. The coal was in no hurry, and neither was he."

All that Coimhdeach na Fine told him that night Clann a' Chlachair kept after, word for word, and carried it in time to every hearth in the hill country that would listen, so that within a few lifetimes there was hardly a fire lit from the northern moor to the eastern farms where a child could not say some part of it back before he was old enough to understand what he was saying. It is not told twice here. What is told is only this: that it was long past the middle of the night before the telling was done, and that Diarmad sat afterward without speaking for longer than he had sat in silence in the whole of his walk north, while his fire burned down to a red eye in grey ash and the gale went on unwearied above him, and that when at last he did speak, it was the one question that had followed him three days north and would follow him three days home again.

## III. Of the Riddle

"He hid it," Diarmad said at last. "Why would he hide a gift?"

"So that it would be found," said Coimhdeach na Fine. "He said to me once: a gift that is not opened is still given."

"Then how is it opened? The mason burned the coals and they burned like any coal. We have burned them since, at every hearth in the valleys, and the colours go out of them every time, and nothing is left but heat like any other fire's heat." He held up gual bogha-froise again, though there was little enough light left in his own fire to show its colours by now, only the last red glow under the ash. "How is it opened?"

"Because you burn them all in the one fire," said Coimhdeach na Fine, "and none of them in its own."

Diarmad waited, but nothing followed that could be called an answer to it, not at once; Coimhdeach na Fine only stood as it had stood the whole of that long night, grey and upright and unmoved by the wind that moved everything else in that grove, and after a moment it spoke again, and this time it spoke slowly, the way a man speaks a thing he means another man to carry away whole and unbroken, first in words Diarmad did not know and then again in the tongue of his own hearth, so that he might keep both:

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

He said the words over once in his own mouth, haltingly, the strange ones first and then the plain ones, to be sure he had them, and Coimhdeach na Fine did not correct him, which he took, rightly or not, for a sign that he had them right. Then he sat with them a while longer, turning them the way he had turned the coal all his life, looking for the seam in them that would open, and found none.

"That is no answer," he said.

"It is the one I was given to give," said Coimhdeach na Fine.

And the wind, which had gone round it all that long night without once so much as stirring the grey of it, went through the place where it stood as though there had never been anything there to go round, and there was nothing there.

Diarmad sat alone by the last red eye of his fire with the two coals of the night in his two hands, the rainbow one that would not light and the three words that would not open, and above him the oaks went on roaring at a wind that had, for the whole of that long telling, seemed to be listening as hard as he was, and now seemed only a wind again.

## IV. Of the Two Days Alone

He did not leave the grove in the morning. When the grey light came up cold and wet through the black branches and found him still sitting where Coimhdeach na Fine had left him, he told himself he would go on looking in the morning; and when the morning had come and gone he told himself the same of the next, because he could not make himself walk away from the one place on the whole island where the answer had stood across a fire from him and spoken, even though it had told him plainly that it had no more to give.

He tried Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich against everything the grove offered him, the way a man tries a key in every lock he can find rather than admit the one door it was cut for is shut to him. Up through a gap in the oaks where the gale had torn the crown from one of them he held gual bogha-froise to the sky, turning it so that whatever light came down through the grey cloud might find it, the way a man holds a thing up to catch the sun; nothing in it changed. Into the black wet earth at the fallen trunk's root he knelt and pressed it, deep as his hand would go, and left it there through the better part of a day, and dug it out again in the failing light no different than he had buried it, cold, dark, and itself. Into his little fire it went, alone this time, none of the other six with it, and it caught black-flamed and low as any other coal, the colours going out of it exactly as they went out at every hearth; he raked it out again with a stick before the flame had eaten more than its edge, and it lay scorched and dark on the leaves, and the colours came back into it only slowly as it cooled. Sky, and earth, and fire; he had none of the sea to try, so far from any shore, and no way, alone in an empty grove with one small coal in his hand, to know what it would mean to try the three at once even if he had had them all.

On the second morning he tried it once more, a way that had not occurred to him the first day: he set the coal down bare on the black wet bark of the fallen trunk and knelt over it and spoke the words aloud into it, both the strange ones and the plain ones, close enough that his breath stirred the ash of his dying fire beside it, as though a thing that had answered to sea-water and open sky and buried earth in the telling he had heard might answer also to a voice alone, if the voice said the right words over it in the right place. The coal lay where he had set it and did not so much as warm. A wren came down out of the fallen trunk's roots while he knelt there, hopped along the bark to within a hand's breadth of the coal, looked at it and at him with its head cocked first one way and then the other, and went off again about its own business in the undergrowth, entirely unconcerned with a god's hidden fire or a man's twenty years of asking after it; and something in the plainness of the bird, going about a wren's morning in a grove where he had spent a night talking with the smoke of the first coal ever woken, struck Diarmad harder than any of his failed trials had, and he sat back on his heels and did not try again that day.

By the second evening he understood, in the flat tired way a man understands a thing his heart has known since the wind first went through the place where Coimhdeach na Fine had stood, that nothing he did alone in that grove was going to open Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich for him. The story was his now, whole and unbroken, more of it than any living soul on the island had ever been given in one night, and the three lines of Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich had been turned over so many times in two days that he could no longer tell whether he was still hearing Coimhdeach na Fine's voice in them or only his own. An answer was not among what he had, and Coimhdeach na Fine had told him, as plainly as it had told him anything, that it had none left to give him.

So he banked what was left of his little fire under turf, the way a man banks a fire he means to leave and come back to, though he did not think, walking away from it, that he would ever stand in that hollow again. Then he took up his coal, wrapped it back into its fold of hide against his chest where it had ridden the whole way north, turned south with the wind now at his back instead of in his face, and began the three days home.

The words came out of him the whole of that first day, aloud where the road let him and under his breath where it did not, the strange ones and then the plain ones, over and over, the way a man says a name again and again on a road so as not to lose it before he reaches the house he means to carry it into: *tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir; meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach*; the sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit; merge the spirits, and they become almighty. By the second day the words had worn a way into him the way a path wears itself into a hillside, so that he no longer had to reach for them; they were simply there, turning under his walking the way the coal turned under his fingers, and he found himself, without quite meaning to, already shaping in his mind how he would say them at his own hearth, and to whom, and what face his mother would make when he told her he had not found an answer after all, only a longer question than the one he had carried north.

The weather turned kinder on the way south than it had been on the way north, as though the grove had kept the worst of the gale for itself and let him go with only ordinary wind and ordinary rain, and he was glad of it in a way he could not quite have explained, glad of the plainness of it, the mud that was only mud and the wet that was only wet, after a night in which nothing had been only itself. He slept the first night on the road under a fold of rock, as he had slept coming up, but he did not sleep well; he woke twice with the sense of a grey shape standing just outside the reach of the small fire he had built, and both times there was nothing there but hazel scrub and the ordinary dark, and both times he lay a long while afterward with the coal held tight in his closed fist before he could make himself sleep again. He thought, walking the second day through country he had barely marked on the way up, so intent had he been then on reaching the grove, how strange it was that a man could carry the whole shape of the world's making folded into him and still feel his feet ache and his belly want feeding exactly as they had before he carried it, as though the story and the body it lived in had agreed to go on about their separate business without troubling each other overmuch.

More than once on that second day his mind went back to the six coals he had not brought north at all, the rainbow alone having gone with him: the black one and the fire-red one and the white-flecked and the silver and the red-brown, all of them kept at the hearth in the leather bag the children still played with when they were small enough not to know better, and it came to him, somewhere on that road, that whatever Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich meant, it was not a thing one man could finish alone with one coal in an empty grove, however many days he gave to trying. It would want the whole bag, and the whole line, and more roads walked than the one under his own two feet; and though the thought should perhaps have weighed on him as one more failure laid on top of the first, it did not weigh that way. It sat in him instead the way the fire itself had sat in the mason's well before anyone thought to carry it home, a thing not yet begun rather than a thing already finished and found wanting, and for the first time since the wind had gone through the place where Coimhdeach na Fine stood he found himself walking a little faster on the road south.

He came down at last into the high valleys on the evening of the third day, footsore and hollow with hunger, with hazel giving way to birch and birch to the bare heath he had grown up on, and somewhere on that last stretch of road, with the smoke of known hearths rising thin and grey against a sky he had walked three days under without once seeing it clear, he stopped saying the words aloud and let them go quiet in him, the way a coal goes quiet under ash and is not gone. He had gone north with one question and come home with a story no one else on the island had ever heard and a riddle he could not open, and he did not yet know, walking down into the valley in the last light with Tobar dhìreach's own smoke rising ahead of him among all the rest, which of the two the hearths would find harder to believe.
