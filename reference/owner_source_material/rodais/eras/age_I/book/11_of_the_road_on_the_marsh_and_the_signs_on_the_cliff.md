# Of the Road on the Marsh and the Signs on the Cliff

*VE 1,170 – 1,330*

## I. Of the Gate in the Rock

At Toll, where a shelf of grey rock ran down into the Abhainn uaine and broke the river's push into two channels before it could reach the sea, a household kept a gate cut into the rock's own body rather than standing open on it, a channel worked into the stone with a wattle gate set only into the channel's narrowest place.

The cutting had been begun long before any of them now living could remember its beginning, a groove picked out stroke by stroke with a harder stone against the softer, following a crack that already ran most of the way where the channel was wanted, so that the work was less a making from nothing than a widening of what the rock half offered on its own. It ran from the still water above the shelf down to a pool below it that filled and drained with the river's own rise and fall, a channel a man's stride across and knee-deep at its shallowest, worn smooth along its floor by the water that had gone through it every year since before the cutting was finished, so that no one could any longer say which of the smoothness was the river's work and which the first workers' own.

At the channel's narrowest place, where the rock pinched in on either side close enough that a man could touch both walls with his arms half spread, a slot had been cut into the stone on each side, deep and true, running from above the water's highest mark down below its lowest. Into the slots, when the river ran low and the pool below wanted emptying, a woven gate of wattle was let down and driven home with a mallet of oak until its foot sat hard against the channel's floor and no fish of any size could pass either way through the gap it closed.

The dropping of the gate was a household's whole work for the better part of a day, from the first light in which the water could be judged low enough to try, through the fetching of the gate from where it hung, dried and mended, under the eaves of the shelter above the shelf, to the driving of it home stroke by stroke with the mallet, one man in the channel to guide its foot true and two above with their weight on the mallet's haft, until the gate stood square in the slots and the water above it began, almost at once, to climb a hand's width against its face where before it had run through unchecked.

Behind the gate, in the pool the channel fed, the fish that had come up with the last high water and not gone back down again with the ebb were left penned in a stretch of water no deeper, at its lowest, than a woman's knee, and it was there, once the gate stood driven and the pool's level had settled, that the real work of the day began: wading in with woven baskets and bare hands both, working the pool's whole width from the gate down to its lower lip, taking what the day's need called for and leaving the rest to swim on when the gate was drawn again with the next rise. It was slower work than a net across open water and it took more hands, but it took fish from a stretch of river where no net could be strung, in a channel the household had made for no purpose but this, and it took them dry-shod but for the one man in the water, which on a cold morning of the wet season counted for something the tellers of that shore never troubled to say aloud, for it did not need saying.

A child born to that household in the years the channel was already old learned to walk its length before she learned to walk the shore proper, led by the hand along the smoothed floor at low water while the elders worked, and learned before she was fit to lift the mallet herself which stretch of the channel's floor stood firm underfoot and which gave a little where the current had worked beneath it, a knowledge no one troubled to teach her in words because there were no words in that household's use fit to carry it, only the leading of a small hand along the same stretch of rock, season after season, until the feet themselves knew what they stood on without asking the eyes.

The slot into which the gate was driven wore, over the years the channel was worked, a groove of its own inside the groove first cut for it, the wood's own foot working the stone a little smoother, a little truer, with every dropping and every drawing, until an old man of that household could set the gate's foot to the slot in the near dark and feel, through the haft of the mallet, whether it sat true before it was more than halfway home. What the river gave in one wet season and withheld in the next, the channel and the gate together took in whatever measure the season allowed, penning a little where a little was there to be penned, and standing open and unremarked the rest of the year while the current ran through the gap the gate had left, carrying down to the sea whatever the rock had not been cut to catch.

There were years the river did not fall low enough, or not for long enough, to make the dropping of the gate worth the day's work, and those years the household lived on what the sea gave a little further down, and on what small game the woods above the shelf held, and did not trouble the gate at all from one wet season to the next; and there were years it fell so low and stayed so low that the gate was dropped twice or three times before the autumn rains filled the channel again, each dropping taking the same day's labour as the last, so that a household's whole late summer might be measured out, in the telling that has come down of it, by the number of times the gate had been driven home and drawn again since the water first began to fail, a count kept in place of any count of days. It was not the same gate from one generation to the next. Wattle rotted where it stood driven into wet slots, and a gate that failed at its foot in the pull of a high river was mended where it could be mended and remade whole where it could not, so that the shape of the gate outlasted any one weaving of it, each in turn gone back into the reed bed it was cut from, the way the channel persisted though no two floods left its floor carrying quite the same silt.

A boy who lost two fingers of his left hand to the mallet's swing, misjudging the moment his own weight ought to come off the haft and leaving it a breath too late, went on working the gate in the seasons after with the hand that remained him and the stumps of the other tucked close against his palm out of the mallet's way, and was neither pitied for it nor spared the work on its account, for a household that lived by the channel wanted every hand it had, whole or not, and the boy himself, so the telling carries, came in time to set a gate's foot into its slot as true by feel as any man of that shore with both hands unhurt.

## II. Of the Road Across the Reeds

Some way to the west, where a wide loch lay cupped among low hills and its western shore ran out into a reed marsh a full mile across before the ground rose firm again, another household turned, generation on generation, to a slow work of shell laid upon reed and mud, a little each season, until what had been water underfoot for half the year became, at last, ground a person could walk dry across without once feeling the marsh reach for the foot.

It began, as such things begin on that shore, without any one person setting out to make a road. A family whose gathering-ground lay on the firm rise beyond the marsh had worn a path through the reeds by the plain repetition of walking it, a soft track that held for a season and sank again with the winter's water, so that every spring the same feet found the same reeds standing where the path had been the autumn before, as though the marsh forgot each year what had been asked of it. What changed it was the shell.

The household that worked the loch's edge gathered a great deal of shell from the shallows there, freshwater mussel mostly, and had always let the broken and the empty shell fall back near the water where it was worked, the way any household lets its waste fall near its work. Someone among them, one wet season when the reed track had sunk further and sooner than most years, began instead to carry the broken shell out along the worst of the sunken stretch and let it fall there, underfoot, rather than by the water where it did no one any good; and where the shell fell and was trodden in, the reeds beneath it held firmer through the following winter than the reeds a stride to either side.

It is not told which season this began in, nor whose hands first carried a basket of shell out onto the reeds rather than leaving it by the shallows, for the telling that has come down of it keeps no name and asks for none. What is told is that it was done again the next season, a little further along the same stretch, and again the season after that, until the carrying of shell out onto the worst ground had become, without anyone deciding it should, a thing the household did each year as part of its ordinary work at the loch's edge, the way the net-menders of other shores mended nets each spring because the nets wanted mending.

The shell was crushed before it was carried, broken small underfoot on a flat stone kept by the water for the purpose, for whole shells worked loose and slid where crushed shell packed and held; and it was carried in the same woven baskets that carried the day's gathering home, so that a basket that went out full of mussel in the morning might come back in the evening full of its own broken shell, emptied onto the reeds on the way out and refilled with the day's take on the way back, two errands made of one walk. Over years enough that no one household's own memory held the whole of it, the crushed shell built up along the worst of the marsh a hand's depth at a time, packed down by every foot that crossed it and topped again the next season where the winter water had worked it thin, until the track that had once sunk under every autumn's rain stood instead a good forearm above the reeds on either side, pale with shell against the dark water, a raised way running the better part of a mile from the firm rise to the loch's own shallows.

A girl of that household, grown just old enough to be trusted with an errand of her own, was sent one autumn to carry word to the loch's edge that the boats were wanted in before the light failed, and went the whole way out along the shell road at a run without once setting a foot into the reeds on either side, the first, so far as the telling remembers, to cross the whole finished length of it without the older habit of testing the ground first with a toe before trusting it with a step. She came back the same way, still at a run, and it was some while before anyone thought to remark that the road, worked at by so many hands over so many years that none of them had set out to build a road at all, had that day carried a child at a run from one end to the other and not once given way beneath her.

The marsh on either side of the shell road kept its own way through all the years the road was built, standing wet and reed-thick and treacherous to any foot that stepped off the packed shell, so that the road did not drain the marsh or change it, only crossed it, a single dry thread laid over ground that remained, on both sides of that thread, exactly what it had always been. Boats still went out from the shallows at the loch's edge to work the deeper water, and the reed-cutting for thatch and for matting still went on along the wet margins either side of the road, and the road carried nothing more than feet and baskets and, on the days the catch was heavy, fish strung on cords over a shoulder; it asked for nothing and gave nothing but the one dry way across, and the household that had built it without meaning to kept it, once built, the way any household keeps a well or a hearth, mending the low places each spring with a fresh basket of crushed shell before the winter's washing had quite worn them through.

It was not the only crossing of that marsh, for the household beyond the firm rise had, in the years before the shell was ever carried out, waded the reeds at need the way any household wades a marsh it must cross, wet to the thigh and watching for the softer ground that would take a leg to the knee if a foot found it unlucky; and even once the road stood finished, a hand at least a forearm above the water on either side, those who worked the reed-cutting close along its edges went on wading as they always had, for the road served the crossing and not the cutting, and no one thought to lay shell out over ground that only wanted standing in a while and not walking across. The two ways of using the marsh went on side by side the whole time the road was building, the wet way and the dry, neither troubling the other, and a child of that household might grow to a grown height knowing both, the firm shell under the sole for an errand to the far rise and the cold reed-water at the shin for the cutting, without ever once thinking the one had made the other less needful.

In the autumn a family from further round the loch's northern shore, whose own crossing lay a longer way about over higher ground, came down one year to see the road for themselves, having heard of it from a household that fished the loch's open water and had watched the shell-carrying from their boats for seasons without once setting foot on the finished road themselves. They walked its length from the firm rise to the water's edge and back again, the whole family together, saying little that the telling has kept, and went home the long way about all the same, for the road belonged, in whatever unspoken reckoning that shore kept of such things, to the household that had built it, and a stranger's foot on another household's shell was not a thing lightly done, however plainly the shell lay open to any foot that wanted to try it.

## III. Of the Signs on the Cliff

Far along the same coast, where the land rose in a line of pale cliffs above a shore too broken with rock for any mole or any road, a household of a different trade turned, in the years the gate was worked at Toll and the road was laid at the loch, to a labour that left behind it neither a channel to catch fish nor a way to walk dry-shod, but rows of small cut marks on the standing rock, and nothing else that the telling has ever been able to say the marks were for.

A man of that household is remembered, though not by any name, for the care of his hand at the cutting, a care that others of the same trade along that cliff did not always trouble to match. He worked with a chisel of flaked flint, held close to the eye and struck with a small stone hammer no bigger than could be closed in one fist, and he cut each sign the same careful way: a line begun at one point and carried through without lifting the chisel until the whole shape of it stood cut, then a second line crossing or joining the first as the shape called for, each stroke of the hammer light enough that the chisel bit only a little at a time, so that a single sign that a careless hand might have cut in a dozen blows took him, by the count of a boy who once sat a whole afternoon watching him at it, the better part of two hundred.

The signs he cut, and the signs others of his trade cut beside his along the same stretch of cliff face, ran in rows, one row above another where the rock stood high enough to carry more than one, each row running some way along the face before it stopped, sometimes at the rock's own edge and sometimes, it seemed, at no edge at all, simply where the cutter had judged the row done. No two rows ran the same length. No two signs within a row, to an eye set beside them and asked to judge it, looked quite the same as any other, though each carried some kinship with its neighbours that the eye caught without being able to say in what the kinship lay, the way a hand knows one household's pot-rim from another's without being able to say which curve of the thumb made the difference.

The careful man was watched, more than once, by others of his own household standing a little off while he worked, and none of them, so far as the telling carries, ever asked him what the rows said, for there was no word in any of their use for what a row of cut signs on a cliff face might be asked to say, any more than there was a word for what the three strokes on a net-weight far along the coast might be asked to say. He worked at his rows in whatever hours the tide and the weather left him free of the trade that fed his household, some days cutting three signs and some days none, and when a row was as long as he judged it wanted to be he began another above it or beside it, without troubling, so far as anyone watching him could tell, to look back at what he had already cut before he began the next.

Not every row on that cliff stood as its first cutter had left it. Here and there along the face a run of signs had been gone over a second time, the first shallow cutting deepened and widened by a later hand until the old shape beneath it was lost under the new, or cut across at an angle that broke the row's own line into two halves that no longer read, if they had ever read, as one thing. The tool that did this second cutting was of the same kind as the first, a flint chisel struck with a small hammer, and the care taken with it was, where it could still be judged, of the same order as the care taken with the first cutting it undid; whoever went back over an old row worked it with the same patient stroke-by-stroke care that had made the row in the first place, neither careless nor hurried nor angry at the stone. Why the second hand undid what the first had made, the telling does not carry, and no one now living, on that shore or any other, has ever claimed to know.

The rain that fell on that coast in every wet season found the new-cut rows before it found anything else on the whole exposed face, running down into the fresh grooves and darkening them a shade the older, weathered signs around them had long since lost, so that for a little while after every cutting a row stood plain against the paler rock about it, sharp-edged and dark-lined, before the weather over the following seasons drew it down toward the same pale, softened grey as everything cut before it. The careful man cut on through his own years at the trade regardless, adding row to row along a stretch of cliff that already, by the time a boy watched him at it for an afternoon, carried more rows than any one person standing at its foot could take in with a single turn of the head; and when in time his own hand grew too unsteady for the fine work of it, it is not told that anyone else of his household took it up in the same close way, or that the rows he had already cut were ever added to again by any hand as careful as his own.

The boy who watched him that one afternoon came back more than once as he grew, drawn to the foot of the cliff on days his own work left him free, and stood looking up at the rows the way he had once sat and watched them made, though he himself never lifted a chisel to the rock in all the years the telling follows him. He came to know, by looking alone and by no other means, which rows the careful man had cut and which belonged to other hands of the same trade working the same stretch of cliff in the same years, for the careful man's own signs kept, to an eye grown patient enough to find it, a straightness in the line and an evenness in the spacing that the others' did not quite match, however close they came to it; and this knowing he kept to himself, for there was, in that household's whole use of words, none fit to carry it further than the looking had already carried it.

Below the rows, where the cliff's foot ran down into a shingle no different from any other shingle on that coast, the same household that fished and gathered the tideline there went on with its ordinary work the whole time the signs were being cut above them, hauling boats up past the high-water mark and mending nets and splitting driftwood for the fire, most of them never once climbing the short scramble up to where the rows stood, for the cutting was the careful man's trade and not theirs, the way the gate was the Toll household's trade and the shell road the loch household's, each a labour one people took up for reasons the telling has never been asked to give, and each left, by every other people near it, entirely to the hands that had begun it.

## IV. Of the Rain on the New Signs

It fell to a woman of the household at Toll, in the years the gate had been worked at longest and the slot wore its own groove within the first, to walk the coast one summer on an errand that is not told, and to come, in the course of it, along the cliff where the signs stood cut in their rows, and to stand a while at their foot looking up at them the way the boy had once sat and watched the careful man cut them, though her own eyes found only what was already finished and could say nothing of how it had been made.

She had never seen the like before, for no household of the river-gate or the marsh-road cut anything into stone beyond what bare use demanded, a groove for a gate's foot, a notch for a cord; and she stood at the cliff's foot a while in a rain that had begun while she walked and had not yet decided whether to lift, watching the water run down into the freshest of the rows and darken it against the older signs around it, and then she went on the way her errand called her, and the telling, which follows her only that far, does not say that she ever spoke of it at either of the two households she came from or came to.

The rows stood after her as they had stood before her, taking the rain the same way they had always taken it, darkening under it and paling again as it dried, without asking of any hand that passed beneath them, hers or another's, more than the looking she had given them; and along the whole of that coast, where the gate was dropped and the road was mended and the rows were cut a little longer every season they were worked at, no telling passed from the gate's household to the marsh's, or from the marsh's to the cliff's, so that what was made at each stood, so far as the tellers of that shore have ever carried it forward, entirely on its own, known to no household but the one whose hands had made it.
