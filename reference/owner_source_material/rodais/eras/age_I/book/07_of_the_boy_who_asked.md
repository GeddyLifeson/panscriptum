*The Second Part: Of the Question in the Grove, and the Gold on the Sea*

## I. Of the Pot and the Bag

At Tobar dhìreach the hearth had not gone dark since Oidhche an Dealanaich, and after some thirty fathers no one troubled to say so any more, the way no one troubled to say that the sun rose. The household had grown the way a good field grows, year on year, until it was less one house than a scatter of houses about the old ring of stones where Teine a' Chlachair had been set down, and the line that kept the ring had a name in every mouth from the high valleys to the eastern hearths, though the name meant no more to most of those mouths than "the people who keep the fire and ask the question nobody can answer."

The fire itself was ordinary enough to look at: peat and birch and whatever else the year gave, banked low at night and built up at morning, the same as any hearth from the coast to the moor. What set it apart from every other hearth on the island, the household said, and had said for so many generations that the saying had worn as smooth as a much-handled stone, was that it had never once been struck new. It had come down, coal to coal, unbroken, from the storm that had put it into Neachdan's well; and to keep that unbroken thread alive, one woman of the line at a time kept a clay pot near the ring, banked with ash of its own, in which a single ember of the old fire was never let go out, so that even if the whole wide hearth should somehow die in the night — which it never had, but the women who kept the pot did not build a custom on what had never happened, they built it on what must never happen — there would still be, in the little pot, enough of the mason's own fire to light the hearth again from that one ember, and owe nothing to any neighbour's fire for it.

The pot had a name in the household, though not, in those years, one the wider hills had troubled to learn: they called it simply Poit an Teine, and the woman who kept it, the keeper of the pot, and both titles had passed, mother to daughter or aunt to niece as the fires of a household usually pass, through more hands than the household could easily number by then. In the year this telling opens, the keeper of the pot was an old woman known to her own kin only as the old woman of the pot, for she had outlived every teller who might have troubled to fix a better name to her; and it was into her charge, and after her into the charge of a woman named Fionnghuala, that the pot came down.

There was a second thing kept at Tobar dhìreach that the wider hills knew a little better, because children carried word of it home from visiting cousins the way children carry word of anything strange and bright. This was a leather bag, worn soft at the seams, that held seven pieces of stone unlike any stone dug from any other ground on the island. Black, one of them was, and took a polish like still water at night. A second had a sheen of gold on it, so that it seemed, held to the light, to hold a small still sea inside it. A third was flecked all through with points of white, like a hard black sky full of small stars. A fourth had a gleam of silver under its black; a fifth was the deep red-brown of an old oak's heart; a sixth held the colour of flame itself, red and gold together, moving in it as flame moves when it is turned; and the last was a colour no one at Tobar dhìreach had ever agreed how to name, for it changed as it was turned, gold to green to a blue no sky ever wore, and back again, never twice the same, the way oil sits on the top of still water and shows every colour there is without keeping any of them. This last piece the household called, when it called it anything, the rainbow piece.

The pieces had come home in Neachdan's own tunic on Oidhche an Dealanaich, part of the same breaking of stone that had let the fire into the world; and being pretty and being useless — they had been tried in the fire, long before, and had burned low and dark and given up their colour to the flame like any other coal, and no one troubled the trying twice — they had passed, in time, out of the keeping of the elders and into the hands of the children, the way a household's outworn treasures generally do. Every child born at Tobar dhìreach for as long as the pieces had been kept there had, at some point in a short and undistinguished life of being minded by an older cousin while the grown households worked, tipped the bag out on the hearthstone and set the pieces in a row, and turned them to the fire to watch the colours move, and lost interest in them by the time the next meal was called, the way children lose interest in anything that does not need feeding or chasing.

That was the whole history of the coloured pieces, so far as the household of Fionnghuala's girlhood understood it: a wonder found with the fire, tried once as fuel and found no better than any other coal, kept afterward because throwing away a thing that had come with the lightning felt, to every keeper who had ever considered it, an unlucky sort of economy. No one at Tobar dhìreach, in all the generations between the mason and the boy this telling is about, is remembered to have asked why the pieces were coloured at all, when the coal of the common hills was not; or if any had asked it, the asking had died with them and left no mark on the telling, the way most questions do.

## II. Of the Question

Diarmad was born at Tobar dhìreach some thirty-four fathers after Conall, the third child and second son of Fionnghuala, who was by then some four-and-twenty and not yet keeper of the pot, that office still resting with her own mother's sister, the old woman the household called, without much ceremony, the aunt. He was a quiet child by the account of those who remembered him quiet, and by all other accounts a child who asked more than his fair share of questions even for a child, which is a kind of creature that asks a great many; but the household had raised enough children by then to have a settled patience for it, and most of what he asked — why the geese went, why the salmon came, why the frost took some plants and spared others — got him a plain answer or an honest "no one knows that," and the matter closed there the way such matters generally close.

He was seven years old, in the summer of his seventh year, when he tipped the bag out on the hearthstone one wet afternoon with two cousins for company, and set the seven pieces in their row as every child before him had set them, and turned them, one after another, to the low grey light coming in at the door. It was a game he had played a dozen times before without it meaning anything more than an idle afternoon. This time, holding the black piece up and turning it so its polish caught what light there was, he asked the aunt, who was working wool by the same hearth and paying the children the same half-attention she paid the weather, why it was that this stone had colour in it and the coal from the cutting on the hillside, which she burned every day of her life, did not.

The aunt did not know, and said so without much thought, the way she would have said she did not know why the frost took some plants and spared others; and that, on any other afternoon with any other child, would have been the end of it. But Diarmad set the black piece down and picked up the gold-sheened one and asked the same question of that, and then of the silver one, and then, turning the rainbow piece so the colour ran gold to green and back, asked it of that piece hardest of all, for it seemed to him, he said afterward more times than the telling can count, the strangest of the seven and therefore the one most owed an answer. By then the aunt had given over three or four answers of the "no one knows that" kind and found the child unsatisfied with every one of them, and she said at last, not unkindly, only with the weariness of a grown woman whose wool has stopped taking her attention, that the stones were coloured because the sky had made them so on the night it sent the fire, and a boy who wanted a better answer than that could go and ask the sky.

It was meant, by every account the household later gave of it, as the kind of thing said to close a matter, not open one. Diarmad took it, by every account he himself is remembered to have given, as an answer worth pursuing.

## III. Of the Years of Asking

He did not go and ask the sky, being seven, and having some idea, even at seven, of where a question could sensibly be carried and where it could not. He went instead, over the years that followed, to everyone at Tobar dhìreach and beyond it who might plausibly know a thing that his own household did not, and asked each of them, in his own words and in his own time, why the coloured pieces held colour when no other stone on the island did.

The oldest of the line, a bent man who had buried more of his own kin than he had living, told him only that the fire had come from the sky and the pieces with it, and a thing that comes from the sky need not answer to a boy why it looks as it looks. The fire-bearers, the men and women whose office it still was, in those years, to carry a coal in a clay pot to any household that asked one, and who therefore travelled further and saw more of the island than most, gave him one true morsel among the rest: a woman among them had heard, from a household three valleys over, that colour in a stone was only the same colour the eye puts into a rainbow, no more real than a rainbow is, and vanishes the same way when you go close enough to look. This pleased Diarmad not at all, for he had gone close enough to look at the pieces a hundred times and their colour had never once thinned for the looking. Travellers passing through on their way to somewhere else, strangers with no stake in the household's pride and therefore, he reasoned, no reason to fob him off with an answer meant only to be rid of him, gave him, from most of them, either a shrug or a longer answer that amounted, when turned over, to the same shrug dressed in more words.

He took to carrying the rainbow piece with him, in a small bag of its own hung at his belt, from about his tenth year onward, and the household grew used to seeing him turn it in whatever light was going: the low red light of the hearth of an evening, the white light of noon on a clear day, the flat grey light of the rain, which he seemed, if anything, to prefer, for he was known more than once to sit out under the eaves in a shower with the piece held bare to the wet, turning it slowly, watching to see whether the rain drew the colour out of it or only lay on top of it the way rain lies on any other stone. It lay on top of it. He noted this, in the way a child notes a thing he means to come back to, and went on asking.

By his thirteenth year the asking had become, without his quite meaning it to, a settled feature of the household's talk, the kind of thing a family jokes about the way it jokes about an old dog's habits or an uncle's temper. "Diarmad's question," the valley began to call it, and the phrase travelled further and faster than any answer ever had; a fire-bearer from Tobar dhìreach arriving at a strange hearth with a coal in her pot might be met, before the coal was even set down, with someone asking, half in earnest and half to raise a laugh from the rest of the house, whether she had brought word yet of why the pieces were coloured, for the boy at Tobar dhìreach was still asking, was he not, and had anyone yet given him an answer that stuck. It was said, for the most part, the way a valley says most things about its own, with more affection in it than the words alone would suggest to a stranger. But it was said often, and a joke said often enough wears a groove, and by the time Diarmad was old enough to notice the groove for what it was, he had been the valley's fondest jest for longer than he could clearly remember not being it.

His mother did not always find it so fond. Fionnghuala had taken the office of keeper of the pot in her two-and-fortieth year, the aunt having died the winter before at a good age and by a quiet death, and she kept it, by every account, with the same close attention she had always given her son's asking, neither of which she was minded to have laughed at in her hearing. There is a telling kept at Tobar dhìreach still, worn thin with retelling, of an evening when a cousin by marriage, newly come into the household and not yet schooled in its ways, made the joke of the coloured pieces once too carelessly at the hearth, in front of a full house and in front of Diarmad himself, who by then was near enough grown to feel a joke land where a child does not; and of Fionnghuala setting down her work, and saying, in a voice the telling swears carried to every corner of the house though she never once raised it, that a question asked twenty years running by the same boy was not the same thing as a boy who could not let a matter drop, and that she would thank the house to remember the difference, for she had raised the one and would not sit easy hearing him called the other. The cousin, the telling says, made no more such jokes in that house again; the rest of the valley, having no such mother to answer to, went on making them a good while longer, though a little more quietly when word of that evening reached them.

Diarmad, for his own part, seems from every account to have taken the joke and the mother's defence of him both with much the same even temper, neither much stung by the one nor much comforted by the other, as a man goes on with a long walk regardless of whether the weather along it is fair or foul. He kept asking. He kept turning the rainbow piece to whatever light the day offered. And by the time he had passed twenty years, with the household's fire and the pieces' silence both unchanged since the day he first set them in a row on the hearthstone, he had begun, without much announcing it, to ask a narrower question than the one he had started with: not why the pieces held colour, which no one on the island seemed able to tell him, but whether there was anyone, or anything, on the island at all that might know.

## IV. Of the Herder of the North Moor

It was a fire-bearer, in the end, who gave him the first thread worth following, though she herself did not know she was giving it, and would have been surprised, the telling says, to learn what came of it.

In the summer of Diarmad's four-and-twentieth year she had gone on the longest circuit the household's fire-bearers still walked in those days, up past the last of the herding grounds and onto the high moor that lay north and a little west of the valley, where the households thinned to a scatter and a fire, once given, might not be asked for again for a generation. She came home some weeks later with little enough to report of the households she had found, for there had been few, and those few had wanted only the fire and no talk with it; but she mentioned, more in passing than as a matter she thought worth much, that one old herder on the moor, a solitary sort who kept more to his sheep than to any hearth, had told her, when she asked the way onward, to keep well clear of a grove of oaks a further two or three days north, for the wind in it, he said, did not blow the way wind blows anywhere else, and a man who camped near it on a bad night might hear it say things a wind has no business saying.

The fire-bearer told it as she had heard it, as an old herder's fancy, the kind of tale every solitary keeper of a lonely ground tells to anyone who will listen, more to hear his own voice than to be believed; and most of the household who heard it that evening took it exactly so, and forgot it by the next day's work. Diarmad did not forget it. More than once over the following weeks he asked her to say again exactly what the herder had said, and exactly how he had said it, until she grew tired of the asking and told him plainly that she had already told him everything there was in it, three times over, and that he would do better to go and hear it from the herder's own mouth if he wanted more than that.

He took the suggestion more seriously than she had meant it. Over the two years that followed he sought out every traveller who passed through Tobar dhìreach from the north and west and asked each of them the same thing: had they heard, in their own travels, of a grove where the wind spoke, or of the old herder who kept sheep near it, or of anything at all that a teller might set beside such a tale. Most had heard nothing. A few had heard something, thinner scraps mostly, an old woman's warning to keep no fire lit within sight of a certain stand of trees, a half-remembered saying that the birds would not roost in it though the ground round it was as good as any for nesting; and one traveller, an old man himself, said he had passed near enough to see the tops of the oaks from a distance once in his youth, standing taller and greener than any wood he knew elsewhere on the moor, and had felt no wish at all to go nearer, though he could not, when Diarmad pressed him, say what exactly it was he had felt or feared.

None of it, set together, amounted to much that a careful man would have called evidence of anything beyond a lonely moor's fondness for its own stories. Diarmad set it together anyway, the way a man who has been asking one question for twenty years learns to set together whatever scraps come his way, for he has long since given up waiting for a whole answer to arrive complete. What struck him, he is remembered to have said at the hearth more than once in those years, was not that the wind in a grove might speak, for winds say a great many things to a lonely ear and he did not think himself fool enough to build much on that alone. It was that of all the tellings he had chased in near twenty years of chasing them, this was the first that had not simply shrugged the coloured pieces off as a matter closed, the sky's business and no one else's. A grove where something spoke, whatever it was, was at least a place where a question might be put and an answer, of some kind, might come back.

## V. Of the Walk

He was seven-and-twenty in the late winter he finally went, old enough by the reckoning of that country to have long since taken a wife and a household of his own, which he had not, being by then a man the valley had grown used to thinking of chiefly as the boy who still asked the question, and finding, most of them, little enough reason to change the habit of thought even though the boy had grown, in every other respect, into something closer to a settled man. His mother was the first of the household he told what he meant to do, for he judged, rightly, that she alone among them would neither laugh at it nor try seriously to talk him out of it; and she gave him, in place of either, the rainbow piece to carry loose in his hand rather than shut in its bag, saying that if he meant to ask his question of a wind in a grove, he had best have the thing itself before him while he asked it, and not only the memory of its colours.

Diarmad set out on a clear morning in the month of the lambs' first coming, walking light, with a small clay pot banked round a coal from the hearth, a small store of food, a cloak against the weather, and the rainbow piece carried, as his mother had told him, loose against his palm inside the fold of his cloak, where his thumb found it without his troubling to look. The way north and a little west from Tobar dhìreach ran first through country he knew, herding ground and the thinning edge of it, and then, by the second day, through country he did not, where the households grew as few and as far between as the fire-bearer had found them, and the moor opened wider on every side than any ground he had walked before.

The weather held fair through the first day and turned, on the second, the way weather turns on a high moor without much warning given for it: a wind coming up out of the west in the early afternoon, cold and steady, and clouds massing behind it faster than a walking man cares to watch. He camped that night in the lee of a fold of ground with what shelter he could find, and woke before light to a wind grown harder still, and pushed on regardless, for he had counted the days of his walking against the little food he carried and knew he had no days to spare for waiting out weather on open moor.

By the third afternoon he could see, some way ahead across the last stretch of open ground, a dark line along a low rise that was neither heather nor bare rock but stood taller than either, and greener even at that distance and in that light than anything else the moor showed him; and he knew, before any traveller's telling could have told him so for certain, that this was the grove he had walked three days to find.

The gale was on him in full by the time he came under the first of the trees, a wind such as he had not felt even on the open moor, for the moor at least let a wind run clear and did not throw one gust back against another the way the oaks now did, roaring in their high branches and flinging down showers of last year's leaves and small broken twigs in the gusts between. He put his back to the nearest broad trunk he could find, a great old oak fallen half over and caught against its neighbours, and crouched in what lee it gave him, and got, after long blowing at the coal from his pot with his hands shaking from the cold and the wet, a small fire of gathered twigs going in the shelter of the fallen wood, low and guttering and streaming sideways in every gust that found its way through to him.

He sat with his back to the trunk and the rainbow piece turned to the small unsteady light of his own fire, watching the colour run in it the way he had watched it run a thousand times before at the hearth of Tobar dhìreach, while the gale went on through the branches overhead with a sound that was, he thought, turning it over afterward as he turned over every part of that night for the rest of his life, less like any wind he had ever camped under and more like a great many voices speaking all at once and none of them quite loud enough, or quite close enough, to be made out.

Whether that was the truth of the place or only the tiredness of three days' walking working on a mind that had waited twenty years to come this far, he did not yet know. He meant to sit up and listen, all the same, for as long as the fire held and his strength with it, and see what the grove, if it had anything to say, would say to a man who had come this far to ask it.
