# Of the Panther Roads

*VE 5,170 – 5,299*

## I. Of the Cats That Kept Their Own Time

Beithir had herded on the high shoulders of the central mountains since he was a boy too small to carry a full crook, and he had never in all those years thought the great cats of the passes kept anything that could be called a custom, any more than the weather kept one, or the frost. They were there in the high country the way stone was there, a thing a herder learned to walk around rather than a thing that came and went by any order he could set his own days by. His own father had lost a ewe and a half-grown lamb to something on the high ground in a single bad summer of Beithir's childhood, and had said afterward only that the high pasture took its toll the way any pasture with a drop and a river and a hungry winter took its toll, naming no one thing above the rest as the cause of it. It was in the twenty-second year of a life spent among those shoulders, in a winter cold enough that the sheep's breath hung about them like a second, whiter fleece, that he first let himself believe otherwise.

He had gone up past the last shieling with two dogs and no company to look for three ewes that had wandered from the flock in the days before, and he found, in a saddle of ground between two peaks where the snow lay unbroken from one week's end to the next, a line of prints going through it that were not sheep and not deer and not his own dogs: broad, round-toed, sunk deep, spaced in the long easy stride of something that had not been hurrying and had not needed to. He had seen such prints before, singly, here and there over the years, and thought no more of them than a man thinks of a single crow. What stopped him now was that the line ran straight, from the saddle's western lip to its eastern one, as though the ground between held nothing to turn a beast aside for, and that when he cast about he found the same line, worn a little into the frozen turf as though feet before these had gone the same way, running on both sides of the saddle as far as the fall of the ground let him see it.

He did not find his ewes that day, and did not go looking for them the day after either, for the same track, followed a short way east with the dogs close at his heel and their hackles up the whole time, brought him within scent, though not within sight, of what had made it: a smell low and rank on the wind that put both dogs flat to the ground with their ears back before he had done more than take three steps past the last drift. A long while he crouched at that distance with his hand on the older dog's neck, more to still his own breathing than the animal's, and saw nothing move against the white of the slope above him, though the smell came and thinned and came again on the wind as if whatever carried it were pacing rather than standing still. Turning home without argument, he told what he had seen and smelled to the oldest man of his own hearth, who heard him out over the evening fire without any great surprise in his face, and said only that his own father had told him the same of that same saddle, in a different year, and that his father's father was said to have told it before him: that the great cats of the mountains did not wander the high ground at every season as a man might wander it, but crossed it, at some times of year and not at others, along lines they seemed to hold in the same regard a shepherd held a path.

No one in that country could have said, that year or for long after it, what set the times the cats kept, only that they were kept; that the same saddle, and others like it further along the spine of the mountains, lay empty of that smell and that track through most of the year and thick with both at two turns of it, once when the year climbed toward its warmest and once when it fell back toward its coldest, and that a household with beasts on the high pasture in those weeks did well to bring them down before the smell came, and ill to leave them up after it had. Beithir lived to be an old man himself, and told the saddle's prints to his own sons and their sons after him, and by the time he died the households of that country had begun to reckon those two turns of the year as surely as they reckoned the salmon run below or the geese going over, without any one of them being able to say, any more than his own father's father had been able to say, why the cats kept the roads they kept, or what walked them, or where.

## II. Of the Signs on the Gateposts, and the Thirty Head

The custom that grew up around the cats' crossing was not decided in any one place or by any one household, any of those who kept it agreed, when they were asked; it grew the way the rows at a burial ground grow, each household falling into the shape the households before it had already worn into the ground. Within a lifetime it had a shape plain enough that a stranger walking from one valley to the next in the crossing weeks could read it on every gatepost he passed without being told what it meant: a mark cut fresh into the wood at the turn when the smell and the track first came back to the high saddles, and scraped away again, flat and pale against the weathered grain around it, at the turn when both were gone.

It was not, so far as any teller of that country ever claimed, a mark that spoke to the cats themselves, or asked anything of them, or warded anything off; it spoke to the household's own kin and to whatever traveller might pass the gate in those weeks, saying plainly that the high ground behind this post was closed for the season, in the same way a low branch bent across a path said that the ground beyond it belonged, for the time being, to something better left alone. A woman new-married into a hearth on the eastern side of the mountains, coming from a valley too far south to lie under the cats' road at all, asked her husband's mother, the first crossing season she kept there, why the mark was cut so deep it scarred the post rather than simply chalked or tied on with a strip of hide as she had seen other warnings given in her own country; and the old woman told her that a mark meant to be scraped off again wanted cutting deep enough to still be read in the scraping, so that a household coming late to a gate already marked and already half cleared would know, from the pale scar under the fresh growth of weather, that the season had been kept there before and would be kept there again, whether or not the wood itself still carried the cut.

The camps in the high passes emptied with the same regularity the gateposts marked. Shielings that stood full through the warmer half of every year, herders and their dogs and their fires among them from the last frost to the first, stood bare through the two turns the cats kept, walls standing, hearths cold, the paths worn to them by summer's traffic left to close over with grass the herders themselves had trampled flat only weeks before. A boy sent up in error one crossing season, his own father having lost the count of days in a wet spell that blurred one week into the next, found the shieling he was to have wintered sheep at already stripped and shuttered by households further along who had read the signs aright, and came down again the same evening with nothing worse to show for the mistake than a scolding and a night's walk wasted, which the country afterward told as the mildest lesson the crossing season ever gave anyone, set against what it gave others.

Not every household held by the custom with the same care, and the country did not let the ones who broke it forget what it had cost them. Some years after, a man of the lower slopes, whose grazing ran further up into the passes than most and whose herd had grown large enough that the high pasture's extra weeks of grass had come to matter more to him than it had to smaller households, judged that the crossing that year had come early enough in its own turning that he could risk another ten days on the height before he brought his cattle down, the gateposts of his neighbours already scraped and closed while his own stood freshly cut and, in his own reckoning, cut too soon. His wife had asked him, the evening before, why he trusted his own count of the year over the marks his neighbours had already made of theirs, and he had told her that a man who grazed that ground summer on summer knew its temper better than any post cut by a household two valleys off, and that ten days of good grass was ten days he did not mean to give away for nothing.

He was not a fool by any account the country kept of him afterward, only a man who trusted his own eye over the mark on another man's post, and who had grazed that same high ground long enough without incident that the custom had come, in his own mind, to look more like caution than like law. He brought the cattle down himself on the tenth night, later than any neighbour would have risked it, driving them by torchlight down a track he could have walked with his eyes shut, and found in the morning that thirty head of the herd he had driven up whole had not come down with the rest, scattered wide of the track in the dark and never called back to it, though the drovers with him swore they had heard nothing in the night beyond the ordinary complaint of beasts unsettled by a strange path in the dark. What they found, searching the ground above the track by daylight two days after, when the smell that had kept them from searching sooner had at last thinned enough to risk it, the recital does not dwell on past the bare count: thirty head, and not one whole carcass among them worth the skinning, the ground about them torn in a way no wolf of that country tore ground.

He kept cattle after that, and grazed the lower slopes as every prudent household of that country grazed them, but he never again cut a gatepost sign later than his neighbours, and the tale of his thirty head was told at hearths a long way from his own for longer than his own name was, so that by the time Cairbre was a grown man on the southern shore, a full week's walk and more from the slopes where it had happened, the count of thirty head lost in a single crossing season was a thing every household along that coast could recite though few of them could have said whose herd it had first been lost from, or in which of the mountains' many high passes it had happened.

## III. Of the Cats That Turned Back

It was given, among the tellings the country kept of the crossing season, that the cats themselves might be read for a sign, though no one professed to know what mind, if any, lay behind what they were seen to do. In a later year, in a crossing that had begun ordinarily enough, with the smell coming up to the high saddles at the turn it always came and every gatepost along that stretch of the mountains cut fresh in its own week, a family watching from a rise above the track by daylight, at a safe remove and for no better reason than that the rise gave the clearest view of weather coming in from the west, saw the line of beasts that had begun crossing toward the eastern passes turn, without any cause the watchers could see, and go back the way they had come, down off the road and away toward the foothills on the western side, where by every account that country kept, the cats did not commonly linger once the crossing season had called them onto the high ground at all.

The family that saw it told it plainly, without dressing the matter in more meaning than the bare sight gave it: that the beasts had turned, that they had gone down rather than on, and that no one among the watchers could say why, since nothing in the weather or the ground that day looked to a human eye any different from a hundred crossings before it that had gone through to their own end without a turning. It was the households further along that stretch of country, hearing the telling in the weeks after, who first said aloud what the family who had seen it had been careful not to say themselves: that a sign so plain, and so far outside anything the crossing season had shown before, ought to be answered rather than merely watched, and that a hard winter was coming on the strength of it whether or not the turning cats had meant to say so.

They stored double that autumn against a want that had not yet shown itself in any other sign, peat cut and stacked higher than any household along that stretch had troubled to stack it in living memory, grain and dried meat laid by past what the ordinary count of mouths at each hearth called for. Households further off, who had the turning only at second hand and no rise of their own to have watched it from, stored single as they always had, and some among them said afterward, when word of the hard winter reached them too, that they envied their neighbours nothing so much as a rise with a clear view west and a family willing to trust their own eyes over an unbroken habit. The winter that came on after that autumn proved, by the time it broke, hard enough that snow lay to a grown man's waist in drifts along the lower valleys for the better part of two months, and every household that had stored against it said afterward it had been worth the extra cutting and the extra carrying, and not one of them claimed to know any better than before why the cats had turned, only that they had turned, and that the winter had come as if in answer.

## IV. Of Cairbre and the Eleven

Cairbre was thirty-two years old, and had led men of his own hearth on lesser errands into the foothills often enough that no one on the southern shore thought twice about it when he said, one autumn, that he meant to take ten with him up past the last of the low pastures to bring back a household of kin that had settled two valleys further in than any of the shore's own people commonly went, and whose harvest that year, word had come down, had failed badly enough that they would not see the winter through unhelped. His wife stood at the edge of Teallaichean a' Chladaich the morning the eleven set out, with their own two children at her skirts, and asked him only how many days he judged it, so that she would know when to begin watching the track for them; he told her twelve at the most, ten men's worth of harvest to carry down and no cause in the world to linger once it was loaded, and kissed her once before he took the path inland with the others behind him in single file, each carrying less on the way up than he meant to carry on the way down.

It was not, by his own reckoning and by the reckoning of every man who went with him, a crossing-season errand. The geese of the coast below Teallaichean a' Chladaich had not yet gone over in their numbers that autumn, and the shore held, as it had always held, that the geese going over marked the turn of the year toward the cold reliably enough to set a household's own calendar by; and the geese, that particular autumn, held later on the southern water than any of the eleven men setting out could remember them holding in their own lifetimes. Cairbre had said as much himself, the evening before, to an old man of the shore who asked him only in passing whether he had thought to reckon the mountain's own time against the coast's, and not against the season the geese were keeping that year down on the water; Cairbre answered, not unkindly, that the geese were the surest clock the shore had ever kept faith with, and that he saw no cause to trust a stranger's gatepost over a sign his own household had watched every year of his life.

They went up in good order, by the account the search that followed them later pieced together from what could be read of their camp-sites and their fire-scars along the way, keeping to the lower ground as long as the lower ground offered a path and climbing only where the valley they followed narrowed and left them no choice but the higher slopes. Cairbre had grazed no cattle in the high passes himself and knew the gatepost signs of that country only as a traveller knows another country's customs, by report rather than by his own hand on the wood; he read, at the last hearth before the true height, a post scraped bare of any mark at all, which he took, as any stranger reading it without a local tongue to ask, for a plain word that the season the mark was cut against had already come and gone. What the bare post did not tell him, because no post along that whole stretch of mountain was cut to tell it, was that the scraping that year had come early, the household who kept that gate having read the geese's own lateness as the country's other households read it, and judged, wrongly as it turned out, that a season slow to come would also be slow to hold, and cleared their post a full ten days before the smell that should have kept it standing came back up to the saddle above them.

Cairbre and the ten who went with him climbed into ground that the crossing had, in fact, already claimed for that turning of the year, reading the empty shielings and the bare posts around them as leave to go on rather than as the same closed season written in a hand they had not learned to read. They did not come down again. No household of that country, questioned afterward by the searchers who came up from the shore once the errand's length began to trouble those left behind, could say they had seen the eleven pass, or heard anything in the high ground in the nights the errand must have crossed it beyond what every household in the crossing season heard and had long since stopped remarking on. The kin two valleys in, whom Cairbre had set out to help, waited through that autumn and into the winter for a party that never reached them, and came themselves in the spring down to the shore to ask after men they had begun, by then, to fear they would never see arrive.

Cairbre's wife kept the twelfth day and the thirteenth and the fourteenth without word, and told herself, as any wife of that shore would have told herself, that a household two valleys in might have kept the eleven a few extra days over some kindness owed for the carrying, or that the track down was slower loaded than the track up had been empty. She watched the path from the same rise she had stood on to see them go, morning and evening, through a fifteenth day and a twentieth, and by the thirtieth day had stopped telling herself anything at all, and only watched. No household along that shore sent word up into the hills after them that autumn, for none of them knew, any more than Cairbre himself had known, which valley among several the eleven had meant to reach, or by which of several tracks; and winter closed the higher ground before any search could have been made of it in any case, so that the shore was left, through that whole first winter, with nothing to do but wait for a spring thaw that might, at least, let a search be tried.

The same year, at Tobar dhìreach, a son was born to the household that kept the fire-pot and the bag of coals, a big child from his first week who closed his hand round a finger held out to him and would not readily let it go, so that they said of him, as they would go on saying of him, that he had been born holding on. He was given the name Suibhne, and grew, in the years the shore still watched for word of Cairbre and found none, into a boy old enough to be sent with the herds and old enough, when the fire-pot's keeping fell to him in his own turn, to hear the whole of the Line said over him standing, as every keeper before him had heard it. A search was tried in the spring after the eleven went missing, and again the spring after that, men of the shore and of the two valleys both walking the ground the errand should have crossed as far as the season allowed them, and both times came down again with nothing to show for the walking but blistered feet and a country too wide, and too indifferent to a searcher's want of answers, to give up eleven men for the asking.

## V. Of the Bones Along the Road

It was eighteen years before anyone found what had become of Cairbre and the ten who had gone with him, and it was chance, by every account, and not any renewed search, that found it at last: a household driving cattle down off the high pasture at the proper close of the crossing season some eighteen years after, keeping well within the marked track as every household of that country had always kept it, came on a scatter of bone some way off the track itself, where the ground fell steeply enough on one side that a man losing his footing in the dark might easily have gone down it without meaning to. The drover who found the first of it took it at a glance for a deer's remains, weathered white and half sunk into the turf, and might have driven on and thought no more of it had one of his own dogs not worried a length of it out of the ground with a fixed intent no deer-bone had ever raised in the animal before, and set it down at his feet whole and unmistakable for what it was.

They found more, over the days that followed, spread wider than any one fall could account for, some of it gathered close in twos and threes as though the men it had belonged to had stood together at the end and some of it strung out along a line that ran, when the whole of it was at last laid out and looked at together, roughly the same course the cats themselves were known to keep between one saddle and the next. Nothing that had been carried with the eleven, no strap or blade or scrap of the harvest they had gone to fetch, was found among the bones or anywhere near them, so that the searchers who gathered them could not even say for certain, past the count of the bones themselves, that this had been the whole of the party and not some lesser part of it.

Nothing about the bones themselves said plainly what had killed the men they had been, and no one who found them claimed afterward to know for certain; the households of that country, hearing the tale carried down from the shore where word of it travelled fastest, said only what they had always said of that ground in that season, that eleven men had gone up into it at the one time of year no one went up into it by choice, and that the road itself, whatever walked it, had not troubled to leave more of an answer than the bones gave. A young man from Tobar dhìreach, eighteen years old that year and newly given the keeping of his own household's fire though not yet the full charge of the Line, walked with the search that carried word of the find back down from the passes, and came home from it saying little, by the account of those who knew him, beyond the plain fact of what had been found and where.

The southern shore buried what could be gathered of the eleven where they were found, high on the mountain's own ground rather than carried the long way home, for no household among them could say with any certainty which bones had belonged to which man, and it seemed to the country a lesser wrong to lay eleven men together where the road had taken them than to guess wrongly at dividing them among eleven separate graves. Cairbre's own name outlasted the rest in the telling, as a leader's name will outlast the men who followed him, but the shore kept, alongside it, the plainer fact the whole country had already made of the matter before his bones were ever found: that the cats' road asked to be read by its own signs and no other's, that a gatepost cut for one household's season could not be trusted by a stranger's eye, and that the crossing months, whatever walked them and for whatever reason, kept a count of their own that no household's calendar, however carefully watched, had yet learned to keep exactly with it.
