# Of Seathan's Snap

*VE 4,905 – 4,950*

## I. Of the Fire Coal in Seathan's Keeping

By the time the saying at the column had gone the length of the hills and back, so that a shepherd of the Doire uaine could say *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann* to a stranger from Àth gharbh and be understood without a word of telling first, the bag at Tobar dhìreach still held two coals unanswered, and had held them so long that the households nearest the hearth had stopped troubling to ask after them at all. Five of the seven had been given up by then to the sea, the height, the earth, a man's own blood and the river below the house, each in its turn and each after its own long waiting, and the recital that named them had grown so familiar at the fire on the night of the lightning that children said the fathers' names along with the reciter before they were old enough to know what a father was. What was left in the bag was the fire coal, *gual teine*, and the coal that held every colour and answered to none, *gual bogha-froise*, the rainbow; and of the two the fire coal was reckoned, by most who troubled to reckon it at all, the likelier of the pair, since fire was a thing the line already kept and fed and carried from hearth to hearth every day of its life, and it seemed a smaller step from keeping a fire to waking one than from nothing at all.

Seathan was born into the keeping of it, at Tobar dhìreach, the only son of a father whose name the reciters do not give to strangers, being one of the ten they keep back between Colla and himself; and he had the fire coal in his own two hands, by his own account, before he could well remember not having it, turning it over as every child of that hearth turned it over, black and ordinary to the eye but for the faint sheen that ran through it when the light caught it a certain way, like the last colour left in an ember that has gone from red to grey. His father kept the bag until Seathan was twenty, walking with it as every keeper before him had walked, to the sea twice in Seathan's own boyhood and to the high moor once, and coming home each time with nothing to show for the walking but the same two dark coals and the same tired shrug he gave the household at the door, so that Seathan grew up, as Eòghann had grown up seven hundred years before him and Cormac five hundred years before that, watching a grown man's failure become, by long repetition, no more remarkable than the weather.

He took the bag over on his father's death, in his own twentieth year, and did not, in the years that followed, walk with it the way his father had walked. He had watched enough walking from the doorway of that same house to have formed, without ever quite saying so to anyone, a private opinion that the walking answered nothing, that a coal carried a hundred miles and thrown into water was no likelier to catch than a coal held quiet in a man's own palm by his own hearth, and that the recital's telling of five lightings — the sea taken by a man who had never before seen it, the height by a man who reasoned his way up to it, the earth by a man who buried it like the dead, the blood by a man's own hurt, the river by a man too tired to reason at all — argued, if it argued anything, that no manner of asking had yet been tried twice with the same result, and that a man might as well ask in the way that came naturally to him as go hunting after some other man's manner that had happened, once, to answer. So Seathan kept the fire coal near him rather than carrying it abroad, put it into the household's own fire on the night he took the keeping and on every night since when the old fire was rekindled from the pot after some absence, watched it lie in the flame with the wood and the peat about it, unburning and unchanged while everything around it blackened and fell to ash, and took it out again each time no worse for the fire than it had gone in, and set it back in the bag, and waited.

He had put it, by his own count, into the pot once, into the fire the line held to be the mason's own, carried unbroken from hearth to hearth since the night of the lightning; and it had lain in that fire as it lay in every other, dark and cool to the eye though the flame stood all around it, and Seathan had watched it a long while that night with the household asleep behind him, and gone to his own bed no wiser than he had sat down. He did not, after that, expect very much of any fire he put it into, though he went on putting it into fires all the same, the way a man goes on doing a small habitual thing less from any hope in it than from the discomfort of leaving off something that has become, over enough years, a part of how an evening is spent.

Seathan married, in his six-and-twentieth year, a woman named Caitir, of a household two valleys to the east that kept no coal and no telling beyond the ordinary one every hearth in the hill country now knew by heart, and she came to Tobar dhìreach knowing, as every woman who married into that house came knowing, that she was marrying a keeper and not only a husband, and that the bag on its peg above the fire-pot was a third presence in the household that no wife before her had ever managed to be rid of, however she might in time come to be fond of it. Caitir was neither so patient with the waiting as Dearbhail had been at Eòghann's hearth seven hundred years before, nor so impatient with it as some wives of other keepers were remembered to have been; she said of Seathan, to her own kin when she visited them, that she had married a man who thought a great deal and walked very little, and that she would sooner have him sitting where she could see him of an evening, turning a black stone over in his fingers and saying nothing, than have him gone a week at a time over hill and shore the way his father had gone, and if the coal never woke at all she judged that no great loss set against a husband who came home to his own supper every night of the year.

They had children, three of whom lived past their weaning, and Seathan told the Telling to each in turn as it had been told to him, the Keeper in the grove and the smoke that stood still in a gale and the riddle of the three spirits, and watched each of his children, in the way of every child of that house before them, take up the fire coal from its bag on some idle evening and turn it in the firelight, half in earnest and half in play, the way a child handles any wonder it has grown too used to to be properly afraid of. The years went by him in that house in much this manner, the fire coal in its bag or in his hand or in some fire that could not touch it, the recital said each year on the night of the lightning with his own name given now among the fathers who came after Colla, and no more happening in all that span, by his own reckoning of it, than happens to any man who keeps a hearth and raises children and grows, without quite meaning to, into the settled middle of his own life.

## II. Of the Warm Evening

It was the sixteenth day of an t-Ògmhios, and the evening had come on warm and still after a day of full sun, the kind of evening the high valleys got perhaps a dozen times in a good summer, when the heat stood in the ground and the midges hung thick over the burn and no one troubled to build up the hearth-fire before dark because the warmth of the day had not yet gone out of the walls. Seathan sat in the doorway of his own house with the last of the light on his hands. He had no particular business there; the doorway caught what air moved on such an evening, and the inside of the house did not. The fire coal was out of its bag, as it often was on a quiet evening, and he turned it in his fingers less to test it than for the plain unremarkable comfort of a smooth stone in the hand, the way another man might turn a whetstone or a knife-hilt while his mind ran on something else entirely.

He was not, that evening, thinking of the fire coal at all, whatever the recital afterward made of the moment. He was thinking, so far as he could ever afterward say what he had been thinking, of the mason, and of the well the mason had been sinking on a morning nearly five thousand years further back than any living man could properly picture, and of the lightning that had come down into that well out of a storm and set a vein of black stone burning that had lain unburning under the hill since before there were hearths on the island to burn anything at all. It was an old thought, one he had turned over at that same doorway more evenings than he could count, and it came to him now, as it had come to him before, that the mason's fire had needed nothing carried to it and nothing thrown into any sea; it had simply come, in a single crack of light, out of nothing that any man beforehand could have named as its cause, the way a dry year ends in one afternoon's storm and no man can say, watching the sky the morning before, that this was the day the rain would come.

His fingers turned the coal as he thought it, working at it the way a man works absently at a thing his hands have long since learned to handle without his mind's leave, pressing and turning until the coal's outer face, dried by thirty years of handling and by that day's own sun on the doorstone, began to crumble under his thumb into a fine black dust that caught in the lines of his palm.

He did not decide to snap his fingers, and said so every time afterward that he was asked, and he was asked a great many times in the years that were left to him: his hand had done it the way a hand does a thing it has done ten thousand times before, without asking the rest of the man whether this time was different from the last, a boy's trick, a man's idle habit, the small dry crack a finger and thumb make when they are pressed and loosed the right way, and he had done it perhaps a hundred times that same summer alone, at that same doorstone, with dust of one kind or another on his fingers and his mind on something else.

He snapped his fingers.

The dust on them took fire.

It went up off the back of his hand in a small quick rush, no larger than the flare a dry twig gives when it first catches, and it was the colour of the fire coal itself, a clear red-gold, brighter by a good measure than any hearth-flame he had sat beside in all his years of keeping it, and it lit the doorway around him for the length of a breath the way a struck spark lights a dark room, so that Caitir, coming to the door from within at the sound he made, saw his whole face for a moment lit from below as if he held a small sun cupped in his hand.

He shouted, more from the shock of the light than from any hurt, and beat his hand hard against the front of his coat the way a man beats out a spark that has caught in dry cloth, and the flame went out under the blow as quick as it had come, and he stood a moment with his hand still pressed to his chest before he brought it away and looked at it in the last of the daylight.

There was not a mark on it. The skin lay whole and unreddened across the back of his hand and along each finger, no different than it had lain that morning, and the black dust that had covered his fingers a moment before was gone entirely, burned away to nothing that even a careful eye could find on him.

He stood looking at his own open hand for what Caitir afterward said was a good deal longer than the thing had taken to happen, turning it front and back in what light was left, and then he sat down heavily on the doorstone where he had been sitting before, and began to laugh.

He laughed, by Caitir's telling of it, in a way she had not heard from him in all the years of their marriage: a long helpless laughing, far past the short dry laugh he gave at some jest of an evening, that bent him forward over his own knees and would not let him straighten to answer her when she asked, twice and then a third time, standing over him in the doorway with her hands still floury from the evening's baking, what in the world had come over him. Between one fit of it and the next he got out that the fire had been in his hand the whole time, that he had carried it thirty years in a bag on a peg and walked nowhere with it and asked nothing of sea or height or earth, and it had been sitting the whole while in the one place he had never once thought to ask, which was his own two hands doing what his own two hands had always done. Caitir understood perhaps a third of what he was telling her and none of why it should be so funny; she sat down on the stone beside him at last and took his hand in both of hers and turned it over herself, front and back, to see with her own eyes that there was truly no mark on it.

## III. Of the Telling at the Hearth

Word of it went from that doorway to every hearth within a day's walk before the week was out, carried first by the children, who had seen their father laughing on the doorstone in a way none of them could account for and had run to tell their mother's kin of it before they had rightly understood what there was to tell, and then by Caitir herself, who found, once she had turned the thing over in her own mind a night and a day, that she believed it a good deal more than her first hearing of it had let her believe, and said so plainly to whoever asked. Seathan told it himself at the recital that same year, on the night of the lightning, standing at the hearth of Tobar dhìreach with the bag counted out before him as every keeper before him had counted it, one coal now gone from two to one; and he told it, by every account that came down from that telling, in much the plainer words he had used to Caitir on the doorstone, without dressing the thing up in any manner beyond the bare truth of it, that the fire had been in his own hand the whole time he carried it, in the hand and in the snap of it, the way the lightning had been in the sky the whole time before the mason ever set his wedge in the seam that woke it, and that a man could go looking a long way off for a thing he had carried on him from birth without once thinking to look there.

The old women of the line who remembered the silver's lighting in their own girlhood, and had heard from their own mothers of the black and the red-brown before that, wept a little at the telling, the recital says: no grief in it, only the plain accumulated weight of four thousand years since the grove, now come round again to a sixth answer; and there were those among the younger households, who had grown up thinking the two remaining coals no more likely ever to wake than the slab was likely to lift on its own, who came away from that telling with a new respect for the bag on its peg that their own fathers had not troubled to give it. The valley began, that same winter, to call the coal *gual teine*, the fire coal, in the telling that had before called it only the sixth or the last-but-one, and some among the households furthest from Tobar dhìreach, hearing the tale at one remove and then at two, told it back with small changes of their own, so that within a very few years there were tellers who had Seathan snapping his fingers a-purpose, meaning to try the fire coal that way as a fisherman tries a new knot, and had to be corrected by those nearer the hearth who had heard it plain from Caitir's own mouth, that there had been no purpose in it at all, only a man thinking of the mason with dust on his fingers.

There remained, after that telling, only the rainbow, *gual bogha-froise*, the coal that held every colour the eye could give a name to and had answered, in all the four thousand years since the grove, to none of them; and it was this coal, and not the fire coal's own waking, that the household at Tobar dhìreach turned its mind to in the years that followed, for a man does not sit easy with one question answered while a second sits unanswered in the same bag on the same peg. Seathan, for his own part, said more than once at his own hearth that he had come to doubt any single manner of asking would serve the rainbow as it had served none of the rest by the same means twice; the sea had taken the gold, the height the snowflake, the earth the black, a man's blood the red-brown, the river the silver, and his own idle hand the sixth, and no two of those six had come by the same road, so that a man hoping to wake the seventh by copying any one of them was, to his mind, hoping against the very pattern the other six had already shown him.

He carried it to the northern shore himself, in the second year after his own coal's waking, more out of the old custom that every keeper before him had kept than from any real expectation in it, and threw it into the same grey swell his forebears had thrown coals into for generation on generation, and waded in after it in the falling light to find it exactly as dark and salt-wet as it had gone in. He carried it, the following year, down to the crossing below the house where the silver still burned under the current as it had burned since Eòghann's day, a pale fire any traveller might kneel at the bank to see, and held the rainbow a long while over the water without throwing it at all, reasoning that if the river had already taken one coal it might, given the chance, take a second without the trial of a throw; and when nothing in the water's own light or sound gave him any sign that it meant to answer, he threw it in as he had thrown the fire coal's dust into nothing, and fished it out again some way downstream the next morning, cold and ordinary among the wet stones. Caitir went with him on both of those journeys, the recital says, the only keeper's wife of the line remembered to have gone rather than stayed by the fire-pot at home, and it was she, by her own later telling to her daughters, who first said aloud what afterward became a saying among Clann na Ceiste, that the rainbow held every spirit and so belonged wholly to none of them, and might in the end be a coal no single element would ever claim for its own, whatever the line went on doing with it.

## IV. Of Seathan's Death and What Was Left

Seathan kept the bag some fifty years in all, from his father's death to his own, and in that whole span only the fire coal answered him, and it answered him, as he never tired of telling it, on an evening when he had gone looking for nothing at all. He grew old at Tobar dhìreach in much the way his own father had grown old before him, the household about him thickening with grandchildren as his own children married into neighbouring hearths and brought husbands and wives back to sit at his fire, and he told the Telling to each new face that came into that circle as he had told it to his own children, word for word as his father had given it to him, the smoke that stood still in the gale and the riddle of the three spirits and the six lightings that had gone before his own; and he told, along with it now, the seventh telling that was his to give and no other man's, of the dust on his fingers and the snap and the light that had shown his own wife his face from below in a doorway at dusk.

He died in his three-and-seventieth year, at his own hearth, with the fire-pot burning near him as it had burned near every keeper of the line before him back to Conall who first kept the vein alight; and Caitir, who was with him at the end, said afterward that his last clear words left the rainbow alone, though it had beaten him as it had beaten every keeper before him, and asked instead for the fire coal, that she bring it to him from its place in the bag so that he might hold it once more in his own hand while there was still warmth in that hand to hold it with. She brought it to him, and he held it a while, saying nothing further that she could afterward make out, and was found at first light with it still closed in his fingers, cold as it had always been cold, though the hand that held it had by then gone cold as well.

The bag passed from his grave to his eldest son, a quiet man who had never expected, by his own account, to be given so great a charge while his father's hand was still warm; and Caitir stayed on at Tobar dhìreach some six years further, keeping the fire-pot fed as she had kept it fed through the whole of her marriage, and telling, to whoever asked her at her own hearth, the same plain telling of the doorstone that Seathan had told at the recital, growing no fonder of the dressed-up versions that had gone about the valley than she had been the first year she heard them. She tried the rainbow herself once more in that time, at the river below the house, more for the comfort of doing something with it than from any hope she troubled to name, and it lay in her palm afterward as dark and unmoved as it had lain in Seathan's, and she put it back in the bag without any great disappointment in her face, by the account of those who were there, for she had long since ceased, she said, to expect that coal to answer to a woman who only wished it well and asked for nothing in return.

She died in her three-and-seventieth year also, the same age her husband had reached before her, and was buried beside him near the slab, and the bag by then held only the rainbow, one coal against the six that had gone before it, in a leather grown soft and dark with the handling of more generations than anyone could count since the mason first filled his tunic with it in the rain. The keeping passed on from her son's hearth in its turn, as it had passed from every keeper's hearth before, carrying the one coal still unanswered into years that Seathan and Caitir did not live to see; and the households of the high valleys, telling it over at their own fires in the winters that followed, said of the fire coal what they had not troubled to say of any of the five before it, that it alone among the six already answered had asked of its keeper no long journey and no hard trial, only a tired man's idle hand on a warm evening, doing a small thing it had done ten thousand times without ever once, until that evening, being asked to answer for it.
