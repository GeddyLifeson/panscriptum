# Of the Shore, the Sea-Way and the Godkin

*VE 4,275 – 4,474*

## I. Of the High Places Left to the Rain

The temple stood empty a long while before any hill teller thought it worth the telling. It was the oldest of the coast-people's high works, older by every reckoning than the fortress and the outpost both, its platform cut and squared before any teller's grandfather's grandfather had a grandfather of his own, and its altar had looked from the first day of its setting toward the central mountains and the vein beneath them, its face turned inland and its back to the sea it stood in sight of. That was remembered of it after everything else was forgotten: that a people who lived by the water and buried their dead upon islands set in the water had built, once, one thing that faced away from it, and had then, in the fullness of years, walked away from that thing and gone back to the water for good.

No one now living had climbed those steps and found them tended. The rain that stood in the altar's two sockets after every shower stood there now as it had stood for two lifetimes running, giving back the sky and nothing else, and the grass between the fitted stones of the platform had thickened past any hand's troubling to clear it into a green so settled it looked less like neglect than like a second, slower kind of building, the hill itself laying its own claim over the coast-people's old one, stone by stone, season by season. A boy of the eastern moor, sent up one summer to see whether the place still held anything worth a household's fearing, came down again with nothing to report but grass and rain and the two old sockets, and was believed at once, for by then no one asked the question who did not already know the answer it would bring back.

It was not only the temple. Of the three high works that the hill country had once watched from its own safe distance, wall and gate and altar-stone, not one stood tended now, and what the tellers had begun, in those years, to notice and to say to one another at the hearth was not the emptying of any one of them but the shape the emptying made together, seen all at once from a height of years rather than from the ground of any single season. The outpost's gate had stood open to every weather since before the eldest living teller's own birth. The fortress wall had held its last fire out longer than the outpost, but not by much, and its cistern had stood fouled and undrawn for longer again. And now the temple, last of the three and oldest, had gone the same road, so that a man standing at any one of the three ruins on a clear day could, by the tellers' own reckoning of the distances between them, have seen smoke from none of the other two, only bare stone under a bare sky, three heights within a morning's walking of one another and not a fire lit on any of them.

What the coast-people did instead, the shore itself made plain enough without any telling needed to explain it, for a people's own doings, watched close enough and long enough from a safe distance, tell themselves in the end. Every new work of theirs that the hill country's watchers marked in those years, a hearth-ring, a midden, a low wall of piled stone meant to break a wind rather than to keep out a stranger, stood now within sight of salt water, and none stood further from it than a morning's walk would carry a man burdened with nothing heavier than the day's fetching. It was as though the height itself had come, in some reckoning of theirs that no outside eye was ever near enough to hear given, to stand for a kind of cold that the sea did not carry, whatever cold the sea itself might bring on a hard winter's tide; and a people that had once built wall and gate and altar a long climb above their own boats had come round, by the slow turning of one generation into the next, to building nothing any longer that a boat could not reach before dark.

At the southern landing the change was plainest, for it was there that the hill country's own watchers, being nearer that stretch of coast than any other, kept the closest account. The middens that had grown fast enough in the years just after the fortress's last fire to earn their own place in the tellers' reckoning grew faster again now, thick with shell and bone in heaps that a household of the hill country, counting its own leavings against them, would have taken for the refuse of three households where the coast-people's own tellers, had any hill ear ever been given the true count, would have named it the ordinary leavings of one, swollen by kin come down from every height the coast still had left to give up. A landing that a generation before had held its households by the dozen came, in the years a boy might grow to a grown man and bury his own father in turn, to hold them by the score, its fires at night, by the account of a hill traveller who had once passed near enough after dark to count them honestly, standing closer together along that stretch of shore than any watcher of the hill country had ever seen fires stand on any coast before.

No teller of the hill country, then or afterward, claimed to know why the coast-people held so close to the water in those years, any more than any teller had ever claimed to know why they had first climbed to the heights they now gave up, and the hill country, having asked the one question of them for longer than any teller's memory could carry and been given no answer to it that a hill ear could ever make sense of, had by then mostly stopped asking. What was said, when it was said at all, was said the way the shore's own old woman had once been heard to speak of the height she had left, without anger and without relief, only the plain fact of a going and a coming laid one against the other: that a people who had gone up for reasons none outside them had ever been given had come down again for reasons kept just as close, and that the sea, whatever it truly was to them, was at the least a thing they meant now to keep always within reach of their own two feet.

## II. Of the Rowers of the Ancient Way

Along the coast of the south-east, where the land ran low and shelved gently into water calm enough for most of the year to carry a boat without much fear of it, the coast-people kept a lane of their own that no hill traveller had ever seen them mark with post or cairn and that every hill traveller who had ever watched it worked knew for a road all the same, as surely as a man knows a path through heather by the wear in it rather than by any sign set up to name it. It ran from the river mouth below the western landing, round the low cape where the grey on the sea never quite lifted even in fair weather, and on to a narrows in the south where the coast folded back on itself and gave a sheltered water on the lee side. Along its whole length, a day's rowing apart or near enough, lay flat stones set down at the water's edge, some no bigger than a hearthstone and some the size of a small house-floor, and every one of them, where a keel had been hauled up across it often enough and long enough, bore a groove worn into its face, smooth-sided and shining faintly with the wet, running always the same way from the water's edge up to whatever dry ground lay behind the stone.

The hill country had a name for it from its own tongue, Slighe-mhara àrsaidh, the ancient sea-way, and the fishers of the near coast, whose own boats never went far enough out to cross it but whose eyes had watched it worked from the shore often enough to know its pattern, kept the name among themselves and passed it to their children the way a family passes down the name of a ford or a spring, a thing worth knowing though it belonged to no one they knew.

A rower of the coast-people, on any ordinary day of that work, knew none of what the hill country called it and would not have troubled himself over the naming had he known. He knew the weight of the boat under him, a hide-hulled thing built light enough for two to carry between them over open ground where the water gave out, and he knew the pull of his own oar against a current that ran, along that whole stretch of coast, more strongly with the tide than against any set course a rower might choose for himself. He set out from the river mouth in the grey before full light, as the boats of that lane were set out on most fair mornings, with another beside him at the second oar and a third crouched low amidships among what the boat carried, dried fish wrapped in kelp, a coil of worked hide rope, two stone tools whose use no hill eye had ever been near enough to make out.

The morning's work was long before it was hard. He rowed with the low sun coming up somewhere behind the land to his left, the water calm enough that the boat's own wake was the only broken water for as far as he could see in either direction, and he watched, as he had watched on every morning of this same work since he was old enough to take an oar, the low grey line of the coast slide past at the pace of his own two arms, headland after headland, none of them named to him in any word he had ever heard spoken, each one known instead by its shape against the sky and by what lay behind it, the shape a man learns of a coast he has rowed a hundred times rather than the name a man learns of a place he has only heard told.

The first landing-stone of that day's stretch came at the turn of a low headland where the water shallowed enough to show the pale stone through it before the boat was fairly on it, a slab the width of three men lying flat where the shore itself ran flat, its groove worn deep enough by then that the boat's own keel found it without any hand needing to guide it there, the way a cart's wheel finds the ruts of a much-used track without the carter troubling to watch the ground. They ran the boat up it together, the third man out first to take the weight at the bow, and stood a while on the dry stone above the tideline while the man at the second oar worked a stiffness out of his shoulder and the first ate a strip of the dried fish without unwrapping the rest, looking back the way they had come at the water they had crossed and forward at the water still ahead, saying, in the whole of that pause, nothing that any hill ear was near enough that morning to have carried home, for none was; only, once, a short sound from the man at the bow, low in his own throat, that might have been a word in some tongue no hill traveller had ever been given whole, or might have been no more than the sound a tired man makes settling a strap across his own shoulder, and was never afterward told to be one or the other by anyone who heard it.

They ran on before the sun stood high, the second stone and the third taken at much the same pace as the first, the groove in each worn a little differently by the lie of its own ground but worn all the same, and came in sight, by the day's own end, of the narrows where the coast folded back and the sheltered water lay still under the last of the light. What they carried on to that far landing, and what they meant to bring back from it when the lane carried them home again in its own season, no hill watcher, near or far, was ever close enough to say, for the lane's own use was, like every other use the coast-people put their high works and their low both to, a thing shown and never explained: the boat run up, the stone crossed, the groove worn a little deeper by one more day's hauling, and the rowers gone on before any question could be put to them by anyone who might have wished to put one.

## III. Of the Night of the Lightning at Àth gharbh

The gathering at Àth gharbh fell, as it fell every year, on the dark of the year's shortest night, when the households of that country came in from their own scattered hearths to the low stone-walled yard where the telling was kept, bringing what fuel each family could spare and setting it to one common fire built larger than any single hearth would have burned alone. It was called, by every household that came to it, the night of the lightning, though the lightning itself had struck nowhere near Àth gharbh and no soul now living, nor any soul their own grandparents had known, had stood near enough to that first storm to have felt its heat; the name had travelled with the telling itself, out from the high valleys where the mason had first knelt over his broken well, until it belonged, by the slow right of long use, to every hearth that told the tale rather than only to the one where it had happened.

Rathnait came to that fire the year she was five-and-twenty with her mother's mother's hand still, out of old habit, resting light on her shoulder as they crossed the yard together, though the old woman no longer needed steadying on ground she had walked every year of her own long life and Rathnait no longer needed the guiding it pretended to give. They were of the Mason's own line, near enough in blood that the fire-pot itself, the squat clay vessel that the women of that house carried from hearth to hearth and never once let go cold from one generation's keeping to the next, had passed through Rathnait's own grandmother's hands on three separate winters when the household that ordinarily kept it had been too few, or too sick, or too far travelled, to carry it themselves; and it was set that night, as it was set every year, at the near edge of the common fire where the whole yard could see it and no one but the line's own women were let touch it.

The telling itself Rathnait had heard every year of her life and could have given whole herself by the time she was ten, the wedge and the maul and the black break in the stone, the storm come up the valley at dusk, the light gone into the well and the thing that moved in it that was not a beast, the burning piece carried home under a cloak and set among the coloured pieces that caught, one after another, and gave off their heat and let their colours go. It was told that year by Fearchar, oldest of the reciting men at Àth gharbh, in the same words she had heard him give it every year she could remember, his voice pitched to carry to the yard's furthest wall without ever seeming to be raised, and the households sat or stood about the fire in the same rough order they always kept, near kin near the fire and further kin further out, the young ones nearest the flame's own light and the old ones content with its heat alone.

It was after the telling, when the fire had been fed high again and the yard had loosened into the talk and the passing of food that always followed the telling proper, that Rathnait heard the thing said that she would carry, though she did not know it that night, the rest of her own long life. Two households from beyond the eastern ridge, come to Àth gharbh's gathering that year for the first time in Rathnait's own memory, having kept before to a smaller fire of their own nearer their own hearths, sat near enough to Fearchar's own fire that their talk carried to where Rathnait stood with the fire-pot's warmth still on her hands. One of them, a woman near Rathnait's grandmother's own age, was speaking to Fearchar of the Telling itself, the whole long telling that the Mason's line kept and that the wider hill country had by then had generations enough to learn from them, of Caoran the young god and the seven spirits hidden in the coal, and asking him, in the plain way an elder puts a question to another elder without troubling to soften it first, what a household that was not itself of the Mason's line, that had never once seen the coloured coal nor been let near the fire-pot's own keeping, ought rightly to call itself, if it held the Telling as its own the way the whole hill country by then held it.

Fearchar, by Rathnait's own account of it afterward, was slow to answer, turning the question over the way a man turns a stone to see its underside before he commits to lifting it, and it was the woman herself, growing impatient with his slowness or simply following her own thought past the point of waiting for his, who answered it for him before he had found words of his own. They were not made, she said, the way the coast-people were said to be made, out of whatever the coast-people's own beginnings held; nor were they made, so far as any telling of theirs had ever claimed, by any hand or any working that a person could point to and name. They had the Telling's own word for what they were, given first, the Telling itself said, to Diarmad in the grove by the Keeper's own mouth, and repeated by every hearth that had kept the Telling since: children of the makings that Caoran the young god had made, sky and earth and sea alike, and so children, in the plainest sense any household could put to it, of the gods themselves. Clann nan Dè, she said, was the word the Telling gave for it, and Clann nan Dè, she said, turning her own words over as though trying their weight for the first time even as she spoke them, was as good a name as any household of that country was ever likely to be given for what it was.

The yard did not fall silent at it, for no one word spoken at one fire's edge among the food and the talk after a telling could hush a gathering the size of Àth gharbh's, and most of those nearer the far wall never heard it said at all that night, nor learned of it until the word itself had travelled on its own from hearth to hearth in the weeks after, the way every other thing worth carrying from that gathering travelled. But Rathnait heard it, standing where she stood with the fire-pot's warmth still on her hands and her grandmother's hand no longer resting on her shoulder but close enough still that she felt the old woman go still beside her at the sound of it, the way a person goes still at a thing they had long expected without ever once putting a date to the expecting. Fearchar, when he spoke again, did not gainsay it, and by the time the fire had burned low enough that the households began gathering their own kin for the walk home, the word had gone twice more round that same knot of elders near the flame's edge, each time given a little more plainly than the last, the way a new thing is turned into an old one by the simple wearing of repeated use.

Rathnait did not sleep, the tellers say, on the walk back to her own hearth that night, turning the word over herself the whole way as the woman from beyond the ridge had turned it over at the fire, and it was Rathnait's own telling of that gathering, carried by her in turn to her own children and her children's children in the long years she had yet to live, that the hill country kept afterward as the surest account of the night the households first heard themselves named whole. Whether they had always called themselves so, in some hearth further back than any telling reached, or whether the word was as new that night as it sounded to her own ear, no telling that came down from Àth gharbh's gathering ever claimed to settle; what every telling agreed on, from that night forward, was only the saying of it, once, at the edge of a common fire on the night of the lightning, by a woman of a household new to that gathering, to an old man who did not say her wrong: Clann nan Dè. The children of the gods. Said, and not gainsaid, and carried home whole by everyone near enough that night to have heard it.
