# Of the Altar and the Shafts

*VE 2,679 – 2,800*

> *Na las teine far am faic e thu.*
> Light no fire where it can see you.

*The Fourth Part: Of the Black Coal in the Ground, Uilebheist na Mara and Marbh-bheò*

## I. Of the Platform in the Eastern Hills

The last slab of the kerbed track had lain a bare season before the households of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire and Làrach an Dùin began, without any telling saying which of them first raised a hand to it, a work higher into the hills than either wall or ring: a platform of fitted stone built up in steps on a shoulder of ground in the eastern hills, each step no higher than a man's knee and wide enough along its whole run for two people to walk abreast, so that a climb up its face went easier than a climb up any hillside near it, though no telling says why so gentle a climb was ever wanted there in the first place. On the topmost step, set back from its outer edge by rather more than a stride, a single great block of stone was laid and dressed flat on its upper face, and it is this block, more than the steps that carried a person up to it, that the tellings of the hills came in time to mean when they spoke of Làrach an Teampaill.

The stone for the platform was quarried some way off and dragged up on rollers of trimmed timber, the same labour that had raised Làrach an Dùin-fhaire's wall and Làrach an Dùin's three rings, and a household that had helped haul stone for either of those earlier works would have known this hauling for the same kind of work, only carried higher and further than either. What set the altar block apart from every other stone Seann-Dhaoine had ever cut and laid, in the eastern hills or on any shore before them, was not its size, for stones as large had gone into Làrach an Dùin's walls, but the line its face was cut to lie along, for while every mole, every ramp, every kiln and every round house Seann-Dhaoine had ever raised faced the water that fed it, salt or fresh, near or by sight, the top of this one stone was squared instead to the great mountains standing in the middle of the island, a good many miles off and out of sight of any sea at all from where the block was set.

Who first laid a level along that line, and by what means, no telling that has come down says, and none of the households that climbed the platform's steps in the years that followed ever troubled, in any word remembered of them, to ask. A woman old enough to have climbed those steps as a girl and again as a grandmother said only, of the one time she was asked by a stranger why the great stone at the top looked where it looked and not where the sea lay, that the stone had always looked that way and that a thing so set before her own grandmother's grandmother was no more hers to question than the shape of her own hand.

In the first years of the platform's standing, before habit had worn a clear path up its steps, a household of the southern lowland is remembered to have gone up at first light on the shortest morning of the year, carrying nothing between them that any telling names, and to have stood some while about the great stone as the sun came up over the sea behind them and its first light ran along the top of the block and out toward the mountains it was squared to, touching, for the little while the sun stood low enough to throw a light so far, the same line the stone held. What was said at that standing, if anything was said, has not come down; what has come down is only that the household went up again the next year at the same season, and the year after that, and that others of the lowland, seeing them go, began in time to go up too, until the shortest morning of the year found more households climbing the steps together than any single household could have climbed alone.

A boy of one such household, sent up for the first time old enough to be trusted on the steps without a hand held out for him, is remembered to have asked, on the climb, what it was they were going up to see, and to have been told by whoever climbed beside him only that they were going up to see the light meet the stone, an answer that satisfied him well enough at the time and that he is remembered, grown old in his turn, to have given in the same words to a child of his own asking the same question on the same climb. No one of the eastern hills, in any telling that has come down whole, ever said more than that of what the standing was for, or what, if anything, the mountains the stone looked toward were held to hold. A crowd need not know why it gathers each year at the same season to watch a light meet a stone; the tellings remember the gathering, kept and passed on, far more surely than they remember why it began.

The rite, once it had settled into a yearly thing, kept always to the one morning and never to any other, whatever the weather made of that morning, so that a year came, within living memory of those who told of it after, when cloud lay thick over the eastern hills from the day before the shortest morning until well past it, and no light reached the stone at all that year for any household to see meet it; and it is remembered that the households climbed the steps all the same, in the dark and the cold, and stood about the great stone through the whole of the morning it would have taken the light to cross it, and went down again when the hour had passed without a word of complaint at what the cloud had cost them, holding, so far as the telling says anything of their holding, that the standing was owed whether the light came to be seen or not.

None of the households that kept the rite, in all the years this telling follows, ever cut so much as a single mark into the great stone, though marks were being cut in those same years on the cliffs of the eastern coast by hands of their own people, and the stone stood at the century's end exactly as it had been dressed at the century's start, bare of any sign but the flatness of its own face and the squareness of its own cut. A woman who kept, among her own household's things, a small carved bird of the kind the Seann-Dhaoine of the shore sometimes carved and buried with their dead, is remembered to have carried it up once and set it at the stone's foot rather than take it home again, and to have gone down without it; whether this was the rite's own custom or a thing she alone did, no telling that has come down can now say, for no second such bird, and no third, was ever found at the stone's foot by anyone who came after her.

The steps themselves asked more years to finish than the great stone atop them asked to raise, for a household laying flat stone into a slope worked slower than a household piling wall on open ground, each slab bedded and levelled before the next was set above it, and the work was carried on, by the tellings that name it at all, mostly in the months between one planting and the next, when the hands that dug and hauled were not also wanted for the ground below. A man who gave the better part of his own years to the laying of the upper steps, where the slope stood steepest and the stone wanted the most patient setting, is remembered to have refused more than once to let a slab go into place before he had knelt on it himself and rocked his own weight across it, holding that a step a person's foot could feel give, even by a hand's width, was a step that would, given the years, throw someone from it, and that the platform's whole worth lay in a climb no one troubled to watch their own feet upon. The last step, laid some years after the great stone had already stood on its own step above the unfinished work below it, closed the run at the bottom flush with the moor's own ground, so that a person coming to it from level walking felt no more than the least catch underfoot before the climb began, and it is this last, easiest step, rather than any higher and harder-won one, that the tellings of the households nearest the platform remember best, for it was the one every child among them first learned to find with a bare foot in the dark, going up before dawn on the one morning of the year the climb was made.

A stranger to the eastern hills, come up from the southern lowland on some errand the telling does not trouble to name, is remembered to have stood at the platform's foot one year on an ordinary day, neither the shortest morning nor any day near it, and to have asked of a woman drawing water nearby what the great stone at the top was raised for; and she is remembered to have answered him, in words a granddaughter of hers repeated to others, that a stone which had stood before her grandmother's grandmother needed no raising explained to a stranger who had not troubled to be born to it, and to have gone on with her drawing without looking up again at either the stranger or the stone. He is not remembered to have climbed the steps himself, that day or any other, nor to have come back a second time; and no telling of the eastern hills, from that year to the end of this one, names him again.

## II. Of the Shaft at the Eastern Toll-dubh

Some years into the platform's keeping, and some way off from it, at a place in the south-east the hills already called Toll-dubh before any hand had touched it, a household began a work of a wholly different kind: not up, as the platform went, and not out along a shore, as every mole and every ramp before it had gone, but straight down into the ground, on level moor where no well and no quarry had ever yet been cut.

The first cut was no deeper than a well's own depth, and for the better part of a year it might have passed, to anyone who came upon it without knowing better, for a well begun and left, the kind of half-finished digging a household abandons when the water it hoped for does not come. But the digging went on past the depth any well on that moor had ever needed to reach, and it went on straight, cut round and true as a well is cut, but with its walls worked smooth by hand long after the digging alone would have left them rough, an old man of the household standing at the lip each evening with a lamp let down on a cord to judge, by the reach of its light along the walls, how far the smoothing had been carried and how much of the day's work still wanted doing before the light gave out on him.

The baskets that came up out of the shaft, full of the broken rock the digging threw off, were carried away each evening over the rise at the moor's edge by whoever's turn it was to carry them, and were not, by any telling that has come down of that household, brought back empty the way a basket carried only a little way is brought back; and no heap of the rock so carried was ever afterward found anywhere near the shaft's mouth, nor anywhere on the moor about it that anyone troubled to walk out and look. Where rock enough to fill so many baskets went, over years enough to sink a shaft so deep, no telling that has come down so much as guesses at; the telling gives only the carrying, evening on evening, and the going, and lets the rest lie where the household itself let it lie.

A woman of that household, grown old in the years the digging went on, is remembered to have said to a granddaughter asking after the rock's going, only that some work is carried and some work is asked after, and that a household did well to know which was which before it went asking the wrong question of the wrong work. The granddaughter, so the telling says, never asked again, though whether from being satisfied by the answer or from knowing, by the way it was given, that no better answer would follow it, the telling does not say either.

The lamp was let down further with each year the shaft went deeper, on a longer length of the same plaited cord each time, and it is remembered that a length was added to the cord on three separate occasions before the digging stopped, each addition marked, in the one telling that names it at all, only by the household gathering at the lip on the evening a new length was first paid out, to watch the lamp go down further than it had ever gone before and come up again still burning. On the last such evening the telling names, the lamp went down so far that its light, by the time it reached the bottom, showed no more of the walls around it than a small round patch no wider than a man's two hands held apart, the rest of the shaft's depth standing dark above and below that patch alike, and it was on this same evening, or very near it, that the digging is remembered to have stopped, though no telling says in so many words that the two things were one and the same, or that the small patch of light was the reason the digging went no further.

A stone let fall from the lip on an evening after the digging had stopped, by a hand the telling does not name, gave back no sound of striking anything below, though the household that let it fall stood listening a good while after it left the hand, in a silence that the telling remembers as longer than the silence any of them had kept for anything else in all the years of that digging, well or ill.

The work was not without its cost to the hands that did it, for a shaft cut so narrow and so straight gave a person let down into it no room to fall wrong without striking the wall on the way, and a young man of the household, sent down one autumn on the cord to free a basket that had caught against a shoulder of unworked rock some way below the lip, is remembered to have come up again with a gash along one forearm that kept him from the heaviest carrying the better part of a season, and to have gone down again the following spring all the same, the cord tied about him the same way it had always been tied, as if a single wound was owed no more weight in the reckoning than a blister from the oar or the mattock. The cord was renewed twice in the years the digging went on, the old length kept afterward rather than thrown aside, coiled and hung inside the household's own door, so that a visitor coming to that threshold afterward might see, without being told what it had once measured, two lengths of worn hide cord hanging where another household might have hung a net or a snare.

Winter closed the shaft each year before the ground froze hard enough to trouble the digging, for the lamp burned poorly in the damp that gathered in the depth once the air above turned cold, and guttered more than once, in tellings that name the guttering at all, before it could be drawn up again; and it became the household's own custom, kept without any single year's telling saying when it began, to lay a flat stone loosely over the shaft's mouth each year at the first hard frost and lift it again only when the days had lengthened past the shortest, a covering meant, so far as the telling says anything of its meaning, to keep a wandering beast or a careless foot from the lip through the months no one went down to work it, rather than to shut anything already below away from anything still above.

## III. Of the Shaft at the Western Toll-dubh

Far across the island, on ground the western households likewise called Toll-dubh before the digging began, a second shaft was cut in these same years, by a household that had no more knowledge of the eastern one, by any telling that has come down of either, than the eastern household had of it. The two shafts were never, in the whole of this telling, said to have been begun at the bidding of one crew working both, or of any word carried between the two households that cut them; each is remembered only for its own digging, by its own people, on its own ground, and it is left to whoever hears both tellings together, side by side, to wonder at how closely the one work answers the other.

The western shaft went down the same way the eastern one had gone, straight and round and smoothed by hand past the depth the digging alone would have left rough, and its baskets were carried off in the same manner, evening on evening, by whoever's turn the carrying fell to, and were never brought back to be tipped where any later hand could find what they had carried. A man of the western household, past the age when the heaviest carrying still fell to him, took instead to keeping the lamp trimmed each evening for whoever went down to judge the smoothing, a smaller work than the digging but one the telling remembers him for all the same, as though the wick kept even and the oil kept full mattered to that household as much as the depth the digging reached that day.

A girl of the same household, young enough still to be sent for water rather than set to carrying, is remembered to have asked her mother, on an evening the lamp came up burning low from further down than it had gone the evening before, whether the shaft would be dug all the way down to wherever the mountains kept their roots; and her mother is remembered to have answered only that the ground kept its own matters, and that a household did well not to ask them aloud, an answer that told the girl nothing of what the shaft was truly for, and that the telling gives, in the end, as the nearest any teller of the western shore ever came to saying why the digging was begun at all.

The western shaft, like its twin in the east, was carried down until the lamp at its bottom showed no more than a small patch of smoothed wall with darkness standing above and below it, and it too, so far as any telling says, stopped there and went no further, though which of the two shafts reached that depth first, or whether they reached it in the same year or years apart, no telling that has come down troubles to say, holding them, in the manner of the tellers of that coast, as two halves of one thing rather than as a first and a second.

It fell to a boy of the western household, the youngest old enough to be trusted with the lamp's own cord, to let the last stone go on the evening the digging there, too, was given over, and he is remembered to have counted, under his breath, the whole time it took the stone to leave his hand until he judged, by his own counting, that it must by then have struck bottom somewhere below him; and when no sound came up to him at the number he had reached, nor at any number after it that the household, standing about him in the last light, troubled to go on counting to, he is said to have looked up at the faces around him rather than down the shaft, as if the listening, and not the stone, was the thing that mattered to be seen doing.

The western household kept, through the years of its own digging, a habit the eastern telling does not name for its own shaft: each evening the lamp came up, whoever had carried it down that day set it, still burning, on a flat stone laid for the purpose a little back from the lip, and let it burn there untended until it went out on its own, rather than snuff it the moment the day's work was done, a small wastefulness of oil that the household never once, in any telling that names it, troubled to give a reason for keeping. A grandmother of that household, asked once by a grandchild why the lamp was left to burn out above ground when it had already done its work below, answered only that a light let go out on its own was no more trouble to anyone than a light snuffed by a hand, and that some things, once begun, were better let run their own course than stopped short by whoever happened to be standing nearest when the stopping crossed their mind; whether she meant the lamp alone by this, or the digging with it, the telling does not say, and the grandchild is not remembered to have asked her to say more.

The two shafts stood after that, east and west, neither filled nor covered, each with its own household keeping, in its own way, a distance from the mouth that the digging had never kept, so that a person of either coast coming near either shaft afterward is remembered to walk wide of the lip rather than close to it, and to let a dropped stone, if a stone was ever again dropped there by anyone, go down into a silence that by then no household troubled any longer to break by listening after it. The platform in the eastern hills went on being climbed each year at the one morning it had always been climbed; the two shafts went on standing where they had been sunk, unfilled and unexplained, the rock that came out of them gone wherever it had gone, and no household of that age, on either coast, is remembered to have asked, aloud and before others, where either people had learned to dig so straight, or so deep, or why, having dug so far down into ground that had never before answered any question anyone had put to it, they let the digging stop at all.

Of the three works this telling has followed, one reaching up toward the mountains and two reaching down past every depth a well or a grave had ever asked of that ground, no single teller of the shore or the hills ever set them side by side in one telling while any of the three still stood unfinished; each was kept, in its own years, as its own household's own matter, told by its own people in its own words, the platform's climbers knowing nothing, so far as any telling says, of the shafts' diggers, and the diggers of the one shaft knowing as little of the other. It is only from the far side of those years, gathering what three separate hearths each kept whole in their own way, that a listener comes to see the one thing plainly enough to be surprised by it: that in these same generations, on three pieces of ground far enough apart that no single pair of feet is remembered to have walked between all three, Seann-Dhaoine stopped building only along the water that had fed every other work of theirs before, and turned instead toward the high ground and the low, the mountains that no boat had ever needed and the dark that no net had ever asked for, as if the shore, for those years, had come to hold less than it once had for the reaching.
