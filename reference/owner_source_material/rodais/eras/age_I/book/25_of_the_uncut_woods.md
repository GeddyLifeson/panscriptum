# Of the Uncut Woods

*VE 3,408 – 3,639*

> *Tha blas eile air an uisce seo seach uisce na mara.*
> This water tastes different from the sea water.

*The Fifth Part: Of the Coal of Life, the Silver in the River, and the Shore*

## I. Of the Wood That Knew Itself First

In the east of that country, a long way past the last of the covered hearths and the last of the arguing that had gone with them, there stood a wood that no household living could say who had first set apart. It was not a young wood. The oaks in it had the girth of three men's arms linked, and the ground beneath them lay dim even at noon of midsummer, the light coming down in coins rather than sheets, and every family whose fields ran up to its edge told the same telling of it, passed down as plainly as the shape of the hills: the trees knew it first, and the people after.

No one could say what that meant, put to the question straight, and no one who lived by that wood ever put it to the question straight, for it was not the kind of telling that wanted testing. It meant, near enough, that the wood had been holy before anyone had thought to hold it so, the way a well is cold before anyone thinks to call it cold; that a household new to the country, walking its dogs along the treeline for the first time and feeling the hair rise on its own arms without cause, was feeling a thing the wood had always done and not a thing the older families had taught it to feel. Sadhbh's grandmother had told it to Sadhbh's mother in those words, sitting at the hearth with the shutters closed against an autumn gale, and Sadhbh's mother told it to Sadhbh in turn on a morning much like any other, when the girl was six years old and had asked, the way children ask, why the wood-carriers went the long way round instead of through.

The long way round was no small thing to choose, in that country. The nearest true fuel-wood outside the holy trees lay the better part of half a day's walk beyond them, over a shoulder of open ground that gave no shelter from weather and less from the walking, so that a household cutting its winter store made two journeys where one would have served it, had it been willing to put an axe to anything standing within the wood's own bounds. No one was. Sadhbh grew up watching her father and her uncles set out before light with the cart and the two dogs, swinging wide of the treeline by a field's width even where the ground made the wide way harder, and she grew up too watching the charcoal that came into the household's own hearth arrive black and ordinary from a dozen kinds of tree and never once, in all the years she kept count without meaning to, from that one.

There was talk, now and again, among the younger of the households, of what a wood that size might spare a family in a hard winter if only one tree of it, one, were felled and split and burned like any other. It came up most often at the end of a bad season, when the wood-carriers had made their third or fourth double journey of the month and a man's back had good cause to grumble at the extra mile. Sadhbh heard her own uncle say it once, half in jest and half not, leaning on the cart in the last light of a short winter afternoon with the load still short of what the household wanted: that a tree was a tree, whatever ground it stood in, and a fire did not ask where its wood had grown before it burned warm. No one answered him that evening, not with argument and not with agreement, and by the next wood-carrying he was walking the long way round with the rest of them as though he had never said it, for it was the kind of talk that a household let pass without rebuke and without repeating, the grumble of a tired man rather than a settled wish, and no one in Sadhbh's lifetime, whatever was said at the end of a hard month, ever put an axe within the wood's own bounds to test what the trees or anything behind them might have to say to it.

It was not forbidden in the way a thing is forbidden when someone has said the word aloud and set a penalty behind it. No elder stood at the treeline turning axes back. It wanted no guard, her father told her once, hefting the cart's shaft onto his shoulder on a cold morning with his breath standing white before him, because a thing that wanted guarding was a thing a person might still be persuaded to try, and no one in that country, so far as living memory or the memory before it could reach, had ever tried. He said it without heat, the way a man says a thing he has never once had cause to doubt, and Sadhbh, walking at his side with a hand on the cart's rail to keep her feet in the frozen ruts, understood before she had words fit to hold the understanding that this was the whole of the custom. No law kept it. It was a thing simply not done, the way a person does not eat the fire that warms them.

She asked once, being a child and not yet grown past asking the plain question aloud, what would come of a man who cut there all the same. Her father was quiet long enough that she thought he had not heard her over the cart's creaking, and then he said only that no one had ever found out, and that he for his part had no wish to be the first, and turned the talk to the state of the ground ahead, which was as much of an answer as she got from him that day or any other. It was, she came to see as she grew, the whole hills' answer to the same question, offered in one shape or another by every elder ever asked it. None of them could point to a harm done. What held them back was a caution kept so long and so evenly across so many households that it had worn a path into the ground the way any path wears, needing no one's word to keep it and no one's memory of why it was first walked.

## II. Of the Stag at the Wood's Edge

Sadhbh was past twenty and already a hunter in her own right, known across the eastern households for a steadier hand at the gully-drives than most of the men who worked beside her, when the wood gave the country the telling that outlasted every other telling that came out of it.

It happened at the gully the hunters called the glen of the stags, a deer-drive run down along the wood's own southern shoulder where the ground narrowed between a fall of scree on one side and the first outlying trees on the other, close enough to the holy ground that a beast driven hard down it sometimes broke for the treeline rather than the open flats below, and no hunter had ever yet followed one in. Sadhbh had worked that drive a dozen years by then, knew the wind of it and the footing of it as well as she knew her own threshold, and had never in all those years so much as raised her bow within a spear's throw of the trees, out of the same unspoken caution every hunter in that country carried with them the way they carried their knives.

She was posted that morning at the narrowest turn of the gully, waiting on the drive that two of her cousins were bringing down from higher ground, when a stag broke from the treeline rather than from the open hill, which was a thing she had not seen in all her years at that post and did not, for the length of a held breath, know how to read. It was white: white the way new snow is white before any foot has crossed it, from the muzzle back along the whole run of its flank to a tail that flicked once, catching what little light came down through the trees at its back, its antlers standing up out of that white like something carved rather than grown.

It stood at the treeline a long moment, close enough that Sadhbh could see the working of its ribs with each breath and the dark, unhurried eye it turned on her, not the wild white-rimmed eye of a beast that has just broken from a drive in fright, but a look she found no better word for after, in all the tellings she gave of it in her own long life, than steady. Her bow was in her hand and half drawn before she had thought to draw it, the plain reflex of a hunter's body outrunning her own mind, and it was her mind that stopped her hand where it was, though she could not afterward say what thought had done the stopping, only that the drawing felt, of a sudden, like a thing her body had begun on its own account and her whole self refused to finish.

Two others saw it with her that same morning, her cousin Ruadhan coming down late off the high ground and a boy new to the drive whose name the hills did not trouble to keep, and neither of them drew either, though both had clean sight of it and time enough, by their own later telling, to have loosed and struck. The boy said afterward that his hands had simply gone slow on him, the way hands go slow in a dream where a person means to run and cannot make the legs answer; Ruadhan, older and less given to saying more than he meant, said only that he had looked at it and known, without working out how he knew it, that it was not a thing his arrow had any business touching. The stag held its ground a breath or two longer, long enough that all three of them stood frozen in the same unspoken accord, and then turned, unhurried as it had come, and went back into the trees, and none of them heard so much as a snapped twig to mark its going.

They told it that evening at the nearest hearth, plainly and without much dressing, the way any hunting tale was told in that country, and it went from that hearth to the next the way every tale went, gathering a little colour at each telling but losing nothing of its bones: a white stag at the wood's edge, and three hunters who could not, between them, say why none of the three had drawn. Some of the older women, hearing it, said it was no beast at all, something the wood had put out to be seen, and let the matter rest there; others said only that a beast so marked belonged to no drive that had ever been run in that country and ought, on that ground alone, to be let go by whoever met it. No one that first year put a name to it, god or spirit or anything between. Sadhbh, asked more than once as the years went on whose creature she took it to have been, always gave the same answer: that she had looked it in the eye and known only that it was not hers to kill, and had never wanted to know more than that.

In the years between the first seeing and the second, the tale did its own quiet work on the country's custom without any elder ever setting it down as a rule. Hunters who ran the gully after that first year went to the post at its narrowest turn already carrying the tale with them, so that a drive that broke for the treeline, whatever beast led it, was let go with less argument than it might once have needed, on the plain chance that among the ordinary deer some one unlucky shot might catch the wrong beast in a bad light and bring down on the whole country a grief no one could afterward undo. It cost the households something real, in meat foregone at the wood's edge over years enough to count, and none of them, asked whether the cost was worth the caution, answered the question as though it were a hard one.

It came back. Twenty-two years on, when Sadhbh was past forty and had given the gully post to younger hands, word came down from a fresh pair of hunters, neither of whom had been born the first time it was seen, that a white stag had stood at the same treeline and let itself be looked at a long while before it turned back into the trees, drawing no arrow and taking no harm from any that were there. It was, by every account the hills kept afterward, the same beast, or one enough like it that no one troubled to argue the difference, and from that second seeing on it left off being one hunter's tale carried at one hearth and became a thing the whole country held between them, spoken of at the gully-drives before any beast was run, so that a hunter posted where Sadhbh had once been posted went to that ground already knowing what she might meet and what she was, by the country's own unspoken law, to do about it, which was nothing at all.

## III. Of the Second Wood

Many days' walk to the west, past the southern hills and out into the middle country, there stood a second wood that few of the eastern households had ever seen, though the hearths about it knew it as well as the east knew its own, though every one of them could point the way to it without hesitating.

It was older in its bearing than the holy wood of the stag, or seemed so to every eye that fell on it, though no one living could have said by what mark they judged the age of a tree they had never gone close enough to touch. The ground about it rose in low, broken folds, and out of those folds the trees stood taller and closer-set than any wood a household had ever felled and let regrow, with a floor beneath them that no foot, so far as any telling reached, had ever walked from one side to the other. Three paths ran toward it, worn by generations of feet that had gone that way for water, for grazing, for the plain human want of walking to the edge of a thing and looking at it, and all three paths, without a single household ever having met to agree on it, stopped short of the trees at the same low line of set stones.

The stones themselves were nothing grand to look at, no taller at their highest than a man's knee, running in a broken course that dipped and rose with the ground rather than holding to any straight rule a mason might have set, so that a stranger coming on them without knowing their name might have taken them for no more than the kind of stone a field turns up on its own and a farmer piles at its margin out of the way of the plough. But no field lay behind them. Beyond the line the ground was not worn at all, not by foot nor by hoof nor by anything a person could point to and say a beast had passed this way, and every one of the three paths, worn smooth and hard-packed right up to the stones, went no further than the stones themselves, as though the feet that had made them, generation on generation, had walked to that exact mark and turned back without a word ever needing to pass between them as to why.

The hearths called that country the sìth's, though none of them, pressed on it, could say when the naming had begun or who had first said it aloud. It was the same shape of telling as the first wood's, near enough: no fear anyone could point a finger at and give a cause to, only a thing worn so evenly into the custom of the country that asking after its beginning got a shrug more often than an answer. The sìth, the hearths said, were the people of the hills and the mounds, who had held that ground before ever a household of the mason's own kind set a fence-post in that country, and who kept, by every account, to the far side of any line their own custom or their own leaving had drawn, so long as no one was foolish enough to draw it back the other way.

Children were told it early and told it plainly, the way children in that country were told which mushrooms killed and which fed, with no more ceremony than the telling wanted: go as far as the stones and no further, and if a ball or a stray lamb or a wandering hound crossed the line ahead of you, let it go, for whatever was lost that way stayed lost, and no household in living memory had gone in after anything and come out again the better for it. It was not, most families found, a hard rule to keep. A line of low stones asked little of a child beyond the asking, and the wood beyond it offered nothing a child's eye found it hard to leave alone: no berries grew close along that edge that did not grow just as freely on the near side, no game ran thick enough there to tempt a hunter's arm past caution, and the trees themselves, seen from the stones, gave back only the same dim, unbroken green that every other stand of old timber in that country gave back, holding out no promise a person might be fool enough to chase.

## IV. Of Muireall

Muireall was nine years old in the year the hills lost her, the youngest of four and the only girl among them, a quick, sharp-eyed child who had been told the rule of the stones as plainly and as often as every other child of that country and had, so far as anyone could afterward swear to, kept it as well as any of them until the one afternoon that undid the keeping.

It was late in the summer, the households out at the hay in the low fields a good way off from the second wood, and the children of three families left, as children of that country were often left on a fine working day, to their own charge on the near ground with an older cousin's half an eye on them. Muireall had gone with the others to the line of stones to watch for the deer that sometimes crossed the open ground beyond it in the evening light, a game the children of that country played often enough and thought little of, for the deer kept well clear of the trees themselves and a child standing at the stones to watch them was no closer to any harm than a child standing at its own threshold.

What drew her past the line, none of the other children who stood with her that afternoon could afterward say with any certainty, though every one of them was asked the question more times than any of them, by their own account, cared to be asked again. The eldest among them, a boy of twelve who carried the guilt of that afternoon a long way into his own grown life, said only that he had turned to call the younger ones back from the drop of a gully nearer the stones, and turned again a breath or two later to find Muireall gone from where she had stood, with no cry, no rustle in the bracken, nothing at all to mark which way she had gone save a small, easy gap in the undergrowth at the near edge of the trees, no wider than a child makes passing through without troubling to hold the branches back.

They called for her first among themselves, the way children call for a playmate they think has only wandered off to tease them, and it was some while, by the boy's own shamed account, before the calling turned to fright and the fright sent the eldest running for the hay-field at a pace that left him unable, when he reached it, to get out more than her name and the word gone before her mother was already past him and running the other way.

The search that followed held nothing back that the country had to give it. Every household within a half-day's walk came, men and women both, with torches cut and lit before the light was fully gone, for none of them, whatever they told each other of the sìth's own custom, could bring themselves that first evening to leave a nine-year-old child to a dark wood without at least the trying. They went up the three paths together, all of them, calling her name in a chorus that carried, by every account, a long way over the still evening air, and they reached the line of stones with the last of the daylight still holding in the west and stood there, the whole company of them, in a silence that none who were present that night ever fully found words to fill afterward.

No one crossed the line. It was not spoken of aloud, that first evening, as a thing decided; no elder stood forward to forbid it, and no household voice was raised in argument for going further. It happened the way the custom had always happened, without a word needing to pass between them: torch after torch came up to the stones and stopped there, the light of each one falling a little way into the trees beyond and showing nothing but more trees, and no foot, of all the dozens gathered on that ground that night, went past the line the child had crossed a few hours before. Muireall's mother stood at the stones longer than any of them, past the point where her own kin had begun, gently, to draw her back by the arm, and it was said afterward, by more than one who stood near her, that she asked the wood, once, in a voice pitched low enough that only those closest to her caught the words, to give the child back, and that no sound came from the trees in answer beyond the ordinary small movement of leaves that any wood gives back to any question put to it.

They searched again the next day, and the day after that, working the ground on the near side of the stones as thoroughly as any ground in that country had ever been worked, turning every fold and hollow within a stone's throw of the line on both flanks of the wood, in case the child had strayed along the edge of it rather than in, and found nothing: no print in ground soft enough to hold one, no scrap of the plain wool she had worn, no sound of her voice carried back on any wind that blew those two days from any quarter. On the third day the search narrowed to the stones, and stopped there. No household had tired of looking. There was, by then, nowhere left to look that did not mean crossing a line that no one in living memory, not even for a lost child of nine, had ever yet crossed and come back whole to speak of.

The hearths said afterward that the sìth had taken her, in the same plain, worn way the hearths said everything of that country's own custom, offering it neither as comfort nor as blame, only as the one telling that fit what the ground had shown them: a line long kept, a child who crossed it, and nothing on the near side of the stones, search it as thoroughly as they had, to say she had ever come back across. Muireall's father dug no grave, for there was no body to lay in one, and the household kept instead, in the years that followed, a small stone of their own setting near their own threshold, not at the wood, bare of any mark, so that whoever asked after it, whenever they asked it, would be told, in as many or as few words as the asking seemed to want, of a child who had gone as far as a line of set stones on a fine summer evening and had not, for all the searching the whole country gave her, ever been found on the near side of it again.

The rule of the stones, which had asked little of any child before that year and had been kept, in the main, without any household troubling to test how hard a rule it truly was, was kept a good deal more plainly after. No elder needed, in the years that followed, to remind a child twice of where the near ground ended, and no household ever again let its children stand watching for deer at that particular stretch of the line without a grown hand somewhere near enough to close on a small arm before it could slip free. The torches that had halted at the stones on the night Muireall was lost halted there still, in every telling the hills gave of that summer afterward, a whole country's worth of light standing at the edge of a wood it had searched to the very limit it would allow itself, and going no further.

Sadhbh lived to hear the telling of it many times over before her own death, an old woman by then and long past any drive of her own, sitting at hearths that were not her own household's and were asked, more often than she cared to count, to set the white stag she had once met at the gully's turn beside the child the second wood had taken, as though the two tellings answered each other. She never granted the two woods a common cause, not once in all the years she was asked it. The stag's wood had given nothing but a look and a going, she said, and had taken from the country only the meat it might otherwise have lost at a hunter's hand; the wood of the stones had taken a child outright and given nothing back at all, not so much as a mark in the ground to say which way she had gone. They were two uncut woods holding to two different silences, she told whoever asked her plainly enough that few asked her twice, and a country that meant to go on living beside either of them would do well to leave the working-out of why to those with more patience for it than a hunter had ever needed.

By the year the households about that wood counted, later, as the year the search finally stopped being spoken of at every gathering and became instead a thing simply known, the way the covered hearths of the southern hills had become a thing simply built, the line of stones had stood a full generation as the one mark on that country's ground that no household crossed and no household argued the crossing of. Children born in the years after Muireall's going grew up knowing the rule before they knew the reason for it, in the same worn, unquestioned way their grandparents had grown up knowing to carry their fuel the long way round the holy wood far to the east, two customs kept by two countries far apart for two griefs that had never, in all the years since, given either of them cause to test what lay beyond either line.
