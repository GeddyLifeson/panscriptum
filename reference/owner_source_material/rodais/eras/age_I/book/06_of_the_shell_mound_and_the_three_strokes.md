# Of the Shell Mound and the Three Strokes

*VE 751 – 820*

## I. Of the Heap at Dunn

On the eastern coast, where the land breaks into low cliffs and the tide goes out a long way over flat rock, there was a heap of shell already old before any of this is told. No one living could have said how it began, and no teller of the hills, then or after, ever went near enough to ask, for the hill-folk and the people of that heap did not go about among one another, and what passed between them, if anything passed, is not told here.

A woman came down to the shore each morning while the tide was out, and worked the rock pools and the wet sand with a stick of bone worn smooth at the grip, and filled a woven bag with limpets prised loose one at a time, each with its own small crack and give, and with the flatter shells that lay half buried at the tideline, and with whatever crab or urchin the falling water had left behind in a hollow. She had done this since she was old enough to carry a bag of her own, following her mother down the same path over the same rock, and her mother had done it before her, and no one troubled to ask further back than that.

What she gathered she carried up a short rise above the shore to where the heap stood, and there she opened the bag and let the shell fall where the heap wanted it, working it in with her foot so that it lay level and did not slide, the way she had been shown. Bones went into it too, seal bones mostly, cracked for the marrow and picked clean and then given to the heap along with everything else, and weed burned black on a fire kept at the heap's foot went into it in its ashes, so that the whole mass of the thing was shell and bone and burnt weed together, layer under layer, each layer settled by the weight of the one above it.

The woman's mother had told her, as her own mother had told her before that, where each kind of shell was to be set and where the bone, and why the burnt weed went in thin and not thick, and why the whole was worked with the foot and never simply thrown; and the woman kept it as she had been given it, without asking herself why it should be kept in that order rather than some other, for it had always been kept so, and a thing kept so long needs no further reason than that.

The work took most of a morning and something of the afternoon too, in every season but the deepest of winter, when the household drew in closer to the fire and lived a while off what the summer and the autumn had put by. In spring the pools ran thick with young limpets easily taken, and the gulls came down onto the rocks in numbers to work the same ground a little behind the gatherers, so that a woman crouched at her work through half a morning might straighten to find the whole shore behind her white with birds, waiting on whatever she left uncovered. In the drier months of summer the low cliffs above the shore carried nests of their own, and eggs went into the bag along with the shell when a ledge could be reached, though never so many from one ledge that the birds did not come back to it the next year; that much, at least, every gatherer on that coast seemed to know without having been told it in words, the way a hand knows not to close entirely round a coal.

The fire at the heap's foot never went fully out. Who had first set it there, or how, no one now living could say, any more than they could say who had first carried a shell up from the shore and let it fall in that one place rather than any other; it was simply a thing the heap had, the way the shore had tide and the sky had weather, and it was kept the way anything is kept that a household cannot do without, fed through the wet months with driftwood carried up from the strandline and through the dry months with whatever scrub grew close enough to spare. In the worst of the winter gales, when the wind came in off the water hard enough to lay a grown man flat, two or three would crouch about it through the black hours with their backs to the weather, feeding it a stick at a time, and in the morning it would still be there, low and red under its own ash, however far the gale had driven the rest of them to shelter.

One such gale, in the woman's own girlhood, tore the roof from the low shelter her household kept behind the heap and drove the sea itself up over the rocks where the gathering was done, so that for three days no one went down to the pools at all, and the household sat close about the fire at the heap's foot with what little was left from before the storm, listening to the wind take whatever it could still find to take. When the gale broke and the water drew back off the rocks it left the shore scoured clean and strange, familiar pools filled with sand and new pools opened where none had been, and the gathering had to be learned freshly over that stretch of coast as though the sea had asked a question and expected the answer found again from the beginning. The fire, banked low and fed through all three days by whoever could be spared to crouch over it, had not gone out; and though nothing was said of that in any words the telling has kept, it was noticed, and it was remembered, the way a thing is remembered that a household has come near to losing.

By the time the woman had a daughter old enough to carry her own bag down to the shore, the heap already stood higher than the woman's own waist along its seaward face, and higher again where the older shell had settled hardest, grey with age and crusted with a growth the newer shell did not yet have. It smelled, on a still day, of the sea gone stale, of weed and old bone and smoke, a smell that clung to the hands and the hair of anyone who worked at it and that none of them, having grown up inside it, ever noticed until they had been away from the shore a day or two and come back to it.

## II. Of the Height of a Man

The years went by the way years go by for people who keep no count of them: by the return of the birds that nested on the low cliffs each spring and left again each autumn, by the runs of fish that came in close to shore at one season and out again at another, by the slow climb and fall of the tide against marks on the rock that every child of that shore learned before any other lesson. The woman's mother grew old working the pools in the cold months long after her hands had stiffened past any real use for the work, unwilling to sit by the fire while there was shell still to be carried, until one winter she did not come up from the shore at the day's end, and was found sitting against a rock with the tide already turning round her feet, gone as quietly as she had lived.

They buried nothing of her at the heap. What was done with her the telling does not follow, for it went on below the rise, among the household's own places, out of sight of anything that would carry the doing of it further than it was meant to go; only that after that day the woman worked the pools alone for a season, and then with her own daughter beside her, and the heap went on growing the way it had grown before, a little higher each year, as though it had not noticed, and in a sense it had not.

For that one season the woman went down to the shore before first light and came up from it after dark, working alone the ground she had always worked beside another, and those who watched her said afterward that she never once let the fire at the heap's foot go lower than she had kept it when there were two pairs of hands to feed it. She did not speak of her mother at the work, then or later; grief in that household, whatever shape it took, took it below the rise and out of sight, and what showed at the heap was only the steadiness of a woman going on with a thing that wanted doing whether she grieved or not.

The daughter, grown near a woman's height herself by then, found once, digging her stick down into an old shoulder of the heap to loosen a stubborn crust of shell, a tool of her grandmother's time worked into the mass below: a scraper of flint, broad and short and struck from the stone in a way that showed, to anyone who set it beside a tool freshly made at the heap's fire that same season, that the hand which had made it had held the stone differently, and struck it at a different angle, and finished its edge by a different kind of working altogether. It was not a strange thing to find an old tool in the heap, for tools went in with everything else once they were worn past use, but this one the daughter kept out a while before she let it go back into the shell, turning it in her fingers and running her thumb along its edge, which was blunted now but had once, by the set of it, been sharp along a line no one now living struck a stone to match. She said nothing of it to her mother that she is remembered to have said, and put it back where she had found it before the light went, and the heap took it in again the way it took in everything given to it.

The heap stood, by the year that the woman herself began to feel her own hands stiffen in the cold months as her mother's had, taller than any of them, higher at its crown than a grown man could reach with his arm stretched over his head, a long low mass along the rise above the shore that could be seen, on a clear day, from boats working the water some way off, a pale ridge against the darker ground behind it. No one had set out to build it to any height. It had simply grown, morning by morning, bag by bag, foot-fall by foot-fall, for as long as anyone could remember and a good while longer than that, until it had become, without any one person meaning it, the tallest thing that shore had.

The day it was first remarked on for its height, rather than simply worked at as it had always been worked at, was an ordinary one: a boy of the household, sent up from the shallows to fetch fire down for a smoking of fish, stood at the heap's foot and looked up its face the way he had never troubled to before, and said, to no one in particular, that a man standing at the top of it could see further out over the water than a man standing anywhere else on that stretch of coast. It was true, and no one had thought to say it before him, and after that it was said again from time to time, when a boat was overdue or a weather sign wanted watching for, that the top of the heap was the place to stand for it; and so the heap that had grown for no purpose beyond having somewhere for the shell to go came, without anyone setting out to make it so, to be used as a place from which to watch the sea.

On other shores of that same coast, in those same years, other households kept other heaps in the same slow way. None of them is this telling's business, save to say that the heap at the eastern shore was neither the largest of its kind on that coast nor the smallest, and that whatever moved the hands that heaped the others moved the hands at this one too, without any of them, so far as anyone can now say, ever comparing their work with any other household's along the whole long line of that shore.

## III. Of the Net-Maker of Cwen

Some way along the coast, where the land turned in on itself and made a wide bay sheltered from the worst of the open sea, another household kept a different trade, and a woman of it who is remembered only as the net-maker worked stone weights for her lines the way her own mother had worked them before her.

She took beach stones for it, rounded ones about the size of a fist, and pecked a groove about the middle of each with a harder stone struck again and again in the same place until the groove was deep enough to hold a cord without slipping, a slow careful work that ruined more stones than it finished, for a stone that split wrong at the wrong moment was no use to her and went back onto the beach with the others. When a weight was finished and true, she did one thing more to it before she judged it done: she cut three short strokes into the stone's face, close together, always in the same place and always in the same order, the first stroke a little longer than the two beside it, the whole no bigger than the width of two fingers laid flat.

No one asked her why she did it, and the telling carries no answer, for there is none to carry. Every stone she finished carried the same three strokes, no more and no fewer, whatever its size or its place on the line, always the same close order whether it was the first weight cut that season or the fiftieth. It was simply a thing she did to a stone once the stone was ready to be a weight and not before, the way a woman elsewhere might finish a pot with a rim pressed by her own thumb, or a scraper with one last stroke along its edge past the point where the edge itself needed it. Whether it was hers alone, or a mark that had come to her from someone before her the way the notching of the groove itself had come to her, no one now can say; only that every weight found afterward along that stretch of shore, however old, carried the same three strokes cut the same way, so that a stone worked a lifetime before her own could be told from a stone worked by any other hand only by the eye that had learned the small differences a lifetime leaves in even the same three strokes cut again and again.

The net itself was her mother's work as much as the weights, plant fibre twisted and knotted by hand into a mesh wide enough to let the smallest fish through and hold the rest, a labour of whole winters spent close by the fire with the cord running through fingers grown hard and quick at it, knot after knot after knot until the mesh lay in coils as tall as a child. For years the nets that household set were short ones, reaching only a little way out from the shallows on either side of the bay's mouth, enough to take a fair catch on a good tide but leaving the deeper water of the channel between them open, and through that gap, on the years the run was heaviest, more fish went than ever came against the line.

In the year the net-maker judged her mesh long enough at last to close the whole distance, she set it across the bay from shallow to shallow in one unbroken run for the first time in that household's memory, staking it through a low tide with every hand that could be spared, the net-maker herself wading the deepest stretch of the channel with the heaviest weights slung at her waist to sink the centre of the line true. The tide came in behind them as they worked, and they finished the last stake with the water already at their thighs, and stood back on the shingle in the dusk to see the whole long line of floats and weights holding steady from one side of the bay's mouth to the other, a barrier where there had never before been more than two arms reaching toward each other and failing to meet.

What came against that net in the days that followed was more fish than the household's smoking-racks could hold, and word of the catch went along the shore the way such word went, so that households some way off came to see the thing for themselves and went home again to lengthen their own nets by whatever means each knew, though none of them, so far as anyone remembers, ever matched the closed line at the net-maker's own bay in that same year. She set her nets across the mouth of the bay in every season after that the fish ran thick, staking the ends where the water shallowed and letting the weighted line hang between, low enough to sit just under the surface where the fish did not see it until they were already against it, work that wanted more than one pair of hands and drew the whole household down to the shallows at both ends of the day, wading out to their knees in cold water to set stakes and gather in whatever the night had brought against the line.

## IV. Of the Three Strokes

The net-maker had a daughter who came to the work early, as children of that shore came early to any work that wanted small quick hands, and who sat beside her mother through whole afternoons watching the groove deepen in stone after stone, the same patient strokes falling in the same place until the stone gave way and took the shape wanted of it. She learned to judge, before she was trusted to peck a groove herself, which stones would split true under the working and which would shatter wrong however carefully they were struck, a judgement her mother never troubled to put into words and that the girl came to only by watching enough stones ruined and enough stones finished to feel the difference in her own hands before she could have said what the difference was.

It was longer still before her mother let her cut the three strokes, for a weight badly grooved could be reworked or set aside with little lost, but the strokes were cut only once, on a weight already finished, and a bad cut there spoiled a stone that had cost a morning's careful work to bring that far. The girl watched her mother's hand do it a great many times before she was given a stone of her own and the smaller flint used for the cutting, and was shown, without being told in any words the telling has kept, where the first stroke began and how long it ran and at what small angle the two beside it were meant to fall, close enough together that a glance took in all three at once and none as its own mark apart from the others.

Her first attempts went wrong the way a child's first attempts at any close work go wrong, the strokes too long or too short or crossing where they should have stood apart, and each spoiled stone went back to the pile of stones not yet fit to be weights, without anger and without much comment, for every hand at that work had spoiled stones before it made good ones and the girl's mother had spoiled her own share in her time. The girl kept at it through a good part of a year, cutting the three strokes on stone after stone in whatever quiet hour her mother judged she could be spared from the heavier work of the nets, and each time she looked harder than the time before at the depth and the angle and the closeness of the cuts, holding the flint the way she had watched it held rather than the way that first came easiest to her own hand.

On a morning in the calm weather that closed one summer of that stretch of years, she cut three strokes into a finished weight and held it up to the light the way she had seen her mother hold a hundred stones up before her, turning it so the low sun caught the grooves and threw a small shadow along each one, and found nothing in it that her eye, trained now to the fault, could fault. Her mother took it from her without a word and turned it the same way in the same light, and gave it back, and said nothing more than what her hand did next, which was to take up another stone from the unworked pile and set it down before the girl for the next one.

From that stone onward she kept a way of turning a finished weight over in her hand before she set it down that her mother had never quite had, holding it a breath longer in the light than the work itself required, as though the three strokes, once she had learned to cut them true, had become a thing worth looking at for its own sake and not only for the weight's sake.

Her mother grew old at the work in the years after, her eyes going first, so that she gave over the finer judging of a stone's fitness to the daughter's sharper sight some while before her hands themselves failed her, and the daughter took up the whole of the weight-cutting in her own right well before she had any daughter of her own to sit beside her and watch the groove deepen in stone after stone. She kept the strokes as she had been given them, the first a little longer than the two beside it, the same close order every time, on every stone she ever finished from that year to the end of her own working life; and when in time she came to teach the cutting to a child of her own, she taught it exactly as it had been taught to her, without adding to it and without troubling to explain what, if anything, it stood for, for there was nothing more to give than the shape itself and the knowing of it complete.

The weight the girl had cut went onto the net with all the others that season, indistinguishable among them to any eye but her own and her mother's, one more small stone among a great many doing the same plain work at the edge of a bay that had held a household at that work for longer than either of them could reckon; and if it were possible to lay every weight ever cut on that shore out in a single row, the three strokes on each would run the same close order down the whole long line of them, stone after stone, hand after hand, the one mark on all that coast that any of them ever troubled to make the same way twice, for no other reason that the telling keeps than that it had always been made so, and that having a shape to give a finished thing was itself, it seems, a kind of answer to something none of them ever asked aloud.
