# Of the Covered Hearths and the Nine

*VE 3,304 – 3,407*

## I. Of Laoghaire and the Hearth Under the Slab

Forty-two years had gone by since the herder-girl who found the defaced lines on the rock faces of the Doire uaine hills had died an old woman with her own hearth still open to the sky, unwilling in the end to live any other way, though she had lived the last of her years knowing what the hills now knew and could not unknow: that the lines running for yards across the stone, cut over and cut over again by the same hand or the same kind of tool, told of something that walked without breathing and fell upon whoever lit a fire where it could see. On the eastern side of that country the first household to dig its own fire into the ground and roof it with a slab had by then been dead two generations, and most of the hearths that side of the hills had long since followed it below the turf. But the custom had come slower, and less certainly, to the quieter western valleys, where the ground ran higher and stonier and a household's neighbours were a morning's walk apart rather than a shout across a field; and it was there, in a valley that still kept as many open hearths as covered ones, that the argument the eastern side had already half settled went on being argued still. Two of the earliest pits dug on that side had shown, on windless nights, a thread of glow at the smoke-vent that had frightened the households that dug them nearly as badly as an open fire would have, and it had left a fair part of that country unsure whether the ground itself had made them any safer or only given them a new way to be careless.

Whether a fire kept low was fire enough hidden, whether the walker minded smoke or only flame, whether a people that had carried its light from hearth to hearth since before any of them could count backward should now go creeping about in the dark of its own valleys like thieves in another man's house: all of it was argued over again, that western valley's own way, at every gathering.

Laoghaire was no thinker on the matter, by his own telling of himself and by everyone else's telling of him after. He was a digger of ditches for the water off the high pastures, a big slow-spoken man with a spade-shaped scar across one forearm from a fall on wet rock as a boy, and what he brought to the question that had worn a generation thin with talking was a habit of hands rather than any answer. He had spent half his life putting things into the ground where the weather could not get at them: seed corn against the wet, the hides of slaughtered beasts against the crows, the small store of hazelnuts his household kept for the lean months, sunk in a clay-lined pit and lidded with a slab no fox or badger had ever worked loose. It was his wife, by the account the hills kept longest of the two of them, who said it to him first, more in weariness than in hope, on an evening when the argument had gone round the fire circle for the hundredth time and got nowhere: that the eastern hearths had already shown a fire could be put into the ground, and that if theirs still glowed at the vent on a still night, then the fault lay in the digging and not in the notion, and a man who could sink a pit for hazelnuts that no fox nor badger had ever worked open ought to be able to sink one for a fire that no eye on a ridge could ever find.

He did not answer her that night. He sat a long while with the fire between them going down to embers, turning the notion over the way he turned a stone over before he set a spade to it, looking for the grain of it. "A pit for hazelnuts keeps out crows," he said at last. "It does not keep out cold, or breathe, or want a way down a man can walk without breaking his neck." His wife said only that she had not told him it would be easy, and went to bed, and left him sitting by the fire a good while longer, watching the low light of it climb the smoke-blackened roof-poles and thinking, by his own account to his sons, that a fire seen from above at that hour would carry a very long way over open ground.

He went out at first light instead, alone, to a shoulder of ground below his own house where the soil ran deep enough for a man to dig his own height without striking rock, and he began.

It was harder work than any pit he had cut for corn or hide, for a fire could not simply be dropped in and lidded; it wanted air to breathe by, and a way down to it that would not throw light where light could be seen. He dug a hole a man's height in depth and near two men's lengths across at its widest, and cut into one wall of it a slanting shaft no wider than his own shoulders, angled so that a man going down it went down blind, with the last stretch bent enough that no glow from below could climb it straight to the surface. At the bottom he laid a floor of flat stones and built his hearth upon them as any hearth was built, wood and peat and a coal carried down in a clay pot from the household fire above, and over the whole pit, resting on a low kerb of stone he had set into the sides for the purpose, he laid a great flat slab dragged down from the scree on a hide sledge by himself and his two elder sons, with a gap left at one corner no wider than a man's forearm, for the smoke to find its own slow way up through a crack in the turf a good ten paces off, where it thinned in the heather and told nothing to anyone looking down from above.

The digging took him the better part of three days, working alone through the first and calling his sons to the slab-dragging on the third, and there was a moment on the second evening, kneeling at the bottom of his own half-finished pit with the light gone from the sky above him and nothing but a hand's width of grey dusk showing at the top of the shaft, when he owned afterward to a fright that had nothing to do with any beast that walked the ridges: the plain, ordinary fright of a man who has dug himself into the ground and cannot, for the length of a held breath, remember which way the shaft bends. He came up that evening faster than he had meant to, and said nothing of it to his wife, and went down again the next morning as though the moment had not happened, for a man who dug ditches for a living did not let one bad breath in a hole turn him from a job half done.

He slept beside it the first night it was whole, in the pit, on the excuse of tending it, though everyone who knew him understood well enough that he wanted to be the one below if the thing he had built proved no better than the two glowing pits on the eastern side that had failed to settle anyone's mind before him. On his back on the packed earth with the fire's small light moving on the underside of the slab above him, close enough to touch if he reached, and listened, he said, for a long time to nothing at all: no wind, for the pit took the wind clean away, no voice, no step on the turf overhead that the earth might have carried down to him, only his own fire's small talk and his own blood in his ears. Nothing came. He came up at first light with soot on his hands and grass seed in his hair from the crawl up the blind shaft, and told his wife only that it had held its heat through the dark better than any open hearth he had ever slept beside, which was true and was also, everyone agreed afterward, not the whole of what he had gone down to learn.

It was the whole of what he could tell them, and it proved, in the end, to be enough. Word of the blind bend and the offset vent went from his own hearth to the next and from that to the next again, the way any useful thing went in those hills, carried by whoever had seen it and told plainly, without ornament, to whoever asked; and within the season the two families whose own pits had glowed at the vent came and stood at the lip of his, and went home, and dug theirs afresh on his pattern. Within two years there was a scoffer's name for anyone west of the ridge still keeping an open fire on the old pattern, and within five it was the open hearth that wanted explaining and the covered one that needed none. Laoghaire himself dug three more in the years that followed, one for a widowed sister and two for households that begged the digging of him outright rather than trust their own hands to the angle of the shaft, for he alone among them seemed to hold in his head, without ever setting it down as a rule anyone could repeat back, exactly how far the blind bend had to run before no glow climbed it, and how wide the vent had to be to draw the smoke away thin and slow rather than in a column any eye above could follow to its root.

He was six-and-sixty, and had outlived two of his own children and buried them under no marker louder than a plain heaped stone, when he died in his own house on a night no different from any other, his wife said afterward, save that he had gone to check the pit himself before bed as he had gone every night of his life since the first, out of a habit by then worn as smooth and as unremarked as breathing. He was buried, at his own long-standing wish, not beside the pit he had dug first, which his sons kept in use for another three generations after him, but on the shoulder of ground just above it, where he had stood the morning he began to dig and looked down, his wife said he had told her once, at nothing more than a patch of bare soil that had not yet become anything.

## II. Of the Hearth That Would Not Be Covered

At Tobar dhìreach, a long day's walk to the north, the fire that had come down from the mason's own well had never once, in all the generations since Diarmad first carried the Telling home from the grove, been put under a slab or a skin or anything else that stood between it and the open sky. It burned where it had always burned, in the low stone ring at the centre of the line's own house, with the clay pot that held what the line called the mason's first fire set always within an arm's reach of it, and the leather bag of the six dark coals and the one that would not darken hung on its peg above, and no household of the Mason's line, in all the years the covered hearth spread through the hills to the south, so much as dug the first spadeful of earth to make one of its own.

It was not that the word had failed to reach them. Travellers carried it, as travellers carried everything, and kin by marriage from the Doire uaine households brought it plainer still, standing in the doorway at Tobar dhìreach with soot still under their nails from a pit dug that same season and asking, some of them gently and some of them not, why a people who kept the mason's own fire, of all the fires on the island, should be the one household too proud or too stubborn to learn from its neighbours.

The man who kept the pot in those years — his name is not given here, for it is a rule older than any single teller that the fathers of the line between Brian and the one the hills came in time to call Cormac of the Misstrike are kept by the recital and given to no stranger who asks, and a telling carried beyond the hearth is a stranger before it is anything else — heard the question put to him more than once at his own hearth, and gave, by every account that has come down of it, the same answer each time, in words that varied a little from telling to telling but held to the one shape.

The fire in this house, he said, was not kept for warmth alone, nor even chiefly for warmth, though it warmed them as any fire warmed any house. It was kept because the mason's own hands had carried the first of it out of the ground on a flat stone under his cloak, and because every fire on the island, the pit-fires of the Doire uaine hills not excepted, traced back through some chain of borrowed embers to this one ring of stones and no other. A fire that had never once, in all those generations, been let go dark could not now be hidden without becoming, in the hiding, a fire like any other fire that a household banked low against its own fears; and the line held, rightly or not, that the mason's fire was owed better than to be treated as though it were no different from a peat-stack fire on a hillside that had reason to be afraid.

There were those at Tobar dhìreach, in those same years, who thought this reasoning proud rather than wise, and said so, and one branch of the kin did in the end dig a covered hearth of its own, apart from the line's own house, keeping the open fire only for the recital nights and banking the household's daily fire below ground the rest of the year, a compromise that satisfied neither the boldest nor the most fearful among them and was, by most tellings, quietly let drop again within two generations. But the line's own hearth, the one ring of stones the pot and the bag were kept beside, stayed open through every year of the trouble that follows in this telling, and stays open, so far as any teller of the hills can say, to this day.

## III. Of the Nine

There was a hill camp in those years in the south of the massif, a scatter of huts where three or four households wintered their beasts close together against the worst of the weather, no larger and no smaller than a dozen such camps the hills kept, and no more remarked. It kept its fires the old open way still, being far enough from Laoghaire's own valley and young enough in the custom that the pit under the slab had not yet been dug there, and no ill had come of it in all the winters the camp had stood, so that its households had come, as households will, to hold their own distance from the trouble a lucky thing rather than a borrowed grace.

The shepherd who found it, from a neighbouring valley and kin by marriage to one of the camp's three households, had gone up on an errand no more solemn than the borrowing of a ram, and noticed the lack of smoke only as a man notices any small wrongness, a thing his eye caught before his mind had troubled to ask why. He stood a while at the foot of the last rise looking up at a sky with nothing rising into it from any of the huts, on a morning cold enough that his own breath hung white before his face, and told himself, walking up, three or four reasons a camp might let its fires go low on a still night that had nothing to do with anything worse. None of the reasons held by the time he reached the gate. He found it standing open to a silence he said afterward he had felt on his skin before his eyes had told him anything was wrong.

Nine people lay inside it, about a hearth burned down to fresh grey ash and no further, none of them fallen as a man falls in a struggle, none marked by wound or blood or any hurt a living eye could find on skin or bone, lying instead as though the sleep that had taken them at the fire's side had simply not let them up again. The shepherd did not go in past the doorway. He went for the nearest kin the camp had, and they did not go in either, not that day nor any day after, and the hut was left standing with its dead inside it and its door swinging, until in time the wattle rotted and the roof went down over them and the grass came up through the collapsed thatch and no one from that day to this has ever gone in to bury what the roof by then had already covered.

What had been burning in that hearth, on the night whatever came for the nine had come, no one who found them could afterward say with certainty, though the ash was fresh enough, the shepherd swore, that the fire could not have been out above a day when he climbed to the camp and found the smoke gone. Whether it had been a fire kept open on the old pattern by householders too newly come to that ground to have learned the custom of their neighbours, or a covered hearth whose smoke-vent had for some reason shown more than it should have on a still night, the hills never settled, and the tellers who carried the account onward after gave it, in the end, no answer at all, only the one plain fact that stood at the centre of every version: nine people, a fire gone cold in an ordinary way, and not a mark on any of them to say what had stopped their breath.

The camp was never resettled. Other households, in the years that followed, drove their beasts wide of that shoulder of hill at the season the trouble had come, and in time the ground took on the kind of name that needed no telling to explain it, spoken of by herders passing near it, when it was spoken of at all, in the same low voice a man on the north-western shore might once have used for a stretch of water he had cause to keep clear of, though on the hills that voice had learned to speak of ground rather than water, for it was ground, and not the sea, that the hills of Doire uaine had come to fear.

## IV. Of the Hut That Burned Empty

Some thirty years after the camp was left, a family new to that country came up from the eastern farms to take grazing on a slope the hills had by then gone a long generation without settling, drawn by grass that had grown thick and unclaimed in the years since the neighbouring ground was given up. They were kin of a line that had never lived among the Doire uaine hills and had heard the custom of the covered hearth only as a thing travellers spoke of, half believed and half thought a hill people's fancy, the way any custom sounds when it is heard secondhand from a people one does not yet know well enough to trust.

They built their first house that summer on the old pattern, with the hearth open to the roof-hole as every hearth of their own kin had always been built, and it drew comment from the nearest neighbours before the roof-thatch was even finished, comment given, by every account, kindly enough and more than once, in the plain hope of sparing strangers a grief the hills themselves had already paid for. One old woman of the next valley over, who had herself dug her own pit-hearth as a young wife in the years the custom first spread, walked up twice that summer to say her piece plainly at their door: that she had lost no kin herself to the walker, and knew no one now living who had seen it, and would not for that reason claim to know its mind; only that a people did not go on digging into their own ground for a hundred years over nothing, and that a stranger new to a country did well to take an old fear from its own people at their word before learning, the hard way, what the word had been worth. The family thanked her for it, both times, and kept their fire open through that first autumn all the same, being, as newcomers to any custom often are, readier to hear a warning than to act on one they had not yet felt the weight of themselves.

It was in the dark of that same winter, on a night none of the near households could afterward fix more exactly than "hard frost, and still," that a boy sent at dawn to borrow fire, his own household's having gone out overnight in weather too cold for even banked coals to hold, came down again from the new family's door faster than he had gone up it and could not, for some while, get out more than that the door stood open and no one had answered his call.

Those who went up with him found the hut as he had found it: the door open to a morning white with frost, the beds slept in and thrown back as a bed is thrown back by someone rising in haste or in sleep, and every soul of that household gone from the place without a trace of where or how, no blood, no sign of a struggle, no track in a fresh fall of snow that had come in the night to say which way, if any way, they had gone. And on the hearth, banked the night before by a hand that plainly meant it to hold till morning, the fire burned on into the empty house exactly as it had been left, low and steady and untended, warming a room that held, by the time the neighbours reached it, no one left alive to feel it.

## V. Of the Walker on the Ridge

Twenty years more went by, the ash on the abandoned camp's cold hearth long since blown into the heather and the empty house on the eastern slope fallen in the way any house falls in once no one is left to mend its roof, before two herders of that country, a man and his sister-in-law out after strayed ewes on the ridge above the old camp in the last light of an autumn day, saw what Eimhir's own generation had once seen and lived to tell, and what few enough had seen since to keep the telling fresh: a shape moving along the skyline, at a distance neither of them could afterward agree on more closely than "far enough that we should have felt safe, and did not," that walked as a man walks and was not a man.

It went, they both said, at an even pace neither hurried nor slow, following the line of the ridge the way a shepherd follows a track he knows without needing to look down at his own feet, and it gave off, in the failing light, no breath they could see rise from it into the cold air, though the evening was cold enough by then that every sheep on that hillside stood wreathed in its own small cloud of it. Neither of them called out. Neither of them, by their own account given many times after to kin who asked and to kin who did not, so much as spoke to the other until the shape had passed beyond the shoulder of the ridge and out of sight, and it was only then, with it gone, that the man found he had been holding his sister-in-law's arm the whole time without either of them noticing when his hand had closed on it.

They had, that evening and every evening before it on that slope, no fire lit anywhere near them, their own hearth banked and covered a good half-hour's walk below, and it was this, more than anything either of them could put into clearer words, that both came to believe afterward had let them come home at all: that whatever walked the ridge that evening had passed by two people with no flame anywhere about them for it to find, the way it had passed, in every telling the hills by then kept of it, by every hearth that gave it nothing to see.

It was the last such sighting the hills kept any close account of, though not, in the tellers' own admission, because the walker was never seen again, only because by then there was little left for a sighting to prove that the hills had not already settled among themselves beyond arguing. The pit with the blind bend and the offset vent, the pattern Laoghaire had worked out on the shoulder of his own ground close on a hundred years before, was by the year the two herders came down off that ridge with their flock and their unspoken fright no longer spoken of household by household as it spread, east side or west, nor a scoffer's mark against whoever still kept the old pattern, but simply the way a hearth was built in that country, so plainly and so completely that a child born in those hills in that year would grow to be an old woman herself without ever once, in the whole span of her life, seeing an open fire lit under any roof but the one at Tobar dhìreach, where the mason's own flame went on burning, as it always had, in the one house the hills had long since stopped expecting to change.
