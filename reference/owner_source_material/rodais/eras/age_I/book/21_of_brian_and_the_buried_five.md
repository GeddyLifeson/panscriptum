# Of Brian and the Buried Five

*VE 2,861 – 2,936*

## I. Of the Man Who Dug the Graves

Seven hundred years had gone by since Fearghas let the white-flecked coal fall from the height of his arm and saw it catch fire halfway down the rock and lie burning on the stones like a star come down before its time. Since then the summit had taken the kindred's name and kept it, and herders left a stone on the top when they passed, and at Tobar dhìreach the recital was said every year on the night of the lightning with two colours answered and five still dark in the bag: the black, the red-brown, the silver, the one that woke at a snap of the fingers, and the rainbow that held them all and answered to none. Children of the line grew old hearing it and died leaving it no further on than they had found it. It had come to be said, among households that were not of the line and had no cause to be kind about it, that the Mason's people would go on asking the same question until the island wore down to the sea.

Brian was born into that waiting and paid it little mind. A plain man, thought plain by those who knew him, he wore the word without complaint. He dug the graves of the valley. It was not an office anyone had given him; his father had done it before him, a little, alongside his proper work of herding, and Brian had found in himself a steadiness for it that others lacked, and by the time he was two-and-twenty it was understood that when a death came to any of the hearths above Tobar dhìreach, someone went for Brian.

He was a big-shouldered man, slow to speak and slower to laugh, with hands scarred across the backs from stone he had turned in a hundred diggings. He kept his own counsel about the dead, the way another man kept his counsel about a debt. When a child of the black-well household died of a fever in his twentieth year he dug through frozen ground for the better part of two days without once cursing it, and when he had finished he sat by the raw earth a while before he would take the food that was brought to him, though no one asked him to and he never said why. Those who worked beside him said only that he was good at it, and better than good, and let it go at that, for it is not an easy thing to praise a man for burying your dead well, and the valley had not found the words.

He married Caoimhe in his one-and-twentieth year, before the whole valley had settled on calling him for its dead. She was of a herding household two valleys east, a quick, sharp-tongued woman who could set a whole hearth laughing with a single word and who had, more than one of her kin said, married beneath her wit if not beneath her worth. She used to say of Brian, to those who would listen, that he had courted her the way he dug a grave: without hurry, without noise, and with his eyes down at the ground the whole time, so that it was three seasons before she was sure he had meant it. It was fondly said, and truly.

They had no children who lived past their first winter, three of them, one after another, and this the valley also understood without being told, in the way a valley understands most things about its own people, and let alone. Caoimhe kept a hearth that was warm to visitors and cold to pity. Brian dug on.

It was work that gave a man time to think, more than most, for the labour of it did not fill the mind the way herding or splitting stone filled it, being slow and repeated and requiring no more of him than his back and his hands, hour on hour, in earth that did not care how carefully he opened it. He had grown up, like every child of the high valleys, on the telling of the mason and the lightning and the Keeper in the grove, said at his own hearth and at every hearth he visited, for it belonged to no one household by then; it was the valley's own tale, told to strangers before the strangers had finished sitting down. He had heard the riddle a thousand times before he understood it was a riddle and not merely a saying:

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

Two of the seven had answered already, long before any grandfather now living could remember, and further back than that: the sea had taken the gold coal on the swell of the northern shore, and the sky had taken the white-flecked coal in the empty air above the height that bore the kindred's name now. Of the sea and the sky Brian knew only what every hill-child knew, which was little, for he had never gone so far as either; he had walked as far as the market at Àth gharbh once in his life and no further. What he knew, and knew better than most men in the valley knew anything, was the earth. He had put his hands into it more times than he could count, and taken more out of it than he had put in, if a body counted for anything; the tellers said it did not, and Brian left such reckoning to them.

## II. Of What a Plain Man Thought

The line still kept the five dark coals in the leather bag that had held all seven once, and still tried them, though less often than it had, for the trying had worn down a great many hopes across the generations and left less each time to spend. They had been thrown into rain and left in it a season; they had been carried down into the wet air above the black well and the young well and held there through a whole spring; they had been laid in one fire all together, on the reasoning that if each answered its own element perhaps all together they might answer each other, and burned dark as any stone burns, giving off nothing but the ordinary red glow of heat and none of the promised fire. Diarmad's own grandchildren had tried carrying them in skins of sea-water home from the northern shore and pouring the water over them at the hearth, which struck most of the valley, when it heard of it, as a fair example of how far a household could go on an idea that had already failed once.

Brian had never gone near the bag. It belonged to the eldest women of the line, kept as it had been kept since Fionnghuala's day, and a man who dug graves for his bread had no more claim on it than the herder two valleys off. He heard the trying discussed, as everyone in the valley heard it discussed, at every gathering and every recital, the older folk arguing over which shore, which height, which season, as though the failure of six hundred years could be mended by trying the seventh height instead of the sixth. He listened the way he listened to most talk, without much to say, turning it over slowly the way he turned a spade in unfamiliar ground, feeling for where it gave.

What struck him, when it struck him, was not clever. He said so himself, afterward, more than once, to those who wanted to make more of it than there was. He had been standing at the edge of a fresh-dug grave, waiting on the kin to bring the body down from the hearth, on a grey morning in his seven-and-twentieth year, and he had looked down into the black earth he had opened and thought, the way a man thinks of an ordinary thing without meaning to think of anything at all: *this is where the dead go, and the dead lie still, and the earth takes them and does not give them back.* And on the heel of that thought, without his calling for it, came another: *the sea took its coal by being waded into. The sky took its coal by the coal being thrown into the sky's own air, from as high as a man could climb. If the earth is to take a coal, the coal must go where the earth is deepest, and be left to it, the way a body is left to it.*

He was a slow man with an idea, the way he was slow with everything. He finished the grave he was digging, and saw the dead put into it, and stood at the edge with the rest of the valley while the words were said over it, the same words said over every grave in the high valleys, asking the ground to hold what was given it gently. Three days went by before he said anything to Caoimhe, and a fortnight more before he said anything to the eldest of the women who kept the bag, an old widow named Sireadh who had held it longer than Brian had been alive.

"You want to bury them," she said, when he had got it out, plainly and without much art, the way he said everything.

"I want to try it," Brian said.

"It has been tried," said Sireadh. "In the rain. In the wet air by the wells."

"Not this," Brian said. "Deep, and left there. The whole night through."

The old woman looked at him a long while, the way she might have looked at a piece of ground she was deciding whether to trust, and at last she said what many of the line had said before her, across the generations, to one idea or another: that it could do no harm, since the coals were dark already and had been dark as long as anyone living had known them, and a man who spent his days putting things into the ground might, for once, know something the rest of them did not.

## III. Of the Grave Beside the Slab

She would not let him take the whole bag, nor all seven, for the two that had answered were not hers to risk on a guess, however good the guesser; but she gave him the five that were left, wrapped in a fold of undyed wool, and told him to bring them back whole, whatever came of it.

He chose the ground beside the slab. It was the plainest choice in the world to him and he never understood, afterward, why anyone should think it strange; the slab at Tobar dhìreach was the one place on the island where the earth was known, past doubting, to have held fire once, for the vein still lay under it, covered these seven hundred years and more, and if the earth had a spirit at all it seemed to Brian no more than sense that the spirit should be nearest where the earth had already shown it could hold such a thing. No one drew water beside the slab, and no house was built near it, and the ground there was given over to grass and to the recital that was said standing on it once a year; it was as close to consecrated as any ground in the valley came, though that word belonged to no one yet.

He dug on an evening in the middle of an t-Sultain, when the barley was in and the light was going early and there was a smell of turned earth and woodsmoke over the whole valley. He dug it the depth of a grave, no more and no less, for it was the only depth he trusted himself to judge by feel, and he lined it, as he lined the graves he was known for, so that the sides would not fall in on what was laid there before morning. Caoimhe came out to watch him at it, wrapped against the evening cold. Neither of them held digging to be woman's work, and she offered him no hand with it, but she stayed the whole while, sitting on an old quern-stone near the slab with her knees drawn up, watching her husband dig what half the valley by then, having heard the word of it, was calling the sixth grave he had dug for the line, though nothing of the line's was dead.

"They will say you have buried it," she told him, once, into the quiet. "The hope of it. However this comes out."

"Let them," Brian said, and went on digging.

Others came, in ones and twos, as the light failed, drawn by the word the way word always drew a valley to anything out of the ordinary: an old man of the north hearths who had tried the coals in his own youth and failed with them and had opinions still; two of Sireadh's grandchildren, sent to watch and report back what was done, since the old woman's knees no longer let her stand at a graveside past dusk; a boy who had no business there at all and was sent home twice and came back a third time and was let stay, since by then Brian had stopped noticing who watched him and who did not. The talk among them was low, and one voice did not agree with the next. Some held that burying the coals was no different, in its bones, from throwing them in the sea or the rain, one more trying among many, no likelier to answer than the rest. One or two, more sourly, said that a grave-digger ought to know better than most what the earth did with things put into it, which was nothing, which was keep them, unchanged, or eat them slowly down to nothing at all, and that neither keeping nor eating was the same as answering.

Brian heard all of it and answered none of it. When the hole was as deep as he wanted, he climbed down into it himself, though it came near to his shoulders, and Caoimhe passed the five coals down to him one at a time, unwrapped now from the wool, dark and ordinary-looking in the last of the light, five pieces of blackish stone that might have been swept up off any hearth in the valley. He laid them at the bottom close together, the way a mason sets stones that must bear together, and stood over them a moment with his hands still dusty from the digging.

"I don't know the words for this," he said, half to Caoimhe, half to no one. "There are no words for burying a stone."

"Say what you say over the dead," said the old man of the north hearths, not unkindly. "It cost nothing to try."

So Brian said, standing in the grave he had dug for five coals as he had stood at the edge of a hundred graves he had dug for the valley's dead, the same words, asking the ground to hold gently what was given it; and no one there thought it strange, though the tellers, shaping the night into a telling of its own, would make much of how well the words fit.

He climbed out and filled the grave himself, working by the last light and then by starlight, tamping the earth down the way he tamped it over the dead, firm but not hard, so that the ground would settle true and not sink hollow later. Caoimhe brought him a brand from the hearth to see the last of it by. When it was done there was nothing to mark the place but turned earth, no different to look at from any grave in the valley, lying in the grass a stone's throw from the flat slab that covered the vein.

The watchers went home in twos and threes, most of them saying, in one way or another, that they would come back in the morning to see. Sireadh's grandchildren ran the word back to her before they were properly indoors. Brian and Caoimhe stayed longest, sitting by the fresh earth as the night came down cold and clear, the kind of clear that comes after a warm harvest day, with more stars out than either of them troubled to count, until Caoimhe said at last that there was nothing more to be done by sitting, and they went in.

Neither of them slept well. Brian never said, after, what he had thought through that night, lying awake beside his wife with the ordinary sounds of the valley outside the door, a dog somewhere, the wind working at the thatch; but Caoimhe, old now and telling her own small part of a larger telling, said more than once that she had asked him that night in the dark whether he believed it would work, and that he had been quiet so long she thought him asleep, and then he had said: "I believe the earth keeps what's given it. I don't know if that's the same as answering." And she said that was the truest thing anyone in the whole long history of the question had said about it, though she never said it to his face, not wanting to spoil him with praise he had no use for.

## IV. Of the Black Coal Warm in the Ground

He went out at first light, before Caoimhe was properly up, and by the time she came out to him he had already opened the grave down to where the earth was undisturbed by frost, working carefully now, with his hands more than his spade, the way he worked when he came near a coffin he did not want to mark. Word had gone round the valley faster than either of them, and by the time the five coals were bare in the bottom of the hole there were a dozen people standing at a respectful distance, among them Sireadh, who had got herself out that far on a grandchild's arm and would not be talked out of it.

Brian knelt and lifted them out one at a time, the way he had laid them, and laid each on the flat of his palm before he set it down on the grass beside him.

Four were cold. Cold the way any stone out of cold ground is cold, cold with the whole night's chill still in them, no different to the hand than they had been the evening before, dark, ordinary, dead.

The fifth was the black one, *gual dubh*, black as a wet crow's back, the same black the mason himself had first seen in the broken vein seven hundred years and more before that morning. Brian's hand closed round it out of habit, the same motion he used lifting any stone from a grave, and he had it half out of the earth before he understood what his hand was telling him, which was that it was warm.

He said, afterward, that he thought at first it was only the ground's own warmth, held over from some trick of the night, and it was Sireadh, watching from her grandchild's arm, who called out to him to hold it up so she could see, for she had kept coal and fire-pot both across sixty years and knew better than any living soul what a thing ought to look like when the earth had merely warmed it and what it ought to look like when it had not.

He held it up. In the grey of the morning, with the sun not yet over the eastern shoulder of the valley, a line of red crept across the black surface of the coal where the air first touched it, slow, the way the last coal of a banked fire creeps back to life when the ash is stirred from it, and then widened into a glow, deep in the black, red going toward gold at its heart, and a thread of smoke came up off it into the cold air, and it smouldered in his open hand the way a coal smoulders that has been fed all night and is only waiting to be blown on.

It did not burn him. He said that too, afterward, more than once, for it was the thing people asked him first: did it burn you. It did not. It lay in his palm warm as another man's hand is warm, no hotter, though it went on glowing and smoking gently the whole while he knelt there with it, and the four cold coals lying forgotten in the grass beside him, and the dozen people at the graveside gone entirely silent, so that the only sound in the whole waking valley, for what Caoimhe swore afterward was the length of a held breath and longer, was the small tick and whisper of a coal breathing.

"The earth took it," Sireadh said at last, into the silence, in a voice that had gone thin with more than age. "The answering came at the rising out of the ground, not the lying in it."

It was the truest word said that morning, and it held. The line had tried the coals in rain, and the rain had done nothing, for rain was the sea's business, not the earth's; they had held them in wet air, and the wet air had done nothing either, being neither one element nor another; but the earth, given the whole slow dark of a night to work in, undisturbed, had done what the earth does with anything laid deep in it and left, which was change it, patiently, from beneath, the way a seed is changed, or a body, though a body's changing goes the other way. The black coal had waited seven hundred years for someone to think of asking the earth for that much time, and Brian, who had spent his life giving the earth exactly that much time with everything he laid in it, had been the one man in all the valleys who thought to ask.

He did not go home first. He went, still kneeling, still cradling the warm coal in one hand and the four cold ones gathered up in the crook of the other arm, to Tobar dhìreach itself, to the hearth where the fire-pot was kept, and there before the eldest of the line and half the valley that had come running at the word, he set the black coal down among the embers of the mason's own fire, the fire that Clann a' Chlachair held to have burned, hearth to hearth, unbroken, since the night of the lightning; and it caught there the way any coal catches, low and steady, and burned on, red-hearted, the whole of that day and into the night, and did not go dark again.

## V. Of the Long Keeping

Afterward he kept it in a plain clay pot of his own making, fired for the purpose, with a lid of turf he cut fresh each season to fit it; the pot of the line was another woman's charge, its own long watch, and no part of his to hold. He fed the coal wood and heather as any hearth is fed, and carried it with him, in the pot, banked and covered, wherever the year's work took him, so that for the better part of forty years there was not a night in the high valleys that Brian's black coal was not burning somewhere within a day's walk of the slab.

More times than he ever troubled to count, he was asked whether he thought he had answered the riddle, whether the black coal's burning meant the earth's spirit was in it now and no further finding needed. He always said the same thing, in one form or another, and Caoimhe used to say it word for word behind his back to make the children of the valley laugh, though there was no unkindness in it: that he had only asked the ground a question it was willing to answer, and that whether the answer was the whole of what the Keeper meant, or only a part of it, was a matter for cleverer heads than his, and he had graves to dig.

He went on digging them. The valley did not let a man of sixty stand at every graveside as it had let a man of thirty, and in his last years the heavier digging fell to younger hands he had himself taught the trade to, watching over how deep they went and how they lined the sides, for he held, and said often, that a grave badly dug was a poor last gift to give anyone; but he kept the black coal himself, banked in its pot by his own hearth, until the year he died.

He was three-and-sixty that year, worn more by the work than by any illness the valley could name, and Caoimhe, who outlived him by nine years and never remarried, said that he went the way he had put every one of the valley's dead into the ground before him: without complaint, and with his eyes on the work in front of him almost to the last, which in his case was nothing more than sitting by his own hearth watching the black coal glow.

They buried him beside the slab, near the ground he had first opened for the five coals, for it seemed to the valley, and to Caoimhe, that there was nowhere else so fitting; and the pot with the black coal in it was set beside the grave through the length of the burial, still burning, banked low, so that the last thing said over Brian, grave-digger of the high valleys, was said by a fire that owed its life to him and to no one else in the whole history of the question. When the words were finished and the earth given, Caoimhe herself lifted the pot and carried it home, and kept the coal burning at her own hearth the rest of her days, and after her it passed, as such things do, into other hands, banked and fed and carried on, one more small fire added to the fires the mason's line had already kept unbroken for the better part of a thousand years.

At the recital that was said on the next night of the lightning, the reciter of the line, coming to the old count of the coals, said it new for the first time in living memory: the sea had taken the gold, the sky had taken the white-flecked coal, and now the earth had taken the black, *gual dubh*, slowly, in the dark, the way the earth takes everything it is given time enough with. Four remained unanswered: the red-brown, the silver, the one that would wake, if it woke at all, to no more than the snap of a hand, and the rainbow that held every colour and answered to none of them yet. It was, by every count the line still kept, some six hundred years since the sky, and some seven hundred and fifteen since the sea, and no one standing at that recital thought the finding of the fourth would come soon; but it was said at that recital, and at every one since, that the earth's answer had come the way Brian himself had lived, without hurry and without noise, out of a man who asked it no more than the plain question a plain man asks, and waited, the one night through, for the ground to tell him what it knew.
