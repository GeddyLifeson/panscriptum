# Of the Kilns, the Ramps and the Houses on the Water

*VE 1,443 – 1,669*

> *Dhìrich Fearghas gu mullach an eilein, agus ghabh an gual sneachda teine san adhar.*
> Fearghas climbed to the top of the island, and gual sneachda took fire in the air.
> — from Sloinneadh a' Chlachair

*The Third Part: Of the White Coal on the Height, and the Walls of Seann-Dhaoine*

## I. Of the Kilns at Morn

At Morn the beach ran shingle to a bay of grey water, and above the shingle, where the ground rose enough to stand dry of the highest tide, a household of the Seann-Dhaoine had for longer than any of its own living members could number dug pits into the turf and lined them with flat stones set on edge, so that each pit stood like a shallow box sunk into the ground with its rim just proud of the grass. Seven such pits stood there by the year the kilns are first told of, though the household said, when asked, that there had once been fewer, the way any household says of a thing it keeps adding to as the years and the catch allow.

Each pit had, cut into the turf at its lower side where the ground fell a little toward the shingle, a short channel no wider than a forearm and no deeper, through which a fire lit at the channel's mouth drew its smoke the length of the pit and out through gaps left along the stone lining, so that the whole pit filled with a slow smoke thick enough to hang a hand's blackness on a strung fish inside a morning and a night. It was not, the household would have said had there been anyone of another trade to ask it, a fire built to warm a body or cook a meal; it was built low and kept low and fed sparingly, on purpose, for its work was smoke and not flame, and a pit that flared was a pit whose keeper had misjudged the peat.

The keeping of the fires fell, in the years this telling holds, to a woman of that household who is remembered by no name but by the plain fact of her trade, the keeper of the kilns; and hers was a day unlike any other day kept on that shore. She rose before the light to walk the seven pits in turn, laying a palm flat an inch above each smoking bed to feel whether the night's fire had held or guttered, and where it had guttered she fed it a little dry peat from a store kept dry under an overhang of turf for that single purpose, never green peat and never wet, for green peat flared where dry peat only smoked, and a flare in a kiln full of the day's catch could undo in a morning what a season's fishing had built. Where the fire had held steady through the night she did nothing at all, for a kiln left alone in good order was a kiln doing its work, and it was, she had been taught and taught in her turn, no part of the trade to trouble a fire that troubled no one.

The catch itself came down to her from the boats each evening, strung on cords through the gills the way every household of that coast strung a catch, and she laid the strings across green withies set from rim to rim of each pit, low enough that the smoke closed over them but high enough that no fish hung near the fire's own heat, for a kiln was reckoned well kept when a hand laid flat on the fish by evening found it no warmer than the smoke around it. A fish taken out too soon spoiled within days despite the smoking; a fish left too long in a pit whose fire had crept too high dried hard and lost its worth at the trading; and between the two lay a length of days that the keeper of the kilns judged, so far as the telling can say, by no measure but the one in her own hand and eye, laid on the fish itself each morning and each evening, cord by cord.

It is told that in the years the seventh pit was dug, the smoke of Morn's kilns stood some mornings thick enough over the bay to be seen from the high ground a half day's walk inland, a grey column standing where no cooking-fire's smoke had ever stood long enough to be marked, and that herders of the inland hearths who saw it took it, for a while, as weather of some kind particular to that stretch of coast, a mist that rose from the sea on certain still mornings and no other. It was some years before the herders understood, by what road the telling does not say, that the column was smoke and the smoke was a fire kept on purpose, low and smouldering, day upon day, for no warmth and no cooking and no light against the dark, but only to dry a fish that would keep through a season no boat could fish in.

No hearth of the hills built the like of it in all the years this telling has followed either shore, for the hearths of the hills had no catch to keep in such a quantity that a single winter's eating could not see it through, and a fire built and fed and never let flare, day upon day, for the sake of what it did to meat rather than what it gave to the people standing near it, was a use of fire the hill-folk had not yet had reason to think of. It is the first building raised on Dia-thìr, so far as the telling has ever traced, for a trade and not for a hearth to sleep by or a hearth to lay a body near in death; and the keeper of the kilns, whoever she had been in the years before this telling opens, or her mother before her, or whoever it was first thought to dig a pit and line it and lead a channel of air into it rather than simply hang a string of fish over an ordinary fire and hope the smoke found it, left no name behind for the thinking of it, only the seven pits themselves, and the eighth begun before the telling loses sight of that shore, and the ninth begun after.

It is told of one summer, a good way into the years this telling has followed the kilns, that a night's rain drove in sideways off the bay and put out the fire in the fourth pit before the keeper had made her last round of the evening, so that she came to it at first light and found the withies bare, no smoke standing over them at all, the fish upon them cold and wet and already turned in their smell. She did not relight that pit that day, for a fire started fresh under a full load of soaked fish would have flared before it settled, and a flare would have cost her the load twice over rather than once; she carried the spoiled strings down to the tideline instead, where the gulls of that shore had long since learned to wait on such mornings, and let the pit stand cold and empty until the stones inside it had dried enough, by her own judging hand, to take a new fire without weeping steam back into the smoke. It is the only loss of a whole pit's load that this telling has kept a memory of from all the years the kilns are told of, and it is kept, so far as can be judged, only because the keeper herself is said to have spoken of it once, in her age, to a niece she was teaching the trade to, as the one morning she had ever stood at a kiln and found it had nothing at all to give her.

The niece kept the telling of it, and kept also, from the same teaching, the rule the keeper drew from that one wet morning: that a kiln in a gathering rain wanted watching past the hour a keeper would otherwise have gone to her own bed, for a fire low enough to smoke and not flare was, by that same lowness, a fire near enough to going out that a hard rain could take it before a keeper's next round found it gone. Whether every keeper of Morn's kilns who came after held to that watching in every wet night of her own years, the telling does not say; but the ninth pit, dug in the last years this telling follows that shore, stood a little higher on its rise than the first seven, its channel a little longer and angled a little more against the wind that drove rain in off the bay, as though whoever dug it had learned something from a pit dug lower and had no wish to learn it twice.

## II. Of the Ramps at Brenn

On the north coast, where the land ran down in long shelves of flat pale stone toward water deep enough close in that a boat could float within an oar's length of dry ground, another household of the same people had for its own long span of years built and launched and mended the skin boats common to every shore of the island, each hide stretched on a light frame of bent wood no bigger than two or three grown people could carry between them down to the water and back. In the years this telling now turns to, that household began a work no other shore had yet attempted: they laid, down the same shelving stone their boats had always launched from, long ramps built up of flat slabs set edge to edge and levelled with smaller stone packed beneath the low places, running from well above the highest tide down into water deep enough to float more than a skin boat's draught.

The first of the ramps was the work of more seasons than the telling can count with any confidence, for the slabs were heavy beyond what two or three hands could set true, and each one had to be walked and levered into its place a hand's breadth at a time, tried with a loaded sledge dragged its length, and lifted and reset where the sledge caught or rocked. It is told that the ramp stood finished, its whole length from the turf above to the water below laid true enough that a sledge run down it met no check from the first stone to the last, some years before anyone of that household had cause to build, upon it, anything heavier than the boats they had always built.

What was built there afterward, and drawn down that ramp and launched from its foot into the deep water, was a hull of a different make than any hide boat: a frame of timber built up plank upon plank rather than skin stretched over bent wood, shaped and pegged and caulked with whatever that household used for the purpose, and raised the whole length of the ramp on rollers cut from young trunks, a crew of them at the ropes and more at the rollers, walking it down by inches while the tide stood at the height the ramp was built for. A boy of the household, sent down at first light to see that no roller had shifted in the night, is the only person the telling names as standing close by it in its last hour ashore; the rest of the household came down as the light rose, and stood along the ramp's edge, and did not go into the water themselves.

The hull went down slower than any load the ramp had carried before it, checked twice on rollers that bit unevenly and freed each time with the same patient shifting of weight that had built the ramp in the first place, and took the water at last with no more sound, so far as anyone standing on the shore could afterward agree in the telling, than the ordinary slap of a wave against stone. It floated low and steady, riding in water deep enough that no one standing on the ramp's foot could any longer see its keel, and hands already aboard it, for someone had gone aboard before it left the rollers, though the telling has never agreed how many nor which of the household, took up oars of a length no skin boat's crew had ever needed and worked it out past the last shelf of stone into open water.

No season that the household could later fix a year to brought it back to that shore, so far as this telling has followed the household afterward; and of where it went, or what it was built to reach, or whether it reached it, nothing is told. The household went on building at the ramp after it, wider each time and longer, and launched hulls from it in the years that followed the way any trade repeats what it has learned once it has learned it; but the first hull, and the water it went out into, and the coast it may or may not have raised on some horizon no one of Brenn ever stood close enough to see confirmed, belong, in every telling that has come down of it, to the sea alone.

Of the first ramp's own building, before ever a plank was laid to make a hull for it to carry, more is told than of the hull that came after, for the ramp stood a long while as a thing seen and stepped over by the whole household before it became a thing that launched anything at all. Twice, in the years the telling follows its laying, a slab set well enough by daylight was found in the morning to have settled crooked in the night, the packed stone beneath it having given under its own weight where the ground below ran soft, and both times Leac na Fèithe was levered up again with poles of green oak braced against driven stakes, the low place packed afresh with smaller stone rammed hard by hand and by heel, and Leac na Fèithe let down a second time onto ground that had, by then, been tried and found wanting once already. The household that did this work is not told to have cursed the crooked stone or hurried its second setting; the telling that has come down of it says only that it was done again, the same careful way, as many times as the ground asked for it.

A dog of that household is remembered, though not by name any more than the boy who watched the last rollers or the hands that first went aboard, for lying across the finished ramp on warm days in the last summer before the first hull was framed above it, so that more than one of the household is said to have stepped over or around it on the way down to the water rather than move it, the ramp by then being as ordinary a part of that shore's ground as the shingle either side of it. Whether the dog lay there on the morning the hull was finally walked down to the water, and was moved for that one day only, or was kept well clear of the work for longer than a single morning, the telling has not troubled to say; it is remembered only for the many ordinary days before, lying full length on stone that had cost the household more labour than anything else that shore had ever raised, exactly as it might have lain on any bare patch of turf.

## III. Of the Houses on the Water

Inland from either shore, where the land folded down into a loch called Loch chrom, ringed close about with reed and alder, a scatter of low platforms stood out over the water on legs of oak driven down through the shallows into the firmer ground beneath, each platform joined to the shore, and each to its nearest neighbour, by a walk of split planks laid end to end and lashed to crossbeams below. Whether the platforms were the work of the Seann-Dhaoine, whose kilns smoked on one coast and whose ramps grew on another in those same years, or of some other people who left no other mark the telling has since been able to point to and say *these were theirs and no one else's*, is not a thing this telling, or any telling that has come to it, has ever been able to settle; the oak legs, driven and trimmed and lashed at their tops to bear a floor of split timber, say only that hands did it, and are silent on whose.

The platforms stood a little apart from one another across the loch's shallower reach, each with its own low house of wattle and turf raised on the floor above the water, and the walks between them ran narrow enough that a laden basket carried along one had to be carried square to the body or knocked against the low rail lashed along either side. Beneath the floors, in the water and the mud between the piles, eels gathered in numbers no open bank of that same loch ever drew them to in such close reach of a hand or a woven trap, for the shade of the platforms and the stillness the piles made in the current suited them better than open water did, and the households above learned, within what the telling reckons a very few years of the first platform standing, to lower baited traps through gaps left on purpose in the plank floor rather than working the open shallows from a boat as they had before.

A child of one of those households, too young by the household's own reckoning to be trusted alone on the open walks, was set nonetheless, on a morning this telling has kept though it has kept no name to go with it, to carry a basket of the day's eels the length of the longest walk, from the furthest platform to the shore, while the grown hands of the household were occupied at the traps. The child went the whole distance at a careful walking pace with both hands full and the basket's strap looped twice about a wrist against the chance of a slip, watching the planks underfoot rather than the water on either side, and set the basket down on dry ground at the walk's landward end without once losing footing on planks that another child, a season or two younger, would not yet have been trusted upon at all.

It is told, though not by any teller close enough to the loch to have seen it done, that the child was watched the whole way by an elder of the household standing at the shore end of the walk, arms folded, saying nothing, neither calling encouragement nor warning of a loose plank, for the crossing was by then, in that household's own reckoning of such things, an ordinary errand and not a trial; and that when the child set the basket down at last, the elder only turned back to the day's work at the traps without remark, the way a household turns back to its work once an ordinary thing has been ordinarily done. What the telling has kept of that morning, it has kept for the crossing alone, and not for anything the child or the elder is remembered to have said of it.

The platforms stood on their piles through wet years and dry, mended plank by plank as planks split or piles settled, longer than any one household's memory held whole; and the eels taken from beneath them, smoked or eaten fresh or carried inland on the same slow roads that carried word of the smoke at Morn, fed households that never set a plank of their own over that water nor asked whose hands had first driven a pile into its mud.

The driving of a new pile, when one was wanted, in place of an old one worked loose by years of current and weight, was reckoned the heaviest labour any of the loch's households undertook, heavier by their own account than the raising of a wattle wall or the cutting of a season's reed, for it asked four or five hands working together where most of that shore's other work asked one or two. A trimmed trunk of oak, its foot cut to a rough point and charred a little in a low fire to harden it against the wet ground it would stand in, was walked out along the nearest finished platform on rollers of its own, upended over the gap where the old pile had failed, and driven down by a weighted beam of the same oak, swung and dropped from a frame lashed above the gap, blow upon blow, until a hand laid flat against the standing trunk could no longer feel it give at the strike. It is told that the driving of a single pile in firm ground took the better part of a day, and in soft ground, where the trunk wanted driving deeper before it held, the better part of two; and that the households of the loch counted a new pile driven and holding as an occasion worth a shared meal on the nearest platform once the frame was struck down and the mud had settled around the new wood, though the telling has kept no word of what was eaten or said at such a meal, only that it was held.

The walks between platforms, no less than the platforms themselves, wanted mending against the same wet years that worked the piles loose, for a plank laid flat across open water and walked upon by every foot and every laden basket of a household's daily round wore thin at its centre long before its lashed ends gave way, and a worn plank was watched for and replaced before it broke rather than after, by households that had, by the years this telling holds, come to know the sound a sound plank made underfoot and the slightly different sound a thinning one made, a knowledge kept in the foot and the ear rather than in any word for it. A child too small yet to be trusted with a basket, but old enough to be sent along a walk on no errand heavier than its own feet, is remembered to have been set, more than once, to walk the length of every plank each morning before the grown hands went out to the traps, listening for the sound of a plank gone thin beneath its own light weight before a grown foot, heavier and less patient in its listening, might find the same plank the hard way.

## IV. Of the Shore Grown Bold

Three shores, in the years this telling has followed them, each took up a work heavier and stranger than any before it: pits dug and lined and fired for smoke alone; stone laid the length of a shelf to carry a hull the sea itself would swallow the memory of; timber driven down through water to raise a floor where no floor had stood. None of the three households that raised them knew, so far as any telling carries, what the others were about on their own stretch of coast in those same years, for word between shore and shore travelled slower in those days than smoke rose from a kiln or a plank was laid across a pile, and each work stood, while it was building, as its own household's own matter and no one else's.

Yet a single thing had changed along every coast the telling has followed in this age, and the telling, looking back from beyond it, can call the thing by its right name though none of the three households then building could have called it by any name at all: the sea, which had for so long been taken as it was found, waded and fished and rowed across in a skin no bigger than a boat's own crew, had begun in these same years to be worked. Ground was dug for it that had never been dug before. Stone was laid for it that would outlast the hands that laid it. Water was driven timber into, and floored over, and made to carry a household's own floor above its own depth. The shore that had once only received what the sea gave it had begun, in three places at least, and likely in others the telling has not followed, to shape the meeting place between land and water to its own purpose rather than take that meeting place exactly as the tide had always left it.

Inland at Tobar dhìreach, where the fire that had come down out of the storm still burned as it had burned since before any of these three shores had cut a kiln-pit or laid a ramp-slab or driven an oak pile, the line of the mason kept its seven pieces in the leather bag and told, at every recital, of a gold coal that had taken fire on the sea and six that had not; and no word had yet come to that hearth, in the years this telling holds, of kilns or ramps or platforms on any coast, for the hills and the shore-trades of the Seann-Dhaoine kept, in this age as in every age before it, each to its own doings and its own silence toward the other. What passed between the fire-keeping line of the hills and the smoke-keeping, water-working households of the coasts was carried, when it was carried at all, no faster than a traveller's own two feet, and a traveller with reason to cross from one to the other was not yet, in these years, a common thing on any of the island's roads.

The last that is told of the hull launched from Brenn is the small dark shape of a sail it is said to have carried, seen once, by no name the telling has kept, from high ground some distance along that same coast, standing out against the grey of the water and growing smaller with every count of the watcher's own breath until it could no longer be told from the water it stood on. Whether that sail belonged to the hull raised at Brenn, or to some other hull of some other shore's building that this telling has not followed, is not settled and was not settled then; but the watcher who saw it, whoever that watcher was, is remembered for one thing only, which is that they stood and watched until there was nothing left to watch, and then turned and went back down to whatever work the day still asked of them, the way every shore of that age turned, in the end, back to its own work once the sea had taken what it was going to take and shown what it was going to show.
