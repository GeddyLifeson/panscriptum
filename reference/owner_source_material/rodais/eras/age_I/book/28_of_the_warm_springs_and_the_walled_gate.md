# Of the Warm Springs and the Walled Gate

*VE 3,922 – 4,123*

## I. Of the Springs Under the Mountain

On the southern flank of the central mountains, where the ground drops in broken steps toward the lowland and the rock runs red when the evening light is on it, there is a place where the water comes up warm. It rises out of three low mouths in the hillside, one above another, at a heat a hand can bear for no longer than a slow count of ten, and runs down in a steaming thread over rock stained orange and green by its passing, and in the cold months the steam of it stands over the hillside like the smoke of a hearth that is never let go out. Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh, the warm springs, are called so still.

The people of the landings came to it in the years when their reach inland was widest, when their walls stood on the spur above the southern lowland and their three rings on the height near the south-eastern coast, and the kerbed way ran between the two; and what they built there was the plainest of all their works away from the sea. Below the lowest of the three mouths they cut the rock back to make a shelf, and on the shelf they set stone basins, slab fitted to slab and the joints packed with clay, three of them in a row, so that the water fell from the hill into the first and ran over its lip into the second and from the second into the third, cooling a little in each. In the first a hand could not be held. In the third a body could lie to the chin.

The woman who kept the basins in the years the telling first follows them had come up from the coast as a girl with a brother whose foot had been crushed under a hull at the launching, and had stayed, because the foot had mended and she had watched it mend. She was not young now. Her hands had gone red and soft at the fingertips from a lifetime in warm water, and she could tell by the colour of a wound, and by its smell when the dressing came off, whether it wanted the second basin or the third or neither, and she did not explain the telling of it to anyone, for she had no words for it that were not the thing itself.

They brought the sick to her from every coast. A man came in on a litter of poles and hide with his leg broken below the knee, the bone through the skin in two places, carried four days by six of his kin in turns, and the kin set him down at the lip of the shelf and stood back, the way people stand back from a thing they have carried a long way and no longer know what to do with. The keeper of the basins looked at the leg a long time. Then she had him lifted into the third basin on a frame of woven withies that held him off the stone floor, and she set the bone herself with her own two hands under the warm water, pulling and turning until the ends met as they had met before the fall, and bound it with wet bark and cord, tighter than any healer of the coast would have dared bind a broken leg for fear of choking the foot; and she turned him twice a day for a whole season, morning and evening, and changed the binding, and fed him broth from a horn spoon, and did not once let the wound-mouths go dry or foul.

He walked out on a stick before the winter turned. He walked without it inside two years, and lived to be old, and when he died he was brought back and buried on the slope below the basins at his own asking, with the other dead of the springs, where the ground was warm under the turf all winter long. The bone of that leg, if any hand had ever turned it up again, would have shown the old break as a thickened ring round the shaft and nothing more, knitted clean and straight. So would the bones of a good many of those who lay on that slope in the years that followed: arms and legs and ribs and, once, the bones of a child's skull, all broken, all mended, lying quiet in warm ground with the steam of the springs going up above them.

It was the first place on the island where the sick were brought to be made well. Before it every people had nursed its own hurts at its own hearths, with what skill each hearth had and no more. After it, among the people of the landings, a broken body had somewhere to be carried.

The keeper of the basins grew old at the work. In her last years she sat on the shelf beside the first basin, where the heat was fiercest and her old bones liked it best, and watched a younger woman do the turning and the setting and the binding, and said nothing unless something was being done wrong, which was seldom. When she died they buried her on the slope with the others, nearest the water, and the young woman went on with the turning the next morning, because there was a man in the third basin with a crushed hand who could not be left.

## II. Of the Youth Sent Down

In the south-east, some days' walk from the springs, the eastern shaft at Toll-dubh had stood open since before any grandmother of that coast could remember its making: a hole sunk straight down through the living rock, its walls tooled smooth as far as any lamp had ever shown them, with no spoil heap beside it and no telling of where the stone had gone. Children dropped pebbles into it and listened, and heard nothing, and ran off laughing to hide how the nothing had frightened them. The grown people of that shore walked wide of its lip and did not drop anything into it at all.

The year the youth went down, it was the people of the south-east who plaited the rope. They made it of hide, cut in long strips from the skins of three oxen and plaited four-strand the way a mooring line is plaited, and it took two women the better part of a month, and when it was done it lay coiled on the turf by the shaft's mouth higher than a sitting child. Who first said that someone should go down, and why it should be that year and not some other, the telling does not say. It says only that the youth asked to be the one, and that no one argued with him long.

He was not yet grown to his full height. He sat on the lip of the shaft on a grey morning with the rope knotted under his arms and across his chest, and a lamp of fat in a hollowed stone in his lap, and the wind came up out of the hole, cool and smelling of wet stone, and lifted the hair off his forehead. The men who were to pay out the rope stood behind him with their heels dug into the turf. The women who had plaited it stood a little apart. No one spoke to him. He looked down into the dark a long while, and then he looked round once at the faces behind him, and then he slid off the lip and hung, turning slowly, and they began to let him down.

The lamp went down with him. That is the part of the telling that has been kept most carefully, and it is kept in the same words every time: the lamp went small. It burned as steadily as any lamp burns, and it did not go out, and it only grew smaller and smaller as the rope paid out hand over hand through the men's fists, a yellow point on the smooth wall, and then a smaller point, and then the dark closed over it and there was only the rope going down, creaking through the hands, and the wind coming up out of the hole past the watchers' faces.

The rope ran out to its last knot. They held it there. It hung straight and heavy from the lip and gave no sign, no jerk, no slackening, and the men holding it stood with their arms trembling from the weight and did not let go, and the women stood behind them, and no one said anything at all for so long that a woman at the back began, very quietly, to weep, and was not hushed.

Then the rope jerked twice, and they hauled.

They brought him up slowly, as he had been let down, hand over hand, because a thing that has hung a long while on a rope in the dark is not to be jerked back into the light as if it were a bucket, and because no one wanted to be the one who hurried whatever was on the end of it. It took longer to bring him up than it had taken to let him down. When his head came over the lip, the lamp in its stone was still in his hand, and it was out, and his face was the colour of ash.

He did not speak. They lifted him onto the turf and untied the rope from him and he sat where they set him, and his mother put her hands on his face, and he let her, and looked past her at nothing. They asked him what he had seen. He shook his head. They asked him again, gently, that evening, and the next day, and in the days after, and he shook his head each time, and in the end they stopped asking.

He never said what was at the bottom, not then and not in all the years he lived. He took a wife from a landing two days south, and had children, and worked a boat, and was known on that coast for nothing strange except that he would not let a lamp burn low in any room where he was; he would rise in the night to trim it or put it out altogether, and never said why a failing light troubled him. After him no one went down again. The rope was cut up for mooring lines, and the shaft stood open as it had always stood, and the grown people of that shore walked wider of its lip than before.

## III. Of the Last Stone Set

On the spur above the southern lowland, where the outpost had stood with its one gate facing inland for longer than any watcher on its wall could count back, a season came when the gate was closed.

It was closed from within. The telling of it follows one man inside, and follows him only so far, and it is given here as far as it goes.

He carried rubble to the gate through the last of a short day. There had been others with him at the start, carrying and fitting, and one by one as the light went they had gone to some other part of the work within the walls, and he was left alone at the gate with a heap of broken stone and a lamp. He set each stone into the gap that was left with the same care a mason gives a wall that is meant to stand, turning it in his hands to find its best face, trying it, taking it out and turning it again, and packing the smaller stuff in behind it with the heel of his hand. The gap narrowed. Outside it, through the last of the opening, he could see the kerbed way running down off the spur toward the lowland in the dusk, pale and empty, and beyond it the dark line of the hills. He did not stop to look at it.

The lamp burned low beside him on the packed stone. It had been filled that morning and he had not filled it again, and he did not hurry because of it; he worked by its thinning light as he had worked by the full light of noon, and when the gap had shrunk to the width of his two hands he fitted the last stones by feel as much as by sight. The flame had sunk to a blue bead in the fat. He took up the last stone, a flat piece the size of a loaf, and turned it, and tried it in the gap, and it would not go; and he turned it the other way and tried it again, and it went, and he pressed it home with the flat of his hand and held it there a moment as if it might fall out again when he let it go.

It did not fall. The lamp beside him went on burning, very low.

There the telling stops. It does not say where he went after, or whether there was anywhere to go.

## IV. Of the Empty Places

The outpost stood empty after that. No smoke rose from within the wall on the mornings when smoke had always risen, and no watcher stood on the steps inside the gate, and the kerbed way that ran down off the spur went unwalked, so that within a few years the grass had crept in over the slabs from the edges and the kerbs showed through it like the bones of a thing half buried. The gate stayed as it had been left, choked from within with rubble packed so tight that a hand could not have worked a single stone of it loose. By then there was no one to try.

Whoever had closed it had gone out of it some other way, or had not gone out. The wall ran unbroken round the whole of the spur, and no ladder had been left leaning against it, and no rope hung over it; and inside, among the footings of the houses where the roofs had fallen in, the grass grew as tall and as undisturbed as grass grows anywhere no foot troubles it.

On the height near the south-eastern coast the three rings still stood, and people still lived within them, and drove their cattle in at the gate of the outer ring at dusk and out again in the morning, and drew their water from the great cistern cut in the rock at the crown of the hill. It had served four hundred people and their beasts through summers when no rain fell, as the one who read the ground had meant it should when she stood over its digging. It served them still.

The woman who went to draw from it one morning, in a year a long while after the outpost fell silent, went early, before the rest of the household was up, because the water was sweetest at first light and her father was ill and wanted it. She carried two skins and a rope. She came to the lip of the cistern in the grey half-light and let the first skin down, and it did not go down. It struck something a little below the lip, something that gave under it and did not splash, and she drew it up again and looked, and in the grey light she saw what it had struck.

The cistern was full of cattle. They had been thrown in whole, not butchered, not the leavings of any killing, but whole beasts with their hides on and their horns on, heaped in the water on top of one another so that their backs and their bent legs broke the surface, and the water between them had already gone dark. There were more of them than the household she belonged to had ever owned, and more than the household beside it. She did not count them. She stood at the lip with the empty skin hanging from her hand and the smell coming up at her, and then she let the skin drop on the stone and went back to her father's house, walking, not running, and woke her brother, and told him what she had seen, and he went and saw it too.

No one of the three rings could say who had done it. No one had heard the beasts driven to the crown of the hill in the night, or heard them bellow, or heard the fall of so much weight into so much water; the watch on the outer ring had seen nothing come in at the gate. The cattle were beasts of the rings, and some of those who looked into the cistern that morning knew their own among them by the set of a horn or a mark on a flank, and said nothing of it, and went away.

They did not draw from the cistern again. No one would, not that year and not in any year after, though a people living on a dry height has no easy water but what it cuts into the rock for itself, and the carrying of water up from the springs of the valley below was a whole morning's labour for every household every day. They carried it. The cistern they left as it was, full of bone under water gone black, and they laid no cover over it, and walked round it as they walked round a grave.

What had gone wrong in the high places, in the years when the outpost's gate was closed and the cistern of the three rings was fouled, the people of the landings never said, not to one another in any telling that has been kept and not to anyone else. On the coasts their kilns still smoked and their boats still went out, and the warm springs under the mountain still ran, and the sick were still carried up to the basins on litters of poles and hide and turned twice a day in the third basin by a woman whose hands had gone soft at the fingertips. But on the heights the people who had turned their faces from the sea found that the ground they had turned toward had begun, one silence at a time, to give them nothing back but the rain.
