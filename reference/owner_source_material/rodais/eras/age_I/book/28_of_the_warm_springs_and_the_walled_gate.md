# Of the Warm Springs and the Walled Gate

*VE 3,922 – 4,123*

## I. Of the Springs Under the Mountain

Below the mountain called Muileann ruadh, where the rock ran red in the evening light and gave the height its name, the ground ran warm underfoot. Water came up out of three low mouths in the hillside at a heat no hand could hold in past a slow count of ten, and pooled below them in basins the Seann-Dhaoine had cut and fitted stone to stone, so that a sick body could be laid in water gentler than the springs' own source and be held there by walls a man had built rather than by the accident of the hill. No one of the hill country had ever gone so far west to see it worked. What is told of it comes down from the coast-people themselves, or from what the coast-people's neighbours saw over a season and carried home half understood, the way any wonder not one's own is carried.

They brought their sick there from every landing the mole-builders had raised, walking some of them for the better part of a week on a litter of poles and hide, because the water at Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh was known to knit a broken bone cleaner and faster than any binding of splints alone, and to take the heat out of a wound-fever within three nights where elsewhere it might run a fortnight and kill. The dead of that country, when they came at last to be buried near the springs, as many chose to be, showed old breaks in the leg-bone and the forearm set so true that a digger turning up that same ground in an older year could not always say, from the bone alone, that the break had ever been more than a crack soon mended. It was the one healing the Seann-Dhaoine were ever seen, by any eye outside their own kind, to practise with method and with patience, tending a hurt the way a fire is tended, rather than with whatever else it was they did in their high stone places that no outside eye was ever let see.

A woman of the coast, whose name did not travel with the telling, was carried to the springs in her nineteenth year with a leg broken below the knee in a fall from a cliff path, the bone come through the skin in two places, and every soul who saw the hurt done said afterward that they had looked to see her carried out the way she was carried in, in a season, feet first. She was laid in the warmest of the three basins on a frame of woven withies that kept her weight from the stone floor, and turned twice a day by two of the coast-people whose whole work, that season, seemed to be the turning and the tending of those laid in the water. The leg was bound after each turning in wet cloth bound tighter than any hill healer would have dared bind a broken limb, for fear of choking what blood still ran to the foot; but the water's own heat, it was thought, did what the tightness alone could not, keeping the swelling down and the wound-mouths clean of the rot that killed as many broken legs as the break did. She walked out of Muileann ruadh before the winter turned, on a stick, and without it inside two more years, and it was her own grandchildren, in a telling that had crossed by then from the coast-people's own mouths into the hearths of the north-west moor, who first said what was afterward said everywhere that the springs' healing was told at all: that the mountain had mended her, and the coast-people had only known how to ask it properly.

A second telling, less often carried but not less believed, spoke of a boy struck on the head by a falling spar at one of the coast-people's building-yards, senseless three days and given up by everyone about him but his mother, who had him carried to the springs on her own back the first day and would not be turned from it. He was laid on a slab beside the warmest basin, not in the water where the steam off it could reach him, and turned as the leg-broken woman had been turned, and on the fourth day he opened his eyes and asked, in a voice the telling always kept, for something to eat. What had mended him, the steam or the turning or his mother's refusal to leave his side, no teller ever claimed to know past the plain fact of the asking for food, but the boy grew to be an old man among the coast-people, known in his own country, by what little of his name ever reached the moor, as the one the mountain gave back.

Whether the Seann-Dhaoine held the water sacred, or only useful, none of the outside tellers who carried the story on ever agreed. It was noted, and thought worth the noting, that the springs lay apart from any temple or high stone circle of theirs, cut into no ring of set stone, marked by no altar such as stood square to the central mountains in the eastern hills; it was a healing place and nothing more was built upon it or claimed for it, which was, of everything known of that people, among the plainest things known. A traveller of the northern moor, in the year the tellers later reckoned the springs' first well-attested mending, watched from a safe distance on the hillside above as two basins were emptied, scoured with sand and river-stone, and filled again from the mouths above, the whole labour taking the better part of a day and done, as far as he could see, without a word spoken between the workers the entire time they were at it, as men do a task they have done together so many years that speech has gone out of it and left only the doing. He carried that home too, along with the account of the leg that had healed, and it was added to what little else the hill country knew of the coast-people's ways: patient, silent at their work, and generous, when it came to healing, past any reckoning the hill country made of its own generosity to strangers.

## II. Of the Youth Sent Down

It was some thirty years after the springs' first telling reached the moor that a different account came out of the south-east, carried by the same slow road of a traveller's eye and a traveller's tongue, and this one had no healing in it at all.

The eastern Toll-dubh had stood, by then, longer than any teller could set a beginning to: a shaft sunk straight down through the living rock, its walls tooled smooth as far down as any lamp had ever shown a bottom to them, with no heap of spoil beside it to say what had been dug out to make the hole, or where it had gone. Stones let fall into it were never heard to land. It was the coast-people's own doing, by every reckoning that had ever been made of it, though no coast-dweller had ever been seen to work at its mouth or stand near it for any purpose an outside eye could read, and it had become, over generations, less a wonder spoken of than a fact of that country simply lived beside, the way a cliff or a river is lived beside without being marvelled at every day.

That changed the year the people of the south-east lowered a youth into it on a rope of plaited hide.

He was young, by every account that came down of him, not yet grown to his full height, and it was said after, though no one who said it had been near enough to hear it said at the time, that he had gone down of his own asking rather than being sent, wanting to be the one who finally answered what lay at the bottom of a hole his own grandfather's grandfather had grown old beside without ever learning. Whether that was true or only what a story wants when it wants a hero at the start of it, the hill country's tellers could not say and did not pretend to. What was seen, by the same distant watching that had carried the springs' tale north, was plain enough in its bones: a crowd of the coast-people gathered at the shaft's mouth in the grey of a morning, a rope let down hand over hand with a man at each fathom paying it out steady, and a lamp going down ahead of the youth on a shorter cord of its own, so that he would have some small light below him before his own weight followed it.

The lamp went small. That was the word the watcher carried home for it, and it was the word that stayed in every later telling: the light kept burning, steady as any lamp does, and only shrank, growing smaller and smaller as the rope paid out, the way a star seems to shrink the longer a man watches it without looking away, until it was no more than a point far down in the dark, and by then the youth's own outline had gone from sight a long while before the lamp did.

He hung there, by what could be counted of the rope let out and taken up, the better part of an hour. Then, without any word or signal that the distant watcher could make out, or that the coast-people themselves ever gave any outside ear reason to think had been agreed beforehand, the rope came taut and was pulled. It came up slower than it had gone down, the men at the mouth hauling in a rhythm that had the look of care in it rather than haste, as though whatever was on the other end of the rope was a thing that must not be brought up too fast, the way a diver is brought up slow from deep water for fear of what the quick change does to him.

The youth came over the lip of the shaft ash-faced, in the plain sense of the word: the colour gone clean out of him, grey as old ash under a fire that has not been fed in a day, and he did not speak. Two of the coast-people stood close at each shoulder, ready to catch him if his knees went, but by the watcher's telling his knees held; he walked from the shaft's mouth on his own legs, unsteadily, to where the rest of his people waited, and sat down among them with his back against a stone, and looked, for as long as the distant eye kept its watch that day, at nothing that eye could find in front of him.

He never told what he had seen. That much the hill country's telling kept plain and would not embroider, for it was told the same way in every mouth that carried it onward: not that the coast-people kept his account secret from outsiders, which would have been no more than any people does with what is its own, but that he himself, among his own, never gave an answer to what he had been sent down to find. Whether he was asked and would not say, or was never asked again because the asking had been judged, by whoever among the coast-people judged such things, to have cost more than it was worth, no watcher from the moor or the hills was near enough, or trusted enough, ever to learn. He lived on among his people some years after, by what little further word came north of him, growing neither talkative nor withdrawn beyond the ordinary measure of a young man grown suddenly older than his years. He took a wife, in time, of a household two landings south, and fathered children who by every account grew up hearing only that their father had once gone down a rope at Toll-dubh and come back, never why he had gone or what waited for him at the rope's end; and it was one of those children, grown old herself, who told a trader of the moor, in the one unguarded hour of a long life of guarding it, that her father had never once in her hearing let a lamp burn low in a room without rising, whatever the hour, to trim it or put it out entirely, and would not say why that troubled him more than an ordinary man is troubled by a failing light. The shaft at Toll-dubh went on being lived beside as it had always been lived beside, stones dropped into it now and again by children who had not heard, or did not yet believe, that a grown man had gone down it once and come up with nothing to say.

## III. Of the Last Stone Set

Far to the north of Toll-dubh, on a height that looked down over three valleys at once, the coast-people had raised, generations before, a small walled place with a single gate: not a great fortress such as stood at the mouth of the Abhainn naomh, but a watch-post, low-walled and narrow-gated, the kind of outpost a people sets on high ground to see danger coming from a distance and pass the word along before the danger arrives. It had stood so long, and so quietly, that the hill country beyond it had long since stopped counting it among things that changed, the way a boundary stone is not expected to move.

It changed in a single season, though no outside eye was near enough, at the time, to see the change made. What was found after, by whoever first came near enough to look, was this: the one gate of the outpost, the single way in or out of its low wall, stood blocked from the inside with rubble packed so tight and so deep that no one who found it could shift more than a hand's depth of it without tools, and no one troubled, then or after, to bring tools enough to clear the whole of it and see what lay behind.

Whoever first found the gate blocked did not come near it by chance. The telling of it, thin as it is, agrees on that much: a shepherd of the moor's northern edge, following a strayed ewe further up the height than shepherds of that country commonly went, came on the wall in the low light of a late afternoon and stood some while at a safe distance, waiting for a watch-fire to be lit or a voice to be raised against him, as he had been taught to expect on any approach to the coast-people's places. Neither came. He went nearer than he had meant to, close enough at last to lay a hand flat on the stone, and found a wall where a door should have opened, packed floor to lintel with broken stone from within. He carried the finding home before dark, more frightened, by his own account, of the silence than he would have been of any voice raised against him, and it was his telling, passed on and thinned with every hearth it crossed, that first put the words "the walled gate" into the mouths of the hill country.

Of what was done inside, in the hour or the day the gate was closed, no outside teller ever had an account to give, for no outside eye was there to give one. But the hill country's own tellers, as the years wore on and the story of the walled gate travelled far enough and long enough to want more of a body than a shepherd's frightened hand on cold stone, began to tell it as though from within, the way a teller will sometimes step inside a silence to fill it, saying plainly, when they told it so, that this part came from no witness and was only what the telling demanded of its own accord; and it is worth setting down here as it came to be told, for it is how the hill country kept the memory of the thing, whatever the truth of it had been.

One who was there, the telling goes, set the last stone himself. He had carried rubble to the gate through the last of a short day, working with others at first and then, as the light failed and one by one the others fell to some other part of the closing, alone, fitting each stone into the gap that was left with the same care a mason gives a wall meant to stand a hundred years, though this wall was meant, by every sign of how it was built, to stand for no eye ever to see behind it again. His lamp burned low beside him on the packed stone, the last of its oil, and he did not hurry it, working by its light as it thinned toward the end of its burning the way he had worked by full daylight that morning, setting the last few stones by feel as much as by sight when the flame guttered down to little more than a coal in its dish.

The telling stops there, at the lamp guttering and the last stone set, and gives no more. It does not say what came of the one who set it, whether he had a way out that the gate did not need, or none, or whether the closing of the gate and the fate of the one who closed it were the same closing. The hill country's tellers, having carried the story that far on the strength of no witness at all, would carry it no further, saying only, when pressed, that a telling built on nothing ought not to pretend to more than nothing gives, and that the gate itself, found choked from within and never afterward opened by any hand, said as much as anyone was ever going to know.

## IV. Of the Empty Places

The outpost was found empty. That was the plainer fact, set down by those few of the hill country and the northern moor who, years on, went near enough to look for themselves once the coast-people's watch-fires along that height had gone unlit for longer than any traveller could remember seeing them lit. No one came to the blocked gate to open it. No smoke rose from within the low wall on mornings when smoke had always risen there, cooking-smoke and, on cold nights, the glow of more than one hearth showing faint above the stone. The paths worn into the hillside by generations of coast-people climbing to and from the height went ungrazed by their own feet and began, within a few years, to green over at the edges the way any path does that stops being walked.

It had lain in that stillness, by every reckoning the hill country later made of it, longer than any watcher thought to measure at the time, for stillness of that kind does not announce the day it begins; a place is noticed to be empty only once it has been empty a while. Who had built the outpost, and for what watch it had first been raised, no teller of the hill country, then or later, could say with any more certainty than they had ever had, for the coast-people had never told their own reasons to outside ears, and the wall, low and worn and blocked at its one gate, gave up nothing more of its builders than the plain fact of its standing.

The shepherd who had first laid a hand on the blocked gate never went back to it, in his own lifetime or after; it fell to his grandson, grazing the same slopes a generation on and grown bolder than a grandfather's fright, to walk the full circuit of the outpost's low wall and see whether any other way lay open into it. None did. The wall ran unbroken save at the one gate, and even there a man could see, by standing on a fallen section at the wall's northern turn and looking over rather than through, that the ground within held nothing but a scatter of low stone footings, roof-beams long since rotted or burned to nothing, and grass grown up between the footings as tall and as undisturbed as grass grows anywhere no foot troubles it. He called out, more than once, in the plain speech of the moor, and had no answer but his own voice come back off the far wall. Whatever else the coast-people had been, whatever watch they had kept from that height for however many lifetimes, there was nothing left within the walls to say so beyond the footings and the grass and the one gate still choked from a closing no living eye had witnessed.

South of it, where the great fortress stood above the same three valleys' wider country, the same stillness came, in its own year, in the same unannounced way: fires that had burned along its walls for longer than living memory went dark one season and stayed dark, and no one now living, of any people, could afterward say they had watched the last of them go out. The fortress had held, in the years of its fullness, water enough drawn from its cistern to serve four hundred people through the driest summer the country knew, a great stone-lined pit sunk deep within the walls and fed, by some working of channels no outside eye had ever traced to its source, from a spring higher on the hill than the fortress walls.

It was a party of three, by the telling that came down of it, who went up to the fortress once its own fires had failed to show for the better part of a year: a trader of the northern moor who had once carried salt as far as the coast-people's landings and knew, better than most, how far a man might go before the coast-people's own patience ran out, and two younger kinsmen he brought along more for the company than for any need of their hands. They found the gate of the fortress standing open, unguarded and unblocked, which was itself a wonder to them after the tale of the outpost's choked door, and walked the length of the outer ward calling out as the shepherd's grandson had called out at the outpost, and got the same answer: none. It was the youngest of the three, wandering apart from the other two toward the fortress's inner court where the cistern was known, by old report, to lie, who came first to its stone lip and looked down, and it was his own voice, raised in a single short cry that carried the length of the ward and brought the other two running, that first told the other two what he had found. Into that cistern the water had once been fouled past any drinking: choked from side to side with the bones of cattle, whole carcasses and not the leavings of any butchering, thrown in, by every sign the three could read that day, all together and at once rather than over a season's slow accumulation, so that the water beneath them had long since gone thick and dark and would serve no mouth again. No one who found it could say who had done it, or why a people who had kept that water sweet for lives beyond counting should have fouled it in their own leaving, if leaving was what it had been, rather than have it fouled by some hand not their own; and the trader, who told the finding afterward at more hearths than he could later count, would say only, when pressed for more than the plain fact of it, that he had looked a long while at the bones before he could bring himself to turn from the cistern's lip and lead the two younger men back out through the open gate the way they had come. The hill country, having no answer to give and no wish to invent one where the coast-people's own silence had left none, told the finding plainly and let it stand: the cistern that had served four hundred, choked to the brim with the bones of cattle, and drawn from by no one, ever again.

What had gone wrong, in those years, in the high places the coast-people had held so long and so quietly, no teller of the hill country, near or far, ever claimed to know. The springs at Muileann ruadh went on running warm, and it was said, though less often as the years went by, that they went on being used, though by fewer hands each season that the tellers troubled to count. The eastern shaft at Toll-dubh stood open as it had always stood open, swallowing whatever stone a child still cared to drop into it, and giving back, as it had always given back, no sound of a landing. But the outpost's gate stayed choked with its own rubble, and the fortress cistern lay full of bone under water gone black, and between the one silence and the other the hill country's tellers, who had carried every other wonder of that people north with whatever plain wonder or plain pity the telling would bear, found for the first time that they had nothing left to carry but the emptiness itself, and told that, when they told it at all, exactly as they had found it: filling, now, with nothing but the rain.
