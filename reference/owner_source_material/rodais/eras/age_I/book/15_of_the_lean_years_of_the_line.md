# Of the Lean Years of the Line

*VE 1,951 – 2,097*

## I. Of the Kilns in the East

At the eastern landing the fires were never all lit at once and never all dark at once, for a household that dried its whole catch on the same night had nothing to eat the night the wind failed and the boats came in empty. So it had been kept since before anyone tending the pits that year had first crawled to them on hands and knees to feel the heat off the stones, and so it was kept still: three kilns fed while three stood cold and swept, the ash from the cold ones carried down to the shingle and given to the tide, the short flues drawing peat-smoke up through racks of split fish laid belly-down until the flesh went the colour of old rope and would keep a season without turning.

The one who minded the middle kiln that year had minded it, in one turn or another, for longer than the youngest of the household could remember any other hand at it, and had in that time worn a path in the turf between the drying racks and the door of her own house that no grass had troubled to grow back over. She judged the smoke by its smell more than by its colour, holding her face low over the flue's mouth at first light and again at dusk, and she judged the fish by the give of the flesh under one finger, and neither judgement had she ever put into words that anyone remembered, teaching it instead the way it had been taught her, by standing a child at her elbow through enough dryings that the child's own hand learned what her mother's had learned before it.

A day's rowing up the coast, along a shore that showed nothing but grey water and grey sky to anyone who did not already know the set of it, another household kept the same three kilns and three, the same racks and the same flues, built by whoever had first carried the way of it between the two landings, in a trade or a gift or a theft the telling of neither shore has ever kept. The two places knew of each other the way a household knows of a cousin's household two valleys off: seldom seen, never in doubt. A boat went between them now and again with dried fish traded for something the one place had and the other lacked, a bundle of sinew, a stone good for scraping hide, and came back before the light failed, for neither crew cared to be caught between the landings after dark on a coast where the current ran stronger than it looked.

The woman who minded the middle kiln had a granddaughter old enough that year to be trusted with the racks alone through a whole low tide, and it was this granddaughter, and no one older, who first noticed that the smoke off the middle kiln ran a shade paler than the smoke off the two beside it, some seasons, without the fire beneath it being fed any different. She said so to her grandmother, expecting to be told what it meant. Her grandmother looked at the flue a long while, and then at the girl, and said only that the smoke told her what the smoke told her, and set the girl to turning the racks, which was answer enough for a child who had asked the question mostly to be spoken to.

That same winter a boat came up from the south with word, passed on the way word travelled between the two landings, that a beast had come ashore dead below the southern kilns and been cut up for its oil, and the eastern household sent back, by the same boat when it turned, a share of dried fish it could ill spare that season, for the giving and the taking between the two places ran both ways over the years and no one there kept any closer account of it than that. The woman who minded the middle kiln put the fish into the returning boat with her own hands, and stood on the shingle watching it go until it was a shape and then nothing against the water, and went back to her racks without a word to anyone about how long she had stood there watching an empty stretch of sea.

## II. Of Gormal and the Kin That Went Down

Six hundred years, near enough as the reciters kept it, had gone by since Aodh stood to his knees in the northern sea and watched the gold coal ride the swell before a breaker took it under, and in all that span only the sea had answered. The gold burned in the tellers' mouths at every recital, bright as the night it was seen; the other six lay in the leather bag at Tobar dhìreach dark as any stone off any hillside, tried against the rain and the wind and the fire together and giving up nothing for the trying.

Gormal had grown up on the trying. She had heard the riddle said standing before she could say the fathers' names back whole herself, had turned the six dark coals over in her own hands as a child the way every child of the line had turned them, and had watched, as she grew, more of the kin lose patience with a question that answered no one in a life or in three lives together. By the time she was keeper of the fire-pot in her own right, past thirty and past the birth of the one daughter who lived, she had buried a father, an aunt and two of her own age who had gone to their graves saying the sky-coal would never light and the line was fools to keep hoping otherwise.

She had been a plain child, by her own later telling of herself, neither the quickest of the kin's daughters nor the slowest, and had come to the fire-pot only because the aunt who kept it before her had no daughter of her own living and had taken a liking to the girl who sat nearest the hearth without fidgeting through a whole recital. What Gormal had, that the aunt saw in her before anyone else troubled to look, was a memory that did not let a word go once it had taken it in, and a temper that would not let a thing drop merely because it had gone unanswered a long while. She learned the fathers' names from Neachdan down at nine years old, standing them over to the aunt every evening for a whole winter until not a name came out of its place, and she learned the riddle the same way, and asked, the first time she heard it whole, why no one had simply tried the thing from higher ground; and the aunt, who had heard every manner of fresh notion offered by children and travellers alike over a long keeping, only smiled at her and said that the hills of the high valleys had all been climbed already by hands with the same notion, and that Gormal would learn, given time, how much a hill could be climbed and still leave the question standing.

She would not let the recital go slack for that. It was Gormal's rule, said first to a gathering that had come more out of habit than hope, that the fathers' names and the riddle after them be said standing every dark-of-the-year night whether any soul under that roof cared to hear it or not, for a thing kept only while it was believed was not a thing kept at all, and a hearth-keeper who let the saying lapse one year would find it harder, not easier, to raise it again the year after. Some of the kin thought her stubborn for it. She let them think so.

Her own daughter, Sìomha, grown to a woman with children of her own by the time the branch made ready to leave, had more than once told her mother to her face that the young of the kin no longer sat still for the riddle the way they had once been made to, and that a keeper who wanted the words carried on after her would do better to soften them, to let a child leave the fire once the fathers' names were done rather than hold him through the riddle after, when half of them were asleep on their feet before the last line was out. Gormal heard her through and answered that a child who slept through the riddle at nine would wake to it at nineteen or at forty, the words being in him whether he had listened with his eyes open or shut, and that she herself had slept through the greater part of a good many recitals as a girl and could say every word of them back all the same. Sìomha did not trouble to argue the point a second time, though she went on saying it, in her own house, to her own children, in gentler words than her mother's.

It was in those same years, while the argument between mother and daughter went on being had and never quite settled, that word came up from the eastern slopes of a good harvest two years running, and then a third, and the branch that had been drifting down in ones and twos for two generations began drifting faster.

That branch was cousins three and four times removed from Gormal's own line, kin all the same by the reckoning that mattered at a hearth, and it had for two generations been walking down to the lower ground where the farms of the eastern slopes gave a man back more than the high valleys ever had for the same digging, the high valleys being stone under thin soil at the best of times and a wind off the moor for most months of the year. Twenty years into Gormal's keeping, the last households of that branch still on the high ground made ready to follow the rest down for good, and when they went they went with a coal from no one's hearth but their own daily fire, carrying not one splinter of the mason's vein with them.

Gormal walked out to meet them the morning they loaded the last of their gear onto two ox-sledges, and stood in the yard while the sledges were lashed, and said what she had come to say plainly, without asking them to stay, for she had asked that of others before and had learned it did no good.

"You go with no coal of the vein in your hand," she said. "In all the years the line has kept the bag, no household has gone from it empty before."

The man who answered her, a grandson of her own grandfather's brother, did not stop lashing the sledge while he spoke. "We have carried the dark coals up every hill within a day's walk of here, grandmother's kin, and thrown them off every one, and they have come back dark every time. What is there left in a stone that will not answer for six hundred years?"

"You have thrown them from every hill you could reach in a day," Gormal said. "You have not thrown them from the highest ground this island owns, and no two of you agree, even now, which ground that is."

That checked him, though only for a breath. "Then let whoever agrees on it climb it," he said. "We have farms waiting that do not care what the sky thinks of us." And he went back to the lashing, and within the hour the sledges were moving down the track east with the whole branch walking behind them, children and dogs and all, and none looked back at the high valleys they left, so far as anyone watching from Tobar dhìreach that morning could see.

Gormal did not let the matter drop merely because the branch had. She raised it at more than one recital after, standing at the fire with the leather bag in her lap and the coals laid out on a cloth before her, sky and earth and sea, and put to whoever would hear her the same thing she had put to the man at the sledges: that the sea had been thrown into by a man who had never before seen it, and had answered at once, on the very shore where he first laid eyes on it; that the sky, by the same reasoning that had served the sea, ought to be tried from the ground that stood nearest it, and that no ground on the whole island stood nearer the sky than the highest of it, wherever that proved to be. She named three hills she herself judged for the honour, one south of the black well, one west toward Achadh dhìreach, and the third, the one she came back to oftenest in her last years, a bare shoulder of moor she had walked to once as a young woman and never forgotten the wind of. The old men of the kin argued each for a different one of the three, and some for a fourth she had not named, and none of the arguing settled anything in her lifetime, for no one among them had climbed all of the candidates and come down able to say with a clear conscience which had stood highest under his own feet.

She kept the fire-pot and the bag until the year she died, in her seventy-fifth year, and on the last recital she stood for, her voice gone thin but the words unfaltering, she said the riddle as she had said it every year of her keeping and then added, which was not in the fixed words and was never repeated by the reciters after her exactly as she said it, that the line had thrown the coals from every hill it could be troubled to climb and had still not thrown from the one that mattered, and that whoever first agreed with his own eyes which hill that was would do the line more good in an afternoon than the branch that had gone down to the farms would do it in ten generations of easier digging. She died that winter in the house she had kept the fire-pot in her whole life, with Sìomha at one side of the bed and a granddaughter at the other, the fire fed and the leather bag within her reach though she no longer had the strength to lift it on her own. Near the end she asked to have the six dark coals laid out on the blanket where she could see them, and lay looking at them a long while without speaking. When Sìomha asked if she wanted the riddle said once more, she shook her head; she had said it enough times in her own life, she told her daughter, that saying it again over a dying woman's bed would only ease the living, not answer the sky. It needed saying to someone who still had years enough left to climb a hill in. The bag and the question passed, as the recital passed, to hands that had heard her say both enough times to say them back the same way, and the three hills she had named were argued over at more than one hearth for years after, without the argument once coming nearer an end than it had stood the day she named them.

## III. Of the Oyster Beds in the South

On the southern coast, where a long low headland ran out into water shallow enough that a child could wade past a man's height of it before the bottom dropped away, one shore had built its whole living on a shellfish so plentiful there that no other trade had ever taken firm root beside it. The oysters lay thick along the headland's whole seaward face, packed close enough in places that a rake drawn once through the shallows at low water came up heavier than two hands could easily carry, and generation on generation of the same households had done little else with their days than rake them, open them, eat what could be eaten fresh and dry what could be dried, and pile the empty shells where the tide would not soon take them back.

The pile they made of it, by the time anyone now living there could remember, ran the whole length of the headland and stood, at its oldest and thickest end, higher than a tall man could reach with his arm above his head, whiter at its crown where gulls had picked it clean and greyer with age the deeper a hand dug into it. Children of that shore were set, almost as soon as they could be trusted not to cut themselves on a broken edge, to carrying the day's empty shells up to whichever part of the mound was that season's dumping-place, and grew up thinking of the heap the way a hill-country child thought of the hill behind its own house, a thing that had simply always stood there and would go on standing after they themselves were gone from the shore.

The lowest layers of that mound, where the oldest shells lay, held oysters bigger by half than any taken from the same water in the memory of the raking households still living, broad as a spread hand and thick-shelled enough that opening one wanted a harder blow than a fresh catch ever needed. No one on that shore troubled over why the old shells ran larger than the new; the water gave what the water gave, in whatever size it chose to give it in a given generation, and a household that had raked the same beds for as long as any of them could reckon had learned not to ask questions of a tide that answered none of them. There was little else to that shore beyond the raking and the eating and the piling: no ramps for hulls, no kilns for drying fish, no mole against a current, for the water there ran shallow and gentle the whole year and gave up its harvest to a rake and a strong back with nothing else asked of anyone. A household that wanted a hull for deeper water went without rather than build one, for there was little enough deep water within reach of that shore to make the building worth the trouble, and a family that wanted dried fish traded a basket of oysters for it at whichever landing further along the coast had fish to spare, walking the trade there on its own feet rather than by any boat of its own.

The rake itself was the one tool that shore troubled to make with any care, a length of bent wood with a row of teeth lashed along its lower edge, each tooth shaped and fitted by whichever household's hands were freest that season, and a rake once well made was kept and mended rather than replaced, passed on at a death to whoever in the house had the surest arm for using it. A woman known along that shore for the truest rake anyone there could remember had made it as a girl under an aunt's eye and used no other through a life long enough to see her own granddaughters raking beside her with rakes of their own making, and when she died the rake went to the granddaughter judged strongest in the arm, who used it in her turn until it wanted mending past any mending and was laid, by a custom none of that shore explained to anyone who was not of it, on the mound rather than burned or thrown back to the sea.

An old woman of that shore, whose own hands had raked oysters from the same stretch of headland for longer than she herself cared to count, was known to sit some evenings on the crown of the oldest part of the mound with a broad shell from its lowest layer turned over in her lap, running a thumb along its ridged outer face the way another woman might run a thumb along the rim of a well-used bowl, and to say nothing at all about it to whichever grandchild had come to sit with her, for there was nothing in it that wanted saying beyond what the shell already said by its own size and weight, and a shore that had lived on the same harvest for longer than its own oldest teller could reckon had long since stopped feeling any need to say more about it than that.

## IV. Of the Second Landing

Far to the north-east, along a stretch of coast none of the shore's own tellers ever set beside the first without a pause, a household laid slabs of flat stone edge to edge down the shelving shore into deep water, wide enough for hulls greater than any hide boat, and walked hulls down them exactly as another household had done, generations before, on the northern coast far to the west, at a landing that bore, by whatever chance carried a name across so much land and water, the very same name as this one.

Whether the two households had ever been one people, split by some journey or some quarrel too old for either shore to keep in its own telling, or whether only the name had travelled between them, carried by a trader or a wanderer who had seen the first ramps and given the second place its word without ever giving it the people who built the first, no teller of either coast, then or after, was ever known to say, and none of the hill-folk who might otherwise have puzzled over it ever came near enough that shore to ask.

The work went as such work always went: a slab judged and levered and set, a length of green log laid and greased with fish-oil and left to season under a spread of brush until it would bear a hull's whole weight without splitting, a first small boat walked down the finished ramp on a still morning with half the household standing along the bank to watch it take the water clean, no different in the taking than the first hull had taken the water at the western landing so long before that not one soul now living at either place had any memory, or any telling handed down of a memory, of which ramp had gone into the water first.

The old man who judged the stones for this second set of ramps had learned the judging, as every hand at that work learned it, from another who had learned it before him, back through a line of judging hands that ran further than the household's own memory could reach, so that no one now at the second landing could have said whether the way of laying a ramp down into deep water had been carried whole from the first landing in one crossing, brought by some ancestor who had rowed or walked between the two in a single lifetime, or had grown up new at the second place out of the same sea and the same need working on hands that had never once seen the first ramps with their own eyes. He himself favoured neither telling over the other when a grandchild pressed him for one, saying only that a ramp that carried a hull was a ramp that carried one, wherever the knowing of it had first come from, and that a boy more interested in where a thing came from than in whether it worked would make a poor judge of stones.

By the second summer there were two ramps standing side by side where there had been one, and a household grown confident enough in the work to let its youngest hands set the smaller stones under the old ones' eyes rather than under their hands, and by the third there was a third ramp begun a little apart from the first two, for a household with hulls enough now to want more room than two ramps gave it. A boy of that household, sent one autumn morning to carry word along the bank that the tide had turned, is remembered by whoever first told this of him to have stood a long while at the water's edge before he ran his errand, looking at the two, and then the three, long pale ramps running down into the same grey water that had carried away, out of any memory the shore now kept, whatever hands had first thought to lay stone into the sea in that particular way; and he ran his errand at last, as he had been sent to, and said nothing to anyone of what he had stood looking at, for it was, to him as to every other soul on that coast, no more than the ramps his own household used every day of the boating season, twin to another set of ramps on a shore he had never seen and never would, and no cause, so far as that shore's own telling ever carried it, for a boy already late on his errand to stand and wonder over it any longer than he did.
