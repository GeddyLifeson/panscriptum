# Of the Sliver at Àth gharbh and the Name of the Line

*VE 4,475 – 4,671*

> *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.*
> We did not come; we were here.

*The Sixth Part: Of the Children of the Question, and the Seventh Coal*

## I. Of the Sliver at Àth gharbh

Barabal was five and thirty years old the spring she burned the sliver, and she had carried it in a fold of her own girdle, on and off, since she was a girl young enough to be given a bright stone for a plaything and old enough to remember, all her life after, exactly where she had been given it.

It had come to her the way small things come to children who are not watched every hour: a boy of the households nearest the crack, one of the many who ran errands between the high valleys and were fed at whatever hearth they stopped at, had shown it to her at a ford four miles from her own door, turning it in the light to make her see the colours run, and had then, tiring of her wonder or wanting to be free of his hands for the game they were playing, pressed it into hers and told her to keep it, as boys will give away in an idle hour what they would fight to keep the next. Where he had it from he did not say and she did not ask, being at that age more taken with the having of a bright thing than with the history of it, and by the time she was old enough to wonder where a stone with a rainbow shut inside it might have come from, the boy himself was long gone back to his own valley and grown, for all she knew, into a man who no longer remembered giving it. She kept it the way a woman keeps the small relics of her own childhood, in a fold of cloth at the bottom of a box, taken out now and again to be looked at and put away again, and told no one at Àth gharbh what it was, chiefly because she did not herself know.

She knew only the Telling, which every hearth of the valleys knew by then, of the mason and the fire out of the sky and the coloured stones that had gone into it and burned dark like any other stone; and she knew, because every child of that country was told it before it was told anything else worth telling, that the Mason's line kept a whole bag of such pieces yet, unburned, and turned them over every so often at the hearth on the night of the lightning, trying this shore and that height against a riddle none of them could answer. It had never once occurred to Barabal, in all the years she kept her own small piece folded away, that what she held was of a kind with what the line kept; it was to her a pretty stone a boy had given her at a ford, no more sacred than a good flint or a well-turned spindle-whorl, and if she thought of the Telling at all when she took it out to look at it, it was only the way a person thinks of any old story while handling any old thing, without troubling to draw the two together.

What put the drawing-together into her head, in the end, was nothing more than an idle evening and a low fire. She was five and thirty, married eleven years, mother by then of three living children and one buried, and it was the wet edge of a spring that had been hard on the peat-stacks; she had gone out in worse weather than she liked to cut what she could from a bank already half drowned, and had come in cold to the bone with her hands raw from the wet turf, and sat that evening turning the little bright stone in her fingers by the low fire the way she always did when her hands were tired and wanted something small to do. She had wondered, not for the first time, whether it would burn like any other stone if she put it in, and had never once tried it, out of some half-formed care for the pretty thing that she could not have named a reason for. That evening, with the peat smoking low and green from the wet bank and giving off more smoke than heat, she thought: it is only a stone, and stones do not care what is done to them, and set it, before she could talk herself out of it, on the coals at the fire's edge.

It did not vanish, and it did not shine. It caught the way any coal catches, slow at first and then all at once, a small black-flamed fire no bigger than a thumbnail, burning low and steady exactly as the peat around it burned, giving off the same dark smoke and the same middling heat, so that a person coming in from the yard and finding it burning there would have seen nothing in the fire worth a second look. Barabal watched it the whole while it burned, waiting for some other thing to happen, some flare of colour or some voice out of it such as the Telling gave to the mason's own finding, and none came; it burned down to a black cinder exactly as a lump of ordinary coal burns down, and went out, and by morning was cold ash mixed in with the rest of the hearth's ash, indistinguishable from it.

The hearthstone under where it had lain, though, had taken a stain through its whole thickness, a colour gone down into the grain of it the way water goes into a dry cloth, no soot-mark for a hand to rub away, faint gold at the stone's heart and paling to nothing at its edges. No scrubbing Barabal gave it in the weeks after ever brought the stone back to plain grey. She showed her husband, and her husband, who had no more patience for wonders than any man with peat to cut and cattle to see to, said only that it was a strange stone and she had best not go setting any more strange stones on his hearth without asking him first, and left it there. It was her husband's mother, an old woman who had outlived two households and remembered a good deal of the valleys' talk from further back than most, who said, turning the stained stone over in her own hands by the daylight of the door, that she had heard tell, once, long ago, from a teller who had it from a teller before her, that the Mason's line kept coloured stones just such as this, and that no one outside that line had ever, in any telling the old woman knew, been fool enough or curious enough to try one on an ordinary hearth.

Barabal did not think herself either a fool or unusually curious, only a tired woman with cold hands on a wet evening who had wanted to see what a pretty stone would do, and she said as much when word of the stained hearthstone began, as such things do in a country where every hearth knows every other hearth's business within a season, to travel beyond her own door. It travelled slowly at first, carried by whoever happened to stop at Àth gharbh and see the stone for themselves, and it travelled, in the main, as a curiosity rather than a wonder, a strange small thing that had happened to one household and might never happen to another, since no one else at Àth gharbh or the households near it had a piece of the coloured coal to try, nor any notion of where one might be got. It did not reach the line's own ears, so far as anyone ever afterward could say, for some years, and when at last a fire-bearer of the old carrying custom, passing through Àth gharbh on his way to a hearth further north, heard the tale told over a cup and carried it home with him among the ordinary gossip of the road, the line's own hearth at Tobar dhìreach took it, by every telling that survives of that day, with less astonishment than Barabal herself had felt at the first burning. The old woman who then kept the bag and the pot, whose name the recital does not give to strangers, is said only to have listened to the fire-bearer's telling of the stained stone through to its end, and to have said, when he had done, that a piece thin enough to fit in a boy's palm was thin enough to lose, and that she was neither surprised nor sorry that one had gone astray somewhere in the many years since the mason first filled his tunic with more than he could ever afterward account for.

Barabal lived seven and thirty years more after the burning, long enough to become, in her own valley, less the woman who had done a strange thing once than simply an old woman among her grandchildren, as every woman who lives long enough becomes; she died in her two-and-seventieth year, at her own hearth, with nothing more said over her going than is said over any grandmother's, and the stained stone, by then split at last by some later hand trying to see whether the colour ran all the way through, was kept afterward at Àth gharbh as a plain curiosity, shown to travellers who asked after it and to children who did not, for longer than any single teller's memory could afterward account for.

## II. Of the Stained Wool of Achadh dhìreach

Giorsal was born at Achadh dhìreach fifteen years after Barabal set her stone on the coals, in a household ten miles west of the crack that kept no fire-pot and counted no fathers back to any mason, and grew up, as every child of those hearths grew up, on the Telling and on the plainer, harder business of getting a living out of ground that gave up its crops grudgingly and its dead, in the way of all ground, without asking leave of anyone.

It was the dead that Giorsal came, in time, to know better than most women of her valley knew them, for she was the one her household turned to when a burial had to be seen to properly, the washing and the wrapping and the choosing of what went into the ground with a body and what stayed behind for the living to use, work that fell more often to the women whose own hands had steadied enough of their kin's dying to have lost their fear of the office. She had buried, by the time she was five and thirty, more of her own blood than she cared to count aloud, and a good many besides who were no blood of hers at all but had no one left of their own to see to them properly, and she had come, across all that burying, to a private habit that she never troubled to explain to anyone who did not ask, and that not many did.

She rubbed a little broken coal into the wool a body was wrapped in before the ground took it.

It was not a custom she had invented outright; there was, at Achadh dhìreach as at a scatter of other hearths along that stretch of the valleys, a scrap of broken coloured stone kept in a box much as Barabal's had been kept, come to that household by some road no living teller could any longer trace, whether traded for a good hide or given by a passing kinsman or simply picked up, once, by some forgotten hand, from a place it had no business lying. Giorsal's own grandmother had shown it to her as a girl, calling it only the bright stone and saying nothing of what it was, and Giorsal had grown up handling it the unremarkable way a child handles any small treasure kept in a household box, until the year she first stood over a grave with the wrapping in her hands and found herself thinking, without quite meaning to think it, that the dead ought to carry something of worth into the ground with them, and that a stone worth keeping in a box for three generations was as fit a thing as any to give.

She broke off no more than a sliver's dust each time, rubbed between finger and thumb into the coarse wool at the breast of the wrapping, where a mourner's own hand might rest a moment before the ground closed over it, and she did it seldom, by her own later account, from no rule she had ever set herself, only from the plainer fact that the stone was small and the dead of Achadh dhìreach were, in an ordinary year, more than it could ever have served had she rubbed it into every wrapping that passed through her hands. She judged, in some manner she never put fully into words even to the daughter she taught the office to after her, which graves wanted it and which did not, choosing perhaps one burial in thirty across the whole span of her working years, so that most households of that valley, asked afterward whether their own dead had gone into the ground with any of the coloured stone about them, could say only that they did not know, or that they supposed not, the office being Giorsal's to judge and not theirs to ask after.

The wool took the colour the way Barabal's hearthstone had taken it, a stain gone down through the fibre, no smear for time or damp to wear away, streaks of a hue that no plant Giorsal or anyone else of that valley had ever set to dyeing wool could match, gold in some threads and a deeper red-gold in others depending, it seemed to her, on nothing she could ever predict from one grave to the next. She never dug a wrapping up again to look at it, that being no fit thing to do to the dead for the sake of a woman's curiosity, and so she never knew, nor did any who came after her, whether the stain held in the ground the way it held in the moment of the rubbing, or faded, or spread, or did anything at all once the earth had closed over it; she knew only what her own eyes saw in the last moment before the closing, and she kept that knowledge to herself and to the daughter she taught, telling her only that the stone was a good thing to give the dead and that she would understand, in time, which graves asked for it, the same understanding having been given to her without much explaining by the grandmother before.

Word of Giorsal's habit travelled less far than word of Barabal's burning, partly because a wrapped body going into the ground offers less of a spectacle than a stained hearthstone shown at every passing traveller's asking, and partly because Giorsal herself, being a private woman about the office in every other particular, saw no reason to make more of this one particular than of the rest. It reached the ears of the line, when it reached them at all, by much the same slow road the tale of Barabal's stone had travelled, carried by a fire-bearer or a trader who happened to hear it told at some hearth along his way and thought it worth the telling further, and the line, by the time it heard, had already had some fifteen years to grow used to the plainer fact that its own long-kept treasure was not, after all, the only coloured stone the valleys held between them. Giorsal died in her seventieth year, at Achadh dhìreach, of a wasting that took the better part of a winter to finish its work, and was buried, her own daughter saw to it, with a sliver of the stone rubbed into her own wrapping in her turn, the office passing that once, as it must, from the hand that had always given it to the hand that had only ever received it before.

## III. Of the Bright Thing on the Midden at Bral

Far to the west, on the shore the coast-people called Bral, a woman going over the midden below her own household's huts in the low light of an autumn morning found, among the ordinary wrack of shell and burnt weed and the split bones of whatever the tide and the nets had lately given up, a small hard thing that was neither shell nor bone nor any stone she had ever turned up out of that heap before.

It caught the early sun the way wet slate never caught it, a gleam with more than one colour folded into it, and she turned it over in her fingers a good while, the way a person turns over any small unaccountable thing found where it has no business being, before she did with it what she did with most such finds: she carried it up to the huts and showed it to the others of her own household, who turned it over in their turn and were, by every account the shore afterward kept of that morning, no wiser for the turning than she had been. No one of that household could say how long it had lain among the midden's ordinary leavings before her own eye found it, whether it had come down with that season's shell and bone or lain buried some years already under layer on layer of the heap's slow growing, and no one troubled the question long, a midden being, by its nature, a place where things arrive by more roads than any one household can keep account of: by its own hands, by trade struck at some other landing and carried home, by a stranger's leavings washed up with the tide and taken for the household's own, or by some smaller, quieter road that no one at Bral that morning cared, or was able, to put a name to.

It was not burned. No one at Bral that season, or any season the shore's own tellers afterward reached back to, is known to have set it to any fire, cooking or otherwise; it was handled the way the coast-people handled any bright find that served no plain use, admired for a morning or two and then set aside among the household's other small keepings, a shell too fine to throw back, a bone worked smooth by the sea, a stone that caught the light. Whether it lay afterward in some box or niche of that household's own keeping, or was lost again in its turn to some later midden or some child's careless hand, no telling of the shore or the hill country either one ever afterward claimed to know; it passed, as most small bright things pass, out of the particular notice that had first found it remarkable and into the general keeping of a household that had a great many other things to attend to besides one stone among its trinkets.

The hill country never heard of it. Whatever the bright thing at Bral was, and however it had come there, by gift or by trade or by theft, it remained a thing the shore alone half-wondered at for a season and then let go, the way the shore let go of a great many small unaccountable things the sea and the land between them brought to its notice and never troubled to explain.

## IV. Of the Name Taken at the Gathering

By the year Colla came to the keeping of the bag, it was known across the whole breadth of the valleys about the crack, settled by then into the ordinary stock of things everyone knew rather than kept as any marvel, that the coloured coal did not belong to the Mason's line alone; that a stone had been burned once at a hearth of Àth gharbh and had stained the very rock it burned on; that a woman of Achadh dhìreach had, for a working lifetime, given slivers of it to the dead; and that here and there, at this hearth or that, some household could show a scrap of its own, come by gift or trade or simple finding, and could tell a version of the tale, more or less true to the first telling, of how it had come to them. None of it troubled the line's own long keeping in the smallest degree, the bag at Tobar dhìreach holding still, that year, the two coals that had never yet answered any trial given them, the fire coal and the last, the coal of every colour together that the reciters had taken, since Diarmad's own day, to calling the rainbow; but it had changed, all the same, something in the way the valleys spoke of the line, for a people who had once been the sole and singular keepers of an unaccountable wonder were by then one household among several that held a piece of the same wonder, distinguished from the rest chiefly by how much of it they held and how long they had gone on asking what it was for.

It was this, more than the riddle itself, that a wit of Àth gharbh named Oscar took up, half in jest, at the gathering that fell that year to a hearth two ridges from his own, on the night the valleys kept as the darkest, when every household that could reach another in an evening's walk came together to hear the fathers' names said standing and the old riddle taken up again and laid down again unanswered. Oscar was five and forty that winter, a man well known in his own valley and a good many neighbouring ones for a tongue that found the loose thread in any solemn thing and pulled it, gently enough that most who felt the pull laughed along rather than took offence, and he had, by his own later telling, no plan at all when he opened his mouth that night beyond the plain wish to say something that would make the hearth laugh before the cold walk home.

The recital had been said, the fathers named back through Eòghann and the fifteen whose names the line kept from strangers to the one then living at Tobar dhìreach, a man named Colla, five and thirty years old that winter and new enough to the keeping of the bag that more than one elder present remembered his father's own turn at the reciting better than they yet knew his. The riddle had been taken up after, as it always was, and laid down again, as it always was, for no one that night had brought forward any new trial to try against it, the sea and the sky and the earth and the blood and the river all having long since answered, and the two dark coals lying, as they had lain for longer than anyone at that gathering had been alive to remember otherwise, exactly as dark as the day the mason first burned their fellows.

It was in the loosening after, the crowd breaking into its own smaller talk the way any long-held silence loosens once the thing it was held for has been said, that Oscar, standing near enough the fire to be heard by a good part of the gathering without troubling to raise his voice, remarked to whoever was near him that the line ought by rights to be given a better name than the mason's children, seeing as how the mason's own trade of splitting stone had gone clean out of the family more generations back than anyone could count and been replaced by nothing so much as the steady, unhurried business of asking the same question of the same two coals and getting, every time, the same silence back. He called them, that night, for the first time that any teller after ever troubled to fix a year to, Luchd-ceasnachaidh a' Ghuail Dhìomhair, the long questioners of the secret coal, saying it slow and a little grand, the way a man says a thing he means partly to be funny and partly, though he might not have owned to it that night, to be true.

The hearth laughed, as Oscar had meant it to, and a good part of the gathering with it, the name being long enough and grand enough set against so plain and patient a business to strike most ears there as exactly the joke Oscar intended; but it did not die with the laughing, the way most jests of a cold night die by morning, for a name that fits a thing, however it is first meant, has a way of being said again by mouths that never heard the first saying of it, carried the same slow road any word travels in a country of scattered hearths, from this fire to that one, until it no longer belongs to the man who first shaped it. Colla himself, asked some months after by a traveller passing through Tobar dhìreach what his people called themselves, is said to have given the long name back without any of the laughter that had first carried it, adding only that the short form most mouths found easier was Clann na Ceiste, the children of the question, and that either would serve, his own line having asked one question, in one form or another, for longer than it had done any other single thing worth the naming.

He was five and sixty when he died, having kept the bag some thirty years by then and handed the keeping on before the end to a son whose name, like every father's before and after him along that stretch of the line, the reciters kept from strangers; and it was under his name, said standing at the recital the winter after his death, that the older name of the line, the mason's children, was heard for what the tellers afterward reckoned the last time spoken as the line's own name rather than as an old thing remembered. The households of the valleys had, by then, taken to calling the line simply Clann na Ceiste in their ordinary talk, the way a name settles into use long before anyone troubles to mark the day it settled, and the reciters, who kept the older forms alongside the new the way they kept every name the line had ever answered to, said both that winter at the gathering, the mason's children and the children of the question together, before letting the newer name stand alone in every recital after.

Rathnait's own word for the whole of the valleys, spoken first at a gathering not so many years before Oscar's jest and settled by then into as ordinary a use as any name the hearths had ever taken up, was said that same night alongside the line's new name, the godkin and the children of the question both, two names taken up within one generation of each other, the one by the valleys and the other by the line, which shared, that winter, the one gathering and the one fire and, though neither troubled to remark on it, the one long habit of the valleys: that a name tried once at a cold hearth, if it fitted the thing it was given to, outlived the fire that first warmed it into being said, and was carried on, from mouth to mouth and gathering to gathering, long after the man or woman who first shaped it had gone, in their own turn, into the ground.
