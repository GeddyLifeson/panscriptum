# Of the Mole and the Steps

*VE 951 – 1,169*

## I. Of the Mole at Skell

On the first day of the warm month the old man walked the bank of the river at Skell from first light, looking at nothing anyone else would have troubled to look at, and by mid-morning he had chosen his stone.

He had judged stones on that bank for longer than anyone at Skell could remember him not judging them, walking the shingle at every fall of the water with his hands loose at his sides, turning one boulder with his foot and letting the next lie, so that the younger men who worked at his word had long since stopped asking what marked the ones he chose from the ones he left. It was not size alone, for he passed by boulders bigger than the one he took that morning, and it was not shape alone, for the one he took was no rounder or flatter than a dozen others within reach of it; it lay, when he had finished with it, in the current's edge with its broadest face turned upstream and its weight sitting low and sure against the drag of the water, and that was the whole of what could be said of his judging by anyone who watched him do it and never learned to do it himself.

They rolled it down to the water's edge on green logs cut for the purpose, six men to a stone that size, with the old man walking beside it the whole way and saying nothing until it came to rest where he wanted it, at which point he would put out one hand, flat, and the log-bearers would know to stop, and the stone would go into the current from there, walked the last length on its own logs until it dropped with a sound that carried up the bank and settled into the silt the river had already given it. Then the old man would stand in the shallows with the water at his shins, turning his head one way and then the other at the stone half-sunk before him, and either nod, once, and send for the next, or shake his head and set them to shifting the one already placed a hand's width this way or that, which was harder work by far than the placing of it, for a stone once let go by the current did not want to be moved again.

It went on so through the whole of that warm season and into the wet one after it, boulder after boulder rolled and set and sometimes shifted, walked out from the bank a little further with every stone that held, so that by the time the leaves turned there was a low ridge of rock standing above the water even at the flood, running out from the bank at a slant the old man had set on the first day and never once changed, though he changed everything else about the work as it went. Boats that had beached on open shingle, dragged up clear of the water by main strength at every landing and dragged down again at every setting-out, began instead to come in under the lee of the stone and lie there quiet while the current went past outside it, and by the second winter there were boats enough sheltering there of an evening, a dozen at a time on a bad night, that the households along that stretch of bank had stopped thinking of the beach below their houses as open water at all.

The wet season after the first stones were set brought a flood the like of which the old man said he had not seen at Skell in all his years on that bank, the river coming up brown and fast and carrying whole trees down from higher ground, and the boats that had not been hauled well clear of it went where the water took them, out into the main current and away downstream past where any of them could follow on foot before dark. One boat went that flood, a small one, with nothing worse lost than the boat itself and the fishing gear in it, for the man who owned it had had the sense to leave it rather than go after it into that water; but the mole itself, the low ridge of stone the old man had been building through two seasons, took the flood's whole weight against its upstream face and held, and when the water fell back within its banks the ridge stood there still, scoured cleaner than before and not a stone of it shifted from where he had set it.

After that flood the old man was not troubled for stones. Households that had watched the work from a distance, minding their own boats and their own beaches, came now and asked to have their landings brought under the same lee, and he took them at their word and set them to the rolling and the placing exactly as he had taught the first of them, walking beside every stone still, right hand out flat when it had come far enough, until the ridge that had begun as one man's slow work along a hundred paces of bank had become, by the years his own grandsons were grown men rolling stones themselves, a long low wall of unmortared boulders reaching further out into the river than any one household could have built in a lifetime working alone.

He did not live to see it reach as far as it would go. What he did live to see, in the last summers when his legs no longer let him walk the bank at first light but only sit above it and watch others do the walking, was a whole season's boats come in of an evening one after another under the lee of the stone he had first set with his own hands, riding easy in water that would have driven every one of them onto open shingle in his own father's time, and it is told that he sat above the river on those evenings and counted the boats coming in the way another man might count anything else worth the counting, though what he made of the count, or whether he made anything of it at all beyond the pleasure of watching it grow, the telling does not carry, for it went with him below the rise where the households of that shore keep what is theirs alone.

There was a boy among the old man's grandsons who took to the water in those years the way some children take to it and others never do, and who is remembered, by whoever it was that first told this of him, for standing chest-deep at the mole's outer edge on the calmest days of high summer with his feet braced against the last stone set, letting the current push at him and push at him without once giving ground, as though the stone under his feet had lent him some part of its own stubbornness against the river. His grandfather, watching from the bank with the patience of a man who had spent a lifetime watching water move against stone, said nothing to him about it either way, neither calling him in nor praising him for staying out, and the boy grew to be one of those who rolled the furthest stones in the old man's last working years, walking beside them exactly as he had watched his grandfather walk, right hand ready to go up flat the moment a stone had come far enough.

The foot of the mole he set that first warm month lies under the water still at the lowest fall of the river, and every landing built out from that bank in all the years since has stood, in the end, on stone he chose and placed with his own hand and eye, though none now living could point to which stones were his and which were set by the hands that came after him, for a wall of unmortared boulders holding back a river gives up that kind of memory the moment the current has run over it a single winter.

## II. Of the Steps at Bral

On the far side of the island, where the land fell to the sea in a cliff of raw rock rather than a shore of shingle, a household that kept its beasts on the grazing above the water had for longer than anyone could reckon gone down to the shingle by whatever crack and ledge a man's feet could be trusted on, a scramble that wanted both hands free and was not to be tried at all after rain, when the rock ran wet and the safest holds turned treacherous under a boot.

It was a woman of that household, in a year no one troubled to fix, who first set her shoulder to a slab loosened already by frost and worked it, over the better part of a season, into a step at the foot of the worst of the scramble, wedging it level with smaller stones packed in beneath it until it took her whole weight without rocking. She had no thought, that first year, of doing more than the one step, for the one step alone saved the worst stretch of the climb, the stretch where a slip meant the shingle thirty feet below rather than a bruised hip on the rock; but a step once cut asks, in its own way, for the step above it, and by the time her own daughter was old enough to carry a bag of shellfish up from the shore unaided, there were a dozen slabs set one above another where before there had been only the crack and the ledge.

The work went on as such work goes on among people who keep no count of years, in the odd hour between the beasts and the shore, in the slack season when the grazing wanted less watching, a slab worked loose and dressed rough with a harder stone and set and packed and tried under a full load before the next was begun. Some years saw three steps cut, some years none at all, for the stone did not always give a slab where a slab was wanted, and a household with sickness in it or a hard winter behind it had neither the hands nor the heart to spare for cutting stone that would still be there, unset, the following year. No one who worked at it ever spoke, so far as the telling carries, of finishing the climb entire; each worked the stretch in front of them, the stretch that made the going hardest that season, and left the stretch above or below to whoever came to it next with strength and time enough.

By the time the third generation of that household was grown, the steps ran unbroken from the shingle at the cliff's foot to a shelf perhaps a third of the way up, wide enough there that two could pass without either turning sideways, and the scramble that had once wanted both hands free and a clear head began, above that shelf, all over again. A boy of that generation, sent down for shellfish on a morning when the tide was right and the household short-handed, is remembered to have run the whole finished stretch without once setting a hand to the rock, down and up again both, laughing at the top of it in a way that made the older women watching from above laugh with him, for none of them, not the woman who had cut the first slab nor any who came after her, had ever gone down that stretch without at least one hand out for balance until that morning.

The steps above the shelf took longer than the steps below it, for the rock there ran harder and gave up fewer slabs willing to be freed by frost or fire-cracking, and more than one worker of that household spent a whole season on a single stretch of a dozen feet that yielded, in the end, three steps and no more. It is told that when the full climb was at last finished, two hundred and twelve steps from the shingle to the grazing above, cut by hands that had never once all worked together in the same season, the household that had begun it was three generations removed from the woman who set the first slab, and not one of the three generations between had known her, though every one of them had grown up going down to the shore and up again by steps she had begun and none of them had finished.

The treads of the lower steps, the ones cut soonest and used longest, wore hollow in their middles long before the upper steps had worn at all, a shallow dish worn by bare feet and sandalled feet alike, by the young going down light and the old coming up slow with a full bag, by beasts driven down once in a while to graze the weed left uncovered at a low spring tide and driven up again bawling at every unfamiliar step under their hooves. No one now living could say how many feet, over how many years, it had taken to wear stone that hard into a hollow a thumb's width deep, and none of that shore, so far as the telling carries, ever tried to reckon it; the steps were there, and were climbed, and wore as anything wears that is used every day by every hand and hoof a household has, and that was the whole of what needed saying about them.

An old woman of that household, late in the years the steps took to finish, is remembered to have sat some evenings on the shelf a third of the way up, where two could pass without turning, and to have run her hand along the edge of the topmost step then cut, the one nearest the unfinished stretch above it, the way another woman might run a hand along the rim of a pot she had shaped herself. She had not cut that step, nor any near it, her own working years being long behind her by the time the climb reached that height, but she had carried stone up to whoever did cut it, in her turn, as her own mother had carried stone before her, and she is remembered to have said, of the unfinished rock above, only that it would come when it came, in the tone of a woman who had learned, over a long life on that cliff, not to trouble herself over work that was not hers to finish.

## III. Of the Recital Kept Idle

At the hearth of Clann a' Chlachair the fire that had once broken living out of the rock burned now, as it had burned since before any of the fathers now spoken could remember otherwise, in a pit dug and stone-ringed and fed with heather and split wood the way any hearth on the high valleys was fed, no different to look at than the hearth of any household a day's walk off, save for the clay pot set always at its western edge and the leather bag kept always within reach of whoever tended it.

The recital that Diarmad had fixed, standing on Oidhche an Dealanaich with the fathers' names running back through him to Neachdan who had broken the vein, was said now by hands that had never known Diarmad living, taught mouth to mouth from a woman of his line to a granddaughter of hers, and from that granddaughter on again, each keeper holding the words as she had been given them and giving them on unaltered, for a word changed in the telling, however small, was held to be a word lost, and no keeper of that hearth wished to be remembered as the one under whose watch a word was lost. It was said standing, every year, on the dark-of-the-year night, to whatever household had gathered for it that season, and it was said also, more quietly, to a child of the line old enough to hold still through the whole of it, the first of many such tellings before that child grew old enough to say it back whole and unbroken.

Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich that had come with Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, the sky and the earth and the sea and the three spirits that must somehow be brought together, was said too, every year, in its place after the recital of the fathers, but it was said now the way a thing is said that has been tried and has failed and is not, for the present, being tried again. In Diarmad's own day, and after him in his daughter's, the coals had been carried to a rain that would not stop falling, sunk in sea-water fetched home in skins from a shore three days off, set together seven at once in the hottest fire the line's hands could build, and had come out of every trial the same dull stones they had gone in, no brighter and no darker, giving up nothing of what Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich asked of them. After the third such failure, or the fourth, the telling does not keep the count exact, the trying was let rest, the way any hard work is let rest when the hands that might do it have other work in front of them that wants doing first, a hearth to keep fed and children to raise and beasts to see through a winter; and by the years the mole was rising at Skell and the steps were being cut at Bral, the leather bag of coloured coal hung at the hearth of Clann a' Chlachair much as it had hung for two generations already, opened sometimes for a child to turn the pieces over in wonder at their colours and closed again after, tried against nothing.

It was not that the line had given up Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, for no keeper of that hearth, asked outright, would have said so; it was only that Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich had become, in those years, a thing that was kept rather than a thing that was worked at, said each year in its proper place the way the fathers' names were said in theirs, waiting on whichever of the line's children might yet take it up again with a strength or a notion the ones before had lacked. More than one traveller passing through the high valleys in those years heard the recital said at that hearth and asked, afterward, whether anyone had tried this or that against the coals, some new way that had occurred to the traveller on the spot, a fresh idea offered in good faith by someone with no stake in the failures already behind it; and the keeper of the hearth, whoever she was in that year, would hear the traveller out and thank him for it and set the bag away again unopened, for a household that has tried the sea and the rain and the fire together and failed at all three has little patience left, most years, for a stranger's fresh idea got in an afternoon.

So the recital was kept whole, word for word, said standing every dark-of-the-year night whether the household that heard it that year troubled to ask for it or not, for by then the saying of it had become, in its own right, a thing the line did because it had always been done, in much the way a mole of stone is added to because a mole of stone has always been added to, and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich rode along inside it unanswered, carried forward from keeper to keeper the way the recital's own words were carried, waiting on a hand that had not yet been born.

In the year the crossing to Bral's higher shelf was first made without a hand out for balance, though the hearth of Clann a' Chlachair knew nothing of that crossing and never would, the recital was said, as it was said every year, to a gathering that had come that season without being sent for, walking up from the lower valleys of their own will on the strength of a saying passed hearth to hearth that the night was near; and the keeper of that year, a granddaughter's granddaughter of the woman who had first fixed the words in her own memory, stood and said the fathers' names from Neachdan down to her own father without once being asked to begin, for by then no asking was wanted, the gathering's own silence being asking enough. She said Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich after, as she said it every year, and closed the bag again after, as it was closed every year, and if she felt any want in herself to be the one who opened it in earnest rather than merely kept it, the telling that survives her does not carry so much as a hint of it.

## IV. Of the Dead of Vell

On the low headland in the island's south-east, where the shore ran flat enough for a boat to be launched at any state of the tide, a household kept its own dead the way it kept everything else it valued: apart, and facing the water.

When one of that household died, whether old at the work or young to a fever that came up sudden from the marsh air, the body was washed and wrapped in the household's own way, which the telling does not follow past the wrapping, and carried down to the shore on a litter of poles, and set into the broadest of the hide boats with two rowers to take it out. The crossing to the low island off that coast took less than an hour's pull in calm water, and it was never made light of even so; the rowers went out at first light, before the day's other work began, and came back before the sun was high, and no household of that shore, so far as anyone now can say, ever sent its dead across in bad weather, waiting instead however many days the sea gave them no choice but to wait, the body kept meanwhile in a place apart from the household's own hearth-fire.

On the island the graves were boxes of set stone, four slabs and a lid, raised a little above the ground and packed round with rubble so that the whole would stand against weather and time both, each one built by the household of whoever it was to hold before that person died, if there was time for it, or built in haste after if there was not, by whichever hands could be spared from the rowing. Every box, without exception, so far as the telling carries, was set to face the same way: not toward the open sea beyond the island, where a dead man's household might have thought to send him on to wherever the drowned or the departed were held to go, but back across the strait toward the mainland shore, toward the low headland and the shore and, beyond it, whatever fires still burned in the households that had rowed the boxes' occupants out.

Why the dead of that shore were made to look back rather than out, no teller of that coast, in all the years the telling ran, is remembered to have said, and none of the mainland's own tellers, who came no nearer that shore than any hill-folk came to any of Seann-Dhaoine' places, ever learned enough of the matter to guess at it either. It was simply the way it was done: a box was built, and a box was set, and the lid that closed it faced always toward the mainland and never toward the open water, whatever the last months of the person inside it had been spent looking at while they lived.

The rowing itself fell, in most households of that shore, to whichever two were fittest for it in a given season, and it was not held to be grim work, or at least not spoken of as grim work by anyone the telling remembers, though a woman who had rowed her own mother out one autumn and her own daughter out the spring after is said to have gone to the boat, after that second crossing, with a face that told those who watched her go that this was not, for her, a labour like any other, whatever it might have been for a rower sent out with someone else's dead. She rowed it all the same, for the household wanted two hands at the oars and hers were among the fittest still, and came back before noon as every crossing came back before noon, and took up the day's other work as though the morning's had been no different from any other morning's, which the telling holds to have been, for her, the greater part of what there was to admire in her.

The stone for each box came from the island where the ground gave it up, or was carried across in the same boats that carried the dead when the island's own rock ran short, a labour spread across whatever days the household could spare from its other work, so that a box begun the week of a death was not always finished the week after, and more than one family of that shore is remembered to have gone back and forth across the strait a dozen times over a season, ferrying slabs one or two at a time, before the four walls and the lid stood true over the one they were meant to close in. The lid itself was always the last stone set, and always the heaviest, wanting every hand the crossing boat could carry along with it, and it is told that no household of that shore ever let a grave stand open through a night, whatever the state of the work, choosing rather to lay a covering of brush and stone across an unfinished box until the true lid could be brought than to leave the dead exposed to weather even for the span of a single dark.

The island filled slowly, box by box, over more lifetimes than any one household on that shore could reckon by its own memory alone, each new box set a little further along the low ground than the one before it, following the same face-the-mainland rule without deviation, so that a person walking the length of the island, had any hill-folk ever gone there to walk it, which none ever did, would have found row on row of stone boxes all looking the same way, at the same low headland, out of which every one of their occupants had once been rowed. The households of that shore, so far as anyone now can say, kept some rough sense among themselves of how many boxes stood there, though the telling that survives them does not say by what means they kept it, whether by a mark cut somewhere or simply by the kind of memory a small close-knit shore keeps of its own dead without needing to write any of it down; and it is told, of the year the crossing that closed out these years came, that the box set that season was counted, by whatever reckoning the household used, the fortieth to stand on that low ground, its lid laid and its face turned, like every lid before it, back toward the water it had been rowed across and the shore its household had never once let it out of sight of.
