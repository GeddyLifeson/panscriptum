# Of the Steersman

*VE 2,801 – 2,860*

## I. Of the Word of the West

On the north-west shore, where the household hearths sat back from the water among stunted thorn and the wind never quite stopped, they kept Aithris a' Chruthachaidh the same as the high valleys kept it, but they kept it differently too. The recital of the Line went out to them father by father, the way it went out to every hearth on the island, and they knew it as well as anyone who had never seen Tobar dhìreach: the sky and the earth and the sea, each with its own spirit, and the coal that would answer only its own element. A shore household hears a telling with the sea at its back, though, and what it takes from the telling first is neither the sky's part nor the earth's. Sky was the sea's business one day in a hundred, when the weather turned; earth was under the feet and gave the peat and little else in that stony ground. The sea was at the door every day of a life, grey most mornings, green when it wanted to be kind, white when it did not, and the shore hearths listened hardest to the part of Aithris a' Chruthachaidh that spoke of what lay past it.

That part did not come from Coimhdeach na Fine's own mouth, or none said so. It came, the old women said, from further back than the mason, from an Dia-shluagh's own children before there was fire at all, and it had ridden into Aithris a' Chruthachaidh the way a stray dog rides in among a flock and is let stay because no one has the heart to drive it out. It said that west over the open sea, past the edge of sight and further than any hide boat had gone, there lay a green land that had no equal on Dia-thìr: Tìr nan Òg, and Magh Meall beside it, Magh Meall, where nothing grew old and nothing grew sick and the fruit hung ready in every season at once. And further off again, to the south-west, on a rock the sea went round and round without ever quite reaching, stood Taigh Dhuinn, the house of the lord of the dead, where those who did die on Dia-thìr went over water at the last, whether they had ever set foot in a boat in their lives or not.

Bran was born into that hearth in a year the household counted, later, as a year of good fishing, though he did not remember it, only that his grandmother spat into the fire for luck whenever the year was named. He grew up with the sea a stone's throw from the door and was in a coracle before he could say his own name straight, sitting in his father's lap with both small hands round the withy frame, howling with delight at every dip of it. His father, Fearadhach, was himself a fisher of the middling sort, careful rather than bold, and it was a puzzle to him all his life where the boy's restlessness came from, since nothing in his own blood or his wife's had ever looked past the next day's catch.

By the time Bran was a man grown he was the best boatman on that whole stretch of coast, and everyone on it, kin and stranger alike, said so without being asked. He could read a swell before it broke and tell, by the particular grey of the sky to the west at evening, whether the next day would let a boat out or keep it ashore, and he was right about it far oftener than he was wrong. He took seal and fish for his own hearth and for three others besides, and he mended the hide of every boat on that shore that needed mending, because his stitches held where other men's let the sea in. Neighbours brought him boats from half a day's walk along the coast rather than trust their own kin's needle, and he never once asked payment beyond a share of what the mended boat brought home after. Of an evening, when the tide had turned and there was nothing left to do at the water, he would sit with the other boatmen of that shore mending net or line by the last light, and the talk there was of seal grounds and the run of the mackerel and which headland kept a boat dry in a north wind and which did not, talk that filled a whole life for most of the men who traded it. Bran traded it as freely as any of them. It was only afterward, alone or with his mother, that the other listening began.

Such a man did not sit easily at the fire once the meat was eaten, the way some boatmen were content to, glad of the work and glad to let the rest of the world alone. Bran listened instead. He listened to Aithris a' Chruthachaidh every time it was said, the same words he had heard a hundred times, the way another man might listen for the note in a bowstring that told him it would break before it broke. What he wanted he could not himself have said clearly, only that when the recital came round to the part about the land past the sea's edge he sat forward with his hands gone still in his lap, and his mother used to say, once the matter had stopped being a thing said lightly, that even as a small boy he had looked west at the closing light with an attention no other child on that shore gave to it.

Once, when he was near ten, she found him standing at the tideline in the last grey of an evening, long after he should have been called in, and asked him what he thought he was looking at out there with nothing to see. "The place where it stops being water and starts being something else," he told her, and would not say more, and she did not press him, taking it for a child's fancy that would wear off with the years the way most fancies did. It did not wear off. "It is only a telling," his father told him once, when Bran was near a man and had asked, for the third or fourth time, whether anyone now living had ever gone out past the point where the swell ran long and slow and the gulls thinned to nothing. "The sea is the sea out there the same as it is here, only colder and longer, and a man who goes into it far enough does not come back to say what he found. The telling means no more than that. It is a way of saying: don't."

Bran said nothing to that. He did not forget it either, though, and it never once did the work his father had meant it to do.

## II. Of the Boat He Stitched

He was six-and-twenty, unmarried still though not for want of asking, when he began to gather hides that were not for mending. At first he told no one what they were for, and his kin took it, for a season, as a boatman's habit: a man who works hides gathers hides, and no one thinks to ask why a net-mender keeps cord by him. Only the toughest bull-hides would do, cured longer than any hide he had cured before, worked soft with fat and smoke until they would bend without cracking and hold water without weeping. He cut ribs of bent hazel and ash, more of them and set closer than any boat on that shore carried, and lashed each joint with sinew twisted so tight it sang when he plucked it, the way a good bowstring sings.

He worked through two winters at it in the lee of the house, out of the worst of the wind, most evenings after the day's fishing was done and most of the dark months when the sea kept him ashore anyway. Neighbours came by at first out of no more than a boatman's interest in another boatman's frame, and stayed longer than they meant to, turning the ribs over with their hands and asking, at first idly and then less idly, what water he meant such a hull for. He gave no straight answer for a long while, only that a bigger boat could carry a bigger catch, which was true enough as far as it went and satisfied most of them. By the second winter it satisfied fewer, for no man building only for a bigger catch builds a bow so high, or decks half the length of his hull with skins stretched and sealed against green water, or sets his ribs closer than three fingers' width apart when a hand's width has always served.

By the second spring the thing standing on its trestles in the yard was no coracle and no boat any of his kin had a name for: broad in the beam, high enough at the bow to climb a real sea rather than ride over a river chop, built, though none but Bran yet said so aloud, for water that meant to fight back. His mother asked him straight out, at last, what he meant to do with a boat built for more sea than any man on that coast had ever needed to cross, and he told her straight back.

"I mean to go and see if Aithris a' Chruthachaidh is true."

She did not weep, or not where he could see it. She had half known, she said afterward, since he was the boy who stood too long at the tideline in the failing light. What she said to him that evening, standing at the door of the boat-yard with her arms folded against the wind, was only this: that Aithris a' Chruthachaidh never once said a living man had gone and come back to say what he found, and that a telling with no one to bring it home was not a telling to build a boat on.

"Then I will bring it home," he said, "and it will be a telling with someone to bring it, after."

He found his crew easier than he had looked to, which surprised even him. Four men came, none of them fools, all of them known on that coast for steady hands: two who had fished with him from boyhood and trusted his eye on a swell over their own judgement of it; one older man, a net-mender of that shore, who had buried a wife the year before and said, when asked why he would go, only that there was less now to keep him ashore than there once had been; and last a boy not yet a man, not seventeen, whose name the tellers who kept the hushed part of this telling never gave, saying only that he was Bran's sister's son and had begged so long and so hard to come that his mother let him go at last rather than have him hate her for keeping him. Blàthnaid was that sister, three years the younger, and she alone of the whole kin neither wept over the boat nor argued against it. She asked her brother only one thing, the night before he put out: to tell her the truth of what he found, whatever it proved to be, if ever the chance came to tell her anything at all. He promised her that. It was the promise she held to for the rest of her long life, though there was soon no one left to ask it of.

They put out on a morning in high summer when the sea lay flat and green almost to the edge of sight and the wind, such as there was, sat steady out of the north-east, which Bran judged would carry them long and even without driving them off whatever line he meant to hold. Out past the last skerries to the north the sky carried the grey bank that never lifted off that stretch of sea in any season, the same grey that every boatman on the coast knew to leave well alone; Bran held his bow south of it from the first hour, west and a little south, away from the grey and into open blue water where no boat of that coast had held a course before. He stood the whole of that first morning at the steering-oar himself and let no other hand take it, watching the grey bank fall away astern on the right hand until it was only a smudge and then nothing at all, and the household came down to the shingle to see the five of them off. No one sang, for there was no telling yet of what a voyage like this one ought to be sung with, and the tellers say the silence of that leaving was remembered longer on that shore than any of the words that were said in it.

## III. Of the Boy Who Came Back

What passed on the water in the days that followed, no one on Dia-thìr can tell with a whole mouth, for only one of the five that went out ever stood on the shingle of that coast again, and he was past whole telling by the time he stood there.

He was found near the end of the same summer, a season and a little more after the boat put out, lying below the tide line on a stretch of coast two days' walk south of where he had left, as though the sea itself had carried him round and set him down gently at a place he had never asked to be brought. A woman gathering shellfish found him and thought him drowned until she saw his chest move. He was burned dark by a sun harder than any that fell on that coast, thin as a stripped willow-wand, and his mouth was too cracked to make more than a whisper for the better part of a day after they carried him in and gave him water a little at a time, as a man returning from a long fast must be given it, for fear the giving itself would kill him.

When the whisper came, it came in pieces, and the pieces did not always agree with one another, which the tellers say is only what is to be looked for of a boy half out of his wits with thirst and grief both. He said they had sailed many days, more than he had kept count of, west and a little south as Bran held the course, with nothing before them but water going out flat to a rim that never came any nearer for all they sailed toward it. He said the food ran thinner than they had reckoned and the water thinner still, and that there were whole days none of them spoke, because a man's mouth was too dry to waste on anything but the oar. Then, he said, his voice dropping so low that those who sat by him had to lean in close to catch it, as though he were telling something he half believed himself and half thought he must have dreamed in his thirst, they came on a land.

It rose green out of the water on a morning when none of them had strength left to hope for it, he said. It was a green such as none of them had ever seen on any hill of Dia-thìr, a green that seemed to hold its own light apart from the sun rather than only catching what the sun gave it. There was fruit on the trees down almost to the tideline, and a spring of sweet water broke out of the rock not a stone's throw from where they beached the boat, and they drank until their bellies ached and slept where they fell and woke with strength in them they had not carried since they left home. He said the people of that land came down to them without fear, neither young nor old to look at, none of them marked by time the way every face on Dia-thìr was marked by it, and that they were made welcome as though welcome cost that land nothing at all, because there was more of everything there than anyone could use in a life, and more life, it seemed, than any of them had ever thought to ask for.

He said his uncle would not leave.

That one thing at least, the tellers say, had stayed whole in the boy's mind through everything else the sea had done to it, and he told it plainly where the rest of his telling wandered. Bran had stood on that shore, he said, and looked back once at the boat drawn up on the sand, and told the boy he was free to go home if home was what he wanted, and that no one would think the less of him for wanting it. He himself had followed a question the whole length of his life to get to that shore, Bran told him, and he would not turn from it now that his feet were standing on the answer.

Of the other three men nothing certain is told. Some say they stayed beside Bran of their own will; some that the sea took them on the way home and only the boy, youngest and lightest of the five, lived to be carried the whole way back on whatever swell or storm set him down again on his own coast. The boy himself could not say which was true, and would not guess at it even when pressed. What he did say, more than once and always the same way, was that his uncle had held him by both shoulders on that green beach and told him: go home, and tell them the truth of it, and no more than the truth, and let them believe what they will of it after. Bran had put him back in a small boat alone, with water and dried fish enough for the crossing, though how a small boat crossed what the great one had barely crossed, and how a boy alone found his own coast again when five men together had needed the better part of a summer and Bran's own eye on the swell to find that other shore, none of the tellers who kept this after him have ever been able to say, and none of them pretend to.

The household that carried him home did what could be done for a body so far gone in thirst and sun, and for the first two or three days thought they might yet keep him. They laid him nearest the fire and gave him broth before water, a little at a time through the night, and one or another of the kin sat by him at every hour so that he never once woke to an empty room. Word of him went along the coast faster than any of them could stop it, and by the fourth day there were strangers at the door asking to see the boy who had crossed the western sea, and had to be turned away for his own quiet.

He lived eleven days after they carried him in from the shingle, long enough to say what he said to his mother, and to Blàthnaid, and to two or three others who sat with him by turns, and no longer. He grew quieter each of those days rather than stronger, and on the last of them asked that the door be left open so he could see the water from where he lay. The failing light of that evening was the last he saw, and the last thing he was heard to say, more than once in those eleven days, was that it had not been an unkind land, and that his uncle had not looked afraid.

## IV. Of Blàthnaid's Hushing

What the boy said did not stay in one mouth. It could not; too many had sat by him in his dying to keep it to themselves, and a telling of that kind does not wait to be asked for before it goes from hearth to hearth. Within a year it had gone the length of that coast and some way inland besides, gathering, as every telling gathers in the going, a little more certainty than the boy himself had ever claimed for it. Men who had never been within sight of the boat spoke of the land in the west as though they had walked on it themselves. Boys not yet grown talked, the way boys do, of building craft of their own and finding it for themselves, and one household lost a fifteen-year-old for the better part of a night before he was found sitting on the shingle in the dark with a coracle he had no business taking out, only looking west, and was brought home and beaten and wept over in the same evening.

It was after that night that the elders of the shore, kin of Bran's household and of two others besides, sat together at a fire none of the young were let near and agreed, without much argument, that the telling had done all the good it was going to do and would now only do harm. They did not forbid the speaking of it outright; a thing forbidden is a thing every young man on a coast wants to hear twice over. They let it fall instead the way a fire is let fall when no one feeds it. No one told it new to a child who had not already heard it. No one who came asking after strangers' talk of it got a straight answer. Within a generation there were households on that same shore whose children had never heard Bran's name at all, and grew up knowing only that some kin of theirs, long ago, had once built an over-large boat for reasons nobody now troubled to remember, and had lost it, and that was thought reason enough to build no other like it. A boy of that later generation who asked his grandfather why no one on that shore built boats bigger than the seal grounds needed was told only that a big boat once went out and did not come back, and that the sea did not reward greed in a hull any more than it rewarded greed in a net, and the boy took the answer for the whole of the matter and never asked again.

Blàthnaid alone did not let it fall all the way. She kept the promise she had asked of her brother the night before he sailed, though he was never there again to be asked anything further of, and she kept it, in the years after, the only way left to her: by holding the whole of it in her own mouth and giving it out, once, to one person only, long after the shore's common fire for the telling had been let die. She was an old woman by then, and the daughter she told it to was near grown herself, and the two of them sat alone at a hearth Blàthnaid had banked and covered rather than let its light show through the door, the way that shore had by then come to keep its fires of an evening when a telling not meant for every ear was going to be said over them. The whole of it she told her daughter as the boy had told it to her: the green land, the spring, the welcome that cost nothing, the uncle who would not turn from a question he had followed his whole life to answer. She did not tell it as a warning, the way the elders' hushing had meant it, in the end, to be kept. She told it, her daughter said afterward, the way a woman tells a thing she has been keeping warm a long time against the day someone would finally ask to hear it.

"And his name," she said, when the telling was done and the fire had sunk to nothing under its covering stone, "was Bran."

She died some twenty years after, an old woman by any count that shore kept, and what she had told her daughter that one evening went on after her from mother to daughter in the same low voice at the same covered fire: further, with each telling, from the shore's common talk, and closer, in its way, to what a thing becomes when only one mouth at a time is trusted to carry it. It was kept dim on that coast rather than forgotten outright, and it was never again said where the young could hear it start.
