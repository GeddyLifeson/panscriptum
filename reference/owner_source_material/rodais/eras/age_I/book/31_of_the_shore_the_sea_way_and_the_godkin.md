# Of the Shore, the Sea-Way and the Godkin

*VE 4,275 – 4,474*

## I. Of the Empty Sockets

The last of the people of the landings who climbed to Làrach an Teampaill in that age was a woman whose name did not come down with her, and what she went up to do there, in the last year anyone is known to have gone up at all, no telling carries either. She climbed the steps of the platform alone in the early light, past the small cairn below the top step that the old man had built, with nothing in her hands, and she came down the same steps at evening with nothing in her hands, walking no differently than she had climbed. Whatever had drawn her up there that day she carried away from the hill entire, and she did not come back to it, and no one after her came in her place.

The platform had stood stripped for the better part of a hundred years by then, ever since three men of the valley below had gone up with prising-bars and come down with the altar's top stone slung between them and carried it over the shoulder of the hill to the west. The temple had not been given up all at once on that day. It had gone on being climbed to, now and again, by ones and twos who still held some errand there, as a household goes on setting a place at table for a while after the one who sat in it has stopped coming, before it stops setting the place at all. The stripping of the stone was the wound. The woman with empty hands was only the last visit the wound was paid.

What had lain on the altar's top before the stone was prised away, if anything had, her people did not say. The two sockets on the top step had nothing more to tell for being looked into: two square hollows cut clean into the rock, deep enough to have held the stone fast against any weather the height could throw at it, and holding now only what the sky put into them, rain in the wet months and a skin of ice in the cold ones and, in a dry summer, a little blown grit that the next rain washed out again.

After her no one climbed. The grass that the occasional foot had kept short between the fitted stones grew up thick, the sockets filled and emptied with the seasons, and the height became, for the landings below it, a place spoken of the way any finished thing is spoken of, without the reaching forward that a live question gets.

## II. Of the Middens at Cwen and the Landing-Stones

Whatever had turned the coast-people back from the high places in those years, the fortress abandoned to its choked cistern and the outpost's one gate blocked from within by hands no telling ever named, the turning held. No new work of theirs went up again anywhere out of sight of salt water, not in the years the temple stood emptying nor in the long stretch after it, and the shore took back, little by little and without any single year marking the change, all the labour that the high ground had for a while drawn away from it. A people that had once spent its strongest hands cutting steps and shafts and standing walls into rock spent them now on nets and hulls and the long patient business of a midden, which is only a household's leavings kept in one place across more years than any single household lives to see the whole of, and which grows, for that reason, at exactly the pace a people's numbers grow, no faster and no slower.

At the southern landing of Cwen the midden that had stood modest and slow-growing for as long as the oldest tellers' grandmothers could account for it began, in the years after the temple's last climbing, to thicken season on season in a way that outpaced every midden near it, Dunn's included, which had for a lifetime and more held the name of the largest heap of shell and bone and burnt weed the coast could show. It grew the plain way any midden grows, one household's supper at a time, limpet and whelk and the split bones of whatever the nets and the low-tide gathering brought in, laid down and covered and laid down again across more years than any one pair of hands could claim credit for; and it grew faster at Cwen than anywhere else along that shore for the plain reason that more of the coast-people had come to live and eat and leave their leavings there than had ever gathered in one landing before, drawn down out of the abandoned heights by ones and families and, in the last years of the drawing-down, by whole kin-groups at once, until a landing once counted by the dozen came, within the span the tellers reckoned by, to be counted by the score, and the midden below it rose to match.

It was at that same landing, or near enough to it that the coast-people who kept the one kept the other without troubling to draw a line between them, that the lane of stones was worked hardest and kept plainest: a string of flat landing-slabs laid along the shallow water of the south-east coast a day's rowing apart, grooved deep in places by the passage of keels hauled up across them season after season, running from the river mouth below Dunn round the long cape and on to the sheltered bay that lay beyond it. No single hand had set the whole of it down; each slab, by the plainest reading anyone gave it, had been laid by whichever household found that stretch of shore convenient to its own comings and goings, and the lane had grown, stone by separate stone, into a single continuous way only because each household's convenience happened, by the shape of the coast, to fall a day's rowing from the next household's. It asked no one's leave to be called a road, and no one's labour to keep it one beyond the ordinary wear of use; a keel dragged often enough across the same stretch of stone wears its own groove into it, the way a path wears into a hillside underfoot, and the lane along that coast wore in the same way, by nothing more than the daily habit of people going where they had always gone.

A rower working that lane in the years the middens at Cwen were thickest could put a name to every landing-stone on it without troubling to count the miles between them, the way a man who has walked one road his whole working life comes to know it by its ruts and its particular trees rather than by any measure of distance a stranger might ask him for. He knew which stones took the tide well and which wanted a careful eye at the ebb, which stretch of open water between two landings ran calm on an ordinary morning and which turned ugly if the wind came round from the north before a boat was clear of it, and he knew, without needing telling, that the lane he worked had a beginning at the river mouth below Dunn and an ending at the sheltered bay beyond the cape, and nothing at either end that any teller of his acquaintance had ever claimed lay further. He rowed it, in the season the tellers afterward remembered best of him, with a boy of his own household in the bow to watch the water ahead and call the stones as they came, and he taught the boy the lane the only way it had ever been taught, by rowing it with him stone by stone until the boy's own eye could find the next landing before his father's voice named it, the same teaching a father gives a son the whole run of that coast, before and after, whatever the boat or the cargo or the year.

The lane was not without its own dangers, and every rower who worked it carried a private list of them the way a shepherd carries a list of the ground where a beast might founder. A stretch below the cape ran shallow over a bed of weed that could foul an oar in calm weather and turn a boat broadside in anything rougher, and every rower of that coast learned to feel for the change in the water's colour before his own eye had properly picked it out, the way a foot learns a familiar floor in the dark. Further north, where the lane left the shelter of the cape and ran a while in open water before it found the next landing-stone, a wind out of the north could come up faster than a laden boat could answer to it, and the tellers of that coast kept, among their older sayings, a plain word of caution for any rower new to the stretch: watch the gulls before you watch the sky, for the birds leave that water a good while before a man standing in it feels the first of what is coming. A boat caught there in a quick blow had no landing nearer than the stone it had last left or the one it was making for, and more than one crew, in the tellers' keeping, had spent a hard night pulling for whichever of the two seemed the less far, with nothing to show afterward for the night's work but a boat brought in whole and a story told quietly at the next gathering of boats.

He was rowing that lane, in the last summer the tellers gave him a season at all, when a boat coming the other way hailed him at a landing neither of them had reason to linger at, and the two crews sat their boats together a while in the calm water off the stone, trading nothing more than the news each carried, which was, that season, mostly news of the shore filling: more households at Cwen than either boat's crew could remember from a single year before, and a landing further north near empty of the families that had worked it a generation back, gone down, by every account either crew had heard, to the same swelling numbers at the southern shore. Neither rower troubled to wonder aloud why the pull toward that one landing had grown so much stronger than toward any other; it was enough, for the telling either of them carried home, that the shore was filling where it was filling, the way a valley's herds drift of their own accord toward the better grass without any single beast leading the rest to it, and the two boats parted at the landing-stone with nothing settled between them but the plain fact traded and carried on, each in its own direction, along the lane that neither of them had ever thought to give a name beyond the shore's own word for it: the old way of the sea, worked as long as either crew's memory reached, and older, by every account handed down to them, than that.

## III. Of the Dead Laid in the Ground

In the high valleys about the crack, in those same years, the households laid their dead in the ground as they had always laid them, and it is worth telling how, since it was by this more than by any other thing that the valleys afterward knew themselves.

When one of a hearth died, the women of that hearth washed the body with water drawn that morning and wrapped it in a cloak of its own, or in a hide if there was no cloak to spare, and laid it on its side with the knees drawn up, as a sleeper lies on a cold night. The men dug. They dug on ground near the hearth, or on a rise the household had always used, never on the green mounds of the valleys, which were the sìth's roofs and not to be broken, and never near the slab at Tobar dhìreach, where no one drew water and no one dug save the line itself. The grave went down to the depth of a man's waist, or deeper if the ground was soft, and the body went into it with its face toward the central mountains, and with whatever the household judged it would want: a knife, a comb, a string of shells, oftener nothing. Then the earth went back over it, and the turf over the earth, and a stone was set on end at its head, a plain stone, uncut, and that was all.

No mark was put on the stone. The valleys cut no signs, and did not wish to. A household knew its own dead by where they lay, and by the shape of the rise, and by the stone at the head, which was a stone and not a word; and when there was no longer anyone living who knew whose grave it was, the grave was left alone all the same, because it was a grave. A child of the valleys learned this as it learned which way the burn ran, before it could have said why: that the ground was full of the dead of the valleys, laid in it since before any father's name could be counted back, and that the living walked over them every day and did not break them.

It was this that Rathnait's people meant, afterward, when they said they had not come. The dead of the valleys lay in the valleys, under the turf, facing the mountains, as far back as anyone could dig; and there was no telling at any hearth of any other place they had ever lain, or any water they had crossed, or any shore they had come from. They were in the ground because they had grown out of it. The Telling said so, and the ground agreed.

## IV. Of the Name Said Aloud

Rathnait was not of the Mason's own line, nor of any house that kept a fire-pot or a bag of dark coals against its own hearth, but she was of the valleys about the crack all the same, born the third child of a household two ridges east of Tobar dhìreach, and she grew up hearing the Telling the way every child of those valleys heard it, a little at a time, from whichever hearth she happened to be sitting at when some elder took it into their head to say a piece of it. She was not, by her own account given later in her life, a woman much given to wondering over the coals themselves, dark as they had lain in their bag for longer than any living teller could put a plain number to; what she wondered over, from a fairly young age and without ever quite settling the wondering, was the plainer question of what a household was, set beside all the other households of the valleys, and what a person owed to the ones near her blood as against the ones merely near her hearth.

It was a question that came to a head, for Rathnait as for a good many of her generation, on the night the valleys kept as the darkest of the year, the night the recital called the night of the lightning, when every household that could reach another in a single evening's walk gathered at whichever hearth was that year's turn to hold the gathering and heard the fathers' names said standing, from the first spark to the mason's own line down to the living, and heard after it the older riddle of the sky and the earth and the sea that no one any longer troubled to try against the coals in earnest. Rathnait had sat through that gathering every year of her life she could remember, first on her mother's knee and then on her own two feet among the other children let stay up past their ordinary bedtime for it, and she had never, in all those years, heard the gathered households called by any single word that named the whole of them at once; each hearth was known by its own place and its own kin, this house of the eastern ridge or that house of the ford, and the Telling, when it named the people it spoke of, named them only as the mason's kin or the valleys' own, never by any word that drew a line around the whole gathering and set it apart from whatever lay beyond the line.

In her five-and-twentieth year the gathering fell to a household on the western side of the crack, larger than most and closer by half a day's walk to two other valleys besides its own, so that the crowd that came in for the night of the lightning was thicker that year than Rathnait had seen it in any year before, hearths she barely knew sitting shoulder to shoulder with hearths she had known her whole life, all of them come for the one telling and the one riddle and the one night's watch against a dark that every valley of that country kept in exactly the same way. She sat that year not among the children, being grown enough by then to be counted among the women who kept the fire fed through the long recital, and she watched the crowd rather than the speaker for a good part of the evening, the way a person newly grown into a gathering often watches the gathering before she troubles to listen closely to what it has come together to hear.

It was in the stir after the fathers' names were said and before the riddle was taken up, the crowd loosening for a while into its own smaller talk the way any long-held silence loosens once the thing it was held for has been said, that Rathnait heard a man of the western household, older than her own father and known to her only by sight, answer a stranger's question about who these people gathered here were, all told, with a word she had never heard given to the whole of them before. Clann nan Dè, he said, the godkin, and said it plainly, the way a man says a thing he has turned over long enough in his own mind that it no longer feels new to him in the saying, though it plainly felt new enough to the stranger who had asked, and new enough, too, to more than one of the near hearths who caught the word and turned to hear it repeated. He gave no long account of where the word had come from, only that it seemed to him, and to others he had spoken with, a truer word for the gathered valleys than any word tied to a single ridge or a single ford, since what held the valleys together on a night like this one was neither ridge nor ford but the fathers' names and the mason's fire and the riddle that had come down with them, a thing shared by blood or by hearth or by both at once, in a way no single valley's own name had ever quite managed to say.

Rathnait asked him, when the talk had opened enough for a young woman to put a question to an elder of another household without seeming forward in it, where the households said they had come from before the mason's fire was given them, whether from some shore beyond the water, and the old man answered her without hesitation that they said no such thing of themselves; the households of the valleys held no telling of any coming at all, only of having been there, in the ground they buried their own dead in and cut no mark upon, for as far back as any father's name could carry a listener. The valleys' dead went into the ground itself, he said, under the same earth the living walked over, and the households that buried them that way had never, in any telling Rathnait's own elders could carry back to her, claimed to have arrived from anywhere at all; and the households called themselves, when they troubled to call themselves anything beyond their own ridge or ford, the children of the gods the Telling spoke of, and let whoever asked further make of that claim what they would.

The word went round the gathering that night the way any new word goes round a crowd already loosened by a long recital and a full fire, tried out first in ones and twos at the edge of the firelight and then, as the night wore toward its coldest hour and the riddle was taken up in its turn, said more freely by more mouths, until Rathnait heard it a dozen times before the gathering broke for the households to find their own places to sleep out the rest of the dark. She said it herself before the night was done, trying the shape of it in her own mouth the way a person tries a new word to see whether it sits well or ill on the tongue, and found, without quite deciding to find it, that it sat well enough; she was of no house that kept a fire-pot, and no line that counted fathers back to the mason himself by name, but she was of the valleys about the crack, buried her own kin in the same ground as every other hearth of that country, and had never in her life claimed to have come from anywhere but the place she stood in. Not every hearth at that gathering took to the word so readily. An old woman of the ford, sitting nearer the fire than her years strictly wanted on so cold a night, was heard to say to her own daughter that a name was easily enough taken up in one warm evening's talk and just as easily let drop again once the frost of the walk home had cleared a person's head of it, and that she herself had heard other words tried at other gatherings in her own long life, words that had seemed, for an evening, to fit the valleys as well as any word could, and had none of them lasted so much as a season past the fire that first warmed them into being said. She was not unkind about it, and she did not trouble to argue the man of the western household out of his word; she said only, when her daughter pressed her on why she would not use it herself, that she had buried too many hearths' worth of new names already in her own lifetime to grow fond of one before it had proved itself against more than a single cold night, and that she would wait, as she had waited before, to see whether the word was still being said the next time the valleys gathered, and the time after that, before she troubled to let it sit easy in her own mouth.

Rathnait did not have an answer ready for the old woman that night, being young enough still to feel the weight of an elder's doubt without having any settled reply to set against it, but she carried the doubt home with her all the same, turning it over on the walk back to her own household's hearth the way she turned over most things that unsettled her without quite convincing her. It seemed to her, turning it, that the old woman was right about one thing and wrong about another: right that a word tried once at one fire was no sure thing, and wrong, or so it came to seem to Rathnait across the seasons that followed, in judging this one word by the run of words that had gone before it, for she found herself using it still at the next night of the lightning, and heard others near her using it too, hearths that had not been at the western gathering at all and could only have had the word carried to them by some mouth that had, the way a coal is carried from one hearth to a cold one three valleys off. By the time Rathnait was old enough to sit among the elders at her own hearth's turn to hold the gathering, the word she had first heard tried out at the edge of a stranger's firelight had settled into ordinary use across the valleys about the crack, said as plainly and as unremarked as any of the older words for a single ridge or a single ford, and she herself, asked once by a child of her own household where the households of that country had first got the name they called themselves, could only say what the old man of the western hearth had once said to her: that it seemed to her, and to others she had spoken with, the truer word for what held the valleys together, and let the child make of that what she would.

Clann nan Dè, she said it to herself once more, low, with the fire between her and the western hearth's crowd still finding new mouths to carry the word further, and it was still in her own mouth, unremarked and untroubled, when the gathering at last went down to sleep and left the name standing, for the first time that any teller after ever cared to mark, said aloud.
