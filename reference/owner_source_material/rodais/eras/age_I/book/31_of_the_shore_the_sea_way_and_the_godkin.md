# Of the Shore, the Sea-Way and the Godkin

*VE 4,275 – 4,474*

## I. Of the Empty Sockets

The last of the Seann-Dhaoine who climbed to Làrach an Teampaill in that age was a woman no teller ever gave a name to, and what she went up to do there, in the last year anyone is known to have gone up at all, no telling carries either. She was seen from below, by a shepherd of the moor who had long since stopped expecting to see anyone on that height and stood a good while with his dogs sitting at his heels before he trusted his own eyes, climbing the steps of the platform alone in the early light with nothing in her hands, and she was seen again at evening coming down the same steps with nothing in her hands either, walking, as far as the shepherd's watching could tell it afterward, no differently than she had climbed. He never spoke to her, and she never looked toward him, and whatever had drawn her up there that day, or driven her down again, she carried away from the hill entire, and did not come back to it; and no one after her is known to have come in her place.

The platform had stood empty of any regular tending for the better part of two generations by then, ever since the three coast-people had gone up with their bars of dark wood and come down again with the altar's top stone slung between them and carried it away west, out past the edge of any watcher's safe distance, so that no account of that carrying, then or after, could say where the stone came to rest. The temple had not been abandoned all at once on that day, the way the fortress below it had been let go dark hearth by hearth through a single hard winter; it had gone on being climbed to, now and again, by ones and twos of the coast-people who still held some errand there that outside eyes were never given to understand, for a long while after the stone was gone, as a household goes on setting a place at table for some while after the one who sat in it has stopped coming, before it stops setting the place at all. The stripping of the stone was the wound; the woman with empty hands was only the last visit the wound was ever paid.

What had stood on the altar's top step before the stone was pried loose from it, no telling of the hill country or the shore either one had ever claimed to know with any certainty, though there was no shortage of guessing once the guessing had a clear field to work in. Some said it had held nothing at all, that the top stone was the whole of what the place asked to be given, a flat weight set square to the central mountains for no purpose beyond the squaring; others said a thing had lain on it once, an offering or a marker or some third kind of object no word of any tongue on the island had a name ready for, and that whoever carried the stone away had carried that thing off inside the carrying, hidden in plain sight the way a man hides a small stone in a larger one's shadow. Neither guess could be tested against the sockets, which had nothing more to say for having been asked for two generations running: two shallow hollows cut clean and square into the platform's uppermost slab, deep enough to have held the stone fast against any weather the height could throw at it, and now holding, in the years after, only whatever the sky happened to put into them on any given day, rain in the wet months and a skin of ice in the cold ones and, in a dry summer, nothing at all but a scatter of blown grit that the next good rain washed out again.

There had been a time, within the memory of the shepherd's own grandfather, when a climb to the temple drew no particular notice at all, when the coast-people went up and down the platform's steps on whatever errand the season asked of them as freely as they went to their own nets, and a shepherd grazing the moor beneath it thought no more of the coming and going than he thought of gulls working a tideline. That ease had gone out of the climbing well before the stone was carried off, in the years the fortress below the temple was still lit along its middle wall and the outpost beyond it still held whatever hands had first raised it; the coast-people who climbed in those years climbed already with a kind of care the shepherd's grandfather had remarked on without being able to say what it was care against, fewer of them going up together than had once gone, and none of them lingering at the top past what the errand plainly wanted. Whatever had first put that care into the climbing, the same thing, by every guess the moor ever ventured, had in the end put a stop to the climbing altogether; the three who came for the stone were only the last and plainest sign of a caution that had been growing in the coast-people's own dealings with the height for longer than the shepherd's grandfather had lived to watch it grow.

The shepherd who had watched the nameless woman climb and come down told it after, when he told it at all, without troubling to guess what he had seen, in the plain way a man tells a thing he watched happen and does not pretend to have understood better for the watching. He said only that she had gone up with nothing and come down with nothing, and that a person minded to make more of it than that was welcome to, but that he himself, having stood there the whole while with the dogs gone quiet at his heels and his own breath held without his quite meaning to hold it, had seen a woman climb a hill and climb back down it, and no more than that, and that whatever business she had settled with herself on that platform, if settling was the word for it, she had settled without giving him or any watcher after him so much as a gesture to build a story on. It became, in time, the last telling the moor kept of the temple at all, the way a household's last telling of an old and troublesome hound is often no more than the plain fact of the day it was last seen to walk out past the yard gate and not come home; after her, no one climbed, and the grass that had been kept short by two generations of occasional feet grew up thick between the platform's fitted stones, and the sockets filled and emptied with the seasons, and the height became, for the coast-people below it, a place spoken of the way any finished thing is spoken of, without the reaching-forward a live question still gets.

## II. Of the Middens at Chwen and the Landing-Stones

Whatever had turned the coast-people back from the high places in those years, the fortress abandoned to its choked cistern and the outpost's one gate blocked from within by hands no telling ever named, the turning held. No new work of theirs went up again anywhere out of sight of salt water, not in the years the temple stood emptying nor in the long stretch after it, and the shore took back, little by little and without any single year marking the change, all the labour that the high ground had for a while drawn away from it. A people that had once spent its strongest hands cutting steps and shafts and standing walls into rock spent them now on nets and hulls and the long patient business of a midden, which is only a household's leavings kept in one place across more years than any single household lives to see the whole of, and which grows, for that reason, at exactly the pace a people's numbers grow, no faster and no slower.

At the southern landing the hill country called Chwen, using the Old Ones' own word for it because none of their own had ever troubled to make another, the midden that had stood modest and slow-growing for as long as the oldest tellers' grandmothers could account for it began, in the years after the temple's last climbing, to thicken season on season in a way that outpaced every midden near it, Dunn's included, which had for a lifetime and more held the name of the largest heap of shell and bone and burnt weed the coast could show. It grew the plain way any midden grows, one household's supper at a time, limpet and whelk and the split bones of whatever the nets and the low-tide gathering brought in, laid down and covered and laid down again across more years than any one pair of hands could claim credit for; and it grew faster at Chwen than anywhere else along that shore for the plain reason that more of the coast-people had come to live and eat and leave their leavings there than had ever gathered in one landing before, drawn down out of the abandoned heights by ones and families and, in the last years of the drawing-down, by whole kin-groups at once, until a landing that a hill trader of an earlier generation could have counted by the dozen came, within the span the tellers reckoned by, to be counted by the score, and the midden below it rose to match.

It was at that same landing, or near enough to it that the coast-people who kept the one kept the other without troubling to draw a line between them, that the lane of stones was worked hardest and kept plainest: a string of flat landing-slabs laid along the shallow water of the south-east coast a day's rowing apart, grooved deep in places by the passage of keels hauled up across them season after season, running from the river mouth below Dunn round the long cape and on to the sheltered bay that lay beyond it. No single hand had set the whole of it down; each slab, by the plainest reading anyone gave it, had been laid by whichever household found that stretch of shore convenient to its own comings and goings, and the lane had grown, stone by separate stone, into a single continuous way only because each household's convenience happened, by the shape of the coast, to fall a day's rowing from the next household's. It asked no one's leave to be called a road, and no one's labour to keep it one beyond the ordinary wear of use; a keel dragged often enough across the same stretch of stone wears its own groove into it, the way a path wears into a hillside underfoot, and the lane along that coast wore in the same way, by nothing more than the daily habit of people going where they had always gone.

A rower working that lane in the years the middens at Chwen were thickest could put a name to every landing-stone on it without troubling to count the miles between them, the way a man who has walked one road his whole working life comes to know it by its ruts and its particular trees rather than by any measure of distance a stranger might ask him for. He knew which stones took the tide well and which wanted a careful eye at the ebb, which stretch of open water between two landings ran calm on an ordinary morning and which turned ugly if the wind came round from the north before a boat was clear of it, and he knew, without needing telling, that the lane he worked had a beginning at the river mouth below Dunn and an ending at the sheltered bay beyond the cape, and nothing at either end that any teller of his acquaintance had ever claimed lay further. He rowed it, in the season the tellers afterward remembered best of him, with a boy of his own household in the bow to watch the water ahead and call the stones as they came, and he taught the boy the lane the only way it had ever been taught, by rowing it with him stone by stone until the boy's own eye could find the next landing before his father's voice named it, the same teaching a father gives a son the whole run of that coast, before and after, whatever the boat or the cargo or the year.

The lane was not without its own dangers, and every rower who worked it carried a private list of them the way a shepherd carries a list of the ground where a beast might founder. A stretch below the cape ran shallow over a bed of weed that could foul an oar in calm weather and turn a boat broadside in anything rougher, and every rower of that coast learned to feel for the change in the water's colour before his own eye had properly picked it out, the way a foot learns a familiar floor in the dark. Further north, where the lane left the shelter of the cape and ran a while in open water before it found the next landing-stone, a wind out of the north could come up faster than a laden boat could answer to it, and the tellers of that coast kept, among their older sayings, a plain word of caution for any rower new to the stretch: watch the gulls before you watch the sky, for the birds leave that water a good while before a man standing in it feels the first of what is coming. A boat caught there in a quick blow had no landing nearer than the stone it had last left or the one it was making for, and more than one crew, in the tellers' keeping, had spent a hard night pulling for whichever of the two seemed the less far, with nothing to show afterward for the night's work but a boat brought in whole and a story told quietly at the next gathering of boats.

He was rowing that lane, in the last summer the tellers gave him a season at all, when a boat coming the other way hailed him at a landing neither of them had reason to linger at, and the two crews sat their boats together a while in the calm water off the stone, trading nothing more than the news each carried, which was, that season, mostly news of the shore filling: more households at Chwen than either boat's crew could remember from a single year before, and a landing further north near empty of the families that had worked it a generation back, gone down, by every account either crew had heard, to the same swelling numbers at the southern shore. Neither rower troubled to wonder aloud why the pull toward that one landing had grown so much stronger than toward any other; it was enough, for the telling either of them carried home, that the shore was filling where it was filling, the way a valley's herds drift of their own accord toward the better grass without any single beast leading the rest to it, and the two boats parted at the landing-stone with nothing settled between them but the plain fact traded and carried on, each in its own direction, along the lane that neither of them had ever thought to give a name beyond the shore's own word for it: the old way of the sea, worked as long as either crew's memory reached, and older, by every account handed down to them, than that.

## III. Of the Name Said Aloud

Rathnait was not of the Mason's own line, nor of any house that kept a fire-pot or a bag of dark coals against its own hearth, but she was of the valleys about the crack all the same, born the third child of a household two ridges east of Tobar dhìreach in the year the tellers afterward gave as four thousand four hundred years from the mason's fire, and she grew up hearing the Telling the way every child of those valleys heard it, a little at a time, from whichever hearth she happened to be sitting at when some elder took it into their head to say a piece of it. She was not, by her own account given later in her life, a woman much given to wondering over the coals themselves, dark as they had lain in their bag for longer than any living teller could put a plain number to; what she wondered over, from a fairly young age and without ever quite settling the wondering, was the plainer question of what a household was, set beside all the other households of the valleys, and what a person owed to the ones near her blood as against the ones merely near her hearth.

It was a question that came to a head, for Rathnait as for a good many of her generation, on the night the valleys kept as the darkest of the year, the night the recital called the night of the lightning, when every household that could reach another in a single evening's walk gathered at whichever hearth was that year's turn to hold the gathering and heard the fathers' names said standing, from the first spark to the mason's own line down to the living, and heard after it the older riddle of the sky and the earth and the sea that no one any longer troubled to try against the coals in earnest. Rathnait had sat through that gathering every year of her life she could remember, first on her mother's knee and then on her own two feet among the other children let stay up past their ordinary bedtime for it, and she had never, in all those years, heard the gathered households called by any single word that named the whole of them at once; each hearth was known by its own place and its own kin, this house of the eastern ridge or that house of the ford, and the Telling, when it named the people it spoke of, named them only as the mason's kin or the valleys' own, never by any word that drew a line around the whole gathering and set it apart from whatever lay beyond the line.

The year the tellers afterward marked as four thousand four hundred and twenty-five years from the mason's fire, the gathering fell to a household on the western side of the crack, larger than most and closer by half a day's walk to two other valleys besides its own, so that the crowd that came in for the night of the lightning was thicker that year than Rathnait had seen it in any year before, hearths she barely knew sitting shoulder to shoulder with hearths she had known her whole life, all of them come for the one telling and the one riddle and the one night's watch against a dark that every valley of that country kept in exactly the same way. She sat that year not among the children, being grown enough by then to be counted among the women who kept the fire fed through the long recital, and she watched the crowd rather than the speaker for a good part of the evening, the way a person newly grown into a gathering often watches the gathering before she troubles to listen closely to what it has come together to hear.

It was in the stir after the fathers' names were said and before the riddle was taken up, the crowd loosening for a while into its own smaller talk the way any long-held silence loosens once the thing it was held for has been said, that Rathnait heard a man of the western household, older than her own father and known to her only by sight, answer a stranger's question about who these people gathered here were, all told, with a word she had never heard given to the whole of them before. Clann nan Dè, he said, the godkin, and said it plainly, the way a man says a thing he has turned over long enough in his own mind that it no longer feels new to him in the saying, though it plainly felt new enough to the stranger who had asked, and new enough, too, to more than one of the near hearths who caught the word and turned to hear it repeated. He gave no long account of where the word had come from, only that it seemed to him, and to others he had spoken with, a truer word for the gathered valleys than any word tied to a single ridge or a single ford, since what held the valleys together on a night like this one was neither ridge nor ford but the fathers' names and the mason's fire and the riddle that had come down with them, a thing shared by blood or by hearth or by both at once, in a way no single valley's own name had ever quite managed to say.

Rathnait asked him, when the talk had opened enough for a young woman to put a question to an elder of another household without seeming forward in it, where the households said they had come from before the mason's fire was given them, whether from some further shore the way the telling of the boats spoke of shores beyond the water, and the old man answered her without hesitation that they said no such thing of themselves, that whatever tale the shore-people told of their own coming, the households of the valleys held no telling of any coming at all, only of having been there, in the ground they buried their own dead in and cut no mark upon, for as far back as any father's name could carry a listener. They were not, he said, the shore-people's kind, who rowed their dead out to lie facing the land they had left; the valleys' dead went into the ground itself, under the same earth the living walked over, and the households that buried them that way had never, in any telling Rathnait's own elders could carry back to her, claimed to have arrived from anywhere at all. Whether they had shared the island once with the people of the shore, or come after them, or been there before either the shore-people or the mason's own fire, the old man could not say and did not pretend to, only that the households called themselves, when they troubled to call themselves anything beyond their own ridge or ford, the children of the gods the Telling spoke of, and let whoever asked further make of that claim what they would.

The word went round the gathering that night the way any new word goes round a crowd already loosened by a long recital and a full fire, tried out first in ones and twos at the edge of the firelight and then, as the night wore toward its coldest hour and the riddle was taken up in its turn, said more freely by more mouths, until Rathnait heard it a dozen times before the gathering broke for the households to find their own places to sleep out the rest of the dark. She said it herself before the night was done, trying the shape of it in her own mouth the way a person tries a new word to see whether it sits well or ill on the tongue, and found, without quite deciding to find it, that it sat well enough; she was of no house that kept a fire-pot, and no line that counted fathers back to the mason himself by name, but she was of the valleys about the crack, buried her own kin in the same ground as every other hearth of that country, and had never in her life claimed to have come from anywhere but the place she stood in. Not every hearth at that gathering took to the word so readily. An old woman of the ford, sitting nearer the fire than her years strictly wanted on so cold a night, was heard to say to her own daughter that a name was easily enough taken up in one warm evening's talk and just as easily let drop again once the frost of the walk home had cleared a person's head of it, and that she herself had heard other words tried at other gatherings in her own long life, words that had seemed, for an evening, to fit the valleys as well as any word could, and had none of them lasted so much as a season past the fire that first warmed them into being said. She was not unkind about it, and she did not trouble to argue the man of the western household out of his word; she said only, when her daughter pressed her on why she would not use it herself, that she had buried too many hearths' worth of new names already in her own lifetime to grow fond of one before it had proved itself against more than a single cold night, and that she would wait, as she had waited before, to see whether the word was still being said the next time the valleys gathered, and the time after that, before she troubled to let it sit easy in her own mouth.

Rathnait did not have an answer ready for the old woman that night, being young enough still to feel the weight of an elder's doubt without having any settled reply to set against it, but she carried the doubt home with her all the same, turning it over on the walk back to her own household's hearth the way she turned over most things that unsettled her without quite convincing her. It seemed to her, turning it, that the old woman was right about one thing and wrong about another: right that a word tried once at one fire was no sure thing, and wrong, or so it came to seem to Rathnait across the seasons that followed, in judging this one word by the run of words that had gone before it, for she found herself using it still at the next night of the lightning, and heard others near her using it too, hearths that had not been at the western gathering at all and could only have had the word carried to them by some mouth that had, the way a coal is carried from one hearth to a cold one three valleys off. By the time Rathnait was old enough to sit among the elders at her own hearth's turn to hold the gathering, the word she had first heard tried out at the edge of a stranger's firelight had settled into ordinary use across the valleys about the crack, said as plainly and as unremarked as any of the older words for a single ridge or a single ford, and she herself, asked once by a child of her own household where the households of that country had first got the name they called themselves, could only say what the old man of the western hearth had once said to her: that it seemed to her, and to others she had spoken with, the truer word for what held the valleys together, and let the child make of that what she would.

Clann nan Dè, she said it to herself once more, low, with the fire between her and the western hearth's crowd still finding new mouths to carry the word further, and it was still in her own mouth, unremarked and untroubled, when the gathering at last went down to sleep and left the name standing, for the first time that any teller after ever cared to mark, said aloud.
