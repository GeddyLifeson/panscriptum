# Of the Mason and the Lightning

*VE 1*

> *Bha teine sa chloich.*
> There was fire in the stone.

*The First Part: Of the Mason's Fire, and the Covering of the Vein*

## I. Of the Dry Spring

This is told at the hearth, on the night of the lightning, by whoever in the house is oldest, and it begins before the mason ever touched the wedge, in the season when his valley had no water.

The spring under the shoulder of the hill had fed that household and three others since before anyone living could say. It rose out of a fold of rock, cold even in high summer, and ran a hand's width wide down a groove worn into the stone by every year it had ever run. In the autumn of that year it failed. It did not fail all at once. It thinned through the wet months when it should have been running full, and by the dark of the year it was a trickle a child could stop with two fingers, and then some mornings there was nothing in the groove at all but a damp stain and a smell of old moss.

The valley did not starve. There were becks lower down, and the households sent their water-carriers a mile and a half with skins on their backs, morning and evening, in whatever weather the year gave them, which that year was mostly sleet. But a valley that carries its water on its back through a hard winter learns to want a well of its own, dug where the rock holds water even when the sky will not give it, and the households met at the flat stone below the failed spring and talked of digging one.

They did not choose lightly, for the digging of a well in that country was slow and cruel work, done with a wedge of hard stone and a maul of oak, a hand's depth of rock at a time, and the man who did it would be at it through the worst of the winter with his knees in the mud and his back to the wind. They chose Neachdan, because he was the best splitter of stone that any of the four households could put a name to, and because he had said, when the others were still weighing whether the spring might yet come back, that it would not, and had gone to look for a place to sink the shaft while the rest of them were still talking.

He was not yet thirty. He had split more stone in his life than most men split in two, walling folds and lining graves and setting the flat stones that floored every threshold in the valley, and there was not a household in it that did not owe him some doorstep or some byre wall. His father had taught him the trade and had been dead some years. His wife was Sorcha, and they had a house at the low end of the valley with a boy of six named Conall and three littler than Conall, and it was Sorcha who came out to the new-marked shaft on the first morning with a skin of water and stood a while watching him set the first wedge, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the wedge and the winter had not already said between them.

## II. Of the Wedge and the Rock

He began below the flat stone, on a rise a little east of the dead spring, where the older men of the valley said the rock ran true and would not crack sideways on him and waste his work. It was the dead of the year, past the shortest day, and the light he had to dig by was grey from first light to last, with sleet in most of it.

The way of it was this. He set the iron-hard wedge into a seam of the rock with the flat of his hand, judged it true, and struck it with the oak maul, and struck it again, and went on striking until the rock gave along the seam or did not, and if it did not he moved the wedge a hand's width and began again. A day was perhaps two hand's-depths of shaft, if the rock ran kind, and less if it did not, and there were days that whole short winter when he came up out of the cut at dusk with nothing to show for it but split knuckles and a wedge blunted at the edge.

The cold did that to him before the rock did. His hands split at the big knuckle and across the base of the thumb, the way a mason's hands always split in a hard winter, and the cuts never had time to close between one day's work and the next, so that by the dark of the year his hands looked older than the rest of him, red-rimmed and cracked and always a little wet, whether from the wound or the weather there was no telling. He wrapped them in strips of hide when Sorcha made him, and lost the wrapping within the hour because a man cannot feel a seam in stone through a hide glove, and Sorcha stopped making him after the fourth time, and only rubbed grease from the sheep-fat pot into the cracks at night while he sat by their own small fire, which in those days on Dia-thìr was no fire at all: a heap of stones warmed in embers carried from a neighbour and kept wrapped in bracken through the day, giving off a heat that was more memory than warmth by the time it reached the house.

There was no other kind of fire on the island. No one on Dia-thìr had ever seen one lit; there was the sun, and there was the lightning, which came and went and could not be carried, and there was the slow patient husbandry of warmed stones passed from hand to hand and hearth to hearth, which was how every household on the island had always held off the worst of a winter, and none had ever held off all of it. Old people died of the cold in a hard year, and children died of it, and it was accepted as the weather was accepted, because there was nothing else to do about either.

By the fifteenth day of the digging the shaft was over Neachdan's head, and he worked now with a rope ladder of plaited hide and a leather bucket that Conall's cousin hauled up from the lip, full of chippings, forty and fifty times a short winter day. The rock had begun to run darker as he went down, a grey going to black, harder under the wedge than anything above it had been, ringing instead of cracking clean, so that he had to strike twice or three times where once had done. He did not think much of the darkening. Rock ran however it ran; he had cut through worse.

## III. Of the Black Break and the Colours In It

On the morning it happened the sleet had turned half to rain, which was in its way worse, since rain finds every gap in a cloak that sleet only sits on. He went down into the shaft before the grey light was properly up, because the household wanted water and every day he did not reach it was a day the carriers walked their mile and a half twice more.

A seam ran clean across the face of the cut, a hair's width of a line that his eye, trained on twenty years of stone, told him would open true. He set the wedge and struck.

It went with one blow, which in that hard black rock had happened perhaps twice in the whole digging, and the block came away in his hands heavier than its size promised, and he nearly dropped it. He turned it toward the little grey light that came down the shaft from above.

Every stone he had ever split in his life, in that valley or any other, had broken to the same thing: grey dust, grit, a dull grey face going to a duller grey the longer it lay in the weather. This did not. The break ran black, black as a wet crow's wing, and along it, where the maul had opened the vein, there was colour: a streak that lay gold when he tilted it, the way the sea takes the low sun at evening though he had never seen the sea; a scatter of small points that shone white as frost catches the light before frost has any business shining; a vein of dull red-brown like the true heart of an old oak split for a beam; a thread that ran silver and did not tarnish when his thumb rubbed across it; and, set apart from the rest, a piece that when he turned it slowly held colour after colour after colour, so that he could not have said what colour it truly was, only that it was every one he had a name for and some, he thought afterward, that he did not.

He knelt in the bottom of the cut with the rain running off the shaft's lip onto the back of his neck and turned the pieces over and over in his cracked hands until the cold had taken the last of the feeling out of his fingers and he could no longer tell by touch which piece he held.

He did not know what it was. No one on the island had ever seen coal, coloured or otherwise; there was nothing in any hearth-tale, any wall-lore, any word passed down from any grandfather, that gave this a name or a use or a warning to go with it. He knew only that it was beautiful, and that a man does not leave a beautiful thing in the bottom of a well shaft to be flooded over when the water comes in, so he opened the neck of his tunic and put the pieces inside, against his chest where his own small warmth might keep the rain off them a while, and went on working the seam until there was no more colour in it and only the plain grey-black beyond, which he left where it was, because a well is dug for water and not for stone.

Midday found him climbing out of the shaft with his tunic heavy and lumped against his ribs and the light no better than it had been at dawn, meaning to show Sorcha what he had found and go back down before the light went altogether. He never went back down that day.

## IV. Of the Storm at Dusk

The sky had been low and grey and giving rain in a steady, sullen way since before he woke, the kind of day that does not announce a change in it. The change came in the middle of the afternoon, while he stood at the lip of the shaft eating a heel of bread that one of the little ones had brought him, and it came the way weather comes on that coast when it means to be taken seriously: the wind backed round from its steady quarter and freshened all in a few minutes, and the low grey overhead thickened from the west into a darker grey that had a green cast at its underside, which the old people of the valley said was a colour a sky takes before it does something worth remembering.

He judged, watching it come, that there was no sense going back down the shaft into a storm; a man in a hole in the ground in a hard rain is a man waiting to be drowned in his own well. He gathered his tools, capped the shaft-mouth with the split boards kept there for it, and looked for shelter, and found it under the lip of a low crag a stone's throw above the shaft, an overhang no deeper than a man is tall, but dry, where the herders of that hillside sheltered their beasts in the worst of a summer squall.

The storm came up the valley like something walking. He watched it from under the crag: the rain-curtain moving across the far slope first, greying it out entirely, then the near slope, then his own; the wind driving hard enough to lean the heather flat and to find its way even under the lip of his shelter and set the rain slantwise against his legs; and the light going to a strange thick yellow-brown, nothing like the grey of an ordinary evening, that made the whole valley look, he said afterward, the way a bruise looks a day after the blow.

Then the sky broke.

There was no other way any teller of it, then or since, has ever said it. It did not thunder first and lighten after, in the ordinary way of a storm working up to its worst; it went from the yellow-brown stillness straight to a white light that filled the whole overhang he crouched in, so bright that for a moment he saw the black rock of the crag above him as though it were noon, and in the same instant a sound came that he did not hear so much as feel in the ground under his knees and in his own teeth, a single crack, as if the hill itself had been struck with a maul the size of the sky.

He was thrown back against the rock. When his sight cleared enough to see anything at all, he saw that the light had not simply flashed and gone, as lightning does. It had gone down. He had watched it, in the half-second his eyes could hold it, run down out of the cloud in a white line no wider than a spear-shaft, and strike the ground below the crag, on the very line of the capped shaft, and vanish into it.

For a long moment he did not move. The rain went on falling. The wind went on. Nothing in the valley below him showed any sign that the sky had just done a thing no one living had words for.

Then he saw the light.

It came up faint at first from around the edges of the split boards over the shaft-mouth, a low reddish glow, the colour a coal takes at the heart of an ember, and it did not fade the way an afterimage fades from a struck eye. It grew. By the time he had got his legs under him and come out from under the crag into the driving rain it was strong enough to throw his own shadow, black and long, across the wet heather in front of him.

## V. Of the Thing Alive in the Well

He came to the lip of the shaft with the rain running into his eyes and pulled the split boards aside, and the light came up out of the ground and struck him full in the face, and he knelt there at the edge with the rain hammering his back and looked down at what he had made, though he never afterward believed he had made it, only that he had broken it open and the sky had done the rest.

The whole face of the black seam, as far down as his eye could follow it in the murk of the shaft, was awake. It did not burn the way he imagined fire must burn, if fire had ever been more to him than a word in an old telling of the gods' hearths; it moved low along the vein, a dark flame with red at its root, the colour of peat-water stirred up from the bottom of a bog and lit from within, and it breathed. That was the word he used all his life after, and no other word ever satisfied him or the people who heard him tell it. It swelled and drew in, swelled and drew in, as a chest does, and where it swelled the heat came up the shaft in a wave he felt on his face and on his hands before he felt anything else, a heat with no wind in it and no cold anywhere behind it, the first warmth any living thing on Dia-thìr had ever felt that did not come, however faintly, from the sun.

The rain fell into the shaft in sheets, straight down the sides where the boards had been, and struck the burning vein, and hissed, and the vein did not go out. Rain that should by every reason he had ever been taught have drowned the thing dead went on falling into it and coming off it as a thin white smoke that the wind took away over the hillside, and he could do nothing but watch it, a long while, unable to do anything else.

He put his hands over the shaft-mouth, not into it, only over it, the way a man holds his hands to a fire he has not yet decided to trust, and the warmth came up into his split and blackened knuckles and into the base of his aching thumbs, and something in him that had been clenched since the dead of the year, since the spring failed and the sleet began and his hands began to crack, let go all at once. He knelt in the mud at the lip of his own well with the storm going on over him and wept, not loudly, the way a man weeps who has been cold a long time and has stopped expecting to be warm again, and he was not ashamed of it afterward, though he did not tell it often, and it is the one part of the whole telling that the households of that valley have always agreed was surely true, because it is not the kind of thing a teller invents to make a tale better.

## VI. Of the Coal Carried Home

Kneeling there, he could not know whether the thing would live an hour or a year, and it was that not-knowing, more than anything else, that moved him. A wonder that might go out with the next gust was a wonder that had to be taken while it could be taken.

He found among his tools a flat stone he had set aside from the digging, one of the pale slabs meant for the new well's coping, and he went down the rope ladder into the shaft, into the heat and the strange breathing light of it, with the rain still coming down the sides in ropes, and he chose a piece of the vein near its edge, where the burning had not yet reached its full strength, and worked it free with an iron pry-bar, careful as a man lifting an egg from under a sitting bird. It came away whole, a fist-sized lump of the black seam with the dark flame still moving in it, throwing more heat in his two hands than the whole warmed-stone hearth of his house threw in a night.

He set it on the flat slab. He drew his cloak from around his shoulders, wet through as it already was, and folded it over the burning piece so that only a slit was left for air, the way, without ever having been told to do such a thing, a man's hands simply know to shelter a flame from wind and wet at once; and he climbed the ladder one-handed, the slab and its burden held against his chest with the other arm, and went out into the full weight of the storm to carry it the mile down the valley to his own door.

It is the walk that every teller of the mason's tale slows for, whatever else is passed over quickly, because it is the walk on which the first fire on Dia-thìr was nearly lost a dozen times before it was ever set on a hearth. He went slantwise down the hill so that his own body stood between the coal and the driving rain. He stumbled twice on wet stone and each time caught himself with his free hand and never let the slab tip. Once, crossing the beck that fed the failed spring, swollen now with the storm's own rain, he waded it rather than risk the stepping-stones in the dark, holding the slab high over the water with both arms, the cloak's wet wool steaming faintly where the coal's heat met the flood below it. He said afterward that he had not once, on the whole of that walk, thought about the water in the well or the winter or anything else the household had sent him out that morning to solve; he had thought only of the small red light under the cloak, and of keeping it.

## VII. Of the First Hearth

Sorcha had the door open before he reached it, because she had seen, coming down through the rain, a light where no light on that hillside had any business being, low and red and moving with him rather than fixed as any lightning-struck gorse or burning heather would have been.

He came into the house stooped over the slab like a man carrying something asleep that he feared to wake wrong, and set it down in the centre of the floor, on the ring of bare-swept earth kept clear for the warmed stones, and drew back his cloak from it, and the household saw, for the first time in the history of anything that had ever lived on that island, what a flame was.

Conall, who was six, came close enough that Sorcha had to catch the back of his tunic, and the three littler than him stood behind their mother's legs and would not come close at all, and stared at the low red-black light with the wide unblinking look of children who cannot yet tell whether a thing is dangerous by anything but its strangeness. Neachdan told them, because he did not yet know better himself, that it would not hurt them if they let it be, and for that first night it was true, since none of them touched it and it did nothing but breathe its slow dark flame and give off a warmth that reached every corner of the one room they had, farther and steadier than any warmed stone had ever reached it.

He remembered the rest of the coloured pieces still in his tunic and drew them out, one by one, and set them by the burning one to see what they would do, and this is the part of the telling that every hearth in the hill country still gives the same way. Each piece, laid against the fire, caught. Each burned as the first had burned, low and dark-flamed, and as each caught, the colour went out of it: the gold sheen dulled to the same red-black glow as the rest, the white points dimmed, the red-brown vein and the silver thread and the last strange piece that had held every colour at once all burned down, in the end, to one same low dark flame indistinguishable from any other, so that within the hour there was nothing left in the whole burning ring to tell a man which piece had shone gold and which silver and which had held the rainbow.

Neachdan looked at that a long while, turning the last blackened lump with a stick, and he told his children, and told it again at every hearth-night after, that he had been sorry for it, a little, the way a man is sorry to see any beautiful thing spend its last; and that he had been glad all the same, more glad than sorry by more than any measure could set between them, because his household was sitting round a fire, red-faced in its light, with the rain still hammering the turf roof above them and none of them cold, for the first time since any of them could remember being alive at all.

## VIII. Of What the Fire Taught in One Night

It did not stay a simple wonder for long. A fire, once made, carries its own nature along with its light and its warmth, and that nature had to be learned in the same night it was given, by people who had never had reason to learn it before.

The first lesson came within the hour, when the flame at the heart of the ring began to sink and dull of its own accord. The roof kept the rain off it; it had simply nothing more to take. Neachdan understood, watching it fade, what none of the tellings that came before him could have told him, because there were none: that the thing ate. It had eaten the black rock it was born in, slowly, breathing its heat out of the burning stone, and now the coal he had carried was more ash-grey crust than the black it had been, and the flame under the crust was going the colour of a dying ember rather than the strong dark red it had shown at the shaft.

He broke the crust with the same stick, more from the instinct of a man breaking bread than from any knowledge, and beneath it the coal still glowed, and the glow strengthened when the air reached it, and that told him a second thing, though he could not have put a name to what it told him: that the thing wanted feeding and wanted air both, and would not simply burn until it was done and stop.

It was Sorcha who fed it heather. No one told her to; there was dry heather stacked by the door for bedding, and a mother's instinct in a cold house is to put whatever will hold heat within reach of the thing giving it. She laid a handful of the dry stems against the failing coal, and the fire took them at once, faster and brighter than it had taken the stone, with a small crackling that made the littlest of the children cry out and back further behind her skirt; and the flame leapt up gold-white for a breath and then settled, fed, into a steadier light than it had shown since it left the ground.

That was the turn of the whole night, the one the tellers give weight to above all the rest: that the thing they had brought into the house could be fed, and could therefore also, if it was not fed, go out and die, as surely as a beast dies of hunger or a man of cold; and that it was theirs to keep or lose by their own care, in a way that the sun and the wind and the sea had never been anyone's to keep or lose. Neachdan understood it before he could have said it in so many words, and it frightened him more than the lightning had, because the lightning had come and gone in an instant beyond anyone's holding, and this, this small red thing on his own floor, could be lost through nothing worse than an evening's carelessness, and once lost, by everything he had seen and reasoned that night, might never be got again.

## IX. Of Sorcha's Hand

The second lesson cost more than the first.

Conall had been told, and told again, to keep his hands well back, and did, being six and old enough to be frightened as well as curious; it was Sorcha, feeding the fire a second time as the night wore on, who reached too far in setting a fresh knot of heather against the glowing heart of it and let her palm brush the crust of the coal itself, the ash-grey skin that looked, to every eye in that room, no different from any dead stone that had lain cold in a wall all its life.

She cried out and snatched her hand back, and for a moment the household froze, because none of them, not even Neachdan who had knelt at the shaft's mouth and put his own hands over the breathing vein, had yet understood that the thing could bite a hand that did not ask its leave first. Her palm reddened at once, and by the time Neachdan had her by the wrist in the low light to look at it, a welt was rising across the base of her fingers, and she held it against her own breast and would not at first let him touch it, more from the shock of the thing than from the hurt, though the hurt was real enough and lasted her the better part of a month.

He put grease from the sheep-fat pot on it, the same grease she had rubbed into his own cracked knuckles through the whole hard winter of the digging, and it was, he thought afterward, a strange turn that the fire she had helped bring alive should be the first thing in that house to need tending in the old way, with the same jar and the same hands.

From that night the household kept a rule that needed no telling twice: the fire was fed with a stick's length between the hand and the flame, and never, whatever the cold, with a bare palm laid flat against the glowing crust to test whether it still had heat in it. Sorcha herself taught it to the littler children before dawn, holding up her reddened hand for them to see, and it is said, though this the tellers admit they cannot swear to, that it was from that hurt and that teaching, given the very first night the fire lived in a house, that every hearth on the island since has kept its stick, its poker, its tongs, and has never once gone back to bare hands.

## X. Of the Household Red-Faced at Dawn

They did not sleep, none of the household, through the whole of that first night; there was too much to watch, too much still to be learned about a thing that had never before existed anywhere any of them or their fathers or their fathers' fathers had ever stood. The storm wore itself out somewhere in the small hours, the wind falling first and the rain going after it to a last thin drizzle and then to nothing, so that by the last dark before dawn the only sound in the valley was the low steady crackle of the fire and, further off, water still running high in the beck from all the rain the hill had taken.

Conall fell asleep at last sitting up against his mother's knee, close enough to the fire to feel it and far enough, by then, to be trusted there; the three littler ones slept in a heap under one cloak on the far side of the ring, their faces, when the flame caught a fresh piece of heather and leapt, going from shadow to sudden red and back to shadow as the light moved. Sorcha sat with her hurt hand wrapped and held loosely in her lap and watched the fire the way a woman watches a sick child through its worst night, not trusting it yet, not looking away from it either. Neachdan sat across from her with the last of the dark, half-burned lump of the rainbow-coloured stone turning slowly in his fingers, its colours long since gone the way of all the others, black and glowing at its heart like any other coal, indistinguishable now from the plainest grey chip he had ever split from a wall.

When the grey light of the true dawn came in under the door, thin and cold and utterly ordinary, the fire was still burning, banked low but alive, in the centre of the floor where no fire had ever burned before. Neachdan looked at it a long while, and then at his wife's bound hand, and at his sleeping children reddened by its glow rather than blued by the cold as every dawn of their short lives had found them until that one, and he said the only thing that any teller of the mason's fire has ever agreed he said, the words that are given, in the short form, before the long reciting of the fathers begins at every hearth on the night of the lightning even now.

He said it must not go out.

He did not say it as an order given lightly, the way a man tells a child to shut a door; he said it kneeling by the fire with his cracked hand flat on the cold earth beside it, the way a man swears an oath over something he does not fully understand and is afraid, all the same, to lose. Sorcha, from across the ring, said that it would not, not while she had hands to feed it, and held up the one that had already paid the fire's first toll as though it were a pledge rather than a wound; and Conall, waking at the sound of their voices, looked from his mother's bandaged hand to his father's blackened knuckles to the low red heart of the thing between them, and asked, in the plain way of a child too young yet to know what a question like that would cost his whole line to answer, what it was for.

Neither of them had an answer for him that morning. It would be nine hundred years, and eighteen fathers after Conall's own line was old, before anyone stood in a grove far to the north and asked the same question of a stranger made of smoke, and got, at last, the beginning of one. But that telling belongs to another hearth-night, and this one, the tellers say, ends here: with the rain gone off the valley, the well still uncut to water, and a fire on a swept ring of earth that had not been there the morning before, and has not, by any hearth it was ever carried to, gone out since.
