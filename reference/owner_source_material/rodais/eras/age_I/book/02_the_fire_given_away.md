# Of the Fire Given Away

*VE 1 – 71*

## I. Of the Vein Left Burning

The well-cut above the valley did not fill in. No one would let it. For a hand of years after the night of the lightning the broken vein burned on down in the rock, low and dark, and the mason's household kept a path worn to it as a path is worn to a spring. It did not flare as it had flared under the rain; it lay in its crack like a sleeping thing, breathing a small heat, and whoever went to it went quietly, the way a person goes near a sleeping child, not to wake it wrong.

Neachdan would not let the well be filled, and would not let it be dug deeper either, though there were those who came from three valleys off only to look down into it and see the black stone glowing at the bottom like an ember under ash. He set two flat stones across the mouth of the cut, a hand's breadth apart, so that no one could fall in and no rain could quite put it out, and he told his children that the fire in the ground was the same fire as the fire on the floor, and that both must be fed and neither trusted.

There was no counting of the years then, not by anyone on the island, and the household did not say "the third year" or "the fifth". They said "before Ailbhe could walk" or "the spring the ewes came early" or "since the lightning," and let it go at that. But it is told that in the fourth spring after the well was struck, a girl was born to Neachdan and Sorcha at the hearth, so close to the flame that the women turned her a little sideways with their hands lest a spark should catch her hair, and that she was the first child of the island ever to open her eyes by firelight and not by the light of the sky alone. They named her Ailbhe. The hearth was already old enough by then that no one troubled to remark on the strangeness of a birth beside a fire; only the oldest women said, low, that a child born so would know the fire better than her own kin, and it was not meant unkindly, and it was not wrong.

The boy Conall was six when his father brought the first coal home under his cloak, and he grew through those years the way a boy grows near a thing he has been told never to let go out: watchful of it before he was watchful of anything else. He learned to read the fire the way another child learns to read weather. He knew the difference between the low steady red that meant it would keep till morning and the paler flutter that meant it was hungry, and he knew which wood answered it kindly and which spat and starved it, and long before he was a man grown he could tell, walking into a house from the cold, whether its fire had been fed with love or with laziness, by nothing but the smell of the room. His father set him, young, to watch the well-cut through the short nights of summer, when the household's own hearth needed less minding, and he would sit on the two flat stones with his knees drawn up and look down at the vein burning alone in the dark, and think, as a child thinks, that it did not need him at all, and was glad of him anyway.

It is the household's own telling, kept since, that no fire went out in Neachdan's house in all those first years, not once, not even in the wet spring when the roof let water onto the floor two nights running and every ember in the valley but theirs went dark. Whether that telling is wholly true no one now can say; households make of their own beginnings what they can bear to keep. But there is this much under it: that the fire the mason carried up out of the ground did not go out, that Conall watched it with more care than he gave his own hands in a cold winter, and that the vein down in the rock burned low and steady through all of it, unregarded by most of the valley and utterly regarded by one small household, waiting, though none of them yet knew for what.

## II. Of the Night the Hearth Died

It went out at last in the fifth year, in the black hour before the earliest light, when Conall was eleven and the household had grown careless the way any household grows careless of a thing that has never once failed it.

There had been three days of rain driving sideways off the high ground, the kind that finds every gap in a roof of turf and heather no matter how well it is laid, and on the third night a gust came down the valley strong enough to lift a corner of thatch clean off the byre and lay it across the yard. Water came through where the thatch had been and ran along a rafter and dripped, patient as a slow thief, directly onto the hearth-stones. Neachdan was from home that night, gone two valleys off to look at a spring some other household hoped might be theirs to claim, and Sorcha slept heavily with the new baby at her side, and it was Conall who woke first, in the deep cold hour, to a darkness in the house that was not the ordinary darkness of night.

He knew before he was fully awake what it meant. Every night of his life he had slept within reach of a red glow on the floor, faint even through closed eyes, and its absence woke him the way a silence wakes a person more surely than a noise. His hand went flat on the hearth-stones and found them wet and cold, and when he raked through the black wet mess with his fingers there was no spark in it anywhere, not the smallest eye of red, nothing.

He did not wake his mother. He has told, since, that he thought of it, and did not, because he could not think what she would do that he was not already doing, and because some part of him, eleven years old and frightened in the dark, wanted the thing mended before anyone else knew it had broken.

The clay pot that stood by the door, the one his mother used for carrying water from the burn, went into his hands, and he went out into the rain in his bare feet with nothing over his shoulders but a fold of hide, and he ran. It was black as the inside of a closed hand and the ground was slick underfoot and he fell once, hard, on his knee on a stone, and did not stop to feel it. He ran the whole way to the well-cut by the path his own feet had worn to it, sure of it in the dark the way a hand is sure of a door it has opened ten thousand times, and he came over the two flat stones and looked down, and the vein was there, low and red and breathing exactly as it always breathed, indifferent to the storm and to him and to everything but its own burning.

He had never taken anything from it before. That had always been his father's work, or his father's father's, though there was no father's father now to remember. He lay flat on the wet stone with the pot in both hands and reached down into the cut as far as his arm would go, and the heat of it came up into his face, and he scraped a burning piece of the vein into the mouth of the pot with a scrap of slate, working fast because his arm was shaking with the strain of the reach and because the rain was trying to fill the pot before the coal could sit in it. He got one piece in, no bigger than a hen's egg, glowing through a crust of grey, and he packed cold dead ash from the rim of the cut in around it, the way he had seen his father bank a fire at night, and he held the pot against his chest under the fold of hide and ran home with it as he had run out, only slower now, every step careful, a boy carrying a small ember of a sleeping thing eleven years old and understanding for the first time that a fire need not stay in one place at all. It could be moved.

He tipped it out onto the wet hearth-stones and blew on it with his whole chest and fed it the driest tinder he could find by feel in the dark, a fistful of bracken kept dry under the roof-beam for kindling, kept dry, that one night, by nothing but luck. The fire caught slowly and then all at once, the way fire always does, going from nothing to a small red mouth to a flame standing up off the hearth in the space of a few breaths, and by the time Sorcha woke, roused at last by the light on the wall rather than by any sound her son had made, the hearth was burning as though it had never stopped, and Conall was sitting cross-legged before it with his knee bleeding onto the floor and the empty water-pot still clutched in both hands, and he did not know, himself, whether to be proud or ashamed, and so he said nothing, and let his mother think what she liked.

That hearth still tells it, and every hearth fire has reached since has told it too: that this was the night it was learned that fire need not stay where it is born, that it can be carried, a live coal in a clay pot banked round with its own ash, from one fire to light another. Before that night the household had thought of its fire the way it thought of the well-cut, a fixed thing, rooted in one place in the ground. After it, they held a small living thing that could be taken up and moved, and given, if they chose to give it, and a boy of eleven with wet feet and a water-pot had proved it could be done.

## III. Of the Hut on the Heather

The first house to burn stood not half a mile off, at the near end of the same slope, the house of a household that had begged fire from Neachdan's hearth two winters before and kept it faithfully since, tending it as carefully as anyone tends a thing given by a neighbour rather than found by a father's own hand.

It went wrong on an ordinary evening, no storm in it, no blame that anyone could afterwards lay at any one door. The fire had been laid, as fire was laid in most houses then, on a bed of old heather packed down hard over years until it seemed as good as stone, because heather was what lay to hand on that slope and no one had yet been burned badly enough to think otherwise. A coal rolled, or a child kicked at it in play, or the wind found a gap under the door-hide and breathed on it too long while the household slept; which of these it was, the tellers never agreed, and in the end it did not matter. What mattered was that the packed heather under the fire caught from beneath, unseen, and smouldered there for the better part of an hour doing nothing that any waking eye would have marked, and then broke into open flame all along its length at once, under a roof of the same dry heather thatch, and the whole of it went up together, roof and floor and walls of turf, faster than the household inside could rightly understand what was happening to them.

They got out. That much was mercy: a mother, a father, three children, all out into the cold with nothing saved but each other and the clothes they slept in, and they stood in the yard and watched the one warm, dry, safe thing they owned burn itself to nothing in less time than it takes to walk to the burn and back, and the youngest child screamed the whole while, too small to fear the fire, only screaming because her mother's arms were gripping her too hard to breathe in.

Conall was among the first from the near houses to come with water, though there was nothing by then that water could do but wet the ground around the ruin and keep the fire from crossing to the dry grass beyond. Sixteen that year, a young man's height on him already, he stood in the yard in the smoke and the firelight and looked at the family gathered together in the ruin of their own warmth. His mind went to his own fire on its stones, with the two flat rocks laid over the well-cut a mile off, sitting always on ground that could never catch. Something his father had never quite put into words, though he had lived it every day since the lightning, stood plain before him now: a fire on stone can be fed and watched and trusted, and a fire on heather, however faithfully kept, is a fire lying on its own fuel, waiting only for a moment's inattention to remember what it is.

He said so, that night, to the household that had lost its roof, gently, for grief needs no scolding, and he said it again in the days after to anyone who would stand and listen, and he was young enough that some of the old men smiled at a boy telling them their business. But he had carried a coal alone through a storm at eleven, and there were those who had not forgotten it, and slowly, over that year and the ones after, the word went about the near hearths that a fire wants stone under it if it is to be trusted through a night, not the packed heather of the floor it stands on, and here and there a household began to lay flat stones gathered from the burn-bed beneath the hearth before it lit its fire there at all. It did not happen everywhere at once. Old ways die slowly on a hillside, and there were fires laid on heather for a generation and more after that night, in houses too poor in stone or too far from good rock to do otherwise. But the household that had lost its roof rebuilt on stone, and the near hearths that had watched it burn did the same, and it is from that year, the tellers say, that the custom began, stone under every fire the mason's line has ever given.

## IV. Of Ailbhe and the Reach to the Northern Moor

Ailbhe grew up beside two fires at once, her mother's on the hearth and the vein's own down in the cut, and by the time she was old enough to be trusted with a pot in her hands she knew both better than any of her brothers did, for she had been set from her earliest years to watch the hearth while the others were sent to the herding or the digging, being the youngest daughter and thought too small yet for harder work. It was said of her, later, and said kindly, that she had learned to walk holding a coal at arm's length before she had learned to walk holding her mother's hand, which cannot be wholly true, but is not far from what those who knew her as a girl believed.

It fell to her, as she came into her growth, to be the one who carried the fire outward, because her brothers were needed at other work and because she had, from watching it so long, a steadiness with a live coal that older and stronger hands lacked. When a household two valleys off sent word that their own fire had died and they had heard, as everyone by then had heard, that the mason's people kept a fire that never failed, it was Ailbhe who was sent with a pot banked in ash and a fold of hide to keep the wind from it, walking the whole way herself, a girl not yet grown, carrying a small red heart of the family's fire out across country that most of the valley had never troubled to cross on foot before.

Neachdan never once refused it. That, too, is remembered and told, that though the fire had cost him a well he could no longer use for water and a debt of care his household paid every day of its life, he would not hear of keeping it back from any hearth that asked, and told his children plainly that a fire withheld from a cold house was a worse thing than a fire given away and never thanked for. So Ailbhe walked it, spring and autumn, summer when the light let her go further and winter when it did not, to the near valleys first and then, as she grew surer of the ground and the ground grew more used to her coming, further out: south along the slope to households that had only heard of the mason's fire by report, west to where the burn ran into wider country, and at last, in the year she came fully into her strength, north across the shoulder of the high ground to the edge of the moor that lay at the limit of any household's grazing, a day's walk each way and more if the weather turned.

It was slow work and it was not without its cost. A coal could die in the pot on a hard, wet crossing no matter how carefully it was banked, and there were journeys Ailbhe made twice over because the fire had gone out on her halfway and she would not turn for home without it lit, walking back to the last hearth she had passed to beg a fresh coal rather than arrive empty-handed at the one she was going to. There were households, too, proud or wary, that would not at first take fire from a girl of another valley, thinking there was some obligation in it they did not want to owe; but fire in a cold house wears down pride faster than argument does, and in the end every hearth that Ailbhe reached kept what she brought it and looked for her again.

By the time the fire had been carried, hearth by hearth, as far as the northern moor, no one could any longer say how many households now kept, on their own floors, a flame that had first burned in a well a day and more away. The mason's own fire had not multiplied by magic; it had simply been walked outward, one live coal at a time, by a girl who had grown up believing that a fire not shared was a fire not fully understood, and the valley that had once known one hearth in all its length now knew dozens, none of them less warm for the giving, and all of them, in the telling that mattered most to the households that kept them, still one fire.

## V. Of Neachdan's Death and the Long Watch

Neachdan died by his own hearth, an old man by then for that hard country, in the year his grandchildren had begun to outnumber his children round the fire he had carried home under his cloak. In his last years he grew slow and would sit long hours by the flame doing nothing that any onlooker could call work, and those who watched him closest said he was listening to it, the way a man listens to a stream he has crossed his whole life and never once heard the same twice. Near the end he asked to be carried out one last time to see the well-cut, and they carried him, and he looked down for a long while at the vein still burning steadily in the dark of the rock, unchanged in all the years since the lightning had found it, and said, of it, only that it had outlasted him and would outlast his sons too. He was not wrong. That same night he died at the hearth he had lit, with the fire he had carried home burning beside him as it had burned every night of his life since, and the household kept it fed through the whole of the mourning without once letting it fall, because that, by then, was understood without needing to be said.

It was in the years after his death that Conall, grown into the eldest man of a household grown large, set down in words what he had first felt standing in the smoke of a burning roof: that fire belongs on stone and nowhere else, that no hearth in the valley should be laid on packed heather or bare turf if flat stone could be had and carried to it, and that this should stand, from that day, as every household's law that took fire from the mason's line. He said it standing, as the fathers' names were said standing, at a gathering of the near hearths in the dark half of the year, and though he had no more right to command another man's fire than any neighbour has, the households that heard him kept it, for they too had stood in the smoke of the burned roof, or had heard of it from those who had, and a rule that answers a fear already known is a rule that needs little enforcing.

Through all the years after, Conall kept the watch his father had kept before him, sitting by the well-cut through the dark half of every year when the fire in men's houses is most needed and most easily lost, and it became known among the valley's children, long before they knew why, that the two flat stones over the black cut were not to be gone near without an elder by, and that the old man who sat there through the cold months, saying little, watching the vein burn low and red in its crack in the rock, was not to be disturbed at his work, though no child rightly understood what the work was. He grew old at it. His hands, which had once reached down into the burning cut at eleven with a water-pot and a scrap of slate, grew stiff and slow, and in his last years it was his own son who carried the coals out to him and sat with him through the coldest nights, learning without being taught the same watchfulness his father had learned from his father before that.

Conall died as Neachdan had died, by a hearth, in the fullness of a long life for that country, with the vein in the well-cut still burning as it had burned since the night of the lightning and showing, in all the years of his watching, no sign that it meant ever to stop. His son took the watch from him without any word of ceremony, the way a man takes up a tool his father has just set down, and sat that same winter through the dark half of the year on the two flat stones above the cut, and looked down at the fire that had now outlived two of the mason's line, and understood, as his father and his father's father had understood before him, that it was not his to let go out.
