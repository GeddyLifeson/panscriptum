# Of the Empty Rings and the Stripped Altar

*VE 4,124 – 4,195*

## I. Of the Last Fire in the Rings

The three rings of wall on the height near the south-eastern coast had held fire along their circuit for longer than any hand still living within them could say it had first seen one lit. In the last winter that fire burned there it burned no differently than it had ever burned: a chain of small fires set along the inner face of the middle wall, each tended by whoever was nearest when it wanted feeding, so that a watcher standing at the gate could look along the curve of the rampart on a clear night and see a string of low lights going away from him in both directions until the wall's own bend hid the rest.

That winter there were fewer hands than fires. Since the cistern was found full of bone, every household had carried its water up from the valley springs on its own back, a whole morning's labour every morning, and some had grown tired of the carrying before they grew tired of the height, and gone down. Those who were left let the farthest fires go dark first and fed what peat they had toward the fires nearest the gate, so that the string of lights grew shorter at both ends and thicker in the middle, like a rope fraying from its outer strands inward toward the one core that still held.

A woman who had tended the second fire from the gate for as many winters as she could count on both hands was the last of them awake on the last night. She had buried, in the two years before, more of her own kin than she had watched grow old: a husband who went from a cough to a grave inside one wet spring, a sister's whole household taken by the same spring or by the thin harvest after it, no one troubling any longer to say which. The rest of her blood had gone down the track from the gate one by one, some saying they went only for the winter and meaning it, and none of them had come back.

She had gone as far as the gate herself, more than once, on mornings when the track looked less like a way down than like the only sensible answer to a question the height had stopped troubling to ask. Each time she had turned back to her fire. A wall built to hold four hundred ought not to be left holding none, she had told herself, and her staying was a kind of tending too, no different from the peat she fed the flame each evening, only harder to see burn.

She fed it that night from the last of the store beside her, peat cut two summers before by hands that were no longer there to cut more, and sat with her back to the wall and watched it take. The wind came up off the valleys below as it always came after dark, carrying turf-smoke from some hearth low down that was still lit, and wet stone, and the cold clean breath of ground that never quite dried between one shower and the next. She sat in it a long while doing nothing more than any woman does at her own hearth on an ordinary night, and fed the fire again when it wanted it, and sat on.

In the morning she went the length of the middle wall to see what other fires wanted tending, as she had gone every morning of those two years, and found every one of them dark. Some had been banked to nothing by careful hands. Some were simply out, the peat beside them untouched, as though whoever had tended them last had walked away in the middle of the tending. She walked the whole circuit before she let herself believe it, out past the far bend where the ground fell away and the wind came harder, and found the same cold stone and the same untouched peat everywhere, and stood there a while looking out over the valley rather than back at the wall.

She did not light the far fires. She went back to her own, which still held a little heat under yesterday's peat, and sat by it that day and the day after; and on the third day, finding no one left to share the tending and no reason she could give herself to go on keeping a fire in a ring of wall that no one else needed, she spread the last of the embers thin across the stone, the way a fire is spread when a household means it to die and not to smoulder on unwatched, and walked out through the gate and down the track her kin had gone down before her, carrying nothing but what she had carried in.

The fires along the middle wall were not lit again. The gate she walked out through was never afterward closed by any hand, and stood open through every season that followed, and within a few winters grass had found a footing in its threshold, where the feet of four hundred had once kept the stone worn bare.

## II. Of the Ones Who Went Down

They went down in ones and in small kin-groups, over years and not in any one season, and none of the landings that took them in ever spoke of a single day when the high places emptied, only of finding, season on season, more faces at the shore fires than the shore had raised, and fewer questions asked of where a face had come from than might have been asked in an easier time.

A boy of one of the western landings, too young to remember the height his grandfather had come down from, grew up knowing only that the old man did not speak of the hills the way the landing's other old men spoke of the sea, with a store of small sure facts about a place they had spent their whole lives in. Asked what the height had been like, his grandfather would say only that it was cold there in a way the shore never was, a wind that found a man through wool doubled over wool, and that a fire there wanted feeding oftener than a fire anywhere he had lived since. He would not say why he had left, or when, or whether he had meant at the leaving to go back; and once, when the boy pressed harder than he usually dared, the old man said that a place could go on being a man's own long after it had stopped wanting him in it, and that this was not a thing a boy who had never lived on a height would understand, nor one he wished his grandson ever to have cause to.

It was the same at landing after landing along every coast: old men and old women who had come down from one height or another in their youth, settled among kin who had never gone up at all, and keeping whatever they kept of the high years to themselves. Some brought down small things the height had let them carry, a worked stone, a tool no one on the shore had a use for and no one asked the use of, kept on a shelf afterward or laid in the stone box with its owner when the time came. Some brought down nothing but what they stood in, having judged that a height given up was a height to be left whole and not picked over on the way out. The middens at the southern landings grew faster in those years than in any years before them, and a landing that had once counted its households by the dozen came, within two lifetimes of the last fire in the rings, to count them by the score.

None of the shore's own people ever gave a reason for the coming down, beyond the plain fact of it. What was said, by more than one old mouth at more than one fire, was only this: that a thing begun wants explaining and a thing given up does not; that a people troubles itself over why it went somewhere, and lets the coming back stand.

## III. Of a Household Come Down to the Kilns

One household that came down from the rings in those years came down to Morn, where the seven kilns and the eighth and the ninth still stood above the shingle as they had stood for longer than anyone at that landing could say, and it is told of that household more than of most, because it came down with nothing and had to learn the shore again from the beginning.

There were five of them: a man and his wife, both past the middle of their lives, and their son, and the son's wife, and a girl of eight who had been born inside the innermost ring and had never in her life seen the sea. They came down the old track from the height with what they could carry on their backs and one ox, and the ox died of a fall on the scree below the outer ring on the first morning, so that they came the rest of the way with only their backs. It took them nine days. The girl asked, on every one of the nine, how much further the sea was, and on the ninth her grandfather lifted her onto a rock at the top of the last rise above Morn and turned her to face it, and she looked at it a long while and said nothing at all, and then asked whether it ever stopped moving.

The landing took them in. There was no telling afterward of any argument over it; a household come down with nothing was a thing every landing on that coast had seen by then, some years more than one, and the ground above the shingle had room in it for another roof. What the landing did not do was feed them for nothing, for no landing on that coast had ever fed a strong back for nothing, and within the month the man and his son were out in the boats with the landing's own crews, sick over the side for the first week and then not, and the two women were set to the kilns under the eye of the woman who kept the middle kiln that year.

It was here that the household's learning went hardest. On the height a fire was a thing a person fed to keep warm and to cook by, and fed well, and was glad of when it blazed; and the keeper of the middle kiln, watching the son's wife build up a kiln-fire on her first morning until it roared under the racks, took her by the wrist without a word and led her to the next kiln, where the peat lay smouldering so low that the smoke came up thick and white and no flame showed at all, and held her hand flat over the rack there a long while, until the young woman understood by the heat on her palm what she had been told three times in words and had not understood. The fish on the racks above her own roaring fire had begun to cook where they should have been smoking, and were fit that evening for eating and for nothing else; and that was the whole of the lesson, and it was not given twice.

The older woman learned it faster, having less pride to lose in the learning, and it was she, in the end, who kept the eighth kiln for the rest of her life, and was known on that shingle as a keeper as good as any born there, save that she would never feed the fire from the landward side of the kiln but always from the seaward, and would not say why, and the landing let her have her way in it because the fish she smoked kept as well as any fish on that coast. Her husband never grew easy in a boat. He took to mending nets instead, and sat through the long evenings on the shingle below the kilns with the cord running through his fingers, looking up now and again at the dark line of the hills inland where the height stood that he had come down from, and he did not speak of it, and no one of the landing asked him to.

The girl who had never seen the sea grew up on that shingle as though she had been born to it. She learned to gut and to rack and to feel the heat of a kiln with the flat of her hand before she was twelve, and to handle a small boat before she was fifteen, and when she was grown she married a man of the landing whose people had kept the fourth kiln since before anyone could remember, and bore him children who were never told, by anyone, that their mother had been born inside three rings of wall on a hill above the southern lowland, because by then no one at Morn thought it a thing worth the telling. She told them herself once, when she was old, on an evening when the wind came off the land and not off the sea and carried a smell of heather down onto the shingle; and they listened politely, as children listen to an old woman's tale, and asked her whether the sea could be seen from there, and she said that it could not, and that she had not known, until she came down, that it was a thing a person could be without.

## IV. Of the Prising of the Altar

The temple on its stepped platform in the eastern hills was tended some years after the rings had gone dark and Làrach an Dùin-fhaire's gate had been closed, by fewer people each year, and by the end by no more than one kin of the valley below it, who went up on the shortest morning as their grandparents had gone up, and stood about the great stone while the light came over the sea behind them and ran along its top toward the mountains, and went down again.

Three of them climbed the steps with prising-bars on their backs on a grey morning at the fall of the year, with a thin rain in it, and the bars were long lengths of seasoned oak, shod at their ends with bone, the kind a household uses to lever a boulder out of a field. One of the three was the eldest of that kin, a man past sixty who had climbed the steps on the shortest morning every year of his life that he could remember, and he went up slower than the other two and did not ask them to wait for him.

They worked at the altar's top stone for the better part of the morning. The block had been set into the step below it in two sockets cut deep into the rock, and bedded there with clay and small stone so long ago that the clay had gone as hard as the rock around it, and the two younger men had to break it out a finger's width at a time with the points of the bars before they could get any purchase at all. The old man did not work the bars. He stood at the stone's far edge with both hands flat on its top, where the light used to run on the shortest morning, as if he were holding it steady against a shifting it had not yet begun to make.

It came free a while past noon. It canted first to one side, groaning in its sockets, and then with a shift of the bars it lifted clear of them and was lowered, not dropped, onto the step, and lay there whole. The three of them stood over it without speaking, as men stand over a thing whose weight has only just left their hands and whose next weight, the carrying of it on, they have not yet taken up.

They carried it down. They slung it between them on the bars in a net of hide rope and took it down the steps one step at a time, resting at every third, and out across the hillside below toward the west, where the ground fell away into the valley; and a little before dark they went over the shoulder of the hill with it, all three together, walking slowly under the weight, and the platform behind them stood with its top step empty.

The telling goes no further with them, and the stone was never found. On the top step the two sockets were left standing open, sharp-edged where the bars had broken out the old clay, and they did not soften with the years as a path softens or a doorsill, for a socket cut to hold a great weight keeps its edges as long as the rock around it keeps its shape. What had been cut on the top of the stone, if anything had been cut there, went with it.

## V. Of the Cairn on the Steps

After the stone was gone no one of that kin climbed the steps on the shortest morning again. The grass came in between the fitted slabs where it had never been let grow, and the last step, the easy one that every child of the valley had learned to find with a bare foot in the dark, was lost under turf within a few years, so that the climb began now with a stumble where it had once begun with no more than the least catch underfoot.

Some years after the prising, the old man who had stood with his hands on the stone while the others worked the bars went up alone. He was very old by then and went up slowly, a step and a rest and a step, with a stick; and at the top, below the empty sockets, where a person climbing would set his last foot before the platform, he built a small cairn. He built it out of the loose stones the prising had broken from the edges of the sockets, and out of others he gathered from the scree below, fitting each one to the one beneath it and tapering the whole of it toward a flat cap-stone set square on top, as square as the altar had been set. It took him most of a day. Then he went down again, and did not go up after.

He did not say what the cairn was for, to his kin or to anyone, and no one asked him, for it was plain enough to those who watched him come down with his hands scraped raw that he did not mean to be asked. It stood below the empty sockets after he was dead, kept by no one and added to by no one, weathering as unmortared stone weathers, a little of its shape lost to every hard frost, until in the end it stood no higher than a man's shin, and to an eye that did not know to look for it might have passed for a fall of loose stone off the step above.

## VI. Of the Rain in the Sockets

So the temple stood stripped, though it was not yet wholly forsaken; now and then someone of the valley still climbed its steps, and stood a while by the empty sockets, and came down again. The platform stood as the old man had left it: grass thickening between its stones, the small cairn below the top step, and the two sockets holding, in place of the stone that had been set square to the central mountains, whatever the sky over the eastern hills chose to put in them.

It was rain, oftener than anything. That country's weather was mild and wet and seldom dry for long between one shower and the next, and on any day after rain the two sockets held a little standing water apiece, dark with whatever had blown into them, and if the light fell right and the water lay still, the sky lay open in each of them, cut to the square shape the stone had once filled. From a little way off they looked less like two holes than like two small mirrors set into the hill, giving back whatever passed over them and keeping nothing of what had stood there.

In the south-east the shaft at Toll-dubh stood open as it had always stood. Under the mountain Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh ran on, and the sick were still carried up to the basins, though by fewer hands each year, for the landings they came from were further off now than they had been when the heights were full, and the way up was long. Làrach an Dùin-fhaire and the rings stood empty, and the temple stripped, within a few days' walk of one another, each left to the weather in its own year and its own way; and on every coast the landings filled, and the smoke of more hearths went up over the shingle than the shore had ever carried, and a people who had gone up into the hills came down to the sea again and did not say why.
