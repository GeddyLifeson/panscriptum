# Of the Empty Rings and the Stripped Altar

*VE 4,124 – 4,195*

## I. Of the Last Fire in the Rings

The three rings of wall had held fire along their whole circuit for longer than any hand still living among the coast-people could say they had first seen it lit, and in the last winter that it burned there it burned no differently than it had ever burned: a chain of small fires set along the inner face of the middle wall, each tended by whoever was nearest when it wanted feeding, so that a watcher standing at the gate could have looked along the curve of the rampart and seen, on a clear night, a string of low lights going away from him in both directions until the wall's own bend hid the rest.

That winter there were fewer hands to tend it than the wall had fires to hold, and the ones who were left had come, without ever saying so aloud to one another, to let the farthest fires go dark first and feed what oil and peat they had toward the ones nearest the gate, so that a watcher on that last night would have seen the string of lights grown shorter at both ends and thicker, if anything, in the middle, like a rope fraying from its outer strands inward toward the one core that still held.

A woman who had tended the second fire from the gate for as many winters as she could count on both hands was the last of them awake that night. She had buried, within the two years before, more of her own kin than she had watched grow old beside her: a husband who had gone from a cough to a grave inside one wet spring, a sister's whole household taken by the same wet spring or by the thin harvests that came after it, no one troubling any longer to say which. The ones who were left of her own blood had gone, one by one over that same short span, down the track that ran from the fortress's gate toward the lower valleys, some of them saying they went only for the winter and meaning it when they said it, and not one of them having come back before the fire she tended now burned low enough to want feeding again.

She had gone as far as the gate herself, more than once in those two years, on mornings when the track looked less like a way down and more like the only sensible answer to a question the height had stopped troubling to ask her. Each time she had gone back to her fire instead, telling herself, by what little of her own reasoning ever came down in the tellings that outside ears afterward built around her, that a wall meant to hold four hundred people ought not to be left holding none, as though her own staying were a kind of tending, no different in kind from the peat she fed the flame each evening, only harder to see burn.

She fed it that last night from what was left in the stone-lined store beside her own fire, peat cut two summers before by hands that were no longer there to cut more, and sat with her back to the wall watching it take, the way she had sat and watched it take on a thousand other nights that had not been the last one, though she did not yet know, sitting there, that this one was. The wind came up off the three valleys below as it always came up after dark, carrying nothing she had not smelled every night of her life on that height, turf-smoke from some hearth low enough down to still be lit, wet stone, the cold clean breath of ground that never quite dried between one shower and the next; and she sat within it a long while, by the account the tellers built of her, doing nothing more remarkable than any woman does at her own hearth on any ordinary night, until the fire had burned down far enough to want feeding again and she fed it, and it took, and she sat on.

It was only in the morning, going the length of the middle wall to see what other fires might want tending, as she had gone every morning of the two years she had kept that walk alone, that she found every one of them dark: some banked to nothing by the same hands that had banked hers on other mornings, some simply out, the peat beside them untouched, as though whoever had tended them last had walked away in the middle of the tending and not come back to finish it. She walked the whole circuit before she let herself believe it, out past the middle wall's furthest bend where the ground fell away toward the northern valley and the wind came harder, and found the same cold stone and the same untouched peat at every point along it, and stood there some while, by every account the telling ever gave of that morning, looking out at the valley rather than back at the wall, as a woman looks at the one direction still open to her once every other has been quietly closed.

She did not feed those far fires, but went back to her own, which still held a little heat under yesterday's peat, and sat by it the rest of that day and the day after, and on the third day, finding no one left to share the tending with and no reason she could give herself, sitting alone in a ring of wall built to hold four hundred, to go on holding a fire that fewer than four hundred now had any use for, she let it go down herself, spreading the last of the embers thin across the stone the way a fire is spread when a household means it to die rather than to smoulder on unwatched, and walked out through the gate and down the same track her kin had gone down before her, carrying nothing from the place but what she had carried into it years before.

Of her after, no teller of the hill country ever had an account to give, for she went down among her own people and not among the hill-folk, and whatever her own people made of her coming, and of the fire she had let go out behind her, stayed inside that people's own keeping, as everything of theirs that was not seen from a distance always had.

The fires along the middle wall were not lit again. The cistern within the innermost ring, which had already stood some years fouled past any drawing, went on standing so, and the gate that the woman had walked out through was never afterward closed by any hand, standing open through every season that followed to whatever weather came against it, so that within a few winters the first grass had begun to find a footing in the gate's own threshold, where the passing of four hundred pairs of feet a day had once kept the stone worn bare. A shepherd of the near valley, grazing a flock nearer the height than shepherds of that country commonly grazed, found the gate so a full turn of the seasons after the woman had gone through it, and stood at it a while without going in, for a wall does not need a raised voice or a lit watch-fire to keep a stranger at its threshold; an open gate with no one troubling to guard it will do the same work, in its own way, on a mind used to finding such gates shut.

## II. Of the Ones Who Went Down

They went down in ones and in small kin-groups, over years rather than in any single season, by every account the shore afterward kept of its own swelling numbers, and none of the landings that took them in ever spoke of a single day when the high places emptied all at once, only of finding, season on season, more faces at the shore fires that the shore had not raised, and fewer questions asked of where a face had come from than might have been asked of a stranger in an easier time.

A boy of one of the western landings, too young in those years to remember the height his own grandfather was said to have come down from, grew up knowing only that his grandfather did not speak of the hills the way the landing's other old men spoke of the sea, with a store of small sure facts about a place they had spent their whole lives inside. His grandfather, asked what the fortress had been like, would say only that it had been cold on the height in a way the shore never was cold, wind that found a man through wool doubled over wool, and that a fire fed there had wanted feeding oftener than a fire fed anywhere he had lived since. He would not, however the boy pressed him, say why he had left it, or when, or whether he had meant, at the time of the leaving, to come back; and once, the boy told it afterward to his own children, when he had pressed harder than he usually dared, the old man had said only that a place could go on being a man's own long after it had stopped wanting him in it, and that this was not a thing a boy who had never lived on a height would understand, nor one he much wished his grandson ever to have cause to.

It was the same, by every telling the shore kept of that half-century, at landing after landing along every coast the coast-people held: old men and old women who had come down from one height or another in their youth, settled now among kin who had never gone up at all, keeping whatever they kept of the high years to themselves the way a people keeps a grief too old to want company. Some brought down with them small things the height had let them carry, a worked stone, a tool no one on the shore had a use for and no one asked the use of, kept afterward on a shelf or buried with the one who had carried it, and some brought down nothing at all beyond the clothes they stood in, having judged, whatever their reasons, that a height given up was a height to be left whole rather than picked over on the way out. The middens at the southern landing grew faster in those years than they had grown in living memory, thick with the ordinary leavings of shellfish and bone that a shore people's numbers always left behind them, and a landing that had once counted its households by the dozen came, within two generations of the fortress's last fire, to count them by the score.

At one of the northern landings, a woman old enough by then to have buried a husband and outlived two of her own children was heard, by a hill traveller who had come near enough that once to trade rather than merely to watch, to say a thing of the height that the traveller carried home whole because he judged, rightly or not, that he had understood every word of it though it was given him in no tongue he could himself have spoken back. She had pointed, the traveller said, up toward the dark shape of a ridge inland, and said something short, and touched her own chest after, and it was the touching of the chest, more than anything in the sounds themselves, that made him certain, carrying the account down to the moor, that she spoke of the height plainly, the way a person speaks of a thing once loved and no longer kept, and neither in anger nor in any relief at having left it.

None of the shore's own tellers, then or later, ever gave a reason for the coming-down, beyond the plain fact of it happening, season on season, until the height stood emptier than the shore had ever known it and the shore stood fuller than the height had ever left it. What was said, and said more than once, by more than one old mouth at more than one fire, was only this: that a people who had gone up into the hills for reasons no outside ear had ever been given had come back down again for reasons just as closely kept, and that the coming-down, unlike the going-up, seemed to trouble no one among them enough to want an answer put to it. A thing given up, the shore's own way of putting it ran, wants no more telling than a thing begun; it is the beginning that a people troubles itself to explain, and the giving-up that it lets stand.

## III. Of the Prising of the Altar

The temple stood apart from both the outpost and the fortress, on its stepped platform in the eastern hills, its top step set square to the central mountains as it had been set from the first raising of it, and it went on being tended, by whatever hands still tended it, some years after the fortress's fires had gone dark and the outpost's gate had stood open to the weather. What tending it had was seen, when it was seen at all, only from the same safe distance that had carried every other account of the coast-people's high places north to the hill country: figures moving on the platform's steps at first light, on mornings that no outside watcher could ever fix to any pattern of season or moon, and gone again before the sun stood high enough to show more of them than their shapes.

A herder of the eastern moor, whose own grazing brought him within sight of the platform two or three mornings in most years, was the nearest thing the hill country had to a steady witness of that tending in the years it still went on, though even his account, carried down at second and third hand by the time it reached any hearth far from his own, amounted to little more than a habit of watching kept up over a long life and never once rewarded with anything a teller could build much of a tale from. He said, when he was asked, that the mornings the coast-people climbed were not tied to any season he could name, coming sometimes twice in a month and sometimes not at all for the better part of a year, and that what was done on the platform in those hours, whether rite or labour or something between the two, was done too far off and too briefly for even the sharpest eye to carry home more than a shape against the sky and a stillness after. He had never, in all his years of watching, seen a fire lit on the platform itself, nor heard, at that distance, anything that could fairly be called a voice raised in speech or in chant; only the coming and the going, and the altar standing square to the mountains between one morning's climb and the next.

The last of that tending, so far as any outside eye ever marked it, came in a year no teller troubled to set down more nearly than "after the fortress, and before the shore stood as full as it later stood," when three of the coast-people were seen climbing the platform's steps in the grey of a morning with tools slung at their backs that no watcher, then or later, could put a sure name to: long bars of some dark wood or worked bone, carried the way a man carries a thing he means to use rather than a thing he means to be seen carrying. The herder himself was not the one who saw it; by his own account, given years after to whoever still troubled to ask him, he had given up the early watching some seasons before, his own knees no longer answering readily to a climb begun before first light, and it was a younger kinsman of his, grazing the same ground in his stead, who carried home the fuller account that the hill country afterward kept of that morning.

They worked at the altar's top stone for the better part of that morning, two of them setting their bars into some gap the watcher's own distance could not make out and leaning their whole weight against them while the third stood at the stone's far edge with both hands flat against it, as though holding it steady against a shifting it had not yet begun to do. The stone came free some while past noon, canted first to one side and then, with a shift of the bars, lifted clear of the sockets that had held it since before any teller's grandfather's grandfather had first climbed those steps, and set down, not broken as a stone dropped from such a height and such a weight might have been thought certain to break, but whole, on the platform's own top step, where the three of them stood over it a while without moving, as men stand over a thing whose weight has only just left their hands and whose next weight, the carrying of it onward, has not yet been taken up.

They carried it down. That much the watcher's account held to firmly, though it held to little else: the stone slung between them on the same dark bars that had prised it free, moving slow down the platform's steps and out across the hillside below, west, by every account that came down of it, toward ground the watcher did not follow them onto, being already further from any safe ground of his own than he had meant to go that morning. Whether the stone was carried on to the shore, or buried somewhere on the height itself, or broken up for some other use once it was out of sight of any eye that might have told of it, no telling of the hill country, then or in all the years the tale was afterward carried, ever claimed to know, and the sockets it left behind stood open on the platform's top step from that morning on, sharp-edged still, by every later account, as though the stone had been prised free only a season before rather than a lifetime, for a socket cut to hold a great weight does not wear soft with disuse the way a path does, or a doorway, and keeps its edges as long as the stone around it keeps its shape.

## IV. Of the Cairn on the Steps

Some years after the stone was carried down, a traveller of the northern moor, come further east than his own errand had called for, climbed the platform's steps himself, being alone and curious and finding, on a day of no particular weather, no one anywhere on the height to stop him or to see him do it. He found the steps unswept, grass grown thick between the fitted stones in a way it had not been let grow in the years the platform was still tended, and at the top, where the altar had stood, the two sockets standing open as the telling had already begun to carry them, holding nothing between them but a scatter of dead leaves the wind had put there and a little standing water from a rain some days gone.

Below the topmost step, where a man climbing might set his last foot before the platform itself, he found a small cairn of loose stones, no higher than a man's knee, built with more care than a stone kicked idly into a pile by some passing foot would ever show: each stone fitted to the one below it, the whole tapering true toward a single flat cap-stone set square on top, the kind of building a hand does slowly, and alone, and for a reason it does not trouble to explain to any eye that finds the work after.

Who had built it, and when, and whether the hand that built it had once climbed those same steps to tend the altar before the stone was carried away, the traveller could not say and did not pretend to guess, and no telling that carried his account onward ever added anything he had not himself seen. He stood over it some while, by his own account, turning over in his mind the two or three plain guesses any man might make of such a thing, a marker for a grave that held no body, a marker for nothing but the leaving itself, a thing built by one who had come back once, alone, to stand where the tending used to be done and had needed, before he left again, to put his hands to some work more lasting than standing; and he owned, telling it afterward, that he had settled on none of the three, nor on any fourth, and had come down from the height no wiser for the finding than he had gone up.

It stood there a long while after, by what little further word reached the moor of that height: a small cairn below an altar's empty sockets, kept by no one, added to by no one so far as any later climber ever marked, weathering slowly the way any unmortared stone weathers, a little more of its shape lost to frost and rain with every year that no hand came to set right what the weather had loosened, until in the fullness of time it stood no higher, by the last account any teller troubled to bring down from that height, than a man's shin, and might, to an eye that did not already know to look for it, have passed for no more than a fall of loose stone off the step above.

## V. Of the Rain in the Sockets

The temple was given up in its turn, as the outpost and the fortress had been given up before it, though no teller of the hill country, near or far, was ever near enough at the right hour to say when the last tending stopped or who had climbed those steps last with any purpose beyond a traveller's idle curiosity. The platform stood, in the years that followed, exactly as the traveller from the moor had found it: grass thickening between its fitted stones season on season, the small cairn below the top step neither added to nor thrown down, and the altar's two sockets holding, in place of the stone that had once been set square to the central mountains, whatever the sky above the eastern hills chose to put into them.

It was rain, oftener than anything else, that country's own weather being what it was, mild and wet and rarely long dry between one shower and the next; and a traveller who climbed the steps on a day after rain, in any of the years that followed, would have found the two sockets holding a little standing water apiece, dark with whatever had blown or washed into them since the last shower before, and would have seen, if the light fell right and the water lay still enough, the sky itself lying open in each one, cut to the same square shape the stone had once filled, and looking, from a certain distance and to a certain kind of eye, less like an emptiness than like two small mirrors set into the hill, giving back only what passed over them and keeping nothing of what had stood there before.

Elsewhere on that same height the two shafts of Toll-dubh went on standing open as they had always stood open, east and west, swallowing whatever stone a child still cared to drop into them and giving back, as they had always given back, no sound of a landing; and the springs at Muileann ruadh went on running warm under the mountain that gave them their name, fewer hands coming to them each year that the hill country troubled to count, but the water no cooler for the fewer hands, and no less able, by the last accounts that reached the moor of it, to knit a broken bone or take the fever out of a wound left too long unbound. Of the outpost, the fortress and the temple alone among the coast-people's high works could it be said, by the time the last of the tellings settled into the shape the hill country afterward kept, that they had been altogether given up; and it was those three, standing empty within sight of one another across the same three valleys, wall and cistern and altar each left to the weather in its own year and its own unwitnessed way, that the hill country came to speak of, when it spoke of that half-century at all, as the time the high places lost their people and no one, on either side of the line that had never once been crossed in the telling, could say where the people had gone that the height no longer held, or why the going, once begun, had never once turned back.

What the coast-people themselves made of it, gathered anew at their fuller shore with their kin about them and the height behind them, no telling of the hill country ever claimed to carry, for no hill ear was ever close enough, or trusted enough, to be given it; and the hill country, having carried every other wonder of that people as far as its own watching would take it, let this one stand, in the end, exactly as it had found it, filling only with rain.
