# Of the Cliff-House and the Curved Mole

*VE 2,241 – 2,523*

## I. Of the Long House at Tarr

At Tarr, in the north-east, where the cliff broke back on itself into a cleft wide enough for a household to stand upright in and deep enough that the wind off the water lost most of its strength before it reached the innermost wall, a household built a single long house against the living rock rather than free of it, so that the cleft's own back became the house's back, and no wall needed raising where the cliff already stood higher than any wall a household could have built for itself. The floor they levelled with beach shingle carried up in baskets a load at a time, and the walls that closed the cleft's mouth were laid dry, stone on stone, no higher than was needed to keep out what the cliff itself did not already keep out, so that from the sea the house showed as little more than a darker line across the cleft's opening, easy to pass without marking at all unless a boat came close enough in to see the smoke.

The hearth they set against the rock face itself, a little back from the door, where the draught that found its way past the stones outside would not trouble a low flame, and it burned there through every season the household kept the house, fed on driftwood gathered along the shingle below and on whatever the sea gave up after a hard blow, so that in time the rock above the hearth blackened with soot to the height of a tall man standing, a stain no scrubbing ever lightened and none of the household ever tried to lighten, for a blackened stone over a hearth was no more remarkable to them than a blackened pot, and told only that the fire had been kept a long while in the one place. A child of that household, grown old enough to be sent for wood on her own, is remembered to have measured her own growing by that stain, standing back to it each year at midwinter to see how much further up the rock her own crown now reached, until the year she stood as tall as the soot itself and was told, by whoever stood the measuring with her, that she had grown past what the fire alone could tell her.

The winter that gave the household its hardest year came some years into their keeping of the cleft, when the wind held out of the north for the better part of two months without once swinging round to give the coast a day's rest from it, and the sea below the cliff ran white from the rocks out to as far as the cleft's mouth let anyone see, so that no boat put out from Tarr that whole stretch and the household lived on what it had already dried and salted and on shellfish gathered at low water between one gale and the next, work that fell hardest on whoever's hands were quickest and least troubled by the cold, for a slow hand on the rocks in that weather came back with less than the tide alone would have given it and sometimes did not come back with fingers it could feel at all. The driftwood ran thin before the wind did, and toward the worst of it the household burned what it could spare of a broken boat-frame kept those several years against just such a want, stripping it plank by plank rather than let the hearth go cold, and is remembered to have argued, in whatever words that argument took, over how much of the frame could be burned before there was not boat enough left in it to mend once the weather turned.

It turned at last a little before the shortest day, the wind dropping out of the north in the space of a single night and swinging round to a quarter that let the sea inside the cleft's mouth lie flat enough by morning for a hand to be put over the side without the cold of it stopping the breath outright, and the household is remembered to have gone down to the shingle that morning before the light was properly up, wanting nothing from the calm so soon beyond the standing in it, a place out of the wind at last where they could feel, for the first time in two months, a stillness that did not mean the storm was only drawing breath before it came again. What they found on the tide that morning, driven in against the rocks below the cleft, was a run of small fish thick enough to be taken by hand from the shallows, more than the household could eat fresh or dry before it turned, and it is told, in the one line of that winter's telling that has come down whole, that no year after was reckoned as hard as that one by anyone of Tarr who had lived through it, however hard a later winter might in its own turn have seemed while it was being lived.

The house stood the better part of that household's years in the cleft, mended where the sea reached it and left alone where the cliff already stood firm enough to need no mending, and a household coming after them kept the same hearth against the same blackened rock rather than clear the stain and begin again, holding, by whatever reasoning shaped it, that a fire already so long fed in one place burned the readier for being lit again where it had always been lit, and that a hearth moved even the width of the house lost something of what the years already spent there had given it.

Outside the one door the cleft allowed, a shelf of rock ran a little above the tide's ordinary reach, wide enough for a man to stand and cast from without wetting his feet, and the household kept its lines there through every season the fish ran close enough in to be worth the casting, coiling the wet cord each evening on a peg driven into a crack in the cliff face rather than carry it inside where the damp of it would trouble the floor. A boy of the household, sent out one still morning to draw water from a pool the tide filled and left standing among the rocks below the shelf, is remembered to have called the whole household out at a run for what he took at first sight to be a seal hauled up close under the cliff, and to have been laughed at gently, once the thing was seen plainly for a length of drifted timber turned dark and round-backed by the sea's own working of it, though the timber itself was kept afterward and split for firewood rather than thrown back, the fright it had given him judged no reason to waste good wood.

Winters lighter than the hardest one still asked their own toll of the household, for the cleft that broke the worst of the wind also kept the sun from the door through the shortest months, the cliff standing high enough on either side that the shingle floor saw direct light for no more than an hour at midday even on a clear one, and a household living so many years in that half-dark is remembered to have kept, without any of its own tellings troubling to say who began the custom, a second small fire lit just inside the door on the darkest days, fed lighter than the great hearth against the rock and let burn low, more for the little more light it gave the doorway than for any warmth the great hearth further back did not already give the whole house.

## II. Of the Vell on the Abhainn Bheag

A day's walk and more to the west of Tarr, where the Abhainn bheag ran down to the sea through banks steep enough on either side that a household building close to the water needed to cut its own level ground into the slope before it could raise a wall at all, a small Vell grew up a short way above the river's mouth: four houses set close along the cut ground, and beside them, nearer the water than any of the four, a boat-shed roofed the same way as the houses but longer and lower, built to keep a hull dry and out of the weather between one season's use and the next rather than to shelter anyone sleeping in it. No dwelling of the Seann-Dhaoine on the whole of that coast stood further to the north-east than the four houses at this Vell, a fact none of the four households troubled to remark on in any telling that has come down of them, for a household living at the world's furthest edge in its own day rarely knows itself to be living there, and knows only that its own water runs cold and its own reach up the coast finds nothing further before the shore itself gives out.

The river gave the Vell what the open sea further along the coast could not always be trusted to give: eels taken in wicker traps set where the current ran slow enough near the bank for a trap to hold its place, and salmon in their season, run thick enough some years that a household needed only to wade into the shallows with a spear and strike without much troubling to aim, the fish crowding past in such numbers that a poor cast found another target within the same breath as a good one missed its first. The boat-shed held one hull built for the river's own quieter water rather than for the open sea beyond its mouth, flat enough in the bottom to be poled up past the first rapids in a dry season when the current ran low, and the household that built it is remembered to have gone further up the Abhainn bheag in that boat than any of the four households had gone on foot, coming back with word of ground upriver that none of them, in the years that followed, ever troubled to walk out and see for themselves, holding the word of the boat's own crew good enough without the seeing.

A child of the Vell, born into the youngest of the four households in the years the boat-shed still smelled of its own new-cut timber, grew up knowing the river before she knew the sea it ran to, and is remembered, once grown, for having gone down to the river's mouth on a still evening and stood a long while looking out past it at the open water beyond, water wider and greyer than anything the river itself had ever shown her, before turning back up the bank to the four houses without going any further down the shore to see what lay beyond the mouth in either direction. It is told that she never went that further way in all her years at the Vell, holding, when asked, that a mouth was as far as a river's own child had any business going before the river's own water gave out under her, and that what a boat might carry a person to beyond that mouth was the boat's own business and not hers.

The four houses stood close enough to the water that a hard spring flood, coming once in a run of years when snow high on the central massif melted faster than the ground could carry it away, rose far enough up the bank to reach the boat-shed's lower course and stood there the better part of a day before falling back, leaving the shed's floor silted with river mud that took a household the best part of a week to clear, working the mud out in baskets the same way the shingle at Tarr had once been carried up in baskets, though no one at the Vell on the Abhainn bheag ever knew, or had any way to know, that a household so far along the coast from them was that same year carrying the same kind of load for much the same reason. After that flood the boat-shed was raised a course higher on its uphill side, and no later flood, however high the snow-melt ran, troubled the hull kept inside it again in the years this telling follows.

The four households ate well enough in most years to spare a share of their eel-catch and their salmon both, dried and smoked over slow fires built for no other purpose, for kin further down the coast who kept no river of their own and were glad of a fish that did not taste of salt water in every bite; and word of what the Vell on the Abhainn bheag could spare travelled, by whatever slow paths word travelled along that shore, far enough that a household from two days' walk to the south is remembered to have come up once in a lean year with nothing to trade but a good store of dried seaweed and a promise of help at the next boat-launching, and to have gone home again with more dried fish than the promise alone seemed to the Vell's own households worth, given all the same because a household that asked plainly for help in a lean year was not, by the Vell's own reckoning, a household to be sent away counting.

The eel-traps wanted mending every season the willow they were woven from dried and split in the sun between one setting and the next, and the weaving of a new trap fell, in the years this telling follows, mostly to an old woman of the youngest household who had learned the pattern as a girl from hands older still than her own, and who is remembered to have taught it in turn to two of the Vell's children, a boy and a girl both, holding that a pattern known only in one pair of hands was a pattern one bad season from being lost to the whole Vell, whatever a river running full of eels every year of a person's own life might tempt that person to believe.

## III. Of the Second Mole at Skell

On the northern coast, far from the river mole the Seann-Dhaoine had laid out into the Abhainn naomh at the great Skell many generations before, a household of a second Skell took up the same work for its own stretch of water, laying stone on stone out from a low headland; but where the river mole ran straight against a current, this one was given a curve against the open swell, so that a boat standing off in rough weather with that headland in sight could find, by the shape of the stone alone, water calmer than the sea running past it on either side.

The work went slower than the first mole's own telling remembers its own laying to have gone, for the household at this second Skell had fewer hands to spare from the ordinary work of the shore than the builders of the first mole are remembered to have had, and carried the stone for their own curve down from a scree a little back from the water rather than quarry fresh rock, choosing each piece for its fit against the pieces already laid rather than for the ease of carrying it, so that some years the curve grew by no more than a boat's length of new stone and other years, when the scree gave up pieces that fitted well one against another, by a good deal more. A household further along the shore, watching the curve grow from one season's visit to the next, is remembered to have said of it only that stone laid slow stood no less firm for the slowness, a saying that outlasted, in the tellings that have come down, the names of whoever first said it.

The curve was carried far enough out, by the year this telling now reaches, to break the swell for the length of shore behind it that the household judged worth the sheltering, and no further, for a longer mole asked more stone than the scree behind the shore was ever likely to give up and more years than any one household expected to have hands enough to spend on it. A boat brought in behind the finished curve on a day the open sea beyond it still ran hard rode easier at its mooring than any boat of that shore had ridden before the mole stood finished, and a household that had lost gear more than once to a swell running in past an unsheltered mooring is remembered to have moved its own hull inside the new curve within the same season the last stone was laid, without waiting to see a full year's weather prove the shelter good.

The two moles, the river mole far to the south-west and this curved one on the northern coast, were never joined in any telling that has come down, each household knowing its own stone and its own stretch of sheltered water and little enough, beyond the bare fact that another such mole stood somewhere along the same coast, of the other's. It is told that a boy of the second Skell, grown to a man and travelling some way south-west over the land on an errand this telling does not trouble to name, came once within sight of the first mole and stood some while looking at it before going on, saying to whoever asked him of his travelling, on his return, only that the stone there was laid the same way as the stone he had grown up watching laid at home, stone for stone, though built, so far as either shore ever came to know, without one household ever having set eyes on the other's work before it was finished.

The choosing of the stone asked more of the households than the carrying did, for a piece taken from the scree without care for its shape as often sat loose against its neighbours as true, and a wall of loose-fitting stone standing against an open swell failed sooner than the swell itself troubled to test it fully, so the household that kept the work longest is remembered for a habit of laying each new piece dry against the course already set before committing it, working it back and forth by hand until it sat without rocking, and setting it only then, a slower way of building than simply piling what came to hand but one that let the second Skell's own curve stand, by the time this telling reaches it, through more winters of hard weather than the household that began it had thought, at the outset, the work would ever need to answer for.

One autumn a swell rose out of the north-west harder and longer than any the second Skell had yet tried its unfinished curve against, breaking clean over the stone at its furthest point and running white the whole visible length of it for the better part of two days, and the household that had laid the most recent courses went down each morning of that swell expecting to find its own work of the past several seasons scattered back along the shore the sea had taken it from; it found instead, when the swell dropped at last, every stone still where it had been set, moved perhaps a hand's width here and there but nowhere thrown clear, and took the finding as proof enough of the slow way of laying it had kept to that no household of that shore, in any telling that has come down, is remembered to have argued for a quicker one after.

## IV. Of the Pits in the Western Dune

On the western shore, where the Seann-Dhaoine had long kept a Vell among the dunes a little back from the open beach, a household began, in these same years, to dig a line of pits into the dune's own slope rather than raise anything above it, each pit cut down past the loose upper sand to ground firm enough to hold its shape, lined round with clay carried up from the river mouth a short walk to the south and beaten smooth against the sand walls before it dried, and closed at last with a slab of flat stone heavy enough that no fox working at it in the night could shift it clear.

What went into the pits was whatever the shore gave more of than a household could eat in the season it was taken: fish salted and dried past the point a gull or a fox would trouble to dig for it, shellfish packed in their own liquor and sealed while it still ran fresh, and in a lean season the last of a stranded catch too much for any one household's ordinary stores to hold. A pit once filled and lidded was left alone through the season it was meant for, opened only when the shore's own ordinary catch had run thin enough that a household judged the stored season's food worth more, taken now, than left sealed against a leaner year still to come, a reckoning each household made for itself and is not remembered to have agreed on with its neighbours, so that one pit might stand opened in a season another, dug the same year and filled the same way, was left sealed through.

A woman of the western Vell, whose own hands had dug the first of the line and lined it before any other household of that shore had troubled to dig one of its own, is remembered to have argued, in the years the line of pits grew from one to a scatter of a dozen and more, that a pit dug too near another drew damp from its neighbour through ground neither clay lining alone could wholly stop, and to have set her own later pits a full body's length apart from the first for that reason, a spacing the households that came after her on that stretch of dune are remembered to have kept without troubling to test, for themselves, whether her reasoning held.

Some of the pits, opened after longer than a season had passed over them, gave back less than had gone in: fish bones picked clean of anything a household might still have wanted from them, the flesh gone and the bones themselves gone soft with damp that had found its way past even the best-laid clay in the end, and a household opening such a pit learned, from the finding, to trust the clay less far into a second year than into its first, and to empty a store before the season turned rather than let it stand sealed on the chance a leaner year might still make use of it.

The clay itself was not always got without argument among the households that shared the walk down to the river mouth for it, for a bank cut too often in the same place slumped in the next high water and gave up no good clay at all until a season's rain had settled it back into a workable bed, and the woman who had dug the first pit is remembered to have kept, in her own head if nowhere any telling has set down, a kind of turn among the households as to which bank each might cut in a given season, a turn she never once wrote or marked in any way this telling can point to, holding it only in memory and in the asking, each spring, of whoever meant to dig that year where the last digging had been. A younger household new to the dune, not yet knowing the turn she kept, once cut a bank two others had already agreed between themselves to leave a season fallow, and gave over, when the disagreement that followed reached her, one full pit's worth of its own dried catch to the household whose turn it had truly been, a settling neither side is remembered to have thought unfair once it was made.

The lids themselves were not all of one stone. Some were slabs carried whole from a low reef bared at the lowest tides a little south along the beach, flat enough on both faces to need no working before they were set; others, where no slab of the right size could be got whole, were built up of two or three smaller stones fitted edge to edge and packed round with clay the same way the pit's own walls were packed, a harder seal to keep sound than a single slab but one that let a household close a pit dug in a season the reef gave up nothing of the size it wanted. A pit closed with the pieced lid asked checking, once or twice through a long winter, that the clay between the stones had not dried and cracked enough to let a fox's nose in at it, a small chore that fell, in most households, to whichever child was old enough to walk the dune alone and young enough still to be given so plain a task without complaint.

In these same years a quiet came over that shore that none of its households ever put a plain reason to in any telling that has come down whole: doors that had for generations faced the open water without a second thought began, in one household after another, to be set a little more toward the dune at their backs than toward the sea in front, and a family that had always kept its hearth nearest the door that looked seaward moved it, without remark, nearer the wall that looked inland instead. No teller of that shore ever said what the eyes had begun to look for in that direction, or whether they found it, and none of the households that turned their doors is remembered to have said so themselves; only that where a threshold had once opened straight onto the beach, it came, in those years, to open a little sideways from it, toward the rising ground behind. The last pit of that season was dug, lined and lidded before the first hard frost, the slab set and the sand banked back over its edges the way every pit before it had been closed, and the shore settled, as it had every year before, into the waiting that a sealed store and a closed season together asked of it, though what else it had begun, in those same years, to wait for, none of its own tellings ever say.
