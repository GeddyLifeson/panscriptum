# Of Fearghas on the Height

*VE 2,098 – 2,240*

## I. Of the Two Brothers and the Three Hills

Gormal had been five generations in the ground before anyone climbed for her. The bag with its six dark coals hung where it had always hung, in a niche cut in the wall above Poit an Teine at Tobar dhìreach, and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich was said standing at every dark-of-the-year recital exactly as she had left it, sky and earth and sea, and no one born since her death had thrown a coal from anywhere higher than the shoulder of moor she herself had once favoured and never climbed. The three hills she had named went on being argued at hearths that had never known her, the argument outliving the woman who had started it, the way a fire outlives the hand that first struck it.

Fearghas grew up on that argument the way every child of the high valleys grew up on it, hearing it before he could walk to the door on his own legs and forgetting to be surprised by it long before he was grown. He had it from his mother, as the recital had kept it since Gormal's day, and he heard it said the same way every year at the recital: the sea answered when a man who had never seen it stood on its shore and put a coal into it; the sky, by the same reckoning, wanted the ground nearest it, and no ground on the whole island stood nearer the sky than the highest, wherever that proved to be. He turned the six dark coals over in his own hands more than once as a boy, the way every child of the line turned them, weighing nothing in his palm but stone, and put them back in the bag without either of the wonder or the doubt that older mouths brought to the same handling.

His brother Mathan was three years the elder and had none of Fearghas's patience for sitting still through the whole of a recital, but he had a longer stride and a surer foot on wet rock than any of the kin his own age, and it was Mathan, not Fearghas, who first said aloud what the two of them had each been turning over apart for a year or more: that a question asked since the grove was not going to answer itself while the line sat arguing at its own hearth which hill among three it had never troubled to climb was the one that mattered. Fearghas was nine and twenty that summer, Mathan two and thirty, and neither of them had climbed higher than the ridge behind Tobar dhìreach where the sheep went in the hard months.

"Gormal named three," Fearghas said, "and none of the three agreed with the other two."

"Gormal never climbed further than her own doorstep to name them," said Mathan, who had never had much reverence for a woman five generations dead. "She named what she could see from where she stood. We have legs."

They went first to the hill south of the black well, the nearest of Gormal's three, on a morning fine enough that half the kin thought it no more than an outing, and stood on its crown by midday with the wind pulling at their cloaks and the whole run of the high valleys laid out below them, brown and green and grey, and Mathan looked west and said that the shoulder of moor Gormal had favoured stood plainly higher than the ground they were standing on, and Fearghas, looking the same way, was not sure he agreed, for a hill seen across a valley cheats the eye as often as it tells true, and a man standing on one height has no sure way of judging another he has not stood on himself. They argued it there on the crown until the light began to go, and came down no wiser than they had gone up, save that they had learned between them that Gormal's three hills could not be judged one against another from any one of the three, and that the only honest way to settle it was to walk to each in its turn and stand on it, and then walk on to whatever ground beyond it still looked higher, and keep walking until no ground anywhere in sight looked higher than the ground they stood on.

It took the better part of two years, the walking. They went out in the fair months and came back before the hard ones, for neither of them was fool enough to be caught on open ground in a Dia-thìr winter with no more shelter than a cloak, and each season's walking carried them a little further south than the season before, past hills that had looked from a distance like the last word on the matter and proved, once climbed, to have a taller neighbour standing behind them all along, hidden by the very ridge that had seemed, from below, to be the whole of the sky's edge. More than once they stood on a crown certain they had found it at last, only to see, once the low haze burned off toward noon, a paler shoulder of rock further off still, standing clear over everything nearer, and to know without needing to say it to each other that the walking was not done.

They quarrelled the whole way, as brothers will who are agreed on the errand and disagreed on every step of it. Mathan wanted to trust his eye and be done; Fearghas wanted to trust nothing he had not stood on with his own two feet, and would not let a hill go unclimbed merely because it looked the lesser of two from where they already stood, for he had seen enough by then to know how often the eye lied on ground it had not walked. More than once Mathan called him stubborn to his face, and Fearghas answered, more than once, that a stubborn man who climbed every hill in his path would find the true one eventually, and a quick one who trusted his eye might die old on the wrong summit and never know it.

In the second summer they fell in with a herder driving a small flock along a saddle of moor neither of them had crossed before, an old man with a dog and a stick and a face burned dark by more winds than either brother had yet walked into, and when they told him what they were about, in the plain words the whole high country used for it by then, he stood a while chewing on nothing and looking south past their shoulders before he answered. He had grazed that ground man and boy, he said, and every ground a day's walk from it besides, and there was a shoulder further on, past a burn he named and a stand of stunted thorn he named after it, that his own father had sworn stood higher than anything nearer the valleys, though the old man himself had never gone to prove it, having sheep enough to mind without chasing his father's notions over open moor. He gave them the way of it as well as he could, pointing with the stick, and went on after his flock without asking why two young men of Clann a' Chlachair wanted such ground, for the line's long business with the coals was known well enough by then that a herder needed no telling of it twice.

They found the burn and the thorn on the fourth day after, and beyond them the shoulder the old man's father had sworn by, and stood on it in a driving rain that let them see nothing at all of what lay further south, and came down soaked through and short-tempered with each other over whether the climb had been worth the wetting. It was three days more, the rain cleared and the ground drying under them, before they climbed it again in better weather and saw, from its crown, the same bare grey summit with the cloud on it, which by the third summer they had proved beyond doubting to be the one true height; but that first sight of it, small and far off and half hidden in its own weather, taught Fearghas, he said afterward, more of patience than the whole two years before had taught him, for a man who had climbed a hill in the wet only to find a truer one waiting beyond it, still half a season's walking off, learned either to give the search up for good or to trust that the walking itself would end somewhere, in its own time, whether he could yet see the end of it or not.

It was in the third summer, walking a ridge neither of them had gone near before, bare rock breaking through thin turf and the wind already colder than it had any right to be so early in the season, that they came out from behind a shoulder of stone and saw, standing clear above every other height in every direction the eye could reach, a bare grey summit with cloud dragging along its top like smoke off a hearth that would not settle. Neither of them said anything for a while. Then Mathan said, low, that they had found it, and Fearghas, who had learned by then not to trust a first look, said only that they would know when they had stood on it and looked from there, and not before.

## II. Of the Climb

They went up for it at the turn of the summer in the third year of their walking. He was thirty-two that year, Mathan five and thirty, and both of them had grown lean and hard-legged on three summers of walking ridges, so that the climb, when they came to it at last, felt to Fearghas less like a beginning than like the last stride of a road they had already been three years on.

They carried the six dark coals in the leather bag, wrapped in a fold of hide against the wet, and Fearghas carried it himself the whole way, slung from his shoulder under his cloak the way Neachdan was said to have carried Teine a' Chlachair home under his, though no one at Tobar dhìreach had made that likeness aloud before Fearghas set out, and he did not make it aloud either, only felt the weight of the bag against his hip on every stride and thought of it once or twice without meaning to.

They set out from the camp they had made at the foot of the climb on the first evening of an t-Sultain, meaning to sleep an hour or two and go up with first light, but the wind came on hard after dark and neither of them slept much, lying in the lee of a boulder with their cloaks pulled tight and the coals in their wrapping between them, and so it was still black night, the second of an t-Sultain not yet an hour old, when Mathan sat up and said there was no more sleep to be had lying still and freezing for it, and they might as well be climbing.

The lower slopes went easily enough, turf and loose scree and here and there a burn to cross by stepping-stone, and they climbed in the last of the dark and the first grey of the morning without much need of speech between them, each finding his own footing by feel where the light was not yet enough to see it. Higher up the turf gave out and the rock came through bare, and the wind, which had been at their backs on the lower ground, found them from every side at once as the ridge narrowed, so that Fearghas felt it less as a wind from any one quarter than as a thing that had been waiting for them on the height and now had them where it wanted them.

The cold on the summit, when they came out onto it at last with the sun not yet clear of the eastern sea, was a cold that got into the back of the jaw and made the teeth ache, a cold neither of them had felt on any of the lower hills they had climbed the two summers before, though the season below was still warm enough for haymaking. Fearghas stood a while with his hand over his mouth before he trusted himself to speak, and when he took the hand away his breath went out of him in a plume that the wind tore apart before it had gone the length of his arm.

There was no growing thing on that top, not so much as the toughest moor-grass, only bare stone scoured pale by weather that never let up on it, and a scatter of loose rock at the summit's crown where frost had been working at it longer than any teller could reckon. The two of them stood at the highest point of it, where the ground fell away on every side, and turned slowly on their heels, and saw the sea.

It stood at every edge of the island at once, shining where the early sun caught it, grey-gold to the east and a deeper grey to the north and west where the light had not yet reached, and between the sea and the two of them lay the whole of Dia-thìr laid out below in one sweep neither of them had ever seen it in before, valleys and ridges and the pale threads of rivers, and away to the north, small and grey and impossibly distant, a shoulder of higher moor that Fearghas knew, without needing to be told, for the ground where the mason had struck his wedge into the rock two thousand years before. He had never in his life thought of Tobar dhìreach as a small place. From where he stood it looked no bigger than a thumbnail held at arm's length.

Mathan spoke first, and his voice came out thinner than Fearghas had ever heard it, the wind taking half the words before they had gone a yard. "There is nothing higher than this on the whole island. Nothing anywhere in sight stands over it."

"No," Fearghas said, and found his own voice no steadier. "There is nothing."

He unwrapped the coals with hands that had gone stiff and clumsy in the cold, kneeling at the steep southern edge of the summit where the rock fell away in a long broken slope toward the lower ridges, the wind trying the whole time to take the hide wrapping out of his fingers and carry it off over the drop. Mathan crouched beside him and said nothing more, and for a while there was only the wind and the small sounds of Fearghas working the knots loose, and somewhere below them, very far off, a bird crying over open ground.

Fearghas took the first coal out, the one with the gleam of silver under its black, and held it a moment in his open palm, feeling nothing from it but its own cold weight, and then let it fall from the height of his own arm, out over the drop, the way a man drops a stone into a well to hear how deep it goes.

It struck the slope some way below and lay still. He let the second fall, and the third, watching each all the way down to be sure, and each struck and lay as dark and cold as it had left his hand. The fourth went the same way. Mathan had stopped speaking altogether by then, watching the drop with his jaw set, and Fearghas found that his own hands had begun to shake, not wholly from the cold.

He took out the fifth, the one flecked all over with small white marks like stars caught in dark water, and held it a moment longer than the others, turning it once so the flecks caught what light there was, and then opened his hand and let it go.

It did not fall far.

Fearghas watched it turn once in the air, no more than the height of two men below the edge, and then it was burning. There was no smoke first, no slow catching such as a hearth-fire takes from tinder; one moment it was a falling stone and the next it was a small fierce whiteness in the grey morning air, white as nothing Fearghas had ever seen a flame be, sharp-edged against the dull rock behind it like a star fallen out of its place and still burning where it had no business to burn, with no fuel under it and nothing around it but empty air and the fall. It struck the slope below still alight and did not go out on the striking, and lay there among the cold grey stones burning white and steady in the wind that could not put it out any more than the rain, so long before, had put out the vein in Neachdan's well.

Neither of them moved for a long moment. Then Mathan let out a sound that was half a laugh and half something with no name to it, and gripped Fearghas by the shoulder hard enough to hurt, and Fearghas found that he was on his knees at the very edge of the drop with the cold stone biting through his knees and had no memory of kneeling there, only of watching the small white fire below him and feeling, for the first time in his life, that the thing the whole line had been asking after for twelve hundred years was a true thing that answered, when a man found the right way to ask it, and no mere story told at a hearth.

"It is the sky's," Fearghas said, when he had breath enough to say anything. "Gormal was right. It only wanted the right ground under it."

They climbed down to where the coal lay, a slower going than the climb up, for the cold had stiffened their hands and the drop was steep and loose underfoot, and stood over it a long while before either of them would touch it. It burned on, small and white and even, giving off no smoke that either of them could see and no more heat than a man might feel from a hearth-coal held at arm's length, and it did not sink into ash the way a burning thing should, only went on burning, as steady on the third hour as it had been on the first.

They left it there. Neither of them said so to the other in so many words, but each knew without asking that the coal had found its own place and its own element and had no more business being carried home in a pot than gual òir, so long before, had had any business being fished back out of the northern sea. Fearghas gathered the four cold coals from the slope, and let the sixth fall after them for the sake of the telling, and it struck and lay as cold as the rest; and he wrapped all five again in the hide, and the two of them climbed back to the summit and stood there a last while with the sun well up now and the whole island bright below them, and Mathan, who had never in his life been given to solemn gestures, stooped and set one loose stone from the summit's crown atop another, a small cairn no higher than a man's hand, and said nothing over it, and Fearghas understood the doing of it without needing the saying.

## III. Of the Naming and the Cairn on the Height

They came down off the height in two days, going carefully on legs that had gone stiff with the cold and the standing, and reached Tobar dhìreach on the evening of the second day with the five cold coals in the bag and nothing else to show for a climb of three summers' finding and two days' doing, and yet the whole household knew before either of them had said a word that something had answered, for Fearghas came through the door with a look on him that no one at that hearth had worn coming back from any hill before.

He told it standing, as the telling of such things was always told at that hearth, with the bag open on the floor before him and the five dark coals laid out on a cloth for anyone to see and turn over who wanted to, and the household sat late into the night hearing it twice through, once from Fearghas and once, because Mathan would not be left out of the telling of a thing he had climbed for as long and as hard as his brother, from Mathan, whose telling ran shorter and rougher but agreed with Fearghas's in every particular that mattered. The old women who kept Gormal's saying at the recital wept at the hearing of it, the same weeping the old women were said to have wept the night Aodh came home from the northern sea, eight hundred years before, and by morning the telling had gone out from that hearth to every household within a day's walk, carried by whoever had heard it first and could not keep from carrying it further.

The summit took the kindred's name before that first winter was out, called Binnean a' Chlachair from the first telling of it and by no other name after, Binnean a' Chlachair, though no one household could afterward say for certain who had first called it so; it was as if the name had been waiting on the height the whole two thousand years since the mason struck his wedge into the rock, needing only a household willing to climb high enough to claim it. Herders of the high valleys who drove their flocks anywhere within sight of that grey summit began, within a few years of the telling, to leave a single stone on its crown whenever their own way brought them near enough to climb it, a small stone laid without much ceremony on the little cairn Mathan had begun, so that within a lifetime the crown that had been bare rock scoured clean by weather carried a heap of stone a man could see from some way off, each stone laid by a different hand and none of the hands ever troubling to say why, beyond that it seemed a poor thing to stand on such ground and leave nothing of oneself there.

Fearghas went back to the height himself twice more in his life, once with his own son grown old enough to make the climb, to show the boy the white fire still burning steady among the cold stones of the slope below the summit, unchanged in every particular from the morning he had first seen it catch, and once alone, in his last years, when his legs were no longer what they had been at two and thirty and the climb took him the better part of three days each way instead of two. He found the coal burning that third time exactly as he had left it every time before, and stood over it a long while in the cold, an old man now, remembering a morning when his own hands had shaken with more than cold, and left a stone of his own on the crown before he climbed down for the last time.

## IV. Of the Years After

Mathan did not live to see his brother's second climb. He died in his fifty-sixth year, of a fever that came on him in a wet spring and would not break, and was buried at Tobar dhìreach with the small cairn stone he had kept from the summit's crown, the very first one he had ever set there, closed in one hand at his own asking, for he had told his wife, in the last days when he could still speak clearly, that he wanted something of the height with him in the ground, having given the height, as he put it, three summers of his legs and the best notion he had ever had in his life. Fearghas stood over the grave and said nothing that the household later remembered as fit for the recital, only that his brother had trusted his eye from the first day on the ridge and had been right from the first day, whatever the walking after had cost either of them to prove it.

Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich went on being asked at every recital after, for five of na Seachd Guail still lay dark, the earth and the blood and the river and the fire, and the rainbow last of all, still to be found by hands not yet born, and the line did not grow easy merely because the sky had answered where the sea had answered before it; if anything, Fearghas himself was heard to say more than once, an old man by then, that a question answered twice in twelve hundred years was no cause for slackness, only for the patience to keep asking it a third time and a fourth. But something had changed at that hearth all the same, in a way none of the kin could easily have put into words. A household that has thrown one coal into the sea and had it answer, and thrown a second from the height of the whole island and had it answer too, keeps the other five with a different weight in its hands than a household that has only ever heard the throwing told as another household's story.

Fearghas himself lived into his sixty-second year, an old man by the standards of the high valleys though not the eldest anyone at that hearth had known, and he kept his own hand steady enough to turn the five remaining dark coals over by his own fire almost to the last, saying to whoever sat with him in those final months that he had climbed the highest ground the island owned and stood there with his own two feet under him, and a man who had done that once in his life had no great cause to fear a bed.

He was buried within sight of Leac na Fèithe that covered the mason's own vein, as every keeper and every finder of the line had been buried before him, and at the recital said over his grave that winter, standing as every recital of the fathers was said standing, the reciter gave the fathers' names down from Neachdan and Conall and Diarmad and Aodh, thirty-one fathers whose names the line no longer gave to strangers, down to the last of them, who was the father of Fearghas; and after his father's name the reciter said Fearghas's own, and told, in the fixed words that would be said the same way at every recital after, how he had climbed to the top of the island with his brother beside him and let the six coals fall, and how the snowflake had taken fire in the empty air and struck the stones below still burning, and how the summit bore the kindred's name from that day for as long as anyone should climb it. The old among the household, who had heard Gormal's own three hills argued over at that same hearth every winter of their childhood, stood through the whole of it with their heads bent, and when the reciter came to the naming of the height, more than one of them was heard to say the name back under their breath before the reciter had finished saying it aloud, as though the word had been waiting in them the whole long while since Gormal first named her hills, needing only someone to climb high enough to give it a place to stand.
