# Of the Column

*VE 4,851 – 4,904*

## I. Of the Third Stone

Ciannait was one and thirty the summer the third stone was found standing where no stone had stood the year before, on a low rise of open ground two days' walk south-west of the crack, where the hill households of that country brought their beasts down each autumn to graze the stubble before the frosts came. She had made that same drive every year of her grown life, her own hearth lying a half-day short of the rise, and she had crossed that same ground a hundred times without there being anything on it to cross toward beyond the one thorn tree the drovers used as a mark; so that when her brother's boy came running back along the track that year shouting that there was a great stone standing on the rise where the thorn tree stood alone, she took it at first for a boy's way of making an ordinary walk into something worth the telling, and only went to look because the beasts were slow that morning and there was time enough to spare for looking.

It stood taller than the thorn tree beside it, a single squared pillar of the pale blue-grey stone the hill country called only the blue rock, for want of any better word, set so true to the level ground that a plumb line hung from its top would, by the account of the first men to lay hands on it, have kissed the earth at its foot without a hair's width to spare either way. No cairn stood by it. No path led to it, not yet, though within the year there would be one worn plain enough by the feet of those who came to see it that even a stranger new to that country could have found the rise by the track alone, without ever being told which way to look.

She came up to it with her brother's boy at her heel and the rest of the drove already scattering out over the stubble behind her, and she did what everyone who came to it that first summer is remembered to have done: she put her hand flat to the stone's face, low down where a hand reaches without stretching, and found it warm from the morning sun and rough under her palm with the lines of a cutting she had heard tell of but never yet seen with her own eyes.

The words ran the whole breadth of the lower face, more words than either of the two stones further north had carried, cut in the same narrow, sure hand, or a hand so like it that no one who set the three sayings side by side, then or after, could ever swear to any difference between them:

*Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.*

We did not come; we were here.

Above the saying, where the stone narrowed toward its shoulder, ran a second line in the older, wider script that no one on the island had ever read and no one on the island ever would, the same running, level mark that had stood over the first stone's four words and, fainter and shorter, over the second stone's three; and it was this, more than the new saying below it, that told the hill folk who came to look that whatever hand had cut the older line meant the three stones to be read, if they were to be read at all, as one long saying rather than three short ones, though what that whole saying was, taken together, no one at Achadh a' Chalbh that summer, or in any summer after, ever agreed on closely enough to fix it in words of their own.

Ciannait said the lower saying aloud where she stood, the way every teller before her had said a newly found saying aloud on the ground where they found it, and heard her own voice go out over the stubble and come back thin off the one bare rise, and stood a while with her hand still on the stone after the saying was done, turning over in her own slow way what it meant to be told, by a hand no living person had seen at its work, that her own people had not come from anywhere at all.

Her brother's boy asked her, when she came away from it at last to see to the beasts, who had cut it; and she told him, because it was the only true answer she had to give him, that she did not know, and that no one living knew, and that it did not seem, from the plain freshness of the cutting and the bare turned earth still black at the stone's foot, that whoever had cut it had troubled to wait about afterward to be asked.

He asked her a second question that she liked no better for having no better answer to give it, which was whether the stone would still be standing come morning, boys of his age having heard enough, by then, of the walker on the ridge and the covered hearths of the hill country to wonder whether anything found new on open ground was safe to be left overnight; and she told him only that a stone was not a fire, and had never yet been known to go out, and that they would see for themselves soon enough, the drove being due back over that same ground within the fortnight whether the stone had a mind to bide there or not. It was there when they came back, exactly as they had left it, the bare turned earth at its foot a little more weathered and no wider, and it was there again the fortnight after that, and every fortnight and every season after, so that within that one year's turning the households of the drove had stopped asking, among themselves, whether it would stand, and begun asking only what, besides its own two sayings, it might yet be found to mean.

## II. Of the Field of the Column

Word of the third stone went out from that rise the same slow road the first two sayings had gone, hearth to hearth and gathering to gathering, but it went further and it went faster, for by the year it was found the four words of the first stone and the three of the second had already had the better part of a lifetime to settle into the ordinary stock of what every hearth in the hill country knew, so that a saying come new from a third stone, and a longer one besides, found ears already listening for it in a way the first two sayings had never found when they were new. Within two summers the rise had a name of its own among the households that gathered there, Achadh a' Chalbh, the field of the column, and within five it had a worn ring of bare earth about the stone's foot where feet had stood so often and so long that no grass had troubled to come back into it, much as the ring at the second stone had worn bare under Mòrnait's own feet a day's walk to the north.

It was at Achadh a' Chalbh, in the third summer after the stone's finding, that Ciannait heard the whole saying said standing, before a gathering of some thirty households come together at the dark of that year to keep the night of the lightning together on ground none of them had kept it on before, the recital of the fathers being said, as it was said at every such gathering, by whoever among them had it best by heart; and it was an old woman of a hearth two valleys east of Ciannait's own, a woman near her eightieth year whose name went into no telling that outlasted her own grandchildren, who rose when the fathers had been named and the riddle laid down again unanswered, and said the three sayings of the three stones together for the first time that Ciannait, or anyone else at that gathering, had ever heard them said as one.

She said the four words of the first stone, and the three of the second, and then, turning to face the pillar where it stood pale in the firelight with its own shadow thrown long across the trampled ground, she said the words of the third: *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.* And she said after it, in words that were her own rather than any stone's, that the Telling every hearth in the hill country already kept, of the young god who had made the island of his last coal and the first people who grew up out of its ground like grass, meant a plainer thing than most tellers troubled to draw out of it: that a people grown from the ground they stood on had no other ground to have come from, and no other root than the one under their own two feet, and that the stone said only, in fewer words than the Telling used, what the Telling had always said.

Ciannait was four and thirty that night, old enough by then to have children of her own standing at her side and young enough still to remember, plainly, what it had been to put her own hand to the stone the summer it stood new; and she said afterward, to those of her own hearth who asked her what she made of the old woman's words, that she did not know if the god had meant the saying or only the shape of the telling had come to fit it, but that she had stood a long while that night looking at the pillar with the fire between herself and it, and had felt, for the length of that looking, that the ground under her own feet had a name it had not had before the saying was said over it.

The gathering at Achadh a' Chalbh was kept again the next dark of the year, by more households than the first, and again the year after that, until within Ciannait's own lifetime it had become, for the hearths of that whole stretch of country, the one gathering-ground of the year that every household within two days' walk turned toward as the days shortened, in much the way that other households further north turned each year toward Tobar dhìreach to hear the fathers named at the line's own hearth. No house was built there in Ciannait's time, nor for long after it, the ground about the pillar being kept, by a custom no one troubled to set down as a rule because no one thought it needed one, for the fires and the standing and the going home again rather than for living; but it had, by the time Ciannait's own hair had gone from dark to grey, what neither of the two older stones had ever had on their own bare hillsides, which was a name that every child of that country learned before it learned to walk the day's ground to earn it, and a fire built there, at the very least, once in every year that the dark of the year came round.

The crowds that gathered twice a year, once at the dark of the year and once at the height of summer when the drove-roads were driest and easiest walked, brought with them, as any crowd of that size brings, more business than the saying alone; and it was out of the standing and the going home again, rather than out of any plan laid by any one household, that a market grew up on the stubble about the pillar's foot in the years of Ciannait's own middle life, hides traded for salt and salt for wool and wool for whatever a household further off had to spare, the traders setting out their goods on hide cloths spread over the same trampled ground the fires were built on, and taking them up again within the day, for no one yet thought to leave anything standing there overnight beyond the stone. Ciannait traded there herself more than once, wool off her own beasts for salt she could not get nearer than three days' walk any other way, and she said of it, once her hair had gone grey, that she had gone the first few times for the salt and kept going, in the end, as much for the crowd and the fire and the saying said over it as for anything a trader could set out on a cloth.

## III. Of the Fire Coal in a Child's Hand

While the saying at Achadh a' Chalbh was still new enough that most who heard it had heard it first from someone who had themselves heard it only at second or third remove, a child was born at Tobar dhìreach into the narrow keeping of the bag, the tenth father since Colla whose name the reciters have never given to any stranger who asked it of them; and that child, a boy, had the fire coal in his own hands, by every account that came down from that hearth, before he was old enough to be trusted with anything else his household kept.

He was not yet six the first summer a fire-bearer passing through the valley on his way north carried with him, along with the ordinary coal for the hearths that had let their own go out, the word of the third stone and its saying, new enough then that even at Tobar dhìreach it was still told as news rather than as a thing everyone already knew; and the boy sat that evening at the edge of the household's fire with the fire coal turned in his fingers, as he turned it most evenings for no reason beyond the plain unremarkable comfort of a smooth stone in a small hand, listening to the fire-bearer tell of the pillar and its shadow and the old woman's words without troubling, at that age, to understand more of it than that somewhere two long days south a stone stood that people walked a long way to look at, much as his own hearth was a place people sometimes walked a long way to hear the fathers named.

He asked his mother, when the fire-bearer had gone on his way, whether the coal in his own hand had come from the same place as the stone's saying, and she told him, in the plain way the women of that hearth answered such questions of their children, that the coal had come from the mason's own vein under the slab, and the stone's saying from no one anyone living could name, and that the two were not, so far as she or anyone else of that house had ever heard, the same matter at all, though both were things the valleys had come, in their own way, to keep.

His father, whose own name the reciters have kept from strangers as they had kept the nine fathers' before his, said less of it than his mother had, being a man of few words even at his own hearth, but he took the boy's chin in his hand that same evening and turned his son's face up to his own and told him only that the bag on the peg had waited longer than any stone in the south had waited, and that a thing worth keeping was worth keeping without asking each year whether the keeping had yet come to anything. The boy took it gently, said gently as it was, and kept it in mind ever after, by his own account given late in his own life, better than he remembered most of what either of his parents said to him in those years, for it was the first time anyone had told him plainly that the waiting, and not only the answer waited for, was a part of what the bag asked of whoever held it.

The coal did nothing that evening it had not done every other evening of the boy's short life: it lay black and cool in his palm while the hearth-fire threw its light across the backs of his hands, unburning and unchanged, the way it had lain in every hand of that household back to the keeper who had thrown its six fellows to the sea and the height and the earth and the blood and the river and had this one and the rainbow left over, unanswered, in the bag on its peg. The boy set it back in its place when his mother called him to his supper, and thought no more of the stone at Achadh a' Chalbh, by his own much later account, than a child thinks of any far country he has been told of once and has no cause to think of again; and it would be the better part of thirty years, and the household grown old and passed to his own keeping, before that same coal gave him any cause to think of it a second time.

## IV. Of the Word Carried North, and Ciannait's Children

The saying of the third stone went further north than either of the two before it, carried, in the year the hill country afterward reckoned four thousand eight hundred and seventy, by a woman of the northern shore who had come south to trade hides for salt at one of the market days the valleys about Achadh a' Chalbh had lately begun to keep on the strength of the crowds the gathering-ground drew twice a year; she heard the saying said at the stone, standing among strangers with her own bundle of hides still on her back, and carried it home with her the whole long walk north the way she carried the salt, meaning at first, by her own later telling, only to have something worth the telling to set against the hides she had got for her trouble.

She had walked the better part of ten days to come so far south, by the drove-roads and the fords where the hill households kept the stepping-stones renewed, carrying her bundle of hides the whole way down and her sack of salt the whole way back, and she said afterward, to whoever at her own hearth asked her whether the walking had been worth the trading, that the salt alone would not have answered for it. Never in her own life had she stood among so many strangers at one fire as she stood among at Achadh a' Chalbh, nor heard so many households at once say the same four words together as though they had rehearsed them, which, in a manner of speaking not one of them would have owned to, they had, each in their own valley, for a lifetime before that night.

She said it first at her own hearth on the northern shore, to a household that kept no stone of its own and no gathering-ground within any number of days' walking, and it went from that hearth to the next the way any saying went among people with little enough new to tell one another, until within a handful of years a shepherd of the far north-western moor, asked by a trader what his own people held of where they had come from, could answer him with four words he had never once walked south to hear at any stone, and answer him as though the words had always been his to answer with.

Ciannait lived to see that much of it, though she never herself walked further north than the drove-roads her own beasts had always used, and she said, in the last years of her own life, to the children she had raised at her hearth two days short of the pillar, much what the old woman of that first gathering had said to her when she herself was four and thirty: that the Telling every child of the valleys already knew, of the god who had made the island last of all his makings and the people who grew out of its ground like grass, said the same thing the stone said, in older and longer words, and that a saying found new on a hillside was no less true for having waited so long to be said, any more than a well was less good water for having been dug only last spring rather than in the mason's own day.

She said it to them by her own hearth-fire on more evenings than any of them troubled to count while she was living to say it, and she said it to them once more, plainly and without much dressing beyond the words themselves, in the last winter of her own life, the year the reciters afterward fixed as four thousand eight hundred and ninety-one, sitting close by the fire with a granddaughter's hand in her own because the cold had by then got into her joints past any warming; and it is this last saying of it, rather than any of the summers she had walked to Achadh a' Chalbh in her own strength, that her own children kept longest and passed on furthest, so that the words came down, in that one household at least, less as a stone's saying than as an old woman's, said at her own fire in her own last winter to the children who would carry it after her.

She told the granddaughter whose hand she held that winter, when the girl asked her why the words mattered so much to an old woman who could no longer make the walk to hear them said at the stone, that a saying did not need a body able to walk to it in order to go on being true, any more than the mason's fire had needed the mason himself still living to go on burning from hearth to hearth long after his own death; and she said that the girl might live to see the stone herself, or might not, and that it would be true either way, standing there with its two sayings and the one above them no one could read, whether any of Ciannait's own blood ever stood at its foot again or never did.

Ciannait died that same year, at her own hearth, in her one and seventieth year, and was buried on the ground her own people had always buried their dead on, without any coal of the vein given her in the ground, that custom never having reached so far south-west in her own lifetime; and her children kept the walk to Achadh a' Chalbh after her, some years and not others, much as Mòrnait's own daughter had kept the walk to the second stone, so that the fire at the pillar's foot did not go long unlit in any one generation's span, and the saying she had given them at her own hearth went on being said, at that fire and at others, by mouths that had never once stood at the stone themselves and never once doubted, for all that, that the words were theirs to say.

By the year the hill country reckoned four thousand nine hundred and four, the pillar at Achadh a' Chalbh stood as it had stood the summer Ciannait first put her hand to it, its lower face no less plain for all the years of firelight that had played across it, its upper line no better read; and the boy who had once turned the fire coal in his fingers by a fire far to the north, hearing a fire-bearer's first telling of a stone he had never seen, was by then a grown man keeping that same coal in a bag on a peg above his own hearth, walking nowhere with it, waiting, without much hope left in the waiting, for a day he did not yet know was close upon him.
