# Of the Whale, the Round Houses and the Tower

*VE 1,670 – 1,950*

## I. Of the Whale-Ribs at Dunn

Below the southern Dunn, where the land broke down from turf to shingle in a single short fall a man could jump without troubling to look for a path, a whale came ashore in an autumn gale out of the west, driven in on a sea already grey and heavy long before the wind that carried it had properly begun, so that the household nearest the shingle heard the surf change its voice before first light and were down on the stones, half dressed, before the sky had greyed enough to show them what the noise had brought.

It lay the length of six grown men end to end, and longer, stranded above the ordinary reach of the tide and beyond any hope the next high water might carry it off again, and it lived through that first day and into the next, its one visible eye following the household as they stood off from it at a careful distance, not yet knowing what a thing of that size, breathing still, might do if it heaved. By the third morning it had stopped its breathing, and by then word had gone along the shore both ways, on the two feet of whoever could be spared to carry it, and other households besides the one that had heard the surf change came down to Dunn to see what the sea had put ashore.

The cutting of it was work for every hand the shore could gather, and took the better part of a half-month, the meat carried off in loads no single household could have eaten before it turned, salted where salt could be got and smoked over slow fires where it could not, shared out by a reckoning none of the tellings that have come down of it have troubled to set out in full, save that no household that had come down to help went home again with an empty basket. The blubber was rendered in clay-lined pits dug back from the shingle, the oil skimmed and kept in bladders and in the same clay pots the shore already used for water, and burned afterward in stone lamps through two winters at least by more hearths than the one household that had first heard the whale come in.

What was left, once the meat and the blubber and the skin had all been taken and the gulls and the foxes had begun on what remained, was the frame of it: ribs longer than a man is tall, curved the whole of that length, white already before the flesh had wholly gone from them, and too heavy for fewer than four hands to lift any one of them clear of the shingle. It is told that no one set out, in the first days after the whale came ashore, to do anything with the bones beyond let the sea and the gulls have them back in their own time, the way any household let a carcase go back to the shore once it had given what it could give; and it is told, just as plainly, that before that first winter had closed, the ribs stood upright in the turf a little back from the high-water mark, driven in at their thicker ends and lashed together in threes and fours at the crown, so that three round houses stood where, a season before, there had been only shingle and the smell of rendering.

The turf that roofed them was cut in long strips from the ground a little above the shingle and laid across the curve of the ribs the way turf was laid across any lighter frame of bent wood or driven stake, but the ribs held a wider span and a taller crown than any house the shore had raised before, so that a grown man could stand upright in the middle of one of the three without his head troubling the roof, a thing not every household's own house allowed him. The households that took up the three whale-rib houses that first winter are not named in any telling that has reached this one, save that they were of the shore nearest the shingle and had first cause, by the plain fact of having heard the surf and come down to look, to whatever the sea had left behind once every other hand had taken its share.

The bone did not last as the driven stake and the bent branch of an ordinary frame lasted. Salt and rain and the slow work of years told on it faster than they told on green wood, and a rib that had stood four winters was found, on the fifth, cracked through where the weight of the turf bore hardest, and had to be shored with a driven stake lashed alongside it or taken down before it brought the roof with it. The three houses stood, in the shape the whale had first given them, the better part of ten years before the last of the original ribs failed past shoring; and when they were rebuilt, it was on whale-bone again, for by then a second stranding, smaller than the first but of the same kind, had come ashore some way along the same stretch in the years between, and the household had learned, from the first building, how a rib wanted setting and lashing and how deep its foot wanted driving to stand the winters it could be made to stand.

It is told that the frames were raised a third time in the years this telling now follows, on bone from neither of the first two strandings but carried, this third time, some distance along the shore from a whale that had come ashore further west and been cut there by a household with no thought yet of building anything from what it left behind; word of usable ribs travelled, by then, faster along that coast than word of the strandings themselves had once travelled, for the shore had come to know what a length of whale-bone was worth to a household that wanted a house taller and wider than turf laid over branch could give it, and did not wait, the third time, for the bone to come ashore of its own accord below their own stretch of shingle.

Between one raising and the next, the rotted ribs that were taken down rather than shored were left where they had stood, unburned and uncarried, half sunk already into the turf that had grown up thick around their feet, so that a child of the third household to build there, digging one spring for no purpose sharper than a child's own digging, turned up a length of bone gone soft and dark with the years it had lain buried, and could not at first say what kind of thing it was, until an elder, called over, named it for what it had been: the foot of a rib from the first house, sunk and near forgotten under turf that had by then grown as thick over it as over any ground the whale had never touched at all.

The shingle below the three houses kept, through all the years of the first and the second and the third raising, a scatter of smaller bone the cutting had left behind and no one had ever troubled to clear, vertebrae the size of a grinding-stone and finger-bones no bigger than a child's own thumb, worked by the tide and the years into the ordinary stones of that shore until a stranger walking the shingle for the first time might have crossed the whole stretch below the houses without once marking that half of what lay underfoot had come from the sea's own flesh and not from the island's own rock. A household living there long enough came to know one from the other without looking, by the heft of a stone in the hand and the give of it underfoot, the bone always a little lighter for its size than the true stone lying beside it, though none of that household is remembered ever to have found a word to say why.

Of the second stranding, smaller than the first and further along the same stretch, less is told than of the first, save that it came ashore in calm weather with no gale to announce it, found by a household out at low tide for shellfish rather than woken to it by any change in the sea's own voice, and that the cutting of it, being a smaller thing altogether, was the work of the finding household alone, with no call sent along the shore for other hands to come and share the reckoning. What that household kept from it, beyond the meat and the oil every stranding gave, was the whole set of ribs entire, and it is these, more than the first whale's bones already once rebuilt and once failed, that a household of the third raising is remembered to have chosen, judging bone that had not yet stood a single winter driven upright in wet ground to be worth more than bone twice already shored and twice already failed.

## II. Of the Round Houses of the Eastern Vell

A different shore, a good way to the east and south of Dunn, held nothing so strange as a whale's own bones for a roof, but raised, in these same years, a cluster of houses in stone the whale-rib builders never troubled to lay: fourteen round houses of dry-built stone, walls two courses thick and near shoulder height before the roof began, set close enough together on the ground the eastern Vell held that a household leaving one door could all but touch the wall of its neighbour's without crossing open ground at all.

The stone there was got from a scatter of loose slabs and boulders lying thick enough on the ground above the shore that no one household needed to quarry fresh rock to raise a wall; the labour lay instead in the choosing and the fitting, a wall built without mortar of any kind, each stone set and reset until it sat true against its neighbours on every side, the courses above holding the courses below without any binding but their own good fit. A wall raised this way asked more patience than speed of the hands that raised it, for a stone set badly low down was not found out until the courses above it had grown too heavy to lift off again without bringing half the wall down with it, and the households of the eastern Vell are remembered, in what has come down of their own telling, for unbuilding a wall's top courses more than once rather than build on over a stone they had begun, too late, to mistrust.

Each house was raised round rather than square, the walls curving in a little as they climbed until the last courses could be closed over with flat slabs laid corbel-fashion, one edging in past the one below it, until an opening no wider than a man's two hands together remained at the crown, closed at last with turf and a single flat stone laid over the whole. Inside, the floor was sunk a little below the ground outside the door, and at its centre a hearth-pit cut deeper again than the floor around it and lined with smaller stone set on edge, so that the fire burned below the level a draught from the door could easily trouble, and the smoke found its own way up past the corbelled roof and out through whatever gaps the fitting of the last stones had left open at the crown, for no house of that cluster is remembered to have cut a hole on purpose for it.

What the eastern Vell's builders are remembered for beyond the walls and the sunk hearths was a channel cut under the wall at its lowest point, lined the same way the hearth-pit was lined, running out from beneath the floor to open ground some way beyond the house, so that water that gathered on the sunk floor in a wet season, whether rain found its way in past an ill-fitted door or the ground simply rose wetter than usual under the winter's rain, had somewhere to go that was not simply where it happened to lie until it was trodden into mud by every foot that crossed the floor. It is told that not every one of the fourteen houses was given such a drain from its first raising, and that the households without one, watching their neighbours' floors stand drier through a wet winter than their own, took their own floors up in the following dry season and cut the channel in afterward, working it in under a wall already standing rather than build it in from the first course, a harder labour by every account that has come down of it than laying the channel before the wall went up, but done all the same, house by house, until by the years this telling now closes on, every one of the fourteen had its own dry outfall and its own drier floor to show for the labour.

The fourteen houses stood close enough, and were raised near enough in the same years, that no single telling has kept which household raised which first, or in what order the cluster grew from one house to fourteen; what has come down instead is the cluster whole, as a thing already finished and already lived in, doors facing every way the ground allowed rather than all one way, so that a child born into that press of near neighbours grew up knowing, before it knew much else, which door led to which household's hearth and which threshold belonged to kin close enough to walk in on without first calling from outside. A woman of the cluster is remembered, though not by name, for having been born in one of the fourteen houses, married into another three doors along without ever leaving the same cleared ground the fourteen stood on, and buried at last, by her own household's telling, within sight of both thresholds, the one she was born to and the one she kept; and it is said of her, in the one line the telling has kept whole, that she never in all her years needed to ask a stranger's door twice which household's hearth lay behind it.

The ground between the fourteen doors was worn, within a few years of the last house standing finished, into a hard-packed yard no grass ever troubled again, criss-crossed with the paths every household's own errands wore into it, to the well the cluster shared at its centre, to the midden each kept a little apart from its neighbour's, to whichever threshold a child had been sent to borrow fire from on a morning its own hearth had gone out overnight. A dog loose in that yard on an ordinary day might be fed from three or four hearths before the day was done, none of the fourteen households troubling to ask whose dog it was so long as it answered to being driven off a threshold it had no business lying across; and a stranger come to the eastern Vell for the first time is remembered to have stood some while at the yard's edge before venturing in, unable at first glance to tell, among so many low doors set so close, which belonged to the household with the elder he had been sent to find, until a child playing in the packed yard was asked and pointed the way without stopping its own game.

## III. Of the Tower at the North-Western Skell

Further north again, on the coast the north-western Skell held at its farthest and least sheltered reach, a household raised a single tower the like of which no other shore of the island had yet built: round like the fourteen houses of the eastern Vell, but built with a wall thick enough to be hollow through its whole height, an inner face and an outer face of dry-laid stone with a stair let into the gap between them, climbing the wall's own thickness in a tight turn from the door at the foot to an open crown at the top, so that a person could go up inside the standing wall without once crossing open floor.

Why that household chose to build hollow rather than raise a plain thick wall as the eastern Vell had done, the telling that has come to this one does not say, save that the ground the north-western Skell held there ran out to open water on three sides at once, and a household settled at such a place had cause, more than most, to want a height from which the sea in every direction could be watched without a climb up onto an exposed roof that the wind off that water might not have let a person keep their footing on for long. The tower's crown, once the stair had climbed the whole height of the wall to reach it, stood open to the sky, ringed by a low parapet no higher than a standing man's waist, and from it the water to the west lay open to the edge of sight on a clear day, with nothing between the tower's foot and the horizon but the sea.

The building of it, by what the telling has kept of the labour, went slower than the raising of any single house of the eastern cluster, for two faces of wall had to be carried up together, course matched to course on either side of the stair's gap, with cross-stones laid at intervals through the gap to bind the inner face to the outer and keep the whole from leaning apart as it climbed; and it is told that the household lost the top of one half-finished stair to a winter storm before the tower stood a third of its final height, the outer face alone thrown down while the inner held, and had to clear the fallen stone from the gap and set the cross-stones closer together through the whole climb of what remained before they trusted the wall to carry a stair any higher.

The first night any of that household spent at the tower's crown, once the stair stood whole and the parapet was laid, fell in a season of clear cold weather with little wind, and is remembered, in the one line of it the telling carries whole, for the plain fact that whoever climbed up that night saw more of the sea at once, by starlight alone, than any of that household had ever seen from any roof or headland they had stood on before, the water running unbroken from directly below the tower's foot out to where the dark of the sea and the dark of the sky no longer could be told apart. What was said on the crown that first night, if anything was said at all beyond what the climb and the sea's own width might draw from a person standing where no one of that shore had stood before, the telling does not carry; it keeps only that the climb was made, and that it was made again, by other hands of the same household, on other clear nights in the years that followed, until the crown of the tower had become, for that household, as ordinary a place to stand of an evening as any threshold or any strand.

The tower asked more of its household than the fourteen houses of the eastern Vell had asked of theirs, and the keeping of it once raised was the harder half of that asking, for a stone worked loose high in either face wanted setting again before the next storm found the gap it had opened, and the climb to reach a loose stone near the crown was neither short nor easy; and it is told that the household kept, from the first years the tower stood finished, a habit of walking the whole stair from foot to crown after every storm hard enough to have been worth remarking at all, feeling with a hand along both faces as they climbed for any stone gone slack in its bed, a labour that fell, in the years this telling has followed that household, to whichever of them had the surest foot and the least fear of the narrow dark of the stair, rather than to any one person by right of age or of standing.

The stair was dark through nearly its whole climb, lit only where a gap left on purpose in the outer face let in a narrow blade of daylight at two or three turns of its rise, so that a person climbing learned it as much by the hand trailing the inner wall and the foot's own count of the turns already taken as by anything the eye could make out in the gloom between one blade of light and the next; and a child of that household, sent up for the first time without a grown hand ahead of it to follow, is remembered to have stood some while at the foot of the stair before starting up, and to have counted the turns aloud the whole way, a habit that stayed with the child into a grown age, muttering the count of the stair under its breath even on climbs it no longer had any real need to count.

The household kept, besides the walking of the stair after storms, a second use for the tower beyond the evening climbs to its open crown: when a wind rose out of the west hard enough to make the open water dangerous to any boat still out on it, whoever of the household could be spared went up to the crown and stood watching the water below for as long as the light held, not for any one boat known to be out, for the household's own boats stayed ashore on such days, but on the chance that some other shore's craft, caught further out than its own crew had judged wise, might be seen making for whatever shelter the coast below the tower could offer it; and it is told, though the telling names no year for it, that a boat not of that household's own shore was once seen from the crown standing in toward the rocks below in weather that would have driven it onto them blind, and that a fire was lit on the parapet in time for its crew to see the coast they were running for and bear off from the worst of the rocks before the light failed altogether; where the boat and its crew went once they had borne off, the telling does not carry.

## IV. Of the Huts at Toll and the Sand

Far to the north, on the other coast of the island, where the land ran low into dune country and the wind off the sea had built the sand, over years longer than any telling could set a number to, into ridges taller than a standing man, a household of Toll built a scatter of stone huts in the lee of one such ridge, low-walled and turf-roofed, set close against the dune's own slope so that the ridge's own bulk broke the worst of the wind before it ever reached a wall.

The huts stood some years in the shelter the dune gave them, the dune troubling them no further, the sand that blew loose off its crown in an ordinary autumn gale settling no deeper against the walls than a household's own broom could clear from the threshold each morning, a small and steady labour so ordinary to that shore that no telling has troubled to remark it as labour at all, any more than a shore further south remarked the daily emptying of a fish-trap. Children of that household are remembered for having been set, almost as soon as they could be trusted to swing a broom, to clear the night's blown sand from the door each morning before the grown hands of the household turned to any other work, a task light enough for small hands and steady enough that a child too young yet for heavier work could be given the whole of it and trusted to see it done.

In the year this telling now comes to, a wind rose off the sea that did not settle in a night as the ordinary gales of that coast had always settled, but held through a full day and into a second, driving sand off the dune's whole exposed face in a haze thick enough that a person standing at one hut's door could not, by what the telling has kept of it, make out the next hut along with any certainty, the air gone the colour and near the heft of the ground it carried. The household worked at first as it had always worked, clearing what blew against the walls as fast as the wind piled it there, but the sand came faster than any number of hands could clear it, mounting against the windward wall of the first hut in the row until, by what the telling has kept of the second day's reckoning, it stood higher than the wall itself and had begun to press in over the turf of the roof.

It is told that the household left the huts before the second night of that wind closed on them, carrying what could be carried in the arms and on the back in the little time the sand's own rising gave them warning to judge by, and went inland to higher ground the ridge itself had always sheltered them from having to think about before, waiting out the rest of the storm under whatever cover the higher ground could give a household that had brought no shelter of its own with it. No one of that household is remembered, in any telling that has reached this one, to have been lost in the leaving of the huts, nor hurt beyond what a hard walk through blown sand in the last light of a bad day might be expected to cost any household that made it; the huts themselves are the only thing the telling says the sand kept.

When the wind fell, on the third day, and the household came back down from the higher ground to see what the storm had left them, the row of huts they had built in the dune's lee was not there to be seen at all: the dune had moved forward the whole width of the huts and some way beyond them, and stood, where the huts' doors and thresholds had opened onto level ground the morning before, as an unbroken slope of new sand, paler and looser underfoot than the ridge's older face, with nothing above it to show that a household had ever raised a wall or laid a roof on the ground it now covered. The household dug, in the days that followed, at the edge of the new sand where they judged the nearest hut's door might lie, and found, by what the telling has kept of the digging, enough of a wall and enough of a roof-turf still in place beneath the fresh sand to know that the huts stood yet, whole, under the weight that had come over them, before the digging itself was given up as asking more than it could return, and the household turned instead to raising new walls a little further inland, on ground the dune had not yet claimed and might, by their own reckoning of how the wind and the sand had always worked on that coast before, be trusted not to claim again for longer than any of them now living could expect to see.

Four shores, in the years this telling has followed them, each found its own shelter against what the sky or the sea or the ground itself might otherwise have taken from a household unhoused against it: bone raised against the open air at Dunn, stone and a hidden channel against the wet ground at the eastern Vell, a hollow wall and a climbing stair against the wind and the watching sea at the north-western Skell, and, at Toll, walls that in the end kept out neither wind nor sand, but were themselves kept, whole and unbroken, under the one thing they had never been raised to hold back. The household that built there last turned its face inland and did not trouble the buried row again; and the dune that had taken it stood afterward as it stood on the day the digging was given up, its new paler face already beginning, before that year was out, to darken and settle into the same weathered grey as the ridge behind it, with the roofs of Toll's first row lying still and undisturbed beneath it.
