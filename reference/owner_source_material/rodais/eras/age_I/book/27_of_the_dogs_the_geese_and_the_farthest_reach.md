# Of the Dogs, the Geese and the Farthest Reach

*VE 3,720 – 3,921*

## I. Of the Glen of the Stags

Dubhghall was twenty-five the autumn he first drove the gully alone, and he had been driving it in someone else's shadow since he was old enough to hold a stake upright. The glen of the stags lay east of the households, a fold of ground the red deer had used, by every telling the old people gave, since before any teller now living had a grandmother. Dubhghall's own father had walked it, and his father's father, laying the same lines of stakes and cut brush in the same low places each autumn to turn the herd's running into one running, funnelled down through birch and bramble to the narrow throat of the gully where men waited with spears braced against their own weight and the weight of what was coming.

It was plain work, and no one who did it thought otherwise. It wanted patience in the setting and nerve in the standing, and both of those Dubhghall had in the ordinary measure his people had, no more. He set his stakes where his father had set stakes, cut brush from the same low thickets so that the thickets kept their shape year on year and the deer, coming down off the high ground in the first cold, ran the path their mothers had run without knowing there was any other path to run. A drive was a slow thing to build and a fast thing to spend: half a day of quiet labour with mallet and knife, and then, some grey morning when the wind sat right, a noise raised from three sides at once, and the whole autumn's work gone by in the length of time it took a frightened animal to cross a gully.

He stood that first year at the narrow end with his uncle at his shoulder, close enough that Dubhghall could smell the wet wool of the older man's shoulder over the deer-smell coming down the wind ahead of the herd itself. Frost had whitened the bramble in the night and gone soft and dark by the time they took their places, so that the ground underfoot was greasy where the sun had not reached it and hard as bone where it had. The noise began somewhere up the glen, thin at first, then thickening as it was taken up along the line: sticks beaten on sticks, a dog's short bark cut off, a man's voice pitched high and wordless. Dubhghall's hands were cold on the spear-shaft and his knees, he would say afterward, had no more strength in them than a boy's, and then the first of the herd was on him, a hind with two calves close behind her flank, and past before he had thought to move at all.

The stags came after, and it was for the stags the whole day's labour had been spent. His uncle took the first, a big beast with a head like a low branch of oak, catching it low and letting it run itself onto the spear rather than driving the spear at it, which was, his uncle told him after, sitting on a wet stone with the blood still steaming off his hands in the cold air, the whole of the craft and the only part of it that could not be taught by watching. "You can be shown the stand," the old man said, working the feeling back into his fingers, "and you can be shown the throw. What you can't be shown is the moment you let the beast do the work for you instead of doing it yourself. That comes or it doesn't." Dubhghall's own throw went wide and struck a young stag in the shoulder rather than the throat, and the beast went down thrashing not twenty paces past him, bawling in a way that put the hair up on his arms worse than the cold had, and it took his uncle and two others to finish what his bad aim had begun. He was ashamed of it for a season, turning the throw over in his mind on nights when sleep was slow to come, and practising alone through the winter against a bundle of brush hung from a birch, until by the spring his arm knew the throw the way it knew the shape of his own name. No one else thought the wide throw worth remembering past the day it happened. That was the mercy of a household that lived close to its own failures: it forgot them quickly, having too many of its own to keep count of another's.

The antlers of the greatest stags were carried apart from the rest of the cutting and set up together at the mouth of the gully, a stand of them leaning one against another so that from a little way off they looked, in half-light, almost like a thing standing on its own account, a beast made of the parts of other beasts. No one had ever said why this was done, and Dubhghall did not ask his uncle, who would not have known either; it was the shape a good drive took at its end, the meat carried home and the horn left standing where it had fallen, a mark of what the glen had given that year set apart from what had been taken away and eaten. In a hard autumn the stand was thin. In a good one it grew tall enough that a boy could stand in its shadow at noon and cast no shadow of his own that reached past it.

Dubhghall drove that gully for thirty years, first at his uncle's shoulder and then, when his uncle's knees would no longer hold a standing man through a whole autumn morning, at the head of the line himself, setting the stakes where the stakes had always been set and teaching younger men the throw that let a beast run onto the spear rather than the other way about. He was not a hard teacher, by the accounts that outlived him, though he was exacting in the one thing that mattered: he would let a young man's aim go wide a dozen times before he said a word of blame, but he would not let a young man stand carelessly, feet planted wrong or weight back on his heels, because a badly planted man was a man the glen might yet take instead of giving to. He buried his uncle at the last of those thirty autumns, an old man by then, worn thin by winters, in ground close enough to the gully's mouth that the old man's grandchildren could point to the low rise from the stand of horn and say, without needing to walk the distance, that he lay just there. Dubhghall stood afterward at the mouth of the glen with the season's antlers stacked fresh beside the grey weathered horn of years before, and thought, as much as he thought anything so plainly, that the glen would go on taking its deer and giving its meat when every man now living at its mouth had gone the same way as the horn: standing a while, whitening a while, and then down among the rest, indistinguishable from what had come before it.

## II. Of the Dog at Cnoc fhionn

It was Dubhghall's own grandson, a boy called after no one living, who first buried a dog the way a person is buried, and it was done at Cnoc fhionn, the hill households east of the glen where Dubhghall's daughter had married into a family with fields of their own. The dog had been the boy's from a pup, one of a litter got off a bitch that ran the drives at the glen, and it had grown into the kind of dog that is loved past all reckoning of its use, little good for herding or hunting either one: it herded nothing well, killed nothing bigger than a rat, barked at strangers and at its own shadow with equal conviction, and slept across the boy's feet every night of its life from the week it was weaned, so that the boy learned early to sleep with his knees drawn up rather than disturb it.

It died old, as dogs that are loved past their usefulness are sometimes let to do, lying down one evening by the hearth after a day no different from any other and not rising the next morning. The boy, who was near a man himself by then, found it stiff and cold in the grey light before the household was properly awake, and sat with his hand on its flank a long while before he told anyone, as though the telling would make the thing truer than it already was. When he did tell it, his mother said only that it had been a good dog and a long-lived one, and went back to the morning's work, for a dog's death, however loved the dog, was not a thing a household could stop its day for. The boy dug it a grave the length of a dog and not the length of anything smaller, on the low rise above the house where the ground was dry and did not flood in a wet spring, working through the morning with a mattock too big for the job and getting blisters for his trouble that he did not complain of. He laid the dog in on its side, the way a sleeping dog lies, its legs drawn up as they had been drawn up every night of its life across his own feet, and before he closed the earth over it he set a shell at its head, a sea-shell some trader or fire-bearer had carried up from the coast years before and given to the boy as a boy's trinket, kept on a shelf by the hearth and turned over in idle hands on winter evenings but never used for anything until that morning.

No one told him to do it. That was the thing the household spoke of after, when the story of it had gone round the near hearths and come back changed a little in the telling, as such things do: that the boy had not asked anyone, not his mother, not old Dubhghall, whether a dog might be given a shell and a grave the way a person was given a shell and a grave, but had simply done it, on his own reckoning that the dog he loved deserved no less a leaving than the grandmother he had loved before it, who lay not far off on the same rise. Some of the older women thought it strange, even a little wrong, to put a beast's death alongside a person's in the same ground and by the same rite, and said so plainly enough at the next gathering of the near hearths, one of them going so far as to ask the boy, not unkindly, whether he meant to put a shell at the head of every lamb the household lost too. The boy had no ready answer for her, being young and not much given to argument, and only said that the dog had slept at his feet for nine years and a lamb had not, which the old woman allowed, after a pause, was answer enough. Others, and among them Dubhghall himself, thought it strange only that no one had done it sooner, dogs having slept at hearths and died at hearths since long before any teller now living had a telling of the first fire itself. "A thing that shares your fire that long," the old man said to his daughter, turning the matter over as he was given to turning things over in his last years, "and never once asks for more than it's given, earns more than being left for the crows."

Whatever was thought of it, it was done, and it was done again. Within a few years two more households near Cnoc fhionn had buried dogs the same way, a shell at the head and the grave dug proper, one of them a hunting dog that had put up game for its household for a dozen seasons and the other a pup that had lived scarcely a year before a fever took it, and the custom travelled on quietly, hearth to hearth, the way any custom does that answers something people already feel and only wanted shown how to say. No beast had been given a grave on the island before that boy dug the first one; afterward it was not thought remarkable at all, only ordinary, the way the covered hearth had gone in one lifetime from a strange new digging to a thing no household in those hills would think of doing without.

Dubhghall did not live to see the custom old. He died at Cnoc fhionn in his sixty-fifth year, among his daughter's family and near the hill where the dog and, before long, his own grandson's grandmother lay, an old man by the reckoning of that country though not, by the reckoning of the line at Tobar dhìreach, any great age at all; his people did not live long lives, and did not look for them, and thought a man who had kept his knees and his eyesight into his sixties had been given a fair measure and no cause for complaint. It was said of him at his own leaving, by the same grandson who had dug the dog's grave, that he had never in thirty autumns of driving the glen lost a man to a stag's horn or a fall on wet ground, a modest enough praise as such things go, and in that country no small thing to be able to say, and the grandson set a shell at his grandfather's head too, the last of a small store he had kept since the dog's burying, saying that a man who had taught him the throw that let a beast do the work deserved no less a shell than a dog that had only ever slept across his feet.

## III. Of the Geese of Eilean ghorm

West of the last of the households, off the coast no hearth-kindred had ever settled, there lay an island the tellers called Eilean ghorm, and to it, every year with the first hard storm of autumn, came the grey geese. No one now living could say when the geese had first been noticed rather than merely seen, the way no one could say when the wood in the east had first been held sacred rather than merely walked around; but by Dubhghall's day, and long before it, it was a thing every hearth from the glen to the northern moor could tell a stranger without needing to be asked twice: that the geese came to Eilean ghorm with the first storm and left it with the last, and that in all the count of fathers no one had ever gone out to that island to take them.

It was not, by any telling that survived, forbidden in the way the second wood was forbidden, with its line of set stones and its children taught early to stop at it. No household kept a law against the taking of the geese, because no household had ever needed one; it had simply never been done, the way a well is not fouled and a hearth-fire is not fed with green wood, a thing so plainly against the grain of how things were kept that the not-doing of it wanted no telling to hold it up. A boat could be built to reach the island easily enough, and boats far harder to build had crossed to islands far further out for seals and for less. But the geese came in on the storm-wind with a noise that carried for miles on a still evening, ragged lines resolving into order as they came down out of a sky already darkening toward the season's first real weather, and went out again months later the same way, their going almost always a day or two ahead of whatever storm would be the year's last, so that a household that watched them close enough could read the turn of the whole winter in it. Between the coming and the going the island was theirs and no one's else, and that was held to be simply how the geese and the island stood to one another, needing no more reason than the tide needs a reason to come in.

A young man of the northern moor, some years before Dubhghall's own time, was said to have gone out to Eilean ghorm all the same, in a season of want when the store of dried meat had run low before spring and a household counted every goose it saw fly over as meat it did not have. He built himself a small hide boat over a hard winter, telling no one what it was for, and put out from the shore alone one grey morning with the geese still on the island in their thousands. What happened to him the tellers did not agree on even then, and agreed on less as the years carried the story further from anyone who might have set it straight: some said he found the crossing harder than he had judged and never reached the island at all, his boat found stove in on the rocks below the point a week after; others said he reached it and came back with nothing to show for the going, the geese having risen off the water before his boat was within a bowshot and not settled again while he stayed; a few, and these were the tellers most given to saying more than they knew, held that he had reached the island, and that something on it had turned him back before ever he raised a hand toward a goose, though what, they could not say, having no more of the tale than the bare bones any teller starts from. Whichever of it was true, and none now living could sort the true from the added, the young man was not spoken of afterward as one who had found a rich hunting-ground and kept it to himself. He was spoken of as a warning, the kind of warning a country keeps by instinct, from one hard example, with no need to understand the danger it points at.

Dubhghall himself, in his last years, used to stand at the shore below Cnoc fhionn of a still autumn evening and watch, when the wind was right, for the first grey line low over the sea toward Eilean ghorm, and count it, in the way a man counts a thing he cannot number, as the true turning of the year quite as much as any telling of the cats' crossing or the salmon's run. When his grandson asked him once, as a boy asks, why no one hunted them there when the meat on so many birds together would have fed a household through half a winter, Dubhghall thought a while before he answered, watching the last light go off the water, and gave the boy no better answer than any other man of his country would have given, but gave it plainly: that some things were left because a people had always left them, and a people that stopped leaving what it had always left was not, any longer, quite the people it had been. The boy did not press him further, being at an age when a grandfather's plain answers were still answer enough, and the two of them stood together until the light was gone and the geese, if they had come that evening, had come and settled and could no longer be told from the darkening water.

## IV. Of Skell at Its Height

Far to the west, on a coast none of Dubhghall's kin had walked and none of their tellings named, another people kept a different reckoning of what a shore could hold, and by Dubhghall's own lifetime that people had come to hold every coast of the island there was to hold. From the northern moor to the southern lowland, from the eastern cliffs to the far side of the western capes, there was no stretch of shore left where the old ones of the coast had not, at one time or another in the long count of their own fathers, set a landing, built a mole, cut a mooring-stake into the mud, or laid a kiln to dry the catch over peat-smoke. Their tellings, if they had tellings, went unheard by any of Dubhghall's people, for the two never met on any shore or any hill; what was known of the coast-people at the height of their reach was known only by what a hunter of the northern moor, following a fox down toward the sea, might see from a safe way off and carry home half understood.

Of all their landings the greatest, by any such distant seeing, stood where the Abhainn naomh ran down into the sea on the western coast, at the place the coast-people called Skell, the same name they gave to more than one landing along that shore, as though the word were less a name for one place than a word for what a landing was. The mole that earlier generations of the coast-people had built there, log-rolled boulders judged stone by stone into the current's lie, had stood two hundred years and more by Dubhghall's day, mended and added to season on season until it ran further out into the river's mouth than the first builders could have raised it in one lifetime, and behind it, in the still water it made, lay more boats at once than any hunter of the northern moor had words to count.

A boy of the moor, out after foxes in the year the tellers later set at the height of the reckoning, came down further than he had ever gone before, past the last ground his own people held and into country he had only ever heard of, and lay a long while on his belly at the crest of a dune, looking down at what he had found, his fox forgotten and his heart going hard enough in his chest that he was sure, in that first moment, the sound of it alone would carry down to the shore and give him away. Nothing below him stirred toward him; no one on that shore had any thought to spare for one boy hidden in marram grass a long bowshot off, and after a while his breath slowed enough that he dared lift his head and look his fill.

The telling he brought home that evening did not shrink in the carrying, hearth to hearth, the way most tellings shrink, for there was nothing in it that needed adding to: hulls of every size drawn up along a shore that seemed to have no end to it, smoke going up from more fires than he could set a number to, the shapes of people moving among the boats too far off and too many to be watched one by one, children running along the mole's stones the way children run along any wall built for other purposes, and beyond them, out past the mole's far end, more hulls again riding at anchor on the swell, their masts, if masts they were, thin as reeds against the low western sun. He lay there until the light began to fail and the cold off the ground had worked into him past bearing, and then he went back the way he had come, empty-handed of any fox but full of a thing he would carry the rest of his life.

Who these people were or where they had come from, he did not say, for he did not know, and no one who heard him tell it pressed him for an answer his own eyes had not given him; that was the way of every such telling on the hills, a shape seen and reported and never once explained, for there was no one on either side of the unmet line between the two peoples who could have explained it. It was enough, and more than enough, to say what he had seen: a shore fuller of boats than any shore had any right to be, stretching further than a boy could walk in a morning and still find its end, and smoke enough over it to make the sky above the river's mouth look, from a distance, like a low grey cloud that never moved and never rained.

That was as near as the story went, in Dubhghall's country or in any other, to an account of what the coast-people were or how far their reach had grown. The hills kept their own count of things, the drive at the glen and the shell at the dog's grave and the grey geese coming in on the storm, and the coast, wherever its people had come from and however far they had spread, kept a count of its own that no hill-teller ever heard the whole of. What the boy on the dune had seen was, by every reckoning that survived him, the coast-people at the widest reach they would ever hold: every landing filled, every mole finished that would ever be finished, every boat that would ever be built already built and drawn up on a shore that ran, that autumn, from the northern moor to the southern lowland without one stretch of open coast left empty of them. It could not be told, from so far off and by eyes that never looked again, whether the shore that year was rising still or had already begun, without any sign a boy on a dune could read, to turn.
