# Of the Dogs, the Geese and the Farthest Reach

*VE 3,720 – 3,921*

## I. Of the Glen of the Stags

Dubhghall was twenty-five the autumn he first drove the gully alone, and he had been driving it in someone else's shadow since he was old enough to hold a stake upright. The glen of the stags lay east of the households, a fold of ground the red deer had used, by every telling the old people gave, since before any teller now living had a grandmother. Dubhghall's own father had walked it, and his father's father, laying the same lines of stakes and cut brush in the same low places each autumn to turn the herd's running into one running, funnelled down through birch and bramble to the narrow throat of the gully where men waited with spears braced against their own weight and the weight of what was coming.

It was not clever work, and no one who did it thought it was. It was work that wanted patience in the setting and nerve in the standing, and both of those Dubhghall had in the ordinary measure his people had, no more. He set his stakes where his father had set stakes, cut brush from the same low thickets so that the thickets kept their shape year on year and the deer, coming down off the high ground in the first cold, ran the path their mothers had run without knowing there was any other path to run. A drive was a slow thing to build and a fast thing to spend: half a day of quiet labour with mallet and knife, and then, some grey morning when the wind sat right, a noise raised from three sides at once, and the whole autumn's work gone by in the length of time it took a frightened animal to cross a gully.

He stood that first year at the narrow end with his uncle at his shoulder, close enough that Dubhghall could smell the wet wool of the older man's shoulder over the deer-smell coming down the wind ahead of the herd itself. The noise began somewhere up the glen, thin at first, then thickening as it was taken up along the line: sticks beaten on sticks, a dog's short bark cut off, a man's voice pitched high and wordless. Dubhghall's hands were cold on the spear-shaft and his knees, he would say afterward, had no more strength in them than a boy's, and then the first of the herd was on him, a hind with two calves close behind her flank, and past before he had thought to move at all.

The stags came after, and it was for the stags the whole day's labour had been spent. His uncle took the first, a big beast with a head like a low branch of oak, catching it low and letting it run itself onto the spear rather than driving the spear at it, which was, his uncle told him after, the whole of the craft and the only part of it that could not be taught by watching. Dubhghall's own throw went wide and struck a young stag in the shoulder rather than the throat, and the beast went down thrashing not twenty paces past him, and it took his uncle and two others to finish what his bad aim had begun. He was ashamed of it for a season. No one else thought it worth remembering past the day.

The antlers of the greatest stags were carried apart from the rest of the cutting and set up together at the mouth of the gully, a stand of them leaning one against another so that from a little way off they looked, in half-light, almost like a thing standing on its own account, a beast made of the parts of other beasts. No one had ever said why this was done, and Dubhghall did not ask his uncle, who would not have known either; it was simply the shape a good drive took at its end, the meat carried home and the horn left standing where it had fallen, a mark of what the glen had given that year set apart from what had been taken away and eaten. In a hard autumn the stand was thin. In a good one it grew tall enough that a boy could stand in its shadow at noon.

Dubhghall drove that gully for thirty years, first at his uncle's shoulder and then, when his uncle's knees would no longer hold a standing man through a whole autumn morning, at the head of the line himself, setting the stakes where the stakes had always been set and teaching younger men the throw that let a beast run onto the spear rather than the other way about. He buried his uncle at the last of those thirty autumns, an old man by then, worn thin by winters, and stood afterward at the mouth of the glen with the season's antlers stacked fresh beside the grey weathered horn of years before, thinking, as much as he thought anything so plainly, that the glen would go on taking its deer and giving its meat long after every man now living at its mouth had gone the same way as the horn: standing a while, and then down among the rest.

## II. Of the Dog at Cnoc fhionn

It was Dubhghall's own grandson, a boy called after no one living, who first buried a dog the way a person is buried, and it was done at Cnoc fhionn, the hill households east of the glen where Dubhghall's daughter had married into a family with fields of their own. The dog had been the boy's from a pup, one of a litter got off a bitch that ran the drives at the glen, and it had grown into the kind of dog that is not much use for anything but is loved past all reckoning of its use: it herded nothing well, killed nothing bigger than a rat, and slept across the boy's feet every night of its life from the week it was weaned.

It died old, as dogs that are loved past their usefulness are sometimes let to do, lying down one evening by the hearth and not rising the next morning, and the boy, who was near a man himself by then, dug it a grave the length of a dog and not the length of anything smaller, on the low rise above the house where the ground was dry and did not flood in a wet spring. He laid it in on its side, the way a sleeping dog lies, and before he closed the earth over it he set a shell at its head, a sea-shell some trader or fire-bearer had carried up from the coast years before and given to the boy as a boy's trinket, kept on a shelf by the hearth and never used for anything until that morning.

No one told him to do it. That was the thing the household spoke of after, when the story of it had gone round the near hearths and come back changed a little in the telling, as such things do: that the boy had not asked anyone, not his mother, not old Dubhghall, whether a dog might be given a shell and a grave the way a person was given a shell and a grave, but had simply done it, on his own reckoning that the dog he loved deserved no less a leaving than the grandmother he had loved before it. Some of the older women thought it strange, even a little wrong, to put a beast's death alongside a person's in the same ground and by the same rite. Others, and among them Dubhghall himself, thought it strange only that no one had done it sooner, dogs having slept at hearths and died at hearths since long before any teller now living had a telling of the first fire itself.

Whatever was thought of it, it was done, and it was done again. Within a few years two more households near Cnoc fhionn had buried dogs the same way, a shell at the head and the grave dug proper, and the custom did not stop there but went on quietly hearth to hearth the way any custom does that answers something people already feel and only wanted shown how to say. No beast had been given a grave on the island before that boy dug the first one; afterward it was not thought remarkable at all, only ordinary, the way the covered hearth had gone in one lifetime from a strange new digging to a thing no household in those hills would think of doing without.

Dubhghall did not live to see the custom old. He died at Cnoc fhionn in his sixty-fifth year, among his daughter's family and near the hill where the dog and, before long, his own grandson's grandmother lay, and it was said of him at his own leaving, by the same grandson who had dug the dog's grave, that he had never in thirty autumns of driving the glen lost a man to a stag's horn or a fall on wet ground, which was not the highest thing that could be said of anyone but was, in that country, no small thing to be able to say.

## III. Of the Geese of Eilean ghorm

West of the last of the households, off the coast no hearth-kindred had ever settled, there lay an island the tellers called Eilean ghorm, and to it, every year with the first hard storm of autumn, came the grey geese. No one now living could say when the geese had first been noticed rather than merely seen, the way no one could say when the wood in the east had first been held sacred rather than merely walked around; but by Dubhghall's day, and long before it, it was a thing every hearth from the glen to the northern moor could tell a stranger without needing to be asked twice: that the geese came to Eilean ghorm with the first storm and left it with the last, and that in all the count of fathers no one had ever gone out to that island to take them.

It was not, by any telling that survived, forbidden in the way the second wood was forbidden, with its line of set stones and its children taught early to stop at it. No household kept a law against the taking of the geese, because no household had ever needed one; it had simply never been done, the way a well is not fouled and a hearth-fire is not fed with green wood, a thing so plainly against the grain of how things were kept that the not-doing of it wanted no telling to hold it up. A boat could be built to reach the island easily enough, and boats far harder to build had crossed to islands far further out for seals and for less. But the geese came in on the storm-wind with a noise that carried for miles on a still evening, ragged lines resolving into order as they came down, and went out again months later the same way, and between the coming and the going the island was theirs and no one's else, and that was held to be simply how the geese and the island stood to one another, needing no more reason than the tide needs a reason to come in.

Dubhghall himself, in his last years, used to stand at the shore below Cnoc fhionn of a still autumn evening and watch, when the wind was right, for the first grey line low over the sea toward Eilean ghorm, and count it, in the way a man counts a thing he cannot number, as the true turning of the year quite as much as any telling of the cats' crossing or the salmon's run. When his grandson asked him once, as a boy asks, why no one hunted them there when the meat on so many birds together would have fed a household through half a winter, Dubhghall had no better answer than any other man of his country would have given, and gave it plainly: that some things were left because a people had always left them, and a people that stopped leaving what it had always left was not, any longer, quite the people it had been.

## IV. Of Skell at Its Height

Far to the west, on a coast none of Dubhghall's kin had walked and none of their tellings named, another people kept a different reckoning of what a shore could hold, and by Dubhghall's own lifetime that people had come to hold every coast of the island there was to hold. From the northern moor to the southern lowland, from the eastern cliffs to the far side of the western capes, there was no stretch of shore left where the old ones of the coast had not, at one time or another in the long count of their own fathers, set a landing, built a mole, cut a mooring-stake into the mud, or laid a kiln to dry the catch over peat-smoke. Their tellings, if they had tellings, went unheard by any of Dubhghall's people, for the two never met on any shore or any hill; what was known of the coast-people at the height of their reach was known only by what a hunter of the northern moor, following a fox down toward the sea, might see from a safe way off and carry home half understood.

Of all their landings the greatest, by any such distant seeing, stood where the Abhainn naomh ran down into the sea on the western coast, at the place the coast-people called Skell, the same name they gave to more than one landing along that shore, as though the word were less a name for one place than a word for what a landing was. The mole that earlier generations of the coast-people had built there, log-rolled boulders judged stone by stone into the current's lie, had stood two hundred years and more by Dubhghall's day, mended and added to until it ran further out into the river's mouth than the first builders could have raised it in one lifetime, and behind it, in the still water it made, lay more boats at once than any hunter of the northern moor had words to count.

A boy of the moor, out after foxes in the year the tellers later set at the height of the reckoning, came down further than he had ever gone before and lay a long while on his belly at the crest of a dune, looking down at what he had found. He told it after, hearth to hearth, the way any wonder is told: hulls of every size drawn up along a shore that seemed to have no end to it, smoke going up from more fires than he could set a number to, the shapes of people moving among the boats too far off and too many to be watched one by one, and beyond them, out past the mole's far end, more hulls again riding at anchor on the swell, their masts, if masts they were, thin as reeds against the low western sun. He said nothing of who these people were or where they had come from, for he did not know, and no one who heard him tell it pressed him for an answer his own eyes had not given him. It was enough, and more than enough, to say what he had seen: a shore fuller of boats than any shore had any right to be, stretching further than a boy could walk in a morning and still find its end.

That was as near as the story went, in Dubhghall's country or in any other, to an account of what the coast-people were or how far their reach had grown. The hills kept their own count of things, the drive at the glen and the shell at the dog's grave and the grey geese coming in on the storm, and the coast, wherever its people had come from and however far they had spread, kept a count of its own that no hill-teller ever heard the whole of. What the boy on the dune had seen was, by every reckoning that survived him, the coast-people at the widest reach they would ever hold: every landing filled, every mole finished that would ever be finished, every boat that would ever be built already built and drawn up on a shore that ran, that autumn, from the northern moor to the southern lowland without one stretch of open coast left empty of them. It could not be told, from so far off and by eyes that never looked again, whether the shore that year was rising still or had already begun, without any sign a boy on a dune could read, to turn.
