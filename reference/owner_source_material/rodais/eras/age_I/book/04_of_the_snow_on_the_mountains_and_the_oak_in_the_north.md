# Of the Snow on the Mountains and the Oak in the North

*VE 227 – 505*

## I. Of Conn and the Rising Water

The Abhainn uaine ran narrower in Conn's grandfather's day, or so it was told at the hearths that summered along it, and narrower still in the day before that. No one who herded the high ground doubted it, for the proof of it lay in the gravel of the river's own bed for anyone who cared to walk it and look.

Conn was born to a household of the upper valley some two hundred and five years after the night of the lightning, a fair way inland of Tobar dhìreach and higher up than any household cared to winter, and he grew, as his father and his father's father had grown, following sheep and a scatter of small dark cattle up into the high pastures each year as the snow drew back off them. It was slow, patient work, not glad work and not grim either, the kind that asks little of a man beyond a good eye for weather and the willingness to walk further than he is tired. He had both. By the time he was a young man grown he could tell, looking at the colour of the light on the tops before dawn, whether the beasts would want driving down before noon, and he was seldom wrong, and the older herders of his kin let him choose the ground more often than his years alone would have earned him.

The snow that mattered to Conn was the old snow, lying all the year round in the high corries under the shoulder of the central mountains and fed the river below with its melting, thin in winter when the frost held it fast and full and cold and loud in the first weeks of summer when the sun at last found it. His grandfather's telling held that the snowfields had once come down further, close enough that a man driving cattle to the high grass in an early year might still find drifts of it lying in the shadowed clefts as late as midsummer. In Conn's own boyhood the same clefts showed bare rock by the turn of spring, and every year of his growing the white seemed to draw back a little further up the mountain's shoulder, leaving more grass than before, and more water running down off it than the grass alone could well explain.

He did not think much of the reason for it. A young man following beasts has little use for reasons; he has use only for the ground he is on and the ground he means to reach before dark. But he noticed, the way a herder notices everything about the country he crosses year on year without troubling to ask why, that the river's own bed was widening under him, and that the banks he had walked as a boy were not always the banks he walked as a man.

It was in his twenty-fifth year, the year his kin reckoned by the wet spring that drowned half the low pasture, that Conn first laid hearth-stones on the gravel terrace above the river's summer bend, a day and more up from where the household wintered, in a stretch of ground his father had used before him and his father's father before that. He built the ring the way it had always been built there: a flat stone at the back to break the wind off the water, smaller stones set round in a half-circle, the whole of it a stone's throw above the river's edge and well above, so every herder before him had judged, any water the Abhainn uaine could reasonably be expected to send against it. He kept a coal there from the household's own fire, carried up in a clay pot banked in ash the way every fire on that ground was carried since the mason's day, and he slept by it through the short nights of high summer with the beasts grazing loose around him and the water running on below.

The river rose against him twice in the years he kept that ground. The first time was a small thing, a wet week in an ordinary summer that sent the water a few feet up the shingle and no further, enough to wet the lowest stones of his fire-ring and no more, and he moved his hearth back a body's length without much thought and built it again exactly as before. The second time was not small.

It came in his thirty-fourth year, in a summer that had begun hot and dry and then broken all at once into a run of days when the cloud sat on the high ground and did not lift, day after day, until even the oldest of the household said they could not remember its like. Conn was two days up from the winter ground with the beasts when the water began to climb. He woke on the third morning of the cloud to a sound under the ordinary sound of the river, a deeper note that had not been there the evening before, and came out from under his hide to find the water already at the lowest of his fire-stones, brown and thick with everything it had torn loose above him, carrying whole branches down at a pace no man could have outrun on the bank.

He had time, though not much of it, and he used it the way a man uses time he does not trust to last: he raked the coal out of the ring first, before anything else, into the clay pot, packed it round with cold ash from the rim, and only then began driving the beasts up off the terrace to the higher shelf behind it, shouting and slapping at the slowest of the dark cattle with the flat of his hand until they moved. The beasts he got up; the fire-ring stayed where it was, and by the time he turned to look back at it, from the higher ground with the pot held against his chest, the water had already taken the lowest stones under and was working at the second row, and by evening, when the cloud broke at last and the river began at last to fall, there was nothing left of the ring he had built at the start of that year but the flat stone at its back, half buried now in silt, with a scour mark on its face showing how high the water had stood.

He built again above it, on the higher shelf where he had driven the beasts, and that ring held through every summer he kept the ground after. But he did not fill in the lower one, and he did not let his sons fill it either, when in his old age he walked that stretch of the river with them and showed them where the water had been. He left the old stones where the flood had left them, half sunk in gravel a stone's throw below his own hearth, and beside them, further down still, a third ring older than either, that his father had shown him in the same way his father's father had shown it: three rings of fire-stones on three shelves of the one terrace, each a little higher than the last, each set there in its own year by a man who had judged, as carefully as Conn himself had judged, how far the water could reasonably be trusted not to come.

"The river climbs," Conn told his sons, standing among the three rings with the water a long way below them all that year, low and clear and ordinary, giving no sign that it had ever meant them harm. "It has climbed in my father's life and it climbed again in mine, and I do not know why, only that the high snow gives it more water each year than it gave the year before, and that a man who builds his fire by a river's edge builds it where the river was, not where the river is."

His sons kept the highest ring after him, and moved higher again in their own time, for the water climbed a little further even in their lives, though never again all at once as it had climbed against Conn. Conn died in his sixty-second year, an old man for that hard country, at the household's own hearth in the low valley. It was the high ground and the three rings on it that his kin remembered him by, and travellers who came that way in years after, following the same summer track up the Abhainn uaine, were shown the terrace and told, in as many words as the teller cared to give it, that a man once built his fire there three times over, each time a little further from a river that would not stay where a man could trust it.

## II. Of Eachna and the New Hearths

West of Conn's high pastures, down off the shoulder of the massif and out onto ground that opened flatter and drier than the herding country, there was in those generations no hearth at all. It was good grazing, level enough to run on without looking at one's feet, watered by a burn that ran clean the year round, and it stood close enough to Tobar dhìreach that a fast walker might cover the distance between dawn and midday. But no household had settled there, for a valley that has water and grass and no fire of its own is a valley people pass through on the way to somewhere else, and not a valley people stay in.

Eachna's household changed that, in the year she came into the fullness of her own judgement, some two hundred and eighty years and more after the night of the lightning.

She was born to a household some way south of Tobar dhìreach, one of the many by then that kept a fire lit from the mason's own, carried to them hearth by hearth in the generations since Ailbhe had first walked it outward, and she grew up knowing the fire the way every child of that country grew up knowing it, as a thing fed and watched and never let go dark, without troubling to ask where the first of it had come from. What she wanted, from an early age, was ground of her own, good open ground for a household rather than a corner of her father's, and by the time she was a young woman she had already walked the flatter country to the west more than once, looking at it the way a woman looks at a thing she means, in time, to have.

She took her fire from Tobar dhìreach the year she went to settle it, walking the day's distance with a coal from the line's own hearth carried in a pot the way every fire on that ground had been carried since the mason first carried his. The household that gave it to her asked nothing beyond the giving; it had been the mason's own rule, kept by his son and his son's son after him, that a fire asked for was a fire given, and by Eachna's day the rule was old enough that no one thought to question it. What she did not carry away, and did not ask for, was any piece of the coloured coal. The line kept that apart, as it had kept it since the vein was covered: the fire it gave to other hearths was ordinary fire, burning ordinary fuel, the mason's fire and no other, and no hearth beyond the line's own ever burned, in those years, so much as a sliver of what Neachdan had carried home under his cloak.

Eachna's first fire on the new ground was laid on a bed of pine brought down from the higher slopes and heather cut and dried through a summer for the purpose, the pine for its quick catching and the heather to hold the heat once caught, banked the way every careful hearth by then was banked, on stone rather than bare turf, for that lesson too had gone out from the mason's own household by her day and travelled as far and as freely as the fire. Round the hearth, in the seasons after, lay the ordinary leavings of an ordinary household's fire: scrapers of grey flint worked from stone carried up from the burn-bed, split and cracked for the marrow in them, the bones of red deer taken on the moor above, a scatter of hazel shells from nuts roasted whole in the embers. No coloured stone lay among them, then or for a long while after, and any traveller who came to that hearth in the years after and dug in the ash of it would have found nothing to mark it out from any other fire of that age, save that it was well kept and long kept, the household growing round it year on year as Eachna's own children and their children stayed on ground their mother had chosen rather than moving on.

The place took its name from the ground before it took any name from Eachna, called simply the straight field by those who first worked it, for it lay level and true between the burn and the rise behind it in a way that little ground on that broken country did; and it kept that name, Achadh dhìreach, after the households round it had grown too many to count by any one head, well after Eachna herself was gone from it.

In her old age it was said of her household, once it had grown large enough to be spoken of as more than one woman's fire, that its hearths kept the beasts closer in at night than the herding kin further up the valley troubled to, for the deer that came down off the moor in the dark months to browse the low ground had grown bold in a country still thin of houses, and more than one calf had been lost to nothing worse than a beast wandering too far from the firelight in a black night. Eachna's sons took to hunting the deer by torch after dark rather than waiting on daylight, a burning brand carried low and a second man behind with a spear, working the herds back from the standing crop toward the burn where the ground was open enough to see them by; it was slow work and cold work, and it did not always answer, though it answered oftener than watching alone had, and it kept, through Eachna's own lifetime and some way past it, more calves alive through the dark months than the valley had thought to expect.

Eachna lived to see her hearth become three hearths and then five, the straight field filling the way a valley fills, household by household, as sons and daughters married out and stayed near rather than moving off, each new hearth lit in turn from the one before it, back in an unbroken line to the coal she herself had carried from Tobar dhìreach as a young woman with more will than patience. She died at Achadh dhìreach in her sixty-eighth year, by her own hearth among her own grown children, and the fire she had carried burned on there after her the way every fire on that island by then was expected to burn on, tended by hands that had never seen Tobar dhìreach and never would, and owing it nothing beyond the memory of where the first of it had come from.

## III. Of the Birch That Went and the Oak That Came

It is the grandmothers who keep this part of the telling, more than any single deed of any single household, for it happened the way the snowfields drew back on the high ground: too slowly for any one generation to say they had seen it happen, and plainly enough that three generations set together could see that it had.

The old women of the northern heath, in Eachna's day and after it, told it this way to their grandchildren, sitting by fires their own grandmothers had never known: that when their own grandmothers were girls, the ground north and east of the high valleys had stood thick with birch, pale-barked and quick-grown, and with pine among it on the drier rises, and little else beside heather and the low scrub that birch and pine will suffer to grow beneath them. Fuel from that country was thin fuel, quick to catch and quick to burn away, and a household that wintered near it kept its fire hungry through the cold months and grudged every armful it fed to the flame.

By the time those same grandmothers were themselves old, the telling went, the birch had thinned in places and gone from others altogether, and oak stood where it had stood, broad-crowned and slow-grown, with hazel thick beneath it wherever the light broke through, and the ground under both smelled different in autumn than it had ever smelled under birch alone, of leaf-mould and the sweet rot of fallen mast rather than the thin dry smell birch leaves give as they curl. Fuel from oak was better fuel, slower to catch but far slower to burn away, holding a fire through a night that birch alone would have starved by midnight, and a household that had oak within a morning's walk kept a warmer hearth for less carrying than a household that had only birch, or so every grandmother who told it agreed, whatever else she disagreed on.

No one could say, in Eachna's day or after, why the one tree should give way to the other over so wide a country, or how long the giving-way had truly taken, for it was not a thing any single teller had watched from its beginning to its end, only a thing each teller had watched a portion of and heard the rest from a mother or a grandmother who had watched the portion before. The northern grove that stood furthest into that changing country, far past any household's grazing, was oak already by the time any hearth-teller's grandmother could remember it, standing on ground that the oldest tellings held had once been open heath, bare and windswept, with nothing on it taller than a man's waist; and by Eachna's grandchildren's day it was oak grown tall enough that a man walking under it in summer could not see the sky for the leaves of it, and the ground beneath was thick with a mast of fallen acorns that no one troubled, in those years, to gather. That grove kept, among those few who spoke of it at all, the old name of Doire ghlas, though it lay so far to the north and so far from any hearth that most households of the high valleys had never seen it and never would, and knew it only as a word in an old woman's telling, a place where, it was said, the trees had once been different, and had changed themselves without asking leave of anyone.

What the change meant, if it meant anything at all, no teller of those generations pretended to know, and the wiser grandmothers did not pretend either. They told it as they told the snowfields drawing back on the high ground above Conn's country, as a thing the world did in its own time and by its own reckoning, neither for the comfort of the households that lived through it nor against them, only alongside them, the way the seasons went alongside a life without ever once asking how that life was going.

## IV. Of Luag and the Bare Horn

The households of the northern heath knew, by the time Eachna's grandchildren were grown, that the island did not go on forever in every direction the way a man might once have supposed, from ground he had never left, that it must. Herders following grazing further out than their fathers had troubled to go came back with word of a shore to the north that no fire could be carried past, and of a wind out there that took the warmth from a body faster than any winter in the high valleys, and the word travelled, as word did in that country, from hearth to hearth until it had gone further than any single herder's own feet had carried it.

Luag was the one who went to see, some four hundred and twenty-five years after the night of the lightning, born to a household of the northern heath, near enough the changing country of birch and oak that he had grown up hearing the grandmothers' telling of it at every dark-of-the-year fire, and restless in a way that his own household found hard to account for, since nothing in his upbringing had marked him out for restlessness beyond any other young man of that country. He herded, as every young man of that country herded, but he herded further out each year than the year before, following the grazing past where his own kin were accustomed to follow it, until by his twenty-fifth year he had walked ground that no one of his household, so far as any of them could tell him, had ever walked before.

He set out for the horn in the spring of his twenty-sixth year, alone, with a coal carried in a clay pot the way every traveller of that country carried fire, and dried meat and a hide to sleep under, telling his household only that he meant to see how far the land truly went before it met the sea, and that he would be back before the grazing failed. It took him the better part of four days' walking, out past the last households his own kin had word of, out past the last stands of oak and hazel into country that thinned first to birch again and then to nothing taller than a man's knee, heather and low scrub and bare wet ground that sucked at his feet with every step, until on the evening of the fourth day he came up over a last low rise and saw, for the first time in his life, the open sea.

It was not the sea of the sheltered sounds that the herders of the north had told of, grey water with an island's shape somewhere against the far horizon to give the eye something to rest on. This was open water with nothing beyond it that Luag's eye could find, grey under a grey sky, running in long low swells that broke white on a shore of black rock with no beach to speak of, and a wind coming off it that found every gap in his hide cloak within the first few breaths of his standing there, colder than any wind he had felt in the high valleys even in the dead of winter.

He tried, that first night, to keep his fire as he had kept it every night of the journey out, banked in a shallow scrape with stones set round it for a wind-break. The wind came round the stones as though they were not there at all, and took the flame sideways along the ground faster than he could feed it, and by the third handful of dry heather he understood that the fire he had carried four days across the island to look at the sea was not going to live an hour on that ground, no matter how he built it or how he fed it. He let it go out rather than waste the last of his tinder chasing it, and spent that night in the lee of a rock with the hide pulled over his head and his body curled round the cold pot, colder than he had ever been in his life and too stubborn, or too curious, to turn back for it.

In the days he stayed there, walking the shore both ways as far as the light allowed, he found no hearth-stone, no scrap of charcoal, no sign anywhere on that ground that a fire had ever been kept there by anyone, hill-folk or otherwise, and no tree taller than his own knee standing anywhere he walked, though he went some distance along the shore in both directions to be sure of it. The ground was not poor ground exactly, for there was grass enough in the hollows out of the worst of the wind, and he saw the marks of deer on it and the droppings of hares, but it was ground that would not hold a household, he judged, walking it, no more than it would hold his own small fire, open on every side to a wind that never seemed, in all the days he stayed, to fully drop.

He stood a long while, on the last evening before he turned for home, on the highest rock he could find above the shore, looking west, for the wind that beat at that coast came off the water in that direction rather than from the north as the worst of the high-valley winters did, and it seemed to him, standing there with his hide cloak snapping about his shoulders, that whatever weather his own country knew, fair or foul, came first across that water from somewhere further out than his eye could reach. He had no word for what lay beyond it, and did not trouble himself hunting for one; that evening it was enough to stand at the edge of ground he could walk no further along without a boat under him, and know, in a way he had not quite known setting out, that the island his own people lived on had an edge to it, here as much as anywhere, and that this was where its edge lay bare to the weather with nothing between it and the open sea.

In the morning he turned for home, walking the four days back by the same track he had come, and relit his fire at the first hearth he reached, a herding household two days short of his own that gave him coal and a meal and asked, as everyone by then asked a traveller from a strange direction, what he had found out there. He told them plainly: no tree, no hearth, no shelter a household could keep a fire in, only rock and wind and grass in the hollows and, past it all, water going on further than a man could see. He told the same, in much the same words, at his own hearth when he reached it, and again at the gathering of the near households in the dark half of that year, and the telling went from there the way every telling of that country went, hearth to hearth, until most of the northern heath had heard, in one form or another, that the land ran out at last in the north-west, bare to a wind no fire could stand against, with nothing beyond it that any of them had ever seen or ever would.

Luag lived on at his own household's hearth for the better part of fifty years after, herding the nearer ground in his later life as his knees stopped wanting the long walks of his youth, and he was known to the end of his days, kindly rather than mockingly, as the man who had gone to the edge of the world to see what was there and come back with nothing to show for it but a burnt cloak and a telling. He died in his seventy-second year among his own grown children, an old man by any measure the high country kept, and the telling he had brought back outlived him easily, kept at hearth after hearth well after there was no one left who had heard it first from his own mouth: that the island had an edge in the north-west as sure as it had a middle, and that a man could walk to it and stand on it and feel, in the wind that never dropped there, how small a fire truly was against the whole breadth of the weather the sea sent in.
