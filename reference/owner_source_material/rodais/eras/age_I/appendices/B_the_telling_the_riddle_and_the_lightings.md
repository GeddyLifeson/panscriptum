# The Telling, the Riddle and the Lightings

Three things were said at the hearth of Tobar dhìreach on Oidhche an Dealanaich, and the whole faith of the high valleys in this age grew out of them: the Line of the fathers (Appendix A), Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich. To these the line added, one at a time over more than four thousand years, a line for each coal as it answered. This appendix tells how Aithris a' Chruthachaidh was kept, gives Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, sets out the lightings in order, and describes the other reverences of the hearths, which were kept by households that had never heard Aithris a' Chruthachaidh whole. It ends with what is not known of the holy things of Seann-Dhaoine.

## How the Telling was kept

Aithris a' Chruthachaidh came to the island in one night. Coimhdeach na Fine gave it whole to Diarmad in the oak grove at Doire ghlas, in the gale, sitting by his fire under a fallen trunk: the gods and their line, each god making a world of what was dearest to him; Caoran an Dia Òg, youngest son of an Dagda and Danu, who chose coal; the making of the stars and the sun and the moon out of its sparks and ash, and last of all the island, born out of the sea from drops of fire; An Dia-shluagh grown up out of the heather; Na Seachd Guail laid under the mountains, each colour locked with its own key; the Tuath Dè who came down to see the island, and what each of them loves; Coimhdeach na Fine itself, the smoke of the first coal given a mind and a charge; and the forgetting of the coal by an Dia-shluagh, who threw the coloured stones away as the mountain's droppings.

Diarmad walked home saying Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich over and over to keep it; Aithris a' Chruthachaidh he carried home in his head unspoken, and it took him most of a winter to lay it out at the hearth in the order Coimhdeach na Fine had given it. His wife Niamh said it after him at the first standing of the Line, though she did not yet believe it. His daughter Deirbhile learned it from him word for word and made each of her own children say it back to her a section at a time until they had it as clean as she did, and taught it so to any of the kin who would sit still for the whole. From her the line has it. It is said sitting, unlike the Line, and it is said by whoever of the household says it best. A trader who had heard it once told it back to Diarmad, some years after, with the sea and the sky changed places and the earth dropped out; the line took this as a warning, and never let Aithris a' Chruthachaidh be said at the hearth in any words but the kept ones.

Outside the line Aithris a' Chruthachaidh went out in pieces. Every hearth of the high valleys knew by the close of the age that the island was born of fire from a young god's hand, and that the colours in the coal were his gift, locked; fewer knew the names of the Tuath Dè in their order; hardly any outside Tobar dhìreach could have said the whole. At the gatherings at the column, from Seathan's time, the line's reciter said Aithris a' Chruthachaidh to crowds of some hundreds, and many heard it whole there for the first time.

## The riddle

Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich was given last, after Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, when Diarmad asked whether an Dia Òg had been angry at the forgetting. Coimhdeach na Fine answered that he was not, and gave Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, and would give nothing more:

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

The line said it at every recital after Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, once in the old words and once in plain speech, and left it unanswered. For eighteen fathers after Diarmad they tried the coals against the three things Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich names as they understood them: held to the sky, pressed into the earth, dropped into sea water carried home in skins; in the hearth, in the rain, all seven together. Nothing answered. What no one of the line understood until Aodh was that each coal wanted its own element in the element's own body, the whole sea and not a skin of it, and that each lighting would be a step of that kind: the coal taken to what it holds. Even then Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich's last line stayed shut. Lìobhan, Ailean's wife, is remembered as the one who first said at the hearth what the rainbow wanted: the other six, burning, together.

## The lightings

Each lighting added a line to the recital. A coal once lit burned until it burned out, and the line relit from the bag, which held pieces of every colour broken from the vein before it was covered and, from Aodh's time, fresh pieces taken from where the vein shows in a stream bank above the well, a handful a life.

Ailean relit the six over the better part of his twenty-fourth year, each by its own way: the black coal in the earth by Leac na Fèithe; the gold where Aodh let it burn, wading after it and catching it still burning between his cupped hands; the snowflake on the height; the red-brown with his own blood; the silver in the Abhainn ghorm; Gual teine on his own doorstone. Six fires stood in six clay pots under one roof at Tobar dhìreach through the last weeks of summer, and on the twenty-first day of an t-Iuchar they stood in a ring under the oaks.

## The bag and the pot

Two things were kept at Tobar dhìreach for the whole line. Poit an Teine, is a squat clay pot with a turf lid, said to hold the mason's first fire; it has been remade more than once as clay pots crack, and each time the fire was moved from the old pot to the new before the old was broken up, so that the fire in it has never been let go dark. It stood by the hearth and was kept by the women of the line (Appendix A). The bag is a pouch of greased leather on a peg by the door, holding the pieces of an gual dathach: pieces of every colour broken from the vein before Leac na Fèithe was laid, and fresh pieces taken in each life from where the vein shows in a stream bank above the well. Children of the line played with the pieces when they were small enough not to know better, and learned the colours so. The bag passed from father to son with the Line, and the father who held it said the recital.

A coal that answered was left to burn where it had answered, as the gold burned on the sea and the silver under the river, or kept in a pot of its own, as Brian kept the black coal, until it burned out. The line did not grieve a coal that went out; the bag held more of the same colour, and the way of lighting it was now known. What the line guarded was the knowing, and the knowing was in the recital.

## The reverences of the hearths

These were kept by the hill-folk at large, most of them by households that knew Aithris a' Chruthachaidh only in pieces.

**Leac na Fèithe.** When Teine na Fèithe burned out, Muirne of the line would not have it dug further. A flat stone was laid over the broken vein, no water was drawn beside it, and the well was cut again a stone's throw off. No house stands by Leac na Fèithe, no bucket is set near it, and the turf at its edge is kept whole against the rain. Children of the line are brought to lay a bare palm on it in the dark half of the year.

**The fire given.** Every hearth of the hill-folk was lit from Teine a' Chlachair, by one borrowing or another, carried in a clay pot banked with ash. A fire was never sold. A household whose hearth died begged a coal from the nearest living one, and was never refused.

**The dead in the ground.** The hill-folk lay their dead in the ground, on the side, knees drawn up, facing the central mountains, with an uncut stone set on end at the head and no mark on it. At Tuam Cnoc dhìreach on the south coast the graves came to be laid in rows, heads toward the mountains; the first rows are straight, the later ones bend round older graves no one will disturb. At Achadh dhìreach, in one grave in some thirty, coal of the vein is rubbed into the wool the dead are wrapped in. At Tuam Cnoc dhìreach a child was buried with a sliver of an gual dathach closed in one hand, and there were words between two households over it.

**The covered hearth.** In the Doire uaine hills the fire is kept in pits under slabs, with a blind bend in the shaft, against the thing that walks without breathing (Appendix E). The line at Tobar dhìreach would not cover its own fire, holding Teine a' Chlachair owed better than to hide.

**The uncut woods.** Two woods were let alone: Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, from whose edge no household cut a branch, and Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag in the west, never walked from end to end, where three paths stop at a line of set stones.

**The stones and their sayings.** Three standing stones were found in the west and south-west within some two hundred years, each bearing words in the tongue of the hill-folk: Carragh Cnoc bheag, *Bha teine sa chloich*, "there was fire in the stone," cut low beneath a line of the unread script; Colbh Cnoc chiar, *Cuimhnich an teine*, "remember the fire"; and Calbh Cnoc ghorm, *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann*, "we did not come; we were here." None of the hill-folk could read a letter. How the words came to be known at every hearth is a riddle the tellings leave as they found it; known they were, and said the same way at every hearth that told of them. At Colbh Cnoc chiar a ring of ground was trodden hard and paved with fire-cracked stones, and fires the size of hearths were kept there. At the column the saying was said standing, and the gatherings grew.

**Oidhche an Dealanaich.** The dark of the year, when the vein first took fire, was kept at every hearth that had heard of it with a fire built high and the fathers' names said by whoever knew them. At Tobar dhìreach it was the night of the recital. By the close of the age, in the hill country about the column, it was the night of the great telling.

## What is not known of the Old Ones' holy things

Of the faith of Seann-Dhaoine only what they built remains, and what they built does not explain itself. They rowed their dead to Eilean dhearg and laid them in stone boxes facing the mainland, and no box was left open through a night. They raised a stepped platform of fitted stone in the eastern hills with an altar block on its top step, set square to the central mountains, with its back to the sea; the top stone of the altar was prised off and carried away, and the platform left. Two shafts were cut straight down through the rock at the two Toll-dubhs, far apart, smooth-walled, with no spoil beside them; stones thrown into either are not heard to land. A script was cut on the cliff above their landing at Tarr; the same script stands on the rocks of the Doire uaine hills and on the upper face of Carragh Cnoc bheag, and who cut those is not known. No one of the hill-folk could read a sign of it. They notched their net-weights with three strokes. Whether any of these things was holy to them, and to what, no telling of the hill-folk says, and Seann-Dhaoine' own tellings, if they had any, are not known.
