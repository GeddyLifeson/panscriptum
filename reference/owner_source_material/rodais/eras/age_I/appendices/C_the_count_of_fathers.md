# Cunntas nan Athraichean

No one in this age counted years. There was no tally, no notched stick kept for the purpose, no stone cut with marks, and no number of years from any beginning was said at any hearth. The dates in the annals of this volume, from 20 am Faoilleach VE 1 at the breaking of the vein to 21 an t-Iuchar VE 5,324 in the grove, are the dates of the annals of the island, set against its master record; the people of the age would not have understood them. This appendix tells how they knew time instead: by Cunntas nan Athraichean, by the turning of the year, by Oidhche an Dealanaich, and by the beasts and birds that keep their own seasons.

## The count of fathers

The only long measure the age had was Cunntas nan Athraichean, and only the mason's line kept it whole. A father, in this count, is a span as much as a man: the time from a father's taking the bag to his son's. Asked how long ago a thing was, an elder of the line answered in fathers, *so many fathers back*, and a listener who wanted it closer could ask *in whose father's time*, and be told a name if the name was one the line gave to strangers, or a count if it was not. The line itself reckoned a father at about the time it takes a boy to grow and have a son of his own grown, and the count held true enough that the named fathers, set against the years of the annals, stand spaced as the recital says (Appendix A).

Other households counted fathers too, but shorter. Most hearths of the hill-folk could give four or five fathers back, and a few old women could give eight or nine; past that a household said *long ago*, or *in the mason's time*, or *before the fire*, which meant the same as *never*. Diarmad, gathering the names for the Line, found that old women who had never troubled over his question had kept names back without being asked, and were glad to give them to someone willing to carry the weight of remembering them. Among Seann-Dhaoine no count of any kind is known.

Cunntas nan Athraichean made spans easy to say and dates hard. The line could say that Aodh gave the seven to the sea eighteen fathers after Diarmad went to the grove, but not how many winters Aodh had seen, except by counting them in his own lifetime at his own hearth. Ages were known within a household by the winters a person had lived through, counted on the fingers and remembered by the hard ones: *she was born the winter the seed rotted*; *he was a boy of ten winters when the whale came ashore*. The ages given in this volume for the people of the line are the annals' ages; the households themselves would have said them in winters, and would not always have agreed.

## The turning of the year

The year was known by its halves and its marks. The dark half ran from the first frosts to the lambing, and the light half from the lambing to the frosts. Within them each household knew the year by its own work: the peat cut and stacked, the barley sown in the small fields and cut, the flocks taken up to the shielings and brought down, the seals hauling out on the skerries in the autumn gales, the eels running in the rivers, the kilns smoking on the south-east coast through the summer. A woman of the shore might say a thing happened *at the second hauling-out* and be understood along the whole of that coast and nowhere else.

The dates of the annals name the months, from am Faoilleach in the dead of winter to an Dùbhlachd at the year's close. The hill-folk of the age named stretches of the year and not fixed counts of days, and a stretch began when the weather that named it began. The shortest day was marked at the temple platform in the eastern hills by Seann-Dhaoine, who climbed the steps in the dark and stood about the great stone the whole hour the light took to cross it; the hill-folk marked it, if at all, by a fire.

## The night of the lightning

The one night of the year that the whole of the high valleys kept on the same night was Oidhche an Dealanaich, in the dark of the winter, when the vein first took fire. At Tobar dhìreach the Line was said standing that night, Aithris a' Chruthachaidh after it and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich after that, and any new line for a coal was added. At other hearths the fire was built high and the fathers' names said by whoever knew them. As the age went on the night gathered other things to it: the gathering at Àth gharbh where the valleys first named themselves Clann nan Dè; the ring of fires about Colbh Cnoc chiar; and, from Seathan's time, the great telling at the column, to which some hundreds walked from every quarter of the hill country. By the close of the age the hill country about the column reckoned its year by the gatherings there, one in the dark of the year and one at midsummer, and a thing done between them was said to be done *between the gatherings*.

## The beasts that keep their seasons

Where Cunntas nan Athraichean was too long and the year too short, the hill-folk measured by the beasts.

**The salmon** came up the rivers in their great runs at much the same season each year, and the weirs on the Abhainn ìseal were renewed in the same holes for the runs, so that the households along that river knew the late summer by the salmon before they knew it by anything else.

**The geese** of Eilean ghorm came in with the first storm of the winter and left with the last, and households that watched them close enough learned to read the turn of the winter in their leaving, which came a day or two ahead of the year's last storm. The geese of the southern coast, going over in their numbers in the autumn, told the southern households that the season had turned; in the year the eleven went up from Teallaichean a' Chladaich, the geese were late, and the shore read the season as a slow one.

**The seals** hauled out to pup on the skerries off Eilean fhada in the autumn gales, and on Eilean mhin in their own season, and the hunters went out when the seals were there.

**The great cats** of the mountains crossed the high ground by fixed ways at fixed seasons, and from Beithir's time the households learned the crossing season and kept off the passes while it lasted. A crossing mark was cut on each shieling's gatepost when the season came, and scraped bare when it had been kept, so that a latecomer knew the season had been kept there before. It was the nearest thing to a tally the hill-folk made, and it counted one thing only: that the cats had passed.

**The white stag** of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar was seen at long intervals, and the hunters who saw it did not draw. A household of the east might say a thing happened *the year the stag was seen*, and be understood.

## The span of a life

No one in this age lived long. A child who lived past its fifth winter might hope for fifty or sixty more; a man or woman of seventy was old, and one of eighty was a wonder spoken of along a whole valley. The named fathers of the line died between sixty and their early seventies, most at their own hearths; Suibhne died of a fever at forty-six. Many children died before they could walk, and fevers took the young in bad seasons along whole shores. A household reckoned its strength by how many of its children had lived to be grown, and a woman who had lost children young and trusted, at last, that one would live, sewed it a coat cut to its own growing.

Because lives were short and Cunntas nan Athraichean long, the tellings of this age are a relay: no one lived to see more than one or two of the lightings, and the line's knowledge of the coals passed from hand to hand across two hundred fathers with no one to say from memory how a thing had first been done. What a keeper knew of the lightings before his own was what the recital gave him, and what the recital gave him was what the fathers before him had chosen to keep.

## Spans in this volume

Because the age had no count, spans in the Books of this volume are given as the tellers of the age would have given them: in fathers, in lifetimes, in winters, or in the seasons of the beasts. Where the annals give a year, it is the annals' own year. The two do not quarrel: a father in the recital is about twenty-five of the annals' years, a lifetime some sixty to seventy, and the line's own boast that the bag had waited *longer than any stone in the south* means a little over four thousand of them.
