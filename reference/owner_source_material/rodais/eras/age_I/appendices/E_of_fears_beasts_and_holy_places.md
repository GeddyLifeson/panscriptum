# Of Fears, Beasts and Holy Places

The hill-folk of this age feared few things by name, and named them late. For most of the age a fear was a place a household did not go, a thing a child was told not to do, or a custom kept without a reason given; only toward its close did the fears gather names, and even then the names were few. This appendix gathers the fears, the beasts that the tellings keep, and the places that were let alone or kept holy, with what the tellings say of each and what they do not.

## The fears

**Uilebheist na Mara.** Old sailors of the north-western coast told of a vast creature dwelling in the water off the north-western horn. Four men of that coast saw it rise once, and the youngest of them kept the telling word for word into his old age, adding nothing and taking nothing away; the coast counted this for more than any argument of the doubters. No wreck was ever brought up to prove the tellers wrong. For a long while Uilebheist na Mara was feared only in general, as a thing out there; then, in one calm season, seven boats of Ros ruadh went out on still water and did not come home, and the widows of Ros ruadh named it. It was the first loss laid at its door by name. After that the hunters of the north-west left the open water for the lee of the headlands and the strait, where the seal bones grew in the middens of Bothan a' Chaolais, and Criomthann kept a fire on the north-western headland, from a peat bank cut for it alone, to warn boats off the water where Uilebheist na Mara was seen. The fire was never lit to bring boats in. Its ash lies a man's height deep. What Uilebheist na Mara is, the tellings do not say; the households of Seann-Dhaoine' tower on the north-western horn kept their boats out of the same water, and no one knows why.

**Marbh-bheò.** In the Doire uaine hills, where the rock faces are cut with lines of the unread script more thickly than anywhere else on the island, the tellings came to agree that the writing told of something that walked without breathing and fell upon any who lit a fire in its sight. How the hills came to know what the writing said, when none of them could read a sign, is a riddle; known it was, and told the same way at every hearth. Some lines were cut over and defaced by another hand with the same kind of tool. Two or three people of the hills dreamed, without having spoken to one another, of something walking the ridges without breath; two herders saw a walker on a ridge and lived. The hills' answer was the covered hearth: Laoghaire dug the first pit-hearth, the fire under a slab at the foot of a slanting shaft bent near its foot so that no glow could climb it straight, and within a few lifetimes no open fire showed in those hills. Eimhir, who found the first cut stone, kept her hearth open to her death. Whether Marbh-bheò is, or was, the tellings do not say. The hills say only that a fire built where the ridge can see it is a fire built carelessly.

**The camp of the nine.** At a hill camp nine people were found dead inside one hut, lying where they fell beside a hearth of fresh ash, with no wound on any of them. The hut was never opened again and none was buried from it; the camp was left for good. The hills did not agree on a cause. Some laid it at Marbh-bheò's door, some at the fire itself, burning in a shut hut; the tellings keep both and choose neither.

**The line of stones.** Children of the country about Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag were told early and plainly: go as far as the stones and no further, and whatever crosses the line ahead of you, a ball or a lamb or a hound, let it go. A girl named Muireall went past the stones after a young deer and was never found. After her no household let its children watch for deer at that stretch without a grown hand near enough to close on a small arm.

**The grey on the sea.** On the northern and eastern sea lies a grey bank that never lifts. Scàire of the north shore saw it first from the skerries and would not steer toward it. Bran, a steersman of the north-west, sailed west with the grey bank on his right hand in search of an isle of the blessed, and did not come back; one of his crew came home, and died within days. The hill-folk name the western lands of the dead from Aithris a' Chruthachaidh: Tìr nan Òg, Magh Meall, Taigh Dhuinn. What lies in the grey, no telling says.

## The beasts

**The great cats.** The great cats of the mountains were known from the beginning of the age as a danger to flocks and lone walkers. From the time of Beithir, a herder of the high shoulders who first read their road in the snow, the tellings say that the cats keep their roads, crossing the mountains by fixed ways at fixed seasons. The households learned the crossing season and kept off the passes while it lasted, leaving the shielings of Àirighean a' Bhealaich stripped and shuttered and cutting a mark on each gatepost that was scraped when the season had been kept. Eleven of Teallaichean a' Chladaich went up into the southern hills in the crossing season, reading the geese as late and the season as slow, and did not come home; their bones were found years after along the line the cats use, and were buried where they lay. Why the cats keep their roads, no one has learned.

**The white stag.** Three hunters of the east, Sadhbh and her cousin Ruadhan and one other, saw a white stag at the edge of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, and none of them drew. It was seen again at long intervals, and the hunters of the gully began letting a beast go if a pale one was among the drove, sooner than risk the wrong one. Aithris a' Chruthachaidh says that Flidais keeps the wild things and that the white stag of the wood is hers; no one of this age tied the stag at the wood's edge to that line of Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, and in this age the stag has no god.

**The red deer.** The deer of the eastern woods were driven each autumn into Gleann nan Damh and killed at the gully's throat; the antlers of the greatest stags were stacked apart. The deer of Eilean dhubh, cut off when the sea drowned the neck, grew smaller in the lifetimes that followed.

**The seals.** The grey seals haul out to pup on the skerries off Eilean fhada in the autumn gales, and on Eilean mhin in their season. They were the first quarry for which anyone on the island crossed open water of set purpose.

**The whales.** A whale stranded below the southern Dunn was cut and shared along the whole shore, its oil burned through two winters and its ribs raised for house frames. Word of usable ribs travelled faster, by the end, than word of strandings once had.

**The geese.** The grey geese that winter on Eilean ghorm came in with the first storm and left with the last, and none hunted them there. A young man who sailed for Eilean ghorm to hunt them is told three ways at three hearths, and remembered as a warning.

**The dogs.** At Cnoc fhionn a boy buried his dog beside his family's dead with a shell at its head, and within a few years two more households of that hill buried their dogs so. No beast had had a grave on the island before.

**The crows and the wrens.** The crows of the island are as black as the black coal, and Aithris a' Chruthachaidh gives them to the Morrìgan; the hill-folk let them be. A wren came along the fallen trunk to within a hand of gual bogha-froise on Diarmad's second morning in the grove, looked at it and at him, and went about its business; the line tells it at the recital as a sign of nothing, and keeps it all the same.

## The holy places

**Leac na Fèithe over the vein** at Tobar dhìreach, with no house beside it and no water drawn near it (Appendix B).

**The grove at Doire ghlas**, three days north of Tobar dhìreach, where the oaks came in on ground that had been open heath when the vein was broken. Coimhdeach na Fine came to Diarmad there, Cormac's blood lit the red-brown there, Suibhne sat a night there with the rainbow, and Ailean brought the six there at the close of the age. The line did not build there.

**Binnean a' Chlachair**, the highest ground of the island, where Fearghas let the six fall and the snowflake burned; herders passing the summit each lay a stone on the cairn Mathan began.

**Coille Naomh Muileann chiar**, from which no household cut a branch; the households at its edge carried their fuel from beyond the far shoulder of open ground, two journeys where one would serve.

**Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag**, the second wood, older-seeming than the first, never settled, never felled, never walked from end to end; three paths stop short of it at a low line of set stones.

**Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh**, Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh south of the high valleys, where stone basins were set into the springs and the sick of the island were first brought for healing. The keeper of the basins judged a wound by its colour and its smell, and in a dry summer heated stones were laid in the third basin to bring back its warmth. The dead buried near the springs have old breaks that knitted clean.

**The three stones** and their sayings: Carragh Cnoc bheag, Colbh Cnoc chiar with its ring of fire-cracked stones, and Calbh Cnoc ghorm with the gathering-ground of Achadh a' Chalbh about it (Appendix B).

**Tuam Cnoc dhìreach**, the burial-ground on the south coast that none remembers hallowing, where the rows bend round older graves.

**Seann-Dhaoine' places.** The platform of Làrach an Teampaill in the eastern hills; the two shafts of the Toll-dubhs, into which a youth of the south-east was once let down on a plaited rope and came up silent; the boxes on Eilean dhearg. The hill-folk of this age did not go to them. What they were to their makers is not known.
