# Sloinneadh a' Chlachair

The only roll this age kept was a roll of names said aloud. Sloinneadh a' Chlachair is the pedigree of the mason's line from An Clachair himself down to the father then living, said standing at the hearth of Tobar dhìreach on Oidhche an Dealanaich, the dark of the year when the vein first took fire. Diarmad fixed its form in the years after he came home from the grove, gathering the names from old women who had kept four and five fathers back without being asked, and from then on it was said the same way every winter, with nothing added but the name of each father as he died and the line for each coal as it answered. This appendix gives what the line let strangers hear, the named fathers with their deeds, and the women who kept Poit an Teine. The years beside each name are the years of the annals of this volume; no one living in the age counted them so, and Appendix C tells how they did count.

## The rule of the recital

The recital is said standing, by the father who holds the bag, or by one he chooses when he cannot stand. It opens with the mason and closes with the father living. Between the two it gives every father's name, but to strangers the line gives only the named fathers, each with his deed, and says of the rest only how many stood between: *and so many fathers whose names we keep*. The counts between are said as numbers and the names are kept. After the Line comes the telling of the coals, one line for each lighting as it came, and after that Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, said once in the old words and once in plain speech, and left unanswered. In Gormal's day the recital was softened, at her daughter Sìomha's asking, so that a small child might leave the fire when the fathers' names were done instead of being held through Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich; the full recital was kept for the grown. In the lifetimes of the gatherings at the column, the recital came to be said there too, slowly, with a pause after each named father long enough for the name to be passed back through the crowd.

## The short form

This is the Line as it was given to strangers at the close of the age, in plain speech:

> Neachdan the mason, who broke the vein and carried the fire home; Conall his son, who kept the vein burning.
> Thirty-four fathers whose names we keep. Diarmad, who asked, and heard Aithris a' Chruthachaidh in the grove. Eighteen fathers
> whose names we keep. Aodh, who gave the seven to the sea, and the gold burned. Thirty-one fathers whose names we
> keep. Fearghas, who let the six fall from the height, and the snowflake burned in the air. Twenty-eight fathers
> whose names we keep. Brian, who gave the five to the ground, and the black coal was warm. Thirty fathers whose
> names we keep. Cormac of the Misstrike, whose blood lit the red-brown. Twenty-one fathers whose names we keep.
> Eòghann, who tasted the river, and the silver burned under the water. Fifteen fathers whose names we keep.
> Colla, in whose days we were called Clann na Ceiste. Ten fathers whose names we keep.
> Seathan, who snapped his fingers, and the fire came. Fifteen fathers whose names we keep. Suibhne, who carried
> the rainbow to the grove. Ailean his son, who lives.

## The named fathers

**Neachdan, An Clachair** (died VE 38). A mason of the high valleys north of the central mountains, splitting rock for a well in the dead of a winter when the old spring had failed. His wedge went into a seam he had not seen and the rock broke open on a vein of hard coal that showed every colour in the break. The lightning struck the broken vein that night and it took fire, and he carried a burning piece home in his hands wrapped in a sodden hide. His wife Sorcha was the first to be burned by a hearth and the first to feed one heather. He died in the thirty-eighth winter after the lightning, an old man for that age, and is the first of the dead buried within sight of the vein.

**Conall mac Neachdain** (died VE 71). The mason's son, eleven when he ran barefoot through the rain with his mother's water-pot to bring fire home from the vein to the dead hearth. He kept the winter watch at the burning vein from two flat stones above it for most of his life, and gave fire to any hearth that sent for it. His sister Ailbhe carried it to the hearths further off. He is the last father named before the long count of kept names.

**Diarmad** (VE 862–931), the thirty-sixth father. A boy who would not stop asking why the pieces in the bag held colours, and a young man who went north three days alone to the oak grove at Doire ghlas with gual bogha-froise and asked Coimhdeach na Fine, which came to his fire out of the smoke of the gale. He came home with Aithris a' Chruthachaidh and Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich, and fixed the recital: the Line said standing, then Aithris a' Chruthachaidh, then Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich. He died in his seventieth year at Tobar dhìreach. His wife Niamh said Aithris a' Chruthachaidh at his hearth before she believed it; his daughter Deirbhile kept it word for word and taught it.

**Aodh** (VE 1,344–1,412), the fifty-fourth father. At Muirgheal's bidding he carried all seven coals down to the northern shore, slept a night above the tideline, and threw them into the sea one by one. Gual òir took fire on the water and burned gold until the sea put it under, and a new line was added to the recital for the first time since the mason. He brought the other six home and tried them against everything in reach for the rest of his life. In his last years he first took fresh pieces of the vein from where it shows in a stream bank above the well.

**Fearghas** (VE 2,138–2,199), the eighty-sixth father. With his brother Mathan he walked the ridges south for two summers until there was no taller hill behind the one they stood on, and on the summit of Binnean a' Chlachair he let the six unlit coals fall one by one. Gual sneachda took fire as it fell and burned white; they left it burning where it lay. Mathan set the first stone of the summit cairn. Fearghas died at sixty-one.

**Brian** (VE 2,858–2,921), the hundred and fifteenth father. The grave-digger of the high valleys, who dug through frozen ground for other households' dead without cursing it. The old widow Sireadh, who had kept the bag sixty years, gave him the five dark coals, and he buried them deep beside Leac na Fèithe with the words said over every grave in the valleys. When he dug them up the next day the black coal was warm in his hand and smouldered as the air reached it. He kept it burning at his own hearth and taught the grave-digger's trade to younger men, and died at sixty-three. The black coal burned at his burial. His wife was Caoimhe.

**Cormac of the Misstrike** (VE 3,640–3,712), the hundred and forty-sixth father. The keeping fell to him at fifteen when both his elder brothers died in one season. He carried the four unlit coals to the grove at Doire ghlas to break them there, reasoning that the grove might hold some leaving that would answer; on the last morning of a month under the oaks the maul turned on the red-brown coal and opened the back of his hand, and where his blood fell on it the coal took fire. He came home with his hand bound and held it up at the next recital. His wife was Aoibheann. He died at seventy-two at his own hearth.

**Eòghann** (VE 4,196–4,262), the hundred and sixty-eighth father. A younger son who came to the keeping at twenty when his sister and father died in one season. He carried gual airgid to river after river, and one evening, kneeling to drink at the crossing below Tobar dhìreach, said that the water tasted different from the sea water, and threw in the three unlit coals. The silver caught under the current and burned there, a low pale light any traveller could kneel and see; the two dark coals were found again on a gravel bar downstream. His wife was Dearbhail, of the lake-shore line. He died at sixty-six.

**Colla** (VE 4,590–4,655), the hundred and eighty-fourth father. He held the bag when the valleys, half in jest, began to call the line by a long name for its long questioning of the two dark coals, and he gave the long name back to a traveller without the laughter, and the short form, Clann na Ceiste, with it. It stuck. He died at sixty-five, and the old name Clann a' Chlachair was heard for the last time as the line's own name within a generation of him.

**Seathan** (VE 4,862–4,934), the hundred and ninety-fifth father. He took the bag at twenty and kept it more than fifty years, and handled gual teine every day of them, and put it at last into the pot itself. On a doorstone at the age of forty-three he snapped his fingers when a thought struck him, and the coal-dust on his hand took fire; he beat it out against his coat and was not burned, and laughed until Caitir his wife came to the door with her hands floury from the baking. With her he tried the rainbow at the sea and the river. The bag passed to his eldest son. He died at seventy-two.

**Suibhne** (VE 5,272–5,318), the two hundred and eleventh father. He learned the Line standing as a boy and tried the rainbow by the sea, the height, the river and the earth, but would not try it by blood. In his forty-fourth year he walked the three days north to Doire ghlas with it and sat a night by his own small fire in the grove; nothing answered. He said at the next recital that he doubted the rainbow would answer him, and died of a fever two years later, at forty-six.

**Ailean mac Shuibhne** (born VE 5,300), the two hundred and twelfth father, living at the close of the age. A big young man, slow of speech and broad of hand, who sat by Poit an Teine for whole mornings as a child. He married Lìobhan at seventeen. At twenty-three he told the recital he meant to light the six again, each by its own way, and over the better part of a year he did it: the black coal in the earth, the gold out of the sea where Aodh let it burn, the snowflake on the height, the red-brown with his own blood, the silver in the Abhainn ghorm, gual teine on his own doorstone. In the last week of an t-Iuchar, at twenty-four, he set out north with six fires in six clay pots slung on a pole, and on the evening of the twenty-first day, in the grove at Doire ghlas, Coimhdeach na Fine came and lit the rainbow, and all seven burned together. Lìobhan and their daughter Gormshuil, then six, were at Tobar dhìreach.

## The women of the pot

Poit an Teine, the clay pot said to hold the mason's first fire, was never in the keeping of the father who held the bag. It passed among the women of the line, most often from a woman to her brother's daughter or her sister's, to whoever of the household had the steadiest hands with a live coal and the patience to rise in the night. The recital does not name them; the tellings of the hearth name some.

- **Ailbhe nic Neachdain** (VE 3–82), the mason's daughter, first child born by a hearth, who walked the fire in its pot to more hearths than anyone counted.
- **Muirne** (VE 112–181), who kept the watch of the vein when it burned low, would not let it be dug further, and bade them lay Leac na Fèithe.
- **Fionnghuala** (VE 838–897), Diarmad's mother, who took the pot from the old aunt the household called only the aunt.
- **Deirbhile nic Dhiarmaid** (VE 894–968), who kept Aithris a' Chruthachaidh word for word and made every child of the line say it back to her a section at a time.
- **Muirgheal** (VE 1,290–1,381), who bade Aodh take all seven to the sea and wept at the telling of the gold.
- **Gormal** (VE 1,930–2,004), who kept the pot and the recital in the lean years when a branch of the line went away from the vein, and added her own words at her last recital.
- **Sìomha** (VE 1,958–2,030), Gormal's daughter, who softened the recital for the children.
- **Sireadh** (VE 2,825–2,895), who kept the bag sixty years as a widow and gave Brian the five dark coals.
- **Mòrnait** (VE 4,785–4,850), of Clann na Ceiste though not of the narrow line, who kept fire at the ring about Colbh Cnoc chiar.
- **Caitir** (VE 4,868–4,940), Seathan's wife, the only keeper's wife remembered to have gone with the rainbow to the sea and not stayed by the pot.
- **Lìobhan** (born VE 5,302), Ailean's wife, who named what the rainbow wanted, and kept the house fire while he went north.
