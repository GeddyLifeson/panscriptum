# The Peoples of the Shore and the Hills

Two peoples lived on the island in this age, and no telling of either says that they met. The hill-folk, who called themselves Clann nan Dè from the time of the gathering at Àth gharbh, had fire from the mason's vein, buried their dead in the ground, and cut no script. Seann-Dhaoine kept every coast, rowed their dead to the islands, built in stone, and cut a script of their own that the hill-folk could not read. Things passed between them, a chip of an gual dathach on a midden at Bral, a second hand on a standing stone, but how is not told. This appendix sets out what is known of each: where they lived, how many they were, and what they made.

## Numbers

At the breaking of the vein there were perhaps a few thousand souls on the whole island. By the time the walls of the heights were raised there were some ten thousand, and at the close of the age some eighteen thousand, of whom Seann-Dhaoine were perhaps a third. No one of the age counted them. The largest place on the island was Skell on the Abhainn naomh, with some three hundred souls at its height; Tobar dhìreach held some ninety at the close; most hearth-kindreds of the hill-folk were of twenty to eighty souls, and most landings of Seann-Dhaoine of thirty to a hundred. The three rings of Làrach an Dùin were built with room for four hundred people and their beasts, and are the only place of the age made for more than a landing's worth. A crowd at the column on the night of a great telling was some hundreds.

## The Old Ones

Seann-Dhaoine left their first sure trace at the eastern Dunn, in the great mound of shell, seal bone and burnt weed that the households there laid down over many lifetimes. From there, by the evidence of what they built, they came to hold every coast of the island, and at their widest reach they had landings at every good harbour from the north-western horn to the south-eastern cape. The hill-folk of the close of the age did not say *Seann* before the landings' names, and called each by its bare root, the only words of Seann-Dhaoine' speech that have come down.

**Their works.** Seann-Dhaoine built in stone without mortar, stone set against stone, and their building held: the mole at Skell stands with its foot under water at the lowest tides; the steps at Bral are worn hollow; the round houses at the eastern Vell stand in their rings. They made the first buildings on the island for a trade, the fish-kilns at Morn; the first made road, the shell causeway at Warr; the first quay, the mole; the first ramps for hulls greater than hide boats, at Brenn. They sank their nets with notched stones, the eldest mark on the island made again and again of purpose, and they cut a script on the cliff at Tarr.

**The heights.** In the middle lifetimes of the age some of Seann-Dhaoine went up from the shore. They raised the walled outpost of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire on a spur above the southern lowland, its stones dressed on the outer face only and its single gate looking inland; the three rings of Làrach an Dùin on a height some way from it, with a cistern cut into the bedrock; a kerbed way paved with flat slabs toward both; the stepped platform of Làrach an Teampaill in the eastern hills, its altar set square to the central mountains; and the two shafts of the Toll-dubhs, south-east and west, cut straight down and smoothed. Whether the builders of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire and of the rings were the same people is not known.

**The going down.** Then the gate of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire was walled up with rubble from within, and Làrach an Dùin-fhaire left; the cistern of the rings was choked with the bones of cattle thrown in whole, and the rings left; the top stone of the altar was prised off and carried away, and the temple left. The households of the heights came down to the shore, to Skell and Morn and the southern Cwen and the northern Skell, and after that Seann-Dhaoine made no new work anywhere out of sight of salt water. Their last lifetimes in this age were lifetimes of the shore: the middens of the south grew as fast as Dunn's once had, landings once counted by the dozen were counted by the score, and a lane of flat landing-stones a day's rowing apart ran along the south-east coast from the river mouth below Dunn round the great cape. Who walled the gate, who fouled the cistern, who took the altar stone, and why, is not known.

**Their dead.** Seann-Dhaoine of the eastern Vell rowed their dead to Eilean dhearg and laid them in stone boxes facing the mainland; forty boxes are counted there. No grave of theirs of this age is found on the main island.

## The hill-folk

The hill-folk were the people of Teine a' Chlachair: every hearth of theirs was lit from the vein, by one borrowing or another. They lived in households of twenty to eighty, herding sheep and cattle, hunting deer, growing barley in small fields, and gathering; they built in turf and timber and laid their hearths on stone. They buried their dead in the ground facing the central mountains. They cut no script and read none. They had no king, no chief over more than a household, no war and no walled place; a quarrel between households was settled by the elders of both, or not at all.

**The high valleys**, about the crack on the north side of the central mountains, were the heart of them: Tobar dhìreach, the black well and the young well, Achadh dhìreach, Àth gharbh and the hearths between. Here the Line was said and Aithris a' Chruthachaidh kept, here the valleys first named themselves Clann nan Dè, and from here the fire went out.

**The Doire uaine hills**, south of the high valleys, were herding country, full of rock faces cut with lines of the unread script. After the hollow thing was first told of there, the households of those hills put their fires under the ground (Appendix E), and kept them there to the close of the age. Na Teallaichean Falaichte is their largest hamlet.

**The east** was deer country. The households of the eastern woods drove the red deer into Gleann nan Damh each autumn and stacked the antlers of the greatest stags apart; they let Coille Naomh Muileann chiar alone, and there the white stag was first seen. At Cnoc fhionn they first buried their dogs beside their people.

**The south-east valleys**, the slope of Bruthach nan Teaghlach above the river running down toward the kilns, were settled by herders from the high valleys in the lifetimes of the heights; some of their sick were carried to Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh.

**The south-west hill country**, about the column, was the country of the three stones and, from the finding of Calbh Cnoc ghorm, of the gatherings at Achadh a' Chalbh.

**The northern shore** was seal country. Its households hunted seal on the skerries off Eilean fhada from Bothan an Fhoghair in the autumn gales, fished from skin boats, and watched the grey on the northern and eastern sea that never lifts. The north-western coast, from Ros ruadh to the strait, was Uilebheist na Mara's country after the seven boats were lost, and its hunters moved into the lee of the headlands and the strait at Bothan a' Chaolais. A warning fire was kept on the north-western headland.

**The southern shore** was the country of the first boats: Oisean crossed to Eilean mhin from it, and the seal-camp of Bothan nan Ròn was kept there every season after. Teallaichean a' Chladaich, the households from which the eleven went up into the hills, lay on this coast, and the burial-ground of Tuam Cnoc dhìreach grew above it.

**The lake shore** about Loch chrom held the lake dwellings of Crannagan Loch chrom, whose builders are not known, and the hearths of the hill-folk on Creag Ruadh above the eastern shore. Eòghann's wife Dearbhail was of the lake-shore line.

## How the households lived

No one on the island in this age worked metal. The hill-folk and Seann-Dhaoine alike cut with flint and chert, split with wedges of antler and hard stone, and dressed stone by pecking it with harder stone; a maul was a rounded cobble lashed to a haft of ash. A household's wealth was its beasts, its store of food against the dark half of the year, its boats if it had any, and the skill in its members' hands.

**Houses.** The hill-folk built round and oval houses of turf on a footing of stones, roofed with heather thatch over a frame of birch or, as the oak came in, of oak; the hearth stood in the middle of the floor on a flat stone, and the smoke went out through the thatch. Seann-Dhaoine built in dry stone where they could and in turf and driftwood where they could not: round houses with sunk hearths and drains at the eastern Vell, a long house in a cleft of the cliff at Tarr, huts in the lee of a dune at the northern Toll, and, where a whale gave them the chance, frames of rib. On the lake shore the platforms stood on oak piles over the water.

**Food.** The hill-folk ate mutton and beef, venison, barley bread and porridge, milk and curds, hazel-nuts roasted and stored, berries and roots in their seasons, and fish where they had water. Meat was cooked on stones heated at the fire's edge from the first years of the mason's hearth, and in pits of heated stones for a gathering. Seann-Dhaoine ate from the sea: shellfish above all, as their middens show, and fish, seal, seabirds and their eggs, and whale when one came ashore; they dried and smoked fish in their kilns and stored it in clay-lined pits.

**Fire.** Every fire of the hill-folk was Teine a' Chlachair, lent from hearth to hearth and carried in clay pots banked with ash. A household that let its hearth die begged a coal from the nearest living one. Hearths burned wood, peat and heather; before the first small burning at Àth gharbh no household outside the line burned an gual dathach, and after it very few did. How Seann-Dhaoine came by fire the tellings do not say.

**Boats.** The first boats were of hide stretched on a frame of bent hazel, greased against the wet; Oisean's was of that kind, and the north-shore hunters went out to the skerries in the like. Seann-Dhaoine built bigger boats of the same kind and, at Brenn, the first plank-built hulls, launched down stone ramps; of those hulls nothing is left. River landings kept flat-bottomed hulls for poling up the shallows.

**Cloth.** The hill-folk wove wool from their sheep and wore sealskin and deerskin cloaks in the cold; at Achadh dhìreach, late in the age, the wool of the dead was streaked with the colour of the vein. What Seann-Dhaoine wore the tellings do not say.

## What passed between them

No telling keeps a meeting. The tellings keep three things that passed. A chip of an gual dathach, still staining the shell about it, was thrown on the midden at Bral on the far western coast, late in the age: by gift, by trade or by theft, a piece of the vein came to Seann-Dhaoine. On the upper face of Carragh Cnoc bheag runs a line of the unread script, and the four words of the hill-folk lie lower on the same face, cut with a narrower tool by another hand; which hand came first is not known. And the same script that Seann-Dhaoine cut on the cliff at Tarr stands on the rock faces of the Doire uaine hills among the hill-folk's pastures. Whether the two peoples shared the island knowingly, no telling knows.
