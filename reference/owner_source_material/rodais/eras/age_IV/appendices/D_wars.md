# Wars, Raids and Calamities

An Aois Scaraidh was, for most of its length, a quiet age, and its clerks said so with some pride; a clerk of Queen Mòrag nic Raghnaill wrote that in forty years he had set down no death by war, no famine and no quarrel of Na Seachd Taighean. Its wars were two, both over the crown, and a raiding on the north-west coast. Its calamities were the ordinary ones of an island that fished and farmed and cut coal under a mountain: storms, coughs and fevers, dearths, tremors in the hills and fire in the thatch. This appendix sets them down, the wars in some length and the rest in tables, with the numbers of the dead as Leabhar a' Chùraim and Rolla a' Chalaidh give them. Where the rolls give no number, none is given.

## Cogadh nan Trì Tagraichean

**The cause.** Tormod Bàn's two sons were drowned together when the ferry on the Abhainn fhionn overturned in LE 534, and his only brother, Aonghas mac Iain, had died four years before. The king named no heir in the thirteen years left to him; by the fourth year after the ferry the custodians of Taigh na Fèithe were reckoning openly who stood nearest the crown. Three stood nearer than any others: Dùghall Garbh, the king's half-brother, with a following at Baile Mòr dhomhain that owed him rather than the crown; Catrìona nic Aonghais, the king's niece, daughter of the dead brother, raised at Inis thais; and Raghnall Ruadh, head of Clann Raghnaill and, from LE 540, master of Taigh na Fèithe. When Tormod Bàn died in LE 547 at seven-and-eighty, Na Seachd Taighean could agree on none of the three and held An Talla Dearg in common.

**The first years.** In LE 552 the three claimants' heralds came one after another to the western gate of Seann Warr; its reeve Mairead nic Ruairidh shut it to all three. Dùghall besieged the town a fortnight in LE 553 and gave it up, its salt-pans and brine springs having fed it. In LE 554 Dùghall, led by drovers who had used a hidden ford for forty years to move cattle untolled, crossed his host in the dark above Àth shean and broke Raghnall Ruadh's camp at the ford; Leabhar a' Chùraim counts some three hundred dead, most of them drowned. Raghnall's men, going south with no grain and no standing, burned the granary village of Doire mhin in LE 555, and forty-one households walked six days to winter at Cathair mhòr on the charity of Macha's temple. Dùghall besieged Seann Warr a second time for a month in LE 556 and marched away when Catrìona closed the gates of Cathair dhomhain.

**Sèist Cathair dhomhain.** The city had declared for Dùghall. Catrìona came against it in the autumn of LE 556 with a host she had held together through two lean winters on little more than her word; borrowed fishing coasters shut its water gate by night. With no ground beyond the walls for burying, the dead of the siege lines were laid near the well-heads, and a sickness of the gut ran through the streets and the garrison. On the fortieth day, in LE 557, its captain Niall Mòr mac Leòid surrendered on oath, and Catrìona let the garrison march out between her silent lines, wounded and dead named and carried.

**The hearths refused.** In LE 558 Raghnall Ruadh, holding Taigh na Fèithe, handed the store-master Eòghann mac Dhùghaill a board naming every hearth and temple of the shires that stood against him and ordered none of them measured a handful. The carriers went down the white road with empty baskets, and through Dubhlas the hearths and temple fires of the cold shires went grey, among them Macha's fire at Cathair mhòr where the people of Doire mhin had wintered. A verse with no name to it went about those shires: that no battle was lost, only the fire. Dùghall came to Catrìona's camp with six men and no herald and offered to join their hosts, saying that whichever of them won, it must not be a crown that could shut a door on a shire's hearths.

**Muileann chrom.** In LE 560 the joined hosts met Raghnall Ruadh below Muileann chrom, and Raghnall fell on the field. The next morning his own custodians struck the seal from Taigh na Fèithe store without waiting to be told who now held the mountain, and the carriers went up the white road again. Dùghall Garbh hid a cut below the arm from his physicians and died of it at Baile Mòr dhomhain in LE 561, at one-and-seventy; his captains, led by Artair mac Lachlainn, came over to Catrìona unbidden. She was crowned in LE 562 at six-and-thirty and called Catrìona Bhuadhach, and in LE 565, in Taigh na Fèithe yard, she and the seven heads drafted Riaghailt an Teallaich, binding every claimant, house and king never again to withhold the measured handful. Seann Warr had its gates honoured in LE 594. Seònaid nic Coinnich, who had been a child in the charity of Cathair mhòr, wrote the war's history as Leabhar nan Trì Tagraichean, finished in LE 624.

## The sea-thieves of the north-west

From about LE 782 boats out of the small islands off the north-west coast boarded coasters bound for Baile chrom and took their grain, wearing no mark and hiding no face. Rolla a' Chalaidh of LE 784 named among them a fisherman's son of Baile chrom, Aonghas mac Ruairidh, and his mother told the reeve plainly that a young man who watches the fish thin goes looking for a living wherever it is still to be found. Half of Baile chrom stood empty by LE 789 and the crown remitted its dues. When the thieves put in twice in one moon within sight of the harbour, the reeve Coinneach mac Nèill asked for a force, and Eilidh Bhàn sent Iain mac Thormoid north by sea in LE 800. In LE 801, guided by the town's own fishermen through the channels, his boats landed at first light on Eilean dhubh and burned the thieves' hulls on the strand. Aonghas mac Ruairidh, one-and-thirty, was killed defending a hull; the thieves' leader Uilleam Ruadh, six-and-thirty, was taken and hanged at Cathair dhearg before the season turned. The raiding ended with him, and the dearth that followed emptied the coast the thieves had not.

## Cogadh nam Bràithrean

King Ailean mac Raghnaill died in LE 1,266 at six-and-sixty and left two sons by different mothers, each told openly by the parent who raised him that he was the heir; An Talla Dearg's clerks left a blank on the burial roll where the heir's name should stand. For four years the brothers' households exchanged careful feast-gifts, until a messenger of Alasdair Bàn's was turned back at An Talla Dearg's gate at the feast of Bealltach. In LE 1,270 Alasdair's men seized Cnoc naomh and Iain Ciar took it back, and Na Seachd Taighean divided four to three. Both households set every road under one following's watch or the other. In LE 1,272 Alasdair's ships burned the waterfront of Ceann mhin, which held for his brother, and its rope stores and granary with it; Cathair mhòr fed the town that winter. In LE 1,274 Iain Ciar defeated Alasdair below Muileann ghlas, took him alive, and let his captains go home. Two years later the brothers made their peace at the road-house An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, on benches the keeper Mairead nic Mhurchaidh had measured to a finger's breadth, with the same loaf and the same jug at either end; both loaves were left uneaten. Alasdair held the lordship of Seann Chwen for life under the peace, kept it, and died there in LE 1,291. Iain Ciar was crowned at six-and-thirty in LE 1,276 and reigned fifty years. His clerk Pàdraig mac Ruairidh wrote the account of the war in his praise, finished in LE 1,304; it is the only account that survives whole.
