# The Orders and the Rites

An Creideamh Sean that the island kept at the opening of a' Scaradh had no orders in it. There were the dawn-hawk rite at Cnoc bheag, the sacred woods kept in common by whoever kept them, Scoiltean nan Draoidhean with no ground of their own, the wolf-stone at Inis thais watched by families who had forgotten why, the old spirits of the hearths with their keepers in the east, and the handful of coal burned at every lawful hearth under Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig. By the end of the age the same faith had five orders with their own houses, books and quarrels, and the island had made for itself a set of rites for the departed that no order owned. This appendix gives each as the record gives it: when it began, where it held, and what it quarrelled over.

## The coal and Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig

Under all the orders lay the custody of the vein. Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig, measured to every hearth that held the right a handful of the coal, burned at the winter burning and at the rites of the household, and no more. The custodians of Taigh na Fèithe above Muileann dhearg kept the law in Leabhar a' Chùraim and kept Crom's stone in the inner room, with a lamp before it that was never let go out. The law was bent three times in the age and broken once. It was bent when the custodians ruled in LE 25, after six winters of argument, that each hull of the fleet found ready might carry one sealed measure, a single household's season; when Na Seachd Taighean were made in LE 55 and the right passed by house rather than by apprenticeship; and when a smaller house was granted a neighbour's unused right at the last Cunntas nan Làmh. It was broken in LE 558, when Raghnall Ruadh refused the handful to every hearth and temple of the shires that stood against him, and Riaghailt an Teallaich of LE 565 bound every claimant, house and king never to withhold it again. The first asking to burn the coal for work rather than worship, Athchuingean an Airgid of Clann Choinnich, was refused in LE 1,559, and the second in LE 1,776.

## Òrd an t-Seabhaig

The dawn-hawk rite of An Aois Naomh was kept at Cnoc bheag by a single family of hawk-keepers. In the third century of the count the priestess Gormshuil nic Artair, who took the mews from her father Artair, taught that the hawk of the rite was Mòd himself, Seabhag Acaill, and that the vein under Muileann dhearg was a hawk of his kind: a hungry hawk, to be fed the measured handful and never glutted past it, since a bird fed past its measure is as useless as one starved of it. Her watchers called themselves a house and raised Taigh an t-Seabhaig below the mews block in LE 232; her pupils raised a second house at Ros gheal in LE 239, which took charge of the measured burning for the coast country. Ùisdean mac Pàdraig wrote her sayings after her death as Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, and marked every saying he had not heard from her own mouth; the marked sayings outnumber the rest. Òrd an t-Seabhaig reached the south when the bell-founders of Muileann naomh hung a bronze bell in a hawk-house there, and at Muileann naomh the teaching held whole when it softened elsewhere.

At the council of Òrd an t-Seabhaig at Cnoc bheag in LE 889 a young priest asked whether Mòd would rather starve a grown flock than see his handful grown to meet it. Those who held that a greater household might rightly be fed a greater handful were named An Seabhag Biadhte, and asked to keep their teaching somewhere it would not be heard daily at Cnoc bheag. They settled at the northern Seann Skell on Bàgh an t-Seabhaig, where the priestess Beathag nic Sheumais built them from four families into a school; her petition of LE 919 to raise the handful by a quarter for the north coast was refused, and the house went on as before.

## The schools of the druids

Scoiltean nan Draoidhean of Flidais, lady of the deer, taught Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the stag's teaching: that the vein answers a hand for some measure in it, which the schools never agreed how to name. They had no ground of their own until LE 479, when they took up the yearly walk of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar at Muileann chiar, a procession open to anyone who would walk it. Their elder teacher there, Beathag nic Ruairidh, told the travelling druid Coinneach mac Mhàrtainn that the schools wanted a fixed ground where the old question might be argued whole, and he raised Taigh na Deasbaid at Dùn thais in LE 314 with Carson an làmh seo?, why this hand?, cut on the lintel. The rule of the house was that a question once put was argued a full year before any answer came from the master's chair. Coinneach's own question in LE 324, whether the hands that went with the fleet were those the vein answered best, was forbidden at every one of Na Seachd Taighean' tables, the first time the houses forbade a question rather than a deed. Pupils of Dùn thais founded a second house of disputation at Inis mhòr in LE 769. Raghnall mac Chaluim's Clàr nam Bliadhnachan at Dùn thais was forbidden in LE 1,219 and the house shut; it was opened and shut more than once and last reopened in LE 1,759 on condition that the question of longer lives never be raised.

The rival teaching of An Aois Naomh, that the vein answers labour and not birth, was woken at Doire chiar by Eilidh Dhubh from LE 460. Taigh na Fèithe had some hundred of her tracts burned in its yard in LE 484, and the teaching went on in copies and in her pupil Coinneach Odhar's readings, without an order and without a house.

## Òrd Mhacha, Macha of the two horses

Macha's rite was Òrd Mhacha' rite of the moors, which keeps the two horses of one chariot, foaled on one night, Liath Macha the grey and Dubh Sainglenn the black: light and dark, two powers in balance, and the vein belonging to both. At the rite the horses were brought up together to the yoke-poles and the worshipper stood with a hand on each pole, since one horse pulling alone draws a chariot only in a circle. King Aonghas mac Uisdein took it up in his own old age in LE 441 against his councillors' warning, and within a year half An Talla Dearg's household had taken it up with him, so that Òrd Mhacha became the order of Cathair dhearg without a quarrel fought over it. Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, held in common since An Aois Naomh, passed to Macha's priests alone by royal grant in LE 471. King Aonghas Òg was the first king carried to his grave by the rite in LE 484. Macha's temple at Cathair mhòr fed the burnt-out households of Doire mhin through the winter of LE 555 without asking whose claim they had favoured, and its fire was the first of the cold shires' hearths relit in LE 560. In the fifteenth century the priests raised their great house at Cathair mhòr, with a floor of black and white stone eleven years in the laying, the largest roofed building on Dia-thìr.

## Òrd na Cloiche

At Inis thais the wolf-stone Clach had been watched for generations by families who kept the watch without a name for it. In LE 405 the net-maker Dòmhnall mac Ailein began to say on the strand that the stone was Lia Fàil, which cries out under the rightful, and its watchers an Fhianna, sworn to keep the whole island as a wolf keeps its range. His hearers became an Fhianna and raised the wolf-house, the first temple on the island to Lia Fàil by name, roofed with northern slate, and blew a horn at every turn of the tide. Dòmhnall taught on the strand five-and-thirty years and never took an office in the order named after his teaching. Òrd na Cloiche and Òrd an t-Seabhaig both claimed the harbour shrine at Caol leathan in LE 427; the king's reeve divided its hours between them. An Fhianna nursed the sick of Inis chrom through the spotted fever of LE 624, many of them dying of it. In LE 1,389 the congregation at Inis thais divided over whether the dead, who belong to Donn, might be prayed for by name at Clach; those who said no kept the temple, and the others raised a second house at Baile gheal.

## Òrd Mhanannain, Manannan of the mist

Òrd Mhanannain began on the quay of Seann Skell in LE 654, when Mànas mac Ruairidh, son of a sworn-man of the harbour-court, preached that the one power was Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea, whose cup, Cupa na Fìrinne, bears the yellow scorpion; that Manannan had shut the eastern sea behind the fleet with his mist, which hides Dia-thìr from every ship beyond it; and that the crossing must not be tried again. His own father knelt first. He blessed boats at Cidhe an Diosail by passing a plain wooden bowl of harbour water over each hull, and his congregation later raised the silver for a proper cup of plain silver without his asking. Taigh Mhanannain was raised beside the harbour-court. The teaching went down the coast with the fishing families to Ros bheag and Cuan shean, where the old rope-walker Tormod Ciar swore Cupa na Fìrinne on the rope-walk in rainwater cupped in his hands. In LE 694 the priests at Ros bheag laid down that no boat of the faithful might go out of sight of the headlands, and the rule of sight-of-land was kept on the west coast for the rest of the age. The synod of the order at Seann Skell in LE 1,590 laid down the rule of sight-of-land again and argued two days over a new prayer before agreeing its shortest form, to be said at the foot of the river pool: A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte. From LE 1,714 the priests said it on the day of Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh as well, on the same stones, and neither the keepers of the rite nor the priests were pleased; by the end of the age they said it at every tide. In the age's last years the priest Mànas mac Dhùghaill wrote that the grey beyond the headlands stood thinner each year, and that he could not say whether the prayer held anything back at all.

## The old spirits

The keepers of Na Seann Spioradan, the faith of the hearths, kept the ancestors in the east. In LE 179 they raised a spirit-house at Dùn ìseal and cut the names of the eastern dead on wooden posts, renewed each generation. Lightning burned the posts in LE 1,149 and the keepers cut the names again from memory and from Leabhar a' Chùraim, owning in their roll that some were guesses. The shepherds of the Doire uaine hills lit no fire on the high pastures after dark, for fear of Marbh-bheò of the old carvings, and a reeve sent to argue them out of it came down before dark and never went back. The shrine at Seann Tarr held its feast-market from LE 644.

## The rites of the departed

No order made these, and every order kept them.

**Oidhche nan Lòchran.** On the evening before the sailing, beginning at one widow's window by the fish-stones, a lamp was set on every sill along the Seann Skell waterfront and kept burning till morning, though no one had told the town to do it. It was kept each year on the eve nearest the day of the turn, household by household, and thinned over the centuries until one old woman, Ealasaid nic Iain, kept the one lamp in the harbour-house window; she died in LE 1,800 and no one set a light there again.

**An Tùrsa Geal.** Queen Beathag put on undyed wool for her sister in LE 53, with no grave to stand at and no body to wash, and laid a cup, a trencher and a straight knife at the foot of her table each evening. Within a generation any household that had given someone to the fleet kept the same rite unasked. In the north-east it was kept in the undyed rain-cloth of Baile gharbh.

**Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh.** On the eve nearest the day of the turn the households of Seann Skell brought small lights down to the west quay and set them along the stones the fleet sailed from. The harbours kept it on different days until the bargain of LE 739 fixed one day for all of them, Là an Diosail. It was kept by fewer each generation; eleven people stood on Cidhe an Diosail at the last rite this record tells of, in LE 1,803.

**Faire a' Chinn.** Faire a' Chinn was kept at Ceann mhòr from LE 66, household by household in turn, for returning sails that never came, and lapsed long before the end of the age.

**Roghnaich.** The rolls came to use the word roghnaich, to choose, standing alone to mean one who chose the crossing, and the harbour scribes let it stand.
