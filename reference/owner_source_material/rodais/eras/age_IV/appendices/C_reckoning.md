# The Reckoning of An Diosal

In An Aois Scaraidh the island first numbered its years from a single day, and the day it chose was the day its kin went away. This appendix tells how the count was begun, how two offices came to keep it a year apart and settled the difference by agreement rather than by proof, how the moons were reckoned beneath the years, and how a year was written in the rolls of the age. The years of this record are counted from the first year of the age, the year the tidings of a far coast came to the quays; An Diosal fell in its fortieth year.

## Before the count

At the opening of the age no one on Dia-thìr numbered the years from any day at all. An Talla Dearg counted by reigns and by harvests, and the first year of the age went into its rolls as the ninth year of Queen Beathag, a good year for barley and a middling one for fish. Leabhar a' Chùraim at Taigh na Fèithe counted by burnings: a year was the year in which the handful was measured and burned at every lawful hearth, and it was named, when it had to be named, by the master of Taigh na Fèithe and the winter it fell in. The harbour-house at Seann Skell counted by the scribe's own keeping, the sixth or seventh year of Niall's boards. Clàr nan Rìghrean gave the names of the kings in order and the length of no reign. A debt within living memory could be reckoned so. The distance between two reigns could not, and no one had yet needed it.

## Donnchadh's count

The count was not made at the time of the turn. Donnchadh mac Thormoid, a clerk of Taigh na Fèithe, had entered the turn of the fleet in Leabhar a' Chùraim at six-and-twenty, under a day after Samhain, a full turning of the moon and more after every other account set the sailing itself; the leaf that reached him did not explain the gap, and he copied it faithfully. Four years later, at thirty, in LE 44, for his own ledger's convenience and for no reason he offered anyone, he began to number the years from the turn as Leabhar a' Chùraim entered it, and called them Bliadhnaichean an Diosail. The word means, in the island's speech, a turning sunwise: the way a hand moves, a boat rounds a point or a procession goes about a cairn, with the right shoulder toward the centre and the course following the sun. He worked the count backward through his private book to the turn itself. There was no year nought; the years before the turn were counted backward, as years before the turn. No one above him asked him to keep it, and he told no one that he did.

The crown's own scribes at An Talla Dearg took up the count about a generation later, in the seventies of the age, setting aside the reckoning by a king's reign for the turning Donnchadh had first set his own hand to as a clerk of thirty. From that time the rolls of An Talla Dearg, Leabhar a' Chùraim and the harbour boards dated their entries by An Diosal, and the old reckoning by reigns survived in Clàr nan Rìghrean alone.

## The two counts

The harbour scribe at Seann Skell had set Am Falbh and An Diosal on one morning. Leabhar a' Chùraim set the turn after the next Samhain. The crown's scribes, taking up the count when the court remembered the departure as a single thing, counted from the year the ships sailed; Taigh na Fèithe, following Donnchadh, counted from the turn as its own book entered it. Both began each year at Samhain, by the moon, as Talla na Lasrach had done before Taigh na Fèithe; so the sailing and the turn lay in different years of the old year, and the two counts stood a year apart. Nobody noticed for the better part of seven centuries, for the two offices seldom had cause to set a date of one beside a date of the other.

In LE 738 they found that their counts did not agree. Mòrag nic Ìomhair, a scribe of An Talla Dearg's own household, rode the thirty-nine miles to Taigh na Fèithe with the crown's leaves, and the master granted her Leabhar a' Chùraim for as many days as the matter took. She and Taigh na Fèithe scribe Eòghann mac Pheadair worked three days by lamplight and traced the difference back to a single year early after the turn, entered twice by one office's habit or once by the other's. Neither leaf either scribe had brought could say which office had erred, or whether either had. In LE 739 Taigh na Fèithe took the crown's count for the one that would go forward, since it touched more households than Taigh na Fèithe's own, and Eòghann wrote Taigh na Fèithe's agreement to it in his own hand, the master's mark under his and the crown scribe's office mark under that. A later hand in Leabhar a' Chùraim adds that the scribes afterward ceased to pretend it had been fixed by proof. The same agreement fixed, for the first time, one day on which every harbour of the coast should keep Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh, which the two counts had placed differently; not one harbour wrote to complain of it. From that year there was one count of An Diosal on Dia-thìr, and it followed the harbour scribe, setting the sailing and the turn both in its first year.

## The thousandth year

By the settled count the thousandth year of An Diosal fell in LE 1,039. Na Seachd Taighean asked Queen Mòrag Ruadh whether it deserved a feast or a fast, and she judged between them: one burning on the quay the count began from, no more coal in it than an ordinary hearth burns of an evening. She rode a week over frost-hard roads to stand on Cidhe an Diosail for it, and her own book gives the cost of the coal reckoned to Taigh na Fèithe's weights and, beneath it, five words: one handful, nothing more.

## The night-count and the moons

Beneath the years the island reckoned by the moon, and it counted time by nights and not by days. The night went before its day, as the dark half of the year went before the bright: a feast began at dusk on its eve, and a debt of ten nights fell due at the tenth dusk. Leabhar a' Chùraim calls this reckoning cunntas nan oidhcheannan, the count of the nights.

The year began at Samhain, with the dark half of the year. It held twelve moons of thirty nights and twenty-nine in turn, each begun on the night of the full moon: waning through its dark half to the dark of the moon, then waxing through its bright half to the next full. Below the moon the island had the half-moon, fourteen nights or fifteen as the moon ran, and no week. A debt, a sentence or a turn in the galleries was set by the half-moon; the markets met at the full, as Queen Sìleas's charter to Cathair fhada granted; the night of the full and the night of the dark were rest-nights, on which no coal was cut and no net set.

Twelve moons make some eleven nights fewer than the sun's year, so about every thirtieth moon a thirteenth, Eadrach, the between-moon, was put in at the year's end, after Damhrach and before Samhnas, so that Samhain should not come before the summer was out. In An Aois Naomh Talla na Lasrach declared the between-moon; in this age Taigh na Fèithe declared it, and entered the years in which it was put in beside the burnings. Harbour scribes dated deaths to it like any other moon: Calum mac Nèill died in the between-moon of LE 58.

## How a year was written

An entry of An Talla Dearg in the second half of the age opens with the year of An Diosal and the moon, and, when the day mattered, the night of the moon counted from the full; the reign stands beside it only at a crowning or a death. Leabhar a' Chùraim, being older in its habits, set the burning of the year first and the year of An Diosal after it. The harbour boards kept the number of the year, the weather and the tide, and in the last centuries little else. Clàr nan Rìghrean, recopied onto new hide in LE 829, set its beginning at An Roghnachadh rather than at the sailing and gave each ruler the years of the reign by An Diosal, the first list on the island to measure the distance between two reigns; where a name was lost, as for the fourth and fifth rulers of the house of Catrìona, the years were kept and the name was not. Clàr nam Bliadhnachan that Raghnall mac Chaluim set out at Dùn thais in the thirteenth century was a table of lives and not of the calendar: the birth and death rolls of the custodian houses set beside those of their tenants, which showed the heads of the houses living some years longer. It was forbidden to be copied in LE 1,219, and its working copy entered in Taigh na Fèithe's inventory as one line among the galleries and Clachan a' Chrannchuir.

## The day of the turn

Là an Diosail, the day of Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh, is the one day of the year the count gave its name to. It was fixed by the agreement of LE 739 and kept by every harbour of the coast together from the next winter. It was never agreed on what day the fleet had truly sailed. A scribe sent in LE 1,748 to note the rite for the crown's book asked the last old women of Seann Skell, and no two answers agreed; he wrote as much into the crown's own book, unable to choose among them.
