# Of Things Forgotten

An age that begins with a fleet and ends without a boat that will leave the sight of land is an age of forgetting, and a' Scaradh forgot more than most. It did not forget carelessly. It forgot the way a household forgets the craft of a dead grandfather: nobody asks for it, the last who knew it grows old, and one winter it is gone. This appendix gathers what the record says of the crafts, the lore and the measures the age had and lost, or kept only in a name, and of the houses on the roads that kept what the harbours let go.

## The keel and the fleet

There was no keel on the island before the fifth year of the age. There were keels in the tellings: the old tellers of the west said that the boats of Rìghrean na Cloiche had been built on a spine of oak, the ribs rising out of it like the ribs of a beast from its backbone and the planks pegged to the ribs, not sewn, and that such a boat stood stiff in a sea and could stay out for days. The boats the island actually built were sewn: planks split from oak and ash, laid edge to edge on bent ribs, sewn together with split root and twisted gut, the seams stopped with moss and tallow. A good sewn boat was light and supple and could be carried up a beach by six men.

Alasdair mac Iain, Alasdair Saor, a boat-wright of the strand below Seann Skell, learned the keel from hulls no longer than his arm that he floated in the river pool, and laid the first true keel in the fifth year on a bed of oak blocks above the high-water mark: the Queen oak of Doire fhionn, hewn with a ridge along its underside. The frames rose from it in pairs, one pair for each arm's length of its seventy feet, pegged with treenails of heart-oak and joined across by beams, crooks from the Bent Back and the Old Man's Knee and the wild wood by Loch chrom. The first hull, An Fhaoileag, the gull, went out past Ros àrsaidh for her trial in the sixth year with fourteen aboard and lay down in a following sea. Alasdair redrew the run of the hull by a driftwood fire on the strand, fuller and hollow in the bow, and An Dara Fhaoileag, was built to the changed pattern and rode true. Every hull of the fleet after her was built to it. The rope for their rigging came from the walks of Cuan shean, their sail-cloth from the levy of Baile àrsaidh; the grain for the crossing came down the Abhainn dhomhain in forty-one barges. Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill sailed as leader of the willing aboard An Ròn Glas, the grey seal, with Catrìona nic Iain at the steering oar.

How many hulls went round the river pool sunwise on the morning of the sailing is not known. The harbour-house gives nineteen and one-and-twenty on different leaves; Niall mac Dhòmhnaill, eleven years old, cut a notch on a hazel stick for every hull that passed the quay wall, kept the stick inside his shirt, and was buried with it at his own asking in LE 104, the count never once spoken aloud. The tidings that came four years later told of two landfalls, reached by hulls that had not held together the whole crossing, and of nothing more.

The fellowship of the strand, sworn over a coal in the fifth year, outlived the fleet by two generations. Its master Coinneach mac Fhearghais, the first apprentice Alasdair swore, died in LE 68; his journeyman Griogair released the last journeymen from the oath in LE 84, judging there was no craft enough left in the island's boat-building to keep it; the patterns and keel-moulds, untouched for years in the shed on the strand, were burned for firewood in the hard winter of LE 85. When a merchant of Inis àrsaidh had a deep hull laid in LE 344 for the long coast run, no wright living held the old scarfing of the keel timbers that let a hull flex to a sea, and she listed at launch past any righting. A boat-builder of Seann Skell, Seumas mac Iain, laid another deep keel in LE 1,359; she filled and sank at the launching, and after a year of Manannan's priests preaching against his sunken hull he built no more deep keels.

**Òran na Druime.** What the island kept of the keel it kept in a work-song. The rope-makers of Cuan shean walked their strands to a song their fathers had taught them, and in LE 1,389 Iseabail nic Lachlainn, a gatherer of songs for the royal household, took it down from Tormod Ròpaire's crew. It begins:

> *Druim fon aisne, aisne fon chlàr,*
> *clàr ri clàr is an cuan fodhpa.*
> Keel under rib, rib under plank,
> plank to plank and the sea beneath them.

The verses after it name the parts of a deep keel in words no one on the walk could explain; a young man of the crew thought them older speech worn smooth, and Tormod said it changed nothing about how the strand was walked. Iseabail read the whole of it before the king's hall in LE 1,391, and a harper and a vein-house clerk each said the words fitted no tongue or hand they knew. Her tablets went to Taigh na Fèithe at her death, where the clerks wrote into the inventory that the gathering held one item they could describe only by its sound and its keeping, not its meaning.

## The star-lore

No pilot of the west coast had ever held a course by night when the fleet was building. Six were sent in LE 11 to the old steersmen of Cuan ruadh on the eastern coast, the only sailors of the island who ever had. Seumas Reultach taught them on the headland above the town the wheel of the sky, Reul na Stiùire Reul na Stiùire and the three that find it, the four turning marks of the year, which stars stand over the stern at Samhnas and which at Teasach, the smell of the dark before a change of wind and the look of the water under starlight when a swell is building. At Taigh na Fèithe's asking he had his granddaughter cut the first four of those on a scraped hide, and kept back the rest, saying some things a hide could hold and some things only a life could. He died in the moon of Faolach of LE 14. Catrìona nic Iain steered by the star in the trial off Ros àrsaidh and named it on the quay, and took it over the sea. On the island the lore was not needed again. By LE 698 a pilot's grandson of Ros bheag could not name the star his grandfather had steered by, and no one in his house had thought to write it down. The hide is not in any inventory the record gives.

## The Baile ghorm blue

The dyers of Baile ghorm in the south-east made a fast blue from a marsh plant and a mordant of their own. Oighrig nic Ailein found the mixing that held it through washing after washing in LE 1,064, after ten years changing one thing at a time, and cloth of it became the feast-day dress of the custodian houses. The last dyer who knew the mordant quarrelled with both her apprentices and sent them from her house in turn, and died without teaching either; the blue that followed was the common blue of the island under the old name. The old weavers held a length of the new beside a length of the old and could tell them apart at a glance.

## The grey glaze

The potters of Clann Mhic Phàil at Muileann gheal set a grey-green glaze on the custodian houses' table-ware that no other kiln could match, passed mother to daughter and never written, for a thing kept in the hand and the eye, they said, needs no writing. Iain Mac Phàil, the last of the family, took two apprentices in his last years and tried to set the making down; neither apprentice learned to judge the moment the kiln's heat must be let fall, and the moment would not go into words. He died in LE 1,509, and the town's potters tried for a generation to match it and failed.

## The layered steel

The smiths of Dùn dhearg made the ceremonial blades of the custodian houses from steel folded and refolded in layers, from the building of the walls of Clann Ìomhair onward. In LE 1,568 the fellowship lost the last smith trained far enough in the folding to finish a blade alone. The blades made after him were ordinary work, prized for what the craft had been.

## Firlot, tally and salt

The island had no coin in this age, and wanted none. It measured.

**The firlot.** Queen Sìleas, watching a farmer and a grain-seller come near to blows below An Talla Dearg over the true size of a basket, had oak firlots cut to the capital's standard, bound with three iron hoops and marked with her mark, and sent one to every shire seat in LE 262, so that a basket weighed true at the capital weighed true anywhere. The firlot of Àth shean wore smaller with use than the standard, and had been smaller for generations before a young clerk found it in LE 1,498 and carried it up rather than let it stand.

**The tally.** A bargain was cut on a hazel stick split along its length, one half to each party; the grain of the wood runs true only when the two halves are laid together again, so no notch can be cut after. Queen Sìleas set a tally-house in every royal granary in LE 284, and Cairt Mhargaidh Cathair fhada three years later was the first market right cut on paired sticks. The sail-cloth levy, the barges of the grain strife and the drowned of Inis àrsaidh were all counted so.

**Salt and meal.** Where a toll was owed it was paid in kind. The keeper of Drochaid Ros fhionn took a handful of salt from every load that crossed his bridge from LE 369, and his iron-bound salt-box filled faster than any box on that road; when the last keeper died in LE 1,172 the toll-gate stood open and no crossing was refused again. Beathag nic Thòmais paid for old boards in measures of meal, and a clerk lodging at a road-house left meal for his bed.

## The road-houses

The houses on the roads kept, by their charters, what the harbours were letting go. Am Buabhall Fortanach, the old road-house by Doire shean, burned to its footings in LE 164 and was raised again in stone by Eilidh nic Chaluim, who served a boy of Clann Fhearchair jugged buffalo before the roof was on. An Leòmhann Buidhe, on the northern mining road, fed the coal-carters oatcake and heard their grumbling. Iain mac Mhurchaidh raised a second byre at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, at the halfway stage of the central mining road, and began a book of every ox-team that lodged there through the mountain snows; his line kept the house, and at it Iain Ciar and Alasdair Bàn made their peace. In LE 1,319 Iain Ciar chartered the seven great road-houses of the mining circuit, their keepers to meet once a year at An Sligeanach Fad' às to settle their accounts. The charter-roll records the secret syrupped beef of An Taigh-seinnse Òir and a reeve's complaint of its price; the itemised bill An Taigh-seinnse Reòta sent after a party of reeves snowed in, for every jar of pickled spinach and every cup of fire milk, paid in full; and Peigi nic Dhonnchaidh of An Taigh-òsta Grianach feeding Queen Gormshuil's whole progress on pan-fried spinach and black wine, for which the queen forgave her a year's road dues.

## What was kept

Not everything went. Cidhe an Diosail kept its name, and Là an Diosail its day. The Child of Doire fhionn stood inside its wall. The last barge of the cold way lay chocked level on the strand at Inis mhòr, kept rather than broken up, though few could say any longer what the cold way had been the way from. The loft at Muileann chaol kept every paper left to it, on condition that nothing be burned, among them three copies of the tidings of the two landfalls that did not agree, all three copied fair by Beathag nic Thòmais and none chosen. Òran na Druime was sung on the walk at Cuan shean exactly as it had been given, no better understood.
