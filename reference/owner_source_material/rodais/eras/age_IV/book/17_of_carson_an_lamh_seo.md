# Of Carson an Làmh Seo

*LE 300 – 324*

## I. Of the Carters' Roads

Coinneach mac Mhàrtainn had walked the roads of the middle shires for the better part of forty years before he ever thought to stop walking them, and by his own account he might have gone another forty had a question not caught up with him on the way and refused, at last, to be carried any further on foot.

He was a druid of the schools of Flidais, lady of the deer, one of a scattered handful who kept no fixed hearth but went from house to house and hall to hall teaching what the schools called Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the stag's teaching: that the vein answered a hand for some measure in it, a measure the schools themselves had never agreed how to name. It was old work, older than any house he taught in, and it suited a man who liked roads better than roofs. He carried little: a stick worn smooth at the grip, a satchel of hide with his writing-boards in it, and the coat he had worn so many winters that it had come to feel, to him, like a second skin he could not have shed even had he wished to.

In the summer of {{year:III-0072}} the roads brought him south to the high passes above Imrich nam Pantar, where the herders of that country kept the days of the panthers' crossing as carefully as any feast of the crown or Taigh na Fèithe. He came down off the hill road on the second of those days and found the whole valley emptied of flocks: no sheep on the slopes, no goats on the rock, every gate along the panthers' path standing open and every herder's dog shut up indoors, so that a man walking the valley that morning might have thought some plague had taken every beast on it at once. An old herdswoman he shared a fire with that night told him the great cats had crossed the mountains at the same season for longer than her grandmother's grandmother could number, coming down from the high passes and going on to the southern fens, and that the valley's dead who had ever tried to keep a flock on the path that week were remembered by name still, though none of them by more than a line. "We do not ask why they cross where they cross," she said, working a knot from a length of cord as she said it. "We only know that they do, and we get out of the way." Coinneach thought about that a long while after the fire had burned down, turning her plain sentence over the way he turned most things over, and found in it, though she had not meant it so, something close to the schools' own oldest teaching: that a thing might be true and useful without any man alive being able to say why it was so.

He came north again by the carters' road above Inis mhòr, and stopped, as he had stopped a dozen times before on that stretch of road, at the road-house called An Leòmhann Buidhe, in {{year:III-0073}} as in every year before it, where the coal-carters of the northern mining road broke their journey for oatcakes fried in linseed oil and the barley spirit they called fire water. The keeper that year was a broad, red-faced man who watered his fire water more than his custom deserved and had been fined for it twice, and said so himself, cheerfully, to anyone who asked, as though the fining were a kind of fame. The carters who drank there talked, as carters everywhere talked, of the roads and the weather and the loads on their carts, but Coinneach, sitting apart with his own cup and listening more than he drank, heard something under the talk that year that he had not heard so plainly before: an old carter complaining that the coal came slower off the northern galleries than it once had. The ground held coal enough, the man swore; what it lacked was hands the vein would answer quickly, so that Taigh na Fèithe had taken to setting two men to a face where one had sufficed in his father's day. Coinneach said nothing to this. He had heard its like at a dozen road-houses over a dozen years, always in passing, never dwelt on, a grumble among grumbles about weather and loads. But he had begun, without quite deciding to, to keep a private count of how often he heard it.

He kept that count the way another man might have kept a tally-stick, cutting no notch that any eye but his own would ever read, only a private mark on the inside of his own memory each time the complaint came round again: a vein-house steward at a naming feast saying the same of the western galleries; a woman selling combs at a market fair who told him, unasked, that her own grandfather had worked a face alone that it now took two men to answer at all; a boy of no more than ten who asked him outright, at a hearth where Coinneach had stopped for the night, why the vein loved some hands and hated others, and whether it might simply be running short of the hands it loved best. He answered the boy as he answered every such question on the road, with the schools' old careful phrases about measure and merit that satisfied no one, least of all himself, and walked on the next morning turning the boy's plain word, hated, over in his mind for the better part of a week.

## II. Of the Grove at Muileann chiar

From An Leòmhann Buidhe the road ran on to Dùn thais and, a little beyond it, to the sacred wood the schools kept at Muileann chiar, Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, where Flidais's white stag was said still to be seen on quiet evenings by those patient enough to stand still for one. Coinneach had been coming to that grove since he was a boy sent to learn his letters from the schools' own teachers, and he came to it now, in the autumn of that year, an old man by the reckoning of most trades though the schools counted their own differently, to sit among the oaks with three or four of the older teachers and speak of what he had heard on the roads.

They were not surprised by it. One of them, a stooped woman named Beathag nic Ruairidh who had taught in that grove longer than Coinneach had been alive, said only that the schools had always known the question was there, under the teaching, waiting to be asked whole rather than caught in pieces the way Coinneach had been catching it for years: whether the fleet that went out in the days of the Roll had carried away, along with a fifth of every household's right to the coal, something of the very measure the schools claimed to teach. "You have been asking half a question your whole life," she told him, not unkindly, "on every road you have ever walked, one carter at a time. A half question asked a thousand times is still a half question." She was the one who first said to him what he did not, in the end, forget: that the schools had always taught by scattering their teaching one hearth at a time, as a sower scatters seed, and that a question of that size and that danger wanted a different ground to grow in, a fixed hall where it could be turned over by rule rather than by chance, among pupils who had come to argue and not merely to be fed and housed for a night.

Coinneach walked the bounds of the sacred wood alone the following spring, the way the schools' teachers walked it once in a lifetime to mark it in their own memory before they died, tracing the old boundary stones through bracken and standing stands of birch, and came out at the far side with his mind made up, though he could not afterward have said the exact hour it had been made. The question would not be carried hearth to hearth any longer. It would have a house, and a rule, and be argued properly by those who came to argue it, rather than go on leaking out sideways in a road-house over cups of fire water, half heard and half denied before the words were even finished.

## III. Of the House Raised at Dùn thais

He chose Dùn thais for the house, a walled town on its hill a day's walk from the grove and near enough the carters' road that pupils would not have to leave the road far to find it, and near enough Caol bheag and the coast towns beyond it that word of a fixed hall, once it had a fixed hall to carry word of, might travel without a druid's own feet to carry it. In {{year:III-0074}} the house went up: a long hall of grey stone with a turf roof and one door, built by masons of the town in trade for a season's teaching of their own sons, with benches down both walls and a hearth at the far end and, before the hearth, a table where the master of the house would sit. Coinneach had the old question of the schools cut into the lintel over the one door before the roof was even finished over it, Carson an làmh seo?, why this hand, in letters deep enough, the mason promised him, to outlast three generations of thumbs tracing them. Taigh na Deasbaid, he called it, and when the mason asked what a house needed such a hard question over its door for, he told him only that a door was as good a place as any to keep a question where every soul who came in or went out would have to pass beneath it.

The first pupils were few: two boys of Dùn thais itself, a girl from a farm within a morning's walk, and an old shepherd who had heard Coinneach teach once at a hearth twenty years before and had never stopped turning the memory over since. Coinneach set them a rule that first winter that the house kept ever after: that a question, once set, would be argued on the benches through the turning of a full year, by whoever chose to sit them, with no answer given from the master's own chair until the year was out, and sometimes not then. It was slow going. The old shepherd complained, more than once, that a man of his years had not much year left to spend arguing one question at a time, and Coinneach told him that this was precisely why the rule stood: that a question worth a year's arguing was worth more than an answer swallowed whole in an afternoon and never tasted again.

Word travelled slower than Coinneach had hoped, and faster than he had feared. Within a few years pupils were coming from as far as Caol bheag, three days' walk along the coast road and the carters' track that joined it, sent by parents who had heard, from a trader or a carter or a fisher who had heard it from someone else again, that there was a hall at Dùn thais where a hard question was argued properly and not merely swallowed. One of them, a fisherman's son of no more than sixteen, walked the whole distance with nothing but a sack of oatcakes and a borrowed cloak, and told Coinneach, when he was asked why he had come so far for one question among the schools' many, that his own village priest had set the question once at a naming feast and then refused, when pressed, to say any more about it, which the boy had taken, rightly enough, for a sign that there was more to be got at Dùn thais than at home. Coinneach kept him, as he kept every pupil who arrived hungry enough to walk that far for an argument rather than an answer, and the house grew a bench's width at a time through those years, never crowded, never empty, the register of questions kept in a plain board book that lay open on the master's table for any pupil to read back through. The fisherman's boy, whose name was Aonghas mac Nèill, stayed on past the year of his own first question and became, in time, the pupil Coinneach trusted to keep the register straight when his own eyes tired of the board in poor light, and it was Aonghas mac Nèill who once remarked that a stranger reading through those first years of questions set and argued would have found nothing in the book to trouble a single one of Na Seachd Taighean: whether a hall raised without a hearth-fire kept was truly a house; whether a debt owed to a dead man could be owed still to his line; whether a question, once answered wrongly for a generation, was better mended slowly or torn up and asked again from nothing. Good, solid questions, argued a full year each on the benches, and set down in the register with no more danger in them than a fair.

Beathag nic Ruairidh came down from the grove once in those years to see what he had built, walked the length of the hall with her hand trailing along the cold stone the way she might have tested a wall's mortar, and sat through a full evening's arguing without speaking a word of her own. She told him afterward, on the road back toward the grove, that he had built exactly the ground she had told him a question of that size wanted, fixed and ruled and patient, and that she had not doubted he would, only wondered how long he meant to let the house grow comfortable arguing safe things before he set it the one question it had truly been raised for. He told her, honestly, that he did not know, and she said that this, too, she had expected of him, and left it there.

## IV. Of the Open Disputation

Ten years the house stood before Coinneach set, at last, the question he had been carrying since the valley where the panthers cross and the fire at the herdswoman's hearth. He had turned it over on every road he had walked since, to the knock of his own stick, and it had grown no safer for the turning; if anything it had grown harder, for he was an old man now, two-and-sixty that winter, master of a house with a growing register and a growing name, and a man with a name to lose thinks longer before he risks it than a young man with nothing yet built to risk.

He spoke of it once, and only once, to Aonghas mac Nèill, the night before he set it, the two of them alone in the hall after the benches had emptied and the last candle had been let burn down rather than snuffed, for neither of them wanted to sit in the dark while they spoke of it. Aonghas asked him plainly whether he meant to ask it the next night, having guessed as much from the way his master had gone quiet over supper, and Coinneach told him he did, and that he had put it off eleven years past the year he had first meant to ask it, and could not honestly say the eleven years had made him any braver, only older. Aonghas asked him what he feared would come of the asking. Coinneach said that he did not know, which was true, and that this not-knowing was itself the whole reason the house existed, to hold a question open in a room built for nothing else until whatever came of it came. Aonghas said nothing further, banked the fire himself so his master would not have to rise and do it, and went to his own bed leaving Coinneach alone at the table with the register, where he sat a long while before he, too, went to sleep.

He stood up in his own hall to ask it in the winter of {{year:III-0075}}, on a night when the benches were as full as he had ever seen them and the air inside the door had gone thick with wet wool drying at the hearth and the smoke of tallow candles burned low. The night itself he had chosen without telling anyone so, waiting only until the hall was full enough that the asking, once made, could not be quietly forgotten by the few who happened to be present. He stood by the table with the register closed beneath his hand and let the hall settle before he spoke, the way he had taught every pupil who ever sat those benches to let a silence settle before an answer, and found, when the room was as still as it would get, that his own voice came out steadier than he had expected it to.

"We ask why the vein answers this hand and not that one," he said. "Then let us ask it of the hands that went. Were they the hands it answered best? And if they were, does it answer fewer now?"

No one spoke. A bench creaked as someone shifted on it. Somewhere at the back of the hall a young man laughed, once, a short startled sound with no mirth in it, and then thought better of it and went quiet, and two or three heads turned to find him and did not. Coinneach waited, as the rule of his own house bade every master wait, for someone on the benches to take the question up. When no one did, when the silence had stretched long enough that even the old shepherd, three years dead by then in the register's memory of him but still spoken of on those benches, would have called it answer enough, Coinneach sat down, opened the register, and wrote the question into it in his own hand, dated, unanswered, exactly as every other question the house had ever argued was written.

He had expected argument. He had not, in truth, expected the answer that came instead, which was silence of a different kind, carried out of the hall by whichever of the pupils that night had kin among the seven custodian houses and repeated, shire by shire, until it reached every one of the seven halls before the winter was out. The houses did not send anyone to Dùn thais to dispute the question, and did not send anyone to close the house, and raised, so far as the record of that year shows, no voice against Coinneach himself. What they did, each in its own hall and in its own time, was forbid the question at their own tables: told their households, plainly, that it was not to be asked at a meal where a head of the house sat, nor answered if some fool asked it despite the telling. It was remarked afterward, by more than one clerk who kept a house's accounts of such things, that the seven had forbidden deeds before, walls raised without leave and oaths sworn without witness and grain hoarded against a lean season, but never before a question, and that a forbidding of this new kind unsettled more than one house that had thought itself settled.

## V. Of the Queen's Death and the Road Home

The same year that Na Seachd Taighean carried their ban to their own tables, word reached Dùn thais, by the same slow chain of carters and traders that had once carried Coinneach's own fame there, that the shires of the northern plain around Baile Mòr mhòr had, in the last of {{year:III-0076}}, taken in so many families from the failing upland farms in the run of poor seasons just past that the town there had all but doubled within the memory of pupils not yet grown, and that its reeve had been forced to divide the common grazing anew to make room for flocks that had never grazed it before. Coinneach heard the report the way he heard most things that reached him at the house those years, sitting at his own table with the register open before him, and thought, though he set nothing of the thought down, that a country losing its upland farms one poor season at a time and a schools' house losing its old certainty one question at a time were perhaps not so far apart as the two reports, arriving on the same cart, made them sound. The carter who brought the news of Baile Mòr mhòr had walked the road himself that spring, on other business, and told Coinneach that the reeve there had stood in the mud of the newly divided grazing with a stick of his own, much like Coinneach's, and marked off shares with it for households that had never held a share of common land in their lives. The reeve had said to no one in particular, loud enough for the carter to hear it, that a shire could take in every family the hills sent down to it and still, in the end, run short of grass. Coinneach thought that a plainer way of saying what he himself had spent ten years working up the courage to say in one hard sentence, and told the carter so, and the carter, who had no idea what his master meant by it, agreed pleasantly and asked whether the house had any oatcakes to spare a hungry man before he took the road on north.

It was in the same season, before the year was fully out, that the harder news came: in {{year:III-0077}} Queen Sìleas nic Fhearchair had died at Cathair dhearg, in An Talla Dearg where she had reigned longer than any ruler the record kept before her, one-and-eighty years old and more than fifty of them spent on the crown her father had left her too young, by the houses' own custom, to take at once. Her son Uisdean, himself no young man by then, was crowned in her place before the season turned again, and the crown's scribes, in the way of scribes everywhere, wrote his crowning and his mother's death on the same board, one line after the other, as though the two were a single piece of news rather than a life's whole ending and a life's whole beginning set side by side. Coinneach had never sat in An Talla Dearg nor spoken a word to the queen in the whole of her long reign, but he had cut the firlot's mark once, idly, into the corner of his own writing-board years before, the way half the island had, and found, reading her death off a passing cart's board at the foot of his own hall's steps, that the news struck him harder than he would have expected news of a stranger to strike a man his age. A reign that had run the whole length of his own walking life, road-house to road-house, grove to grove, had ended in a single line on a board, and would be argued over by clerks for a season and then, like most such lines, be carried by the next thing worth carrying.

He walked out from Dùn thais alone that evening, along the carters' road he had walked a hundred times before the house was ever built, past An Leòmhann Buidhe's low lamp and on into the dark beyond it, an old man now with a stick worn smoother at the grip than it had been forty years before and a house behind him with his own question written into its register in his own hand, unanswered, and forbidden, by his own reckoning, at every table on the island but the one bench he had raised for it himself. Walking, he thought that he had wanted the question argued, not silenced, and had got instead a silence larger and better organised than any he had broken by asking it: a silence with Na Seachd Taighean behind it now instead of one old habit of not asking. A long while passed before he turned back toward the house and its one lit window, and he told no pupil, that night or any night after, what he had thought on the road, though more than one of them noticed, in the years that followed, that their master had grown a shade quieter than he had been before that winter, and put it down, as pupils will, to the plain count of years, and were only half right to.
