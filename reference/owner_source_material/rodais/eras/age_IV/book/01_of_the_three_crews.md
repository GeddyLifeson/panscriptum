# Of the Three Crews

*LE 1*

> *Cha chuir mi duine thairis, agus cha chùm mi duine air ais.*
> I will send no one over, and I will keep no one back.
>
> — the queen's word, as the harbour songs of Seann Skell give it

## I. Of the House as It Stood

In those days the island was a settled house, and it thought of itself so. The kings and queens of Dia-thìr kept their hall at Cathair dhearg on the Red Hill, above the place where the mining roads came down to the Abhainn dhomhain. The custodians kept the vein under the mountain from the vein-house above the diggings at Muileann dhearg, which was the heir of the Hall at Dùn ìseal, and they kept the custody-book there, in which every hearth that held the right to the coal was written. At Dùn ìseal itself Brìde's flame still burned in Talla na Lasrach, and the stone cup stood on the threshold where the lords of the shares had sworn their peace, and the Keepers came and went about it as their mothers had. The Small-Burning Law measured out the coloured coal hearth by hearth, a handful at a time, and it had not changed by a word since Fionnlagh Dall first filled the cup.

The orders of the Old Faith sat apart from one another at their own fires, and each thought itself the only true keeper. The dawn-hawk was flown at Cnoc bheag. The wolf-sworn kept their stone at Caol leathan. At Seann Skell in the west the harbour court swore its oaths over Manannan's cup with the scorpion on it, which breaks at a lie. The druid schools argued over the question of the stag, and the wood-watchers read the omens of the two horses in the trees. The ancestors were fed at the hearths of the east in the old way, as they always had been. Of the great shares into which the first king's kingdom had been parted there was little left but the stones on the bounds of the provinces, and the names of a few that the shires still answered to when the clerks were not listening.

And there was the sea. The sea was at the island's back, and nobody looked at it except to fish. The boats of Dia-thìr were sewn-plank coasters, their strakes stitched together with root and gut and caulked with moss and tallow, and they never slept a night out of sight of land. There was nothing out there that anyone wanted. The Otherworld lay west over the open water, the tellers said, Tìr nan Òg and Magh Meall, with Donn's house on its rock on the way; but the living did not go there, and it did not come to them.

In that settled house there was a queen, Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill, who had been crowned in the red hall at fifteen, eight years before, when her mother Mòr nic Coinnich died. She was twenty-three. She had a husband, Uilleam mac Iain, who was kind and liked hounds, and as yet no child, and a younger sister, Mòrag, who was married to a steady man of the western shore and had come up to the capital that winter to be near her. And she had a scribe, Murchadh mac Eòghainn, who had served her mother and wrote a close square hand that the clerks of the custody-book envied.

Into that house there came a rumour.

## II. Of a Word on the Quays

The word came first to the western quays in the deep of winter, in the moon of Faolach, when the storms walk up and down the coast and the boats lie drawn up above the tide-line. It came on {{date:III-0001}}. Where it came from, the tellers at the hearth do not know, and they have never much minded. No one could point to the boat that brought it. It was simply there one morning on the quay at Cuan shean, as a smell of smoke is there, and by evening everyone on the waterfront had heard it: that out over the open sea, farther than any coaster had ever gone, there was a coast, and no one lived on it who was like the Dia-thìrich.

The fishers of Cuan shean said it to one another as they mended nets in the lee of the rope-sheds. They said it at the fire in the long house where the crews ate. By the end of the moon they were saying it at Ros bheag, and at Inis àrsaidh, and up the coast at Ceann mhòr, where the north-west headlands run out into weather. Nobody said he had seen the coast. Everybody knew a man who knew a man.

Eachann mac Lachlainn was the skipper of a boat of six oars out of Cuan shean, a broad slow man with a grey beard cut square, who had fished the same water for thirty years and did not believe anything until he had it in his hands. He heard the tale three times in one day and laughed at it three times. Then his own sister's son, who crewed for him, said that he had been told it by a woman who had been told it by a pilot of Ros bheag, who had been blown off the land in the autumn gales and had come back after nine days with his water gone and his eyes strange; and that the pilot had said he saw the coast before the wind turned. Eachann went to find the pilot. The pilot, when he was found, said that he had seen nothing but grey water and his own death, and that anyone who said otherwise was a liar. But he said it too loudly, and he would not look at Eachann while he said it.

That was the way of it all winter. Every telling led back to another telling. Every man who might have been first said that it was not he.

## III. Of the Three Crews in the Red Hall

By the moon of Samhnas it had reached the capital. The crews came up the river road from the west, as crews did when the fishing was done, to sell salt fish and seal-oil at the autumn market below the Red Hill and drink the proceeds; and they brought the tale with them, and it got into the town and up the hill and through the door of the red hall, and on {{date:III-0002}} the queen sent for them.

Three crews came, one after another, over the course of that moon. The first was Eachann mac Lachlainn's of Cuan shean. The second was a crew of Ros bheag, whose skipper was a woman, Oighrig nic Raghnaill, lean and quick, with a scar along her jaw where a line had once parted. The third came late, from Ceann mhòr in the far north-west, three days on the road: a crew of hard, silent men under Tòmas mac Uilleim, who looked at the red hall as though it might sink.

The queen had them in at the board after the day's pleas, one crew each night, with the fire built up and bread and ale set out, as if they were guests and not witnesses. She sat at the head of the board in her chair of black oak. Her husband sat on her right, and on her left Murchadh mac Eòghainn with his board and his pen and a leaf of good skin. The lesser household ate at the lower board and listened. Mòrag was among them, with her husband, Aonghas mac Fhionnlaigh, beside her.

Eachann told it first, standing, with his cap crushed in his two hands. He told it plainly, as he would have told the price of fish. A coast lay out over the open water, he said, west and north of west, many days off. It was low and dark, with a white edge where the sea broke. There was smoke on it in places but no hall and no boat. The water there was colder than ours, and the fish were bigger. He had not seen it. He knew a man who had.

"Were you the first to hear it?" the queen said.

Eachann thought about that honestly, turning his cap. "No, lady," he said. "I was about the last."

Murchadh wrote it all, in his close square hand, and at the end of the telling he read it back aloud. Eachann listened with his head on one side, like a dog listening for a whistle, and then said, "Aye. That's it," and sat down and drank his ale, and was very much relieved.

The second night Oighrig nic Raghnaill told it otherwise. The coast lay north, she said, a long way north, past the north cape and beyond the Sea-Watcher's water, and it was high: cliffs, and a great river coming down white out of them, and forests running to the edge of the cliffs, taller than any oaks on the island. There was no smoke on it at all. The pilot who had seen it was her mother's cousin, and he had sworn to it on the cup at Seann Skell, and the cup had not broken. Would a man lie on the cup?

"Men have," said Uilleam mac Iain from the queen's right hand, mildly. He was fond of the harbour court's stories.

"Not that one," said Oighrig, and looked at him until he looked away.

"Were you the first to hear it?" said the queen.

"No, lady. Half of Ros bheag had it before me."

The third night Tòmas mac Uilleim of Ceann mhòr told it hardly at all. He stood with his men behind him like a wall and said, in the thick speech of the north-west, that the coast was east, over the grey water where the mist lies, and that you could smell it on certain mornings when the wind came round, a smell of pine and wet ash; and that the old men of Ceann mhòr had always said there was land there, and had said so to their sons since before there was a queen in Cathair dhearg or a hall on the Red Hill or a cup at Dùn ìseal. The rest of the island had only now caught up with them.

"Have you seen it?" the queen said.

"Nobody sees it. It's behind the mist."

"Were you the first to hear it?"

Tòmas looked at her for a while. "Lady," he said, "we never heard anything else."

When the three crews had gone and the fire was low, Murchadh sat at the end of the board and read over what he had written on the three nights. The queen sat and watched him. He was an old man and had written down a great deal in his life, and he did not often frown at his own work; but he frowned now. At last he dipped his pen and wrote a line at the foot of the leaf, below the third telling, and sanded it, and turned the leaf round on the board so that she could read it.

*They agree on the coast, and on nothing else.*

Beathag read it twice, and then smiled for the first time in three nights. "That's the truest thing on the leaf," she said.

"It is the only thing on the leaf I am sure of, lady," said Murchadh.

## IV. Of the Question at the Lower Board

There was one other thing said in the hall in those three nights, and Murchadh did not write it, because it was not a telling. The queen remembered it.

It was on the first night, when Eachann had finished and sat down and was drinking his ale with the sweat drying on him. The questions went round the board as they did after any plea. The queen's husband asked whether the fish were really bigger. A custodian who happened to be at court asked whether there were any coal on the far coast, and whether anyone had broken it to see what colour it was, and Eachann said he had no idea and that the man who had seen it was not the kind to go breaking stones. A lady of the household asked if the people were tall.

"There are no people, lady," said Eachann. "That's the whole of it. There's nobody there."

"There's smoke," someone said.

"Smoke is not people," said Eachann, and that was felt to be a good answer, and people laughed.

Then from the lower board Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill said, not loudly: "How many days' water would a boat need?"

Eachann turned round on his bench to see who had asked. He did not know her. "Days' water?"

"To get there. If a boat went. How much water would she carry, and for how many days, and how many would she carry, to get there and have some over."

There was a small silence at the upper board. It was not a question anyone had thought to ask. It belonged to a different kind of talk.

"No boat of ours could get there, lady," said Eachann. "A boat of ours would come apart in a night out of sight of land. She's sewn, you see. She works in a sea, all her seams working, and she wants to be pulled up on a beach every night and let dry. You couldn't get a boat of ours there."

"Then what kind of boat could?"

He opened his mouth and shut it. He was an honest man. "I don't know," he said. "Nobody's ever built one."

"No," said Mòrag. "Nobody has." And she let the talk go on to other things, and ate her bread, and did not speak again that night.

Beathag watched her sister over the rim of her cup. Mòrag was nineteen. She had been married two years, to a man of the western shore whom she had chosen herself and who adored her quietly and without fuss; she had a house at Inis àrsaidh, near Seann Skell, and no child yet; and she had the same face their mother had had, long and steady, which people called calm. Beathag had known her all her life, and knew better. She had seen that face at eight years old, looking at the ice on the mill-pond at Cathair dhearg in the moon of Faolach, the day before Mòrag walked out on it to see if it would bear her.

It had borne her. That was the trouble with Mòrag. Things mostly did.

## V. Of the Choosing Begun

After the three crews went home, the tale did not go home with them.

It went down the river road into the west, and up the coal-roads into the mountain country, and along the coast in every boat that put in anywhere. By the moon of Dubhlas, the black moon at the dark of the year, it was being told at hearths from Baile chrom in the north-west to Seann Vell in the south-east. At most hearths it was told as one tells of a wonder, and then the talk turned to the price of barley. At some it was not.

The Choosing began then, though nobody called it that, and it began without anyone beginning it. The chronicles call it a vote, and so it is written on {{date:III-0003}}; but there was no day in that winter on which anyone stood up and called for hands, and no roll yet, and no rule. There was only a question that began to be asked at certain hearths, quietly, after the children were asleep: *Would we go?* And the question, once asked at a hearth, did not go away from it again.

It was asked first, and most, on the west coast, where the tale had come in and where the people knew boats. At Inis àrsaidh, the fishing town at the mouth of the river below Seann Skell, two households said aloud in the drying-shed, before the whole town, that if a boat were ever made that could get there, they would be on it. The town laughed at them. The two households did not laugh, and after a while the town stopped. At Ros bheag they argued about it in the long house till the fire went out. At Cuan shean the rope-makers, who had nothing to do with the sea except to sell it rope, said the whole thing was fishermen's nonsense and that nobody would ever build such a boat. But they said it every night.

It was asked, too, in places nobody expected. The custodians of the vein, who held the right to the coal by apprenticeship and by the long training of the hands, turned out to have more in them of the wish to see a new thing than anyone had supposed. Perhaps it was because the coal had taught them that the world had more colours in it than most people could see. Perhaps it was only that they were bored. But when the tale came to the vein-house, some of the young custodians sat up half the night over it; and one of them, a man of the western diggings called Fearchar mac Ruairidh, went down to the capital in the moon of Dubhlas on some errand of the vein-house's and asked, while he was there, to be told the three tellings from Murchadh's leaf.

There was no rule in it, and that is the thing the tellers find hardest to believe. Nobody drew lots. No lord told his people to go, and no priest told them to stay. Those who wished to, went on wishing; those who did not, did not. They have tried every way to find a cause in it: a dearth, a quarrel, a prophecy, a sickness in the coal. There was none. The harvest had been good. The island was at peace. It was only that a door had been opened in a wall that everyone had thought was solid, and some people found they wanted to go through it.

## VI. Of Mòrag and the Skipper

Mòrag went down to Cuan shean before the moon was out.

She told her sister she was going to see to the house at Inis àrsaidh before the worst of the winter, which was true. With Aonghas and two servants she went down the river road, three short days in the rain, and saw to the house. And then she borrowed a pony and rode on down the coast the dozen miles to Cuan shean, alone, and found Eachann mac Lachlainn mending a net in the lee of a rope-shed, and sat down on an upturned tub beside him, and asked him the whole thing again.

He was surprised to see her, and then less surprised. He told it again. She asked him questions that nobody in the red hall had asked: how long the pilot of Ros bheag had been out; which way the wind had been; what the stars had been doing, if he had seen them; how a coaster's seams worked in a sea, and how long before they started, and whether a thicker plank would help, and what wood the planks were, and whether there was any wood on the island big enough for a boat that did not need sewing.

"Oak," said Eachann at last, after a long time. "If you wanted a boat that didn't need sewing. You'd want oak. Big oak, and a keel to her, like the old tellings say the Stone Kings' boats had, and you'd want a man who knew how to set a keel. There's nobody alive that knows that."

"Somebody might learn."

"Somebody might." He tied off his knot and bit the twine. "Lady, is the queen going to send ships?"

"The queen," said Mòrag, "has said nothing at all."

That was true too. Beathag had said nothing at all, all winter. She had heard the three tellings and had Murchadh write them, and then she had gone back to pleas of boundary ditches and grain dues and a quarrel between two millers of Muileann bhàn over a stream, and if anyone raised the far coast at her board she listened politely and changed the subject. The court had decided that she thought it nonsense. Mòrag knew that she did not.

"She'll have to say something," said Eachann. "Sooner or later. If folk start meaning it."

"Yes," said Mòrag. "She will." She stood up and thanked him and gave him a silver ring off her finger for his trouble, which he tried not to take and then took, and rode back to Inis àrsaidh in the dark with the rain behind her.

Aonghas was waiting up for her by the fire. He looked at her face when she came in, and did not ask where she had been.

"There's nobody alive that knows how to set a keel," she said, sitting down and holding out her wet hands to the fire.

"No," said Aonghas.

"But there's oak."

"There's oak," he agreed. He got up and fetched her a dry shawl and put it round her, and sat down again, and after a while he said: "Doire fhionn. Up the north road. The grove there has the biggest oaks on the island. My grandfather used to say you couldn't see the tops from the ground."

Mòrag turned and looked at him. He was looking at the fire.

"I've been thinking about it too," he said.

## VII. Of the Queen at Her Window

Beathag found out, of course. She had the reports of every reeve in the west, and a sister's movements are not a secret in a country where the queen's sister is known by sight in every harbour. A rider brought word to the red hall that the lady Mòrag had been at Cuan shean, and had sat an afternoon with the skipper Eachann mac Lachlainn, and had given him a ring.

She said nothing about it. When Mòrag came back up the river road at the turn of the year, before the moon of Faolach, the sisters ate together as they always did, and talked of the house at Inis àrsaidh and the damp in its north wall, and of Uilleam's hounds, one of which had had pups under the queen's own bed. Mòrag did not speak of the coast and Beathag did not ask.

But on the morning Mòrag and Aonghas were to go back west for good, Beathag stood at the window of her bower, which looked down over the yard of the red hall to the gate and the river road beyond it, and watched them go.

It was a clear cold morning with the frost still on the cobbles. Aonghas saw to the ponies. Mòrag stood in the middle of the yard in her grey travelling cloak and talked to someone. Beathag did not know him at first; then she did. It was Eachann mac Lachlainn, the skipper of Cuan shean, who had come up the river road again with some errand of his own, or with none, and had stopped at the hall to pay his respects.

They were not talking about anything. Beathag could see that from the window. Mòrag was asking something and Eachann was answering and drawing a shape in the air with his hand, long and pointed at both ends, and Mòrag was nodding. Then she laughed, and put her hand on the skipper's arm, and walked with him to the gate as a lady walks a friend to the gate. At the gate she stopped and looked out along the river road, west, toward the sea, as though she could see down it all the way to the coast; and stood so for a long moment, not moving, with the skipper beside her waiting to be dismissed.

Beathag had seen her sister look at a great many things. She had never seen her look at anything like that.

She stood at the window until the ponies had gone out through the gate and down the road and round the shoulder of the hill. Then she went down to the hall, where the day's pleas were waiting, and Murchadh with his board.

"Lady," said the old man, "the millers of Muileann bhàn are here again about the stream."

"Let them wait a little," said Beathag. She sat down in the black oak chair and looked at the empty board for a while. "Murchadh. The leaf of the three crews. You have it safe?"

"In the chest, lady."

"Good. Leave it there." She was silent again. Then she said: "Write nothing of the lady Mòrag's journey to Cuan shean. Nor of the skipper at the gate this morning. None of it is a matter for the roll."

Murchadh looked at her. He had served her mother, and he had known both sisters since they were in the cradle, and he was not a fool. "Lady," he said, "it is not a matter for the roll yet."

"No," said Beathag. "Yet." And she sent for the millers.

## VIII. Of What Stood at the End of the Year

So ended the first year of the age, though nobody on the island knew that an age had begun. The kings of Dia-thìr had always counted their years by their own reigns and by the harvests, and that year was written in the rolls of the red hall as the ninth year of Queen Beathag, a good year for barley and a middling one for fish, and in the custody-book at the vein-house as a year in which the handful was measured and burned at every lawful hearth and the galleries gave as they always gave.

Nothing had been built. No boat had been laid down, no oak felled, no roll opened, no word given. The queen had said nothing. The custodians had said nothing. The three crews had gone back to their fishing, and in the spring Oighrig nic Raghnaill's boat of Ros bheag took the best catch of herring on the west coast in ten years and she forgot, for a whole moon, to talk about anything else.

But there was a question at certain hearths now, and it would not go away. At Inis àrsaidh two households had said aloud that they would go. Up at the vein-house a young custodian had asked to hear the three tellings. On the beach at Cuan shean a skipper who had never believed anything until he held it had begun to draw, in the air and on the sand of the beach and on the planks of his own boat with a burnt stick, the shape of a hull that did not yet exist, long and pointed at both ends, and to stare at what he had drawn. And at Inis àrsaidh, near Seann Skell, the queen's sister and her husband sat up at night by the fire and talked about oak.
