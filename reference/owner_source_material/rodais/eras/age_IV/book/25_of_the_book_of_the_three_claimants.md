# Of the Book of the Three Claimants

*LE 605 – 650*

## I. Of the Old Boards

Seònaid nic Coinnich had counted the hours of the swearing at {{place:burg:19}} the way a woman counts a fever, waiting for it to break into something she could name, and when it broke into nothing more than a new king sitting where an old queen had sat, she found she did not know what to do with the counting afterward. She had been Catrìona's clerk for thirty-eight years. She did not know how to be anyone's clerk who was not Catrìona's.

Lachlann mac Iain kept her on, as his mother had told him to before she died, though he did it, Seònaid thought, the way a man keeps a piece of furniture whose use he has not yet worked out: with respect, and no particular purpose. She had a chest in the scribes' room already, and in the chest the beginning of a work the old queen had set her to two years before her death: an old man's account of the ford, a store-master's son's account of his father's board, a woman of Doire mhin's account of the cold. She had taken them down quickly, in the odd hours a court leaves a clerk, and had known even as she wrote them that they were only a beginning, three voices where a war had had thousands, and that a thing set down quickly is not the same as a thing set down whole.

For the better part of a year after the swearing she did nothing about the chest at all, copying grants and witnessing seals and sitting at the foot of a council table where the talk went on around her as if she were the table itself. It was in that year, doing work that did not matter, that she understood the three accounts she already held were not an ending but only a doorway, and that most of the men and women who had lived through the war she had lived through as a girl were dying, one by one, without anyone having gone back to ask them anything further.

She went down to {{place:burg:489}} in Bealltach on business that was not, in truth, business: a grant of the harbour dues that could as easily have gone by rider, and did not need a clerk to carry it in her own hand. The harbour-house stood where it had always stood, low and long above the strand, its lamp lit though it was midday, out of a habit no one now living could account for.

The scribe who kept the boards that year was a spare, careful man named Ailean mac Mhurchaidh, the fourth or fifth to hold the office since the roll was first opened, though he could not himself have said which; the line of scribes before him had not troubled to number themselves, only to keep the boards. He received her grant, read it, and set it aside without excitement, and when she lingered he seemed to understand, without being told, that she had not come for the grant at all.

"You'll want to see the old boards," he said. It was not a question.

He brought them out from the back room where they were kept, wrapped in oiled hide against the damp off the strand: a dozen of them, the wood gone dark with age and handling, the writing on the nearer ones still sharp, the writing on the far ones softened almost to shadow. He laid them on the counting-table one by one and let her look without saying anything more, which she thought afterward was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in that whole grey year.

She had heard the roll of the willing spoken of all her life, the way one hears of a flood or a fire that happened before one was born: as a fact, worn smooth by telling, with no particular weight to it. She had not understood, until she stood over the boards with her own hands not touching them, that it was a list of names, ordinary names, struck through in some places with a single decisive line and left standing in others, and that every stroke and every name left standing had once been a household deciding something in a room she would never see.

"That one," said Ailean, laying a finger an inch above a name near the board's edge, not touching the wood itself, "is the first that was struck. A widow, it's said, who asked the scribe of that day to scrape her name out so no one would know it had ever stood there. He wouldn't do it. Struck through, he told her, so a man may know a decision was made and unmade, but not scraped clean, as if it had never been made at all. Every board since has kept the rule."

Seònaid looked at the line through the name for a long while. She thought of her own father, who had never once, in the whole of his life, told her whether he had thought of going, in the days before she was born when a fifth of the island still could; he had died at Doire mhin with the coal withheld from his own hearth, and she had never asked him, and now there was no one left to ask.

"Who keeps the count of what's on these," she said at last, "against what's lost?"

"No one," said Ailean. "The boards are the count. When they're gone, so's the roll."

She rode back to {{place:burg:19}} with the grant delivered and forgotten, and a different errand taking its place in her without her having chosen it.

## II. Of the Witnesses

She began, in the winter of that year, with the men and women nearest to hand, which meant, at Cathair dhearg, old soldiers.

The first was a man named Fearchar Ciar, who had fought at the ford under Raghnall Ruadh's banner in the year the custody-book counted some three hundred dead, and who was, by his own account, one of perhaps a dozen still living who had stood in the water that day and come out the other side of it. He was very old, and his hands shook, and he would not at first say anything of use, only that the water had been cold and the noise had gone on longer than he expected and that he had lost a brother he did not care to speak of. Seònaid sat with him three afternoons running, asking nothing, only listening, before he told her, on the fourth, of the moment the line broke and which way the men had run and how he had known, from the sound alone before he saw it, that the ford had turned against his side.

She wrote it down that night by her own lamp, and read it back the next day, and found that half of what he had told her did not match the custody-book's account of the battle at all: the book gave the numbers and the day and the outcome, but nothing of which way a man had run, or what the water had sounded like, or that the fighting had gone on, by Fearchar's memory, well past the hour the book gave for its ending. She kept both. She did not think, setting the two accounts side by side on the same leaf, that either was a lie.

The second witness she sought out with more purpose than the first. Dòmhnall mac Eòghainn was the son of Eòghann mac Dhùghaill, who had been master of the store at the vein-house in the year of the cold hearths and had kept, until his own death, a board with the names of the shut-out shires written on it, face to the wall, never scraped clean. The son had inherited the board along with his father's office, and when Seònaid asked to see it he brought it out with the same unwillingness his father had shown in showing it to anyone, though he did, in the end, turn it to the light.

"He never told me why he kept it," Dòmhnall said. "I asked him once, when I was young enough to ask things plainly, and he said only that a man who has done a thing owes it to the doing to remember it, whether the thing was done to him or by him or only in front of him. He wasn't sure, by the end, which of the three it had been, for him."

She copied the names from the board, all of them, though she never learned what use, if any, she would make of a list of shires that the war itself had already listed elsewhere, in its own harder ledger of the dead. She copied it, she told herself, because Eòghann mac Dhùghaill had thought it worth keeping unscraped for the whole of his life, and she did not think it was her place to be the one who finally let it go.

The third witness found her rather than the other way about. A woman named Mairead nic Aonghais came to the red hall in Faolach of the following year, having walked, she said, most of the distance from Doire mhin on business of her own that she would not name, and having heard, in the market at Cathair dhearg, that the queen's old clerk was asking after the war and would hear anyone who had lived through the cold hearths. She was younger than Seònaid, near enough her own age that Seònaid understood, within a few sentences, that this was no witness from the battle lines at all, but a woman who had been a girl in the same winter Seònaid herself had been a girl, in the same charity at the same temple.

"I don't remember you," Mairead said, looking at her plainly across the table, "and you don't remember me, and I think that's as it should be. We were none of us paying much attention to each other, that winter. We were paying attention to the fire."

She told it as Seònaid herself would have told it, had anyone thought, at the time, to ask a child: the undyed wool cut down for a girl too big for last year's cloak and too small for a grown woman's; the particular hunger of the second week of a cold hearth, which was not the hunger of no food but the hunger of food that would not properly cook; the exact sound, which Seònaid had forgotten until Mairead named it, of ice forming overnight in a water-jar left too near a wall with nothing behind it to warm. Seònaid wrote almost nothing while Mairead spoke; she found, for the length of that afternoon, that she did not need to, because she already had it, had always had it, and had only needed someone else's voice to give her leave to write it down as more than her own single memory.

## III. Of the Book Made One Tale

By Ògach of the year the custody-book, copied at Cathair dhearg, gave as {{year:III-0117}}, Seònaid had more leaves of testimony than she had months left, by her own private reckoning, to make anything of them, and she understood, sitting one evening with the boards from Seann Skell, the store-master's tally, the old soldier's account and the temple-girl's memory all spread before her on the same table, that she had a choice to make that no one before her had had to make in quite the same way.

The custody-book told the war as the custody-book told everything: an entry for each year, a name, a number, a date, no year touching the year before or after it except by the plain fact of coming after. It was the record the crown trusted, and it was, she thought, turning its pages against her own gathered leaves, no more the war than a tally of a hearth's coal was the fire it had fed. She had read it a hundred times and could not, from it alone, have said which way Fearchar Ciar had run at the ford, or what a cold hearth had sounded like to a girl too young to be told why it was cold.

She resolved, in that year, to write it whole: not entry by entry, the way the book had always kept it, but as one telling, from the drowning of the two princes in the ford's spate through the swearing of the houses to Catrìona's son in a single day, with every witness's voice let into it where she had one, and the custody-book's plain word standing in where she had none. She did not know, beginning it, that no one on the island had ever done such a thing before; she found that out only afterward, when others told her so, and she was never sure, to the end of her life, whether it was a thing to be proud of or only a thing that happened to fall to her because she was the one still living who had both the leaves and the leisure.

She wrote through the spring and into the summer, at a table in a room off the red hall that had been Catrìona's own study, working by the queen's old inkstand because it was there and no one had thought to give it to anyone else. Lachlann came in on her once, unannounced, and stood reading over her shoulder longer than she was comfortable with before he said anything.

"My mother is in here," he said at last, meaning the pages, not the room.

"She is," said Seònaid. "So is your uncle Dùghall, and Raghnall Ruadh, and a good many people neither of us ever met."

"Will it say what she was like? Not the queen. The woman."

Seònaid considered the question longer than its length deserved. "It will say what she did," she said, "and what was done because of what she did, and what people who stood near her at the time believed she meant by it. I don't know that any book can do more than that for anyone, king or clerk."

Lachlann looked at the page a while longer, and then went out without answering, and she never learned whether he had found the answer wanting or had only had nothing further to ask.

It was in that same summer, while she worked, that word came down from Inis chrom on the south-west coast of a spotted fever that had begun among the fisher households and was killing, by the time the word reached Cathair dhearg, some forty of the town's people. The Fianna of the wolf-sworn stone, whose house stood nearest the sickness, went in among the dying where the town's own folk would not, and buried as many as they nursed, and lost, before the fever burned itself out, a number of their own that the messenger who brought the news could not say with any certainty, only that it was more than a few. Seònaid set down her pen for that fortnight and wrote nothing, not because the fever touched her book, but because it seemed to her, hearing it, an ill thing to be writing of an old war's dead while a new town's dead were still being carried out, and she judged, rightly or not, that the two griefs should not share an ink-well.

She took the pen up again after, and finished the last of the war's telling — the ford, the burned granary at Doire mhin, the forty days at the fouled wells, the list at the yard gate, the field below Muileann chrom, the swearing in a single day — in the autumn, on {{date:III-0117}}, and sat a long while afterward with the finished leaves in front of her, not reading them, only looking at the height of the pile, which seemed to her a strange measure for the whole of a war and yet was the only measure she had.

## IV. Of the Reading at the Red Hall

She did not live to see the book copied in any hand but her own. She died at {{place:burg:19}} in {{year:III-0117}}, not three years after the last leaf was written, of no illness anyone named as remarkable, only the ordinary failing of a body that had gone eighty-five years without once, so far as anyone could tell, resting from its work; and the last thing she is recorded to have said, to the clerk who sat with her, was that the boards at Seann Skell wanted a better roof over the back room, because the damp would have the far ones illegible within a generation if no one saw to it. It was, her sister said afterward, exactly the kind of thing she would say, and exactly the kind of thing no one else would have thought to say, and between the two the sister was not sure which she found harder to bear.

The book she left behind was read into the record at the red hall the following spring, at Lachlann's own order, by a clerk with a clearer hand than hers had become in her last years, while the king sat and listened without once, those present said, interrupting to correct a fact or soften a memory, though there were passages, concerning his own mother and his uncle and the man his grandfather's rivals had been, that another king might have wished told differently. When it was done he said only that it should be copied again, and kept, and that no copy of it was ever to be made shorter than the one before it, a ruling the vein-house's own clerks found strange, until they came, in later years, to understand that a shortened history is a history with someone's choosing already baked into it, and that Lachlann, whatever else might be said of him, had wanted the choosing to stay visible, the way a struck name stays visible on a board, rather than be scraped away in the name of tidiness.

Of the smaller things that had gone on around her in those same years the book itself says little, being a history of the war and not of the age, but the custody-book and the harbour rolls kept them as they kept everything. At {{place:burg:324}} on the southern shore, in the year the crown's scribes counted as {{year:III-0119}}, a village that had until then lived by its nets alone laid out beds for oysters in the shallow water of its own bay and found, within a few seasons, that the trade made it rich enough to sit as a shire seat, a thing its own elders would not at first believe of a food that had always before been gathered free off the rocks for anyone who cared to wade for it. And at {{place:burg:34}} in the north-east, where the crowds that gathered for the great feasts at the ancestor shrine had always gone home again with nothing more than what they had brought, the crowds of {{year:III-0119a}} began instead to hold a market on the three feast days, chiefly of honey and of the beeswax that fed the shrine's own lights, so that within a few years more wax was burned at ancestor shrines the length of the north-eastern coast than had ever been burned there before, and no one troubled to remember that a market had not always stood where the feasts stood.

Seònaid nic Coinnich never saw either. She had not gone to Doire shean since she was carried past it as a child on the road east from the burned granary at Doire mhin, and she had never in her life gone to Seann Tarr at all. What she had seen, in the last year given to her, was a book finished, and a king who did not flinch from it, and a scribe at a harbour-house eighty miles off who had shown her, without being asked twice, boards that no one before her had thought worth asking to see.

It is told at {{place:burg:19}} that when her book was read a second time, some years after the first reading, an old man at the back of the hall who had fought at the ford under a banner that lost said, when it was finished, that it was the truest account of the war he had heard, though it had made his own side out no better and no worse than the winning one, which he had not expected of a history written by the winning queen's own clerk. He was asked what he meant by truest. He said only that he had been there, and it sounded like being there, and that no one had thought to ask him that of any telling before.
