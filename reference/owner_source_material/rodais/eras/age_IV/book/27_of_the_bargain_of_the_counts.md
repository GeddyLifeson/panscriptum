# Of the Bargain of the Counts

*LE 700 – 769*

> *Cha do chuir mi cunntas air an fhìrinn, ach air an aonta.*
> I set no count upon the truth, only upon the agreement.
>
> — said to be Eòghann mac Pheadair's own saying, kept afterward at {{place:burg:491}}

## I. Of the Boy Set to the Tallies

Eòghann mac Pheadair was born in the shadow of the hill at {{place:burg:491}}, in one of the low stone houses the coal-breakers had raised at the hill's foot two generations before his own, and he grew up knowing the sound of the vein-house's bell for opening and closing the galleries before he knew his letters. His father cut coal in the lower workings and never rose from it, and would have set his son to the same labour without a second thought, had the boy not shown, at seven or eight, a memory for numbers that the men about him came to think worth more than a strong back. It was the old scribe of the vein-house, a stooped, dry man named Murchadh Beag who had kept the tallies of the seven houses' galleries for thirty years and complained of his eyes every winter, who first noticed the boy reciting back a whole day's cartage, load for load, without once looking at the board where Murchadh had chalked it; and it was Murchadh who went to the boy's father and asked, not for the first time in his life, whether a household might spare a son from the coal-face for a scribe's stool.

He was set to it at seven, sweeping the vein-house floor and mixing the ink before he was let touch a leaf, and Murchadh, who owned to no patience for a boy's questions during the day's proper work, would answer any question at all once the leaves were put away and the lamp burned only for the two of them: why a gallery's number changed when a house divided, why the same household might be entered twice in different years under different names, why the vein answered some galleries better than others though the custodians swore they worked them alike. Eòghann learned, by nine, to save his questions for that hour and no other, and Murchadh, who had buried a wife and outlived two apprentices before this one, came in time to keep the lamp burning a little longer than the work alone required.

By fourteen he could keep a day's tally as well as Murchadh himself, though not yet, the old man said, as fast, and it was that year, {{year:III-0125}}, that word came up from the coast to the vein-house of a thing Eòghann was set to enter on the general leaf without at first understanding why it mattered to anyone away from the coast: that {{place:burg:282}} had taken up half its rope-walks and sown the ground with barley. Murchadh explained it to him plainly enough, once, and never again, for he was not a man who liked to say a thing twice: the rope had been laid for the fleet, and after the fleet for the fishing, and the fishing had never once, in all the years since, needed cable of the old length; so half the walks stood idle years on end, and idle ground, on an island that had grown to more mouths than it once fed, was a waste no reeve would suffer forever. The boy asked whether it did not grieve the rope-walkers, to break up ground their own fathers had walked, twisting strand, for a fleet none of them had ever seen sail; Murchadh said that grief was not a scribe's business to enter on a leaf, only the fact of the barley and the year it was sown, and that a scribe who let himself grieve every trade the island outgrew would have no ink left, by forty, for anything the living still needed written down.

Eòghann wrote it as he was told, a line among many, and thought no more of it for years, save that he had, without being asked to, begun to notice that every leaf he copied told the same story from some other angle: a trade shrinking here, a family moving there, all of it circling back, sooner or later, to the fleet that had sailed before any man now living was born. He asked Murchadh once more, the winter he turned sixteen, whether the vein-house's own leaves said anything of where the fleet had gone, and Murchadh told him only that the custody-book held what it held and no more, and that a boy who wanted the rest of that story would have to go looking for it in the harbour songs, which Eòghann never did, having by then more tallies of his own to keep than hours left over for songs.

## II. Of the Wave and the Market

He was four-and-twenty, and had Murchadh's old stool as good as his own though the old man had not yet the grace to die and leave it to him outright, when the report reached {{place:burg:491}} of the wave at {{place:burg:364}} on the south coast: a great swell that rose with no storm behind it to explain it, ran the drying racks off the beach, and carried off two children with them before any hand on the shore could reach them, {{date:III-0126}}. Eòghann entered it on the general leaf as he entered every disaster that touched a household of the coal-right, cold and plain, the way Murchadh had taught him a scribe must enter grief if he meant to go on being of any use to the living: the names, the place, the day, and no word beyond what the day itself would bear. He asked Murchadh once, copying it fair, whether it did not seem a strange thing that a wave should rise from a flat sea with no wind in it, and Murchadh told him that the sea did what the sea did, and that a scribe who wanted an explanation for every strange thing the sea sent up would do better to find another trade, for there were more strange things in the tally-books of a coastal island than any one man's lifetime could account for. Cnoc chaol set its racks higher up the shore after, and the vein-house's leaf recorded, the following season, that the household of one of the drowned children had struck itself from its old gallery and taken up a new one further from the water, which Eòghann understood, better than Murchadh's explanation had made him understand the wave, without needing to be told.

It was some years later, near his thirty-first year, that the master of the vein-house sent him east on his own feet for the first time, to {{place:burg:136}}, the citadel on the straight eastern road, to stand witness for the seven houses when the crown's grant of a market there was read out and the boundary stones of its ground were set, {{date:III-0126a}}. He had never before gone further from {{place:burg:491}} than a day's walk would carry him, and the road east, which took the better part of a week, showed him a coast he had only ever known as names on a leaf: the fish and sail-cloth of the shore towns coming up to meet the furs and hides and dyes of the hill country, the dyers of a neighbouring town selling colours he had never seen a colour merchant in the west carry, all of it converging on a market ground that had, the week before he arrived, been nothing but rough pasture with four stones sunk in it to mark its corners.

He slept the nights of that journey in road-houses he had never had cause to know by name, at tables set with strangers who asked him, once they had his trade out of him, what the vein answered these days for a household's handful, a question he had fielded a hundred times from custodians and never once, until that week, from a stranger who owned no gallery at all and only wanted, it seemed, to know that the thing still burned steady somewhere behind the hills he had never travelled to. Oddly, he found he did not mind the asking nearly so much as Murchadh had always claimed to; there was a kind of easiness, he thought, in a question that wanted nothing written down after it.

He stood at the reading with the other witnesses, said the words the vein-house had sent him to say, and watched the first stalls go up the same afternoon, traders who had clearly been waiting only for the word to be spoken before they set their goods out, as though the market had existed in every mind but the law's for a year before the law caught up to it. A dyer of the hill towns spread lengths of cloth dyed a red Eòghann had no name for across a trestle not ten feet from where he stood, and told him, unasked, that the colour came from a root that grew nowhere lower than the high pastures and had never before had anywhere on the coast worth the carrying it to; and Eòghann, who wrote tallies all his working life and had rarely had cause to look twice at a colour, found himself standing longer at that trestle than his errand from the vein-house had any need of him to stand.

He walked home slower than he had walked out, by his own account afterward; the weariness of the road was in it, but more of it was that he found, for the first time in his life away from a stool and a leaf, that he wanted to look at things a while before he wrote them down.

## III. Of the Scribe of the Red Hall

He was eight-and-thirty, keeper of the vein-house's leaves in his own right by then, Murchadh eleven years in the ground and the stool unambiguously his own, when a rider came up from {{place:burg:19}} in {{date:III-0127}} to say that a scribe of the crown's own household was coming to Taigh na Fèithe on the red hall's business, and would the master of the vein-house grant her the custody-book for a day, or as many days as the matter took. The master, who had dealt with the crown's scribes before and found them, on the whole, no worse than any other visitor with a warrant, told Eòghann to give her whatever the book held and to mind his tongue about the rest of it, by which he meant the seven houses' own accounts, which were not the crown's business and never had been.

The scribe was a woman not much past thirty herself, Mòrag nic Ìomhair, who arrived on a hired horse with a satchel of her own leaves and the particular brisk courtesy of someone who had ridden the thirty-nine miles from {{place:burg:19}} expecting the vein-house to be difficult about its book and was pleasantly, visibly surprised to find it was not. She wanted, she told Eòghann plainly enough once the courtesies were done, to set the crown's reckoning of the years of the Diosal against the vein-house's own, for the red hall's clerks had lately found, comparing an old grant to an old debt in the same year's business, that the two offices' counts did not agree, and nobody at Cathair dhearg could say with any certainty which of them, if either, was right.

She had brought her own bread and a small hard cheese wrapped in cloth, meaning, she said, to trouble the vein-house's kitchen as little as its book, and ate it standing at the north window on her first evening, looking down the hill at the coal-breakers' town below, while Eòghann fetched the custody-book from its chest and blew the dust from its cover in a way he had not troubled to do for a visitor in years. Watching him set it down, she asked him whether the book had ever left that room in all the years it had been kept, and when he told her it had not, not once, she said that a book which had never once been carried out into weather to be tested against another book's claim was a strange thing to call a record at all, and that she meant no offence by saying so, only that it was, in her trade, a kind of loneliness she had come some distance to end.

The two of them spent the better part of three days at the long table under the vein-house's north window, each running a count forward from the sunwise turn by their own office's method, comparing tallies of burnings and gallery-openings and the ordinary business of years that each side had kept for its own purposes and never once, in all the years either count had been kept, set beside the other's. It was on the second evening, both of them ink-stained to the wrist and neither yet willing to say aloud what the columns had already told them, that Mòrag laid her leaf down next to his and said only that she counted the Diosal a year longer than his book did, and asked him plainly whether he could find the year in his own reckoning where the difference had crept in.

He could not, and told her so without pretending otherwise, which she said afterward was the thing that made her trust his count at all: that a scribe capable of admitting his own book might be wrong in a place he could not point to was worth more to her, in that hour, than one who would have sworn to every figure on the leaf sooner than own a gap in it. They went back through both counts a second time, by lamplight, the master of the vein-house looking in once and going away again without a word when he saw how the evening stood, and found, near the end of it, that the fault lay nowhere either of them could finally lay a finger on: a year, somewhere in the first generation after the turn, entered twice by one office's habit or once by the other's, so far back and so quietly done that no leaf either of them had brought could say which office had erred, or whether either truly had.

## IV. Of the Agreement

They settled it, in the end, not by proof, for neither count could furnish proof the other would accept, but by agreement, which Mòrag said, and Eòghann came in time to agree with her, was worth as much to the living as proof would have been and a good deal easier to come by. In the end they took the crown's count for the reckoning that would go forward from that day, since the crown's business touched more households across more shires than the vein-house's own and a single count serving the whole island was worth more than either office's pride in being first; and Eòghann wrote the vein-house's agreement to it himself, in his own hand, on a fresh leaf, with the master of the vein-house's mark set under his and Mòrag's own office's mark set under that, so that whoever read the leaf afterward would see two hands and two seals and no claim beyond what the leaf plainly said: that the offices had differed, and had agreed, and neither pretended the agreement was anything more than what it was.

The same days settled a second matter that had waited, unresolved, behind the first: the day of the year on which the rite of the empty harbour should properly fall, which the two counts had also placed a little apart from each other for the same buried reason, and which every harbour from {{place:burg:489}} round the coast had until then kept each after its own custom, a day or two either side of its neighbours, without much troubling over the difference. Mòrag proposed, and Eòghann saw no cause to refuse her, that the same agreement that fixed the count should fix the day, so that a rite meant to be kept by the whole coast together might, from that year forward, at least be kept on the same day by all of it, whatever the coast might privately go on believing about which count had been the truer one before the two offices sat down together.

She rode back to {{place:burg:19}} with her copy of the leaf in the same satchel she had brought empty, and Eòghann kept his in the custody-book, between two older leaves that had never expected to have a stranger's mark folded in among them. He said to the master of the vein-house, when he was asked how the crown's scribe had been to deal with, that she was a hard woman to argue a figure past and an easy one to agree a figure with, which the master took, correctly, for the highest compliment Eòghann had it in him to pay another scribe. The two offices wrote to each other now and again in the years that followed, mostly on ordinary business, but Eòghann kept, folded inside his own working leaves for the rest of his working life, the short note Mòrag sent him the winter after, saying only that the coast had kept the rite on the agreed day without a single harbour writing to complain of it, which she counted, she said, a better proof than either of their books had managed to furnish on their own.

## V. Of the Year the World Went On Without Him

He was an old man by the time the question the two of them had settled with a leaf and two seals was taken up again, at {{place:burg:17}} in the north, by younger minds who wanted more than agreement and asked, in a house of disputation the pupils of a mainland school had founded there, whether the vein answered any true count of years at all or only the custom of counting it; the house's first master was remembered afterward, by those who studied under him, for refusing every answer he was offered rather than for giving one of his own, and word of it reached the vein-house the way most northern news reached Eòghann in his last years, at second hand, by a rider glad of a fire and a cup and willing to talk while he warmed himself.

The rider, a young man who plainly relished the telling more than he understood the argument, said the pupils at {{place:burg:17}} had gone so far as to ask whether a gallery worked faithfully by a house with no claim of blood to the crown answered any worse than a gallery worked by the oldest of the seven, and that their master had refused that question along with every other, saying only that the vein did not trouble to answer a question it had never once been asked. Eòghann heard it out, said that a house built to refuse every answer sounded a restless sort of house to keep, and went back to the leaf in front of him, for whatever the pupils of {{place:burg:17}} chose to argue about the vein, the vein itself still wanted its tallies kept daily whether the argument was settled or not; though he owned to the rider, before the young man rode on the next morning, that he would not himself have minded hearing the master refuse a question or two in person, had his legs still been equal to a journey of that length.

That same year the crown at {{place:burg:19}} passed, without any disturbance that reached as far as the vein-house's own hill, from the fifth ruler of the house of Catrìona, a woman Eòghann had never once in his life laid eyes on, to her granddaughter, a girl of eighteen crowned {{date:III-0129}} in the same hall her grandmother had held for near fifty years; the seven houses sent their custodians down to {{place:burg:19}} to see the crowning done, as the compact required, though the master of the vein-house went in Eòghann's stead, for by then a journey of thirty-nine miles was more than his legs would answer for in a single season, and he said so to the master plainly, without the shame a younger man might have felt owning it. He heard the crowning described to him afterward, the neck-rings of the seven heads catching the hall's lamplight, the new queen's voice, they told him, steadier than her years had any right to make it, and thought, hearing it, less of the girl herself than of the leaf he had once carried between two seals: that whoever sat in the red hall, the count the leaf had fixed would go on running under her reign exactly as it had run under her grandmother's, needing no crowning of its own to keep it true.

It was that same year, too, that the bath-house over the hot springs below Muileann ruadh was raised again in dressed stone by the house that kept it, and opened its doors one day in seven to the sick of any household, whoever they were sworn to; and it was to that bath-house, in the last cold turn of that same year, that Eòghann let himself be carried on a cart he would have refused, a few years before, as an old man's fussing, his chest gone tight in a way no winter before it had ever quite managed.

The physicians of the house that kept the springs asked him, before they would let him into the water, the ordinary questions they asked every stranger carried to them: his years, his trade, what house his gallery answered to; and Eòghann, giving the answers, found he could not keep himself from adding, as no physician had asked him to, that he had once made an agreement with a woman of the crown's own household over a difference of a single year, and that the difference had never once, in all the years since, been proven either of them wrong. The physician, a woman near his own age who had plainly heard a hundred old men's stray remembrances at that basin's edge and had long since stopped being surprised by any of them, told him only that a single year, set beside the water he was about to sit in, which had come up out of the same mountain warm since long before either count of years began, was a small thing to have carried folded in his working papers for thirty winters, and helped him down the steps without further comment.

He sat in the warm water with men and women of houses he had spent his whole working life keeping separate on a leaf, coal-breakers and salt-carriers and a shepherd's widow from the hills, none of them minding, in that water, whose gallery any of the others answered to, and thought, before he let his eyes close a while against the steam, that it was a strange late lesson for a man who had given his whole life to counting differences to be taught, in a stone basin at the far end of it, how little the water troubled to keep them.

He kept, to the very end of that year, the leaf folded among his working papers where he had kept it since the evening he and Mòrag nic Ìomhair had set their two counts down side by side and found neither of them equal to proving the other wrong; and it was that leaf, and not the crowning at {{place:burg:19}} nor the disputation at {{place:burg:17}} nor even the warm water that had eased his chest a while, that he showed a young scribe he had lately taken to teaching his own trade, the way old Murchadh had once taught it to him, when the boy asked him, as boys will, what the finest thing was that he had done in a life spent mostly at a stool. Eòghann unfolded it for him without ceremony, the ink gone the brown of old tea and two seals still legible under it, and told him only that it was not the count itself the leaf was worth keeping for, since no leaf on the island could prove a count true past what its own scribe chose to believe, but the agreement written under it in two hands and sealed by two offices that had never, before that week, sat down at the same table; and that a boy who learned to write an honest agreement, he said, folding the leaf away again, would have learned more of the trade than any boy who only learned to write a stubborn count.
