# Of the Queen's Word and the Queen's Sister

*LE 2 – 3*

## I. Of the Bower in the Red Hall

Mòrag came up the river road to Cathair dhearg in the moon of Damhrach, when the stags were roaring on the hills above the Abhainn dhomhain and the first gales were stripping the rowans. She came alone but for one servant, and she did not go to the hall. She went straight up the back stair to her sister's bower, as she had done since they were girls, and knocked once and went in.

Beathag was at the window with a child's shirt in her lap, sewing a patch on the elbow. She did not look surprised.

"You've come about the roll," she said.

"I've come about you," said Mòrag. She took off her wet cloak and hung it on the peg by the door, where their mother's cloak had hung, and sat down on the chest under the window without being asked. "Half the west is waiting on you. The harbour-house at Seann Skell has five boards full, and it's full of people every day asking whether the queen will send them or forbid them. Nobody knows what to do. They say the crown will decide."

"And what do you say?"

"I say decide." Mòrag leaned forward. "Beathag. Either tell them to go, and put the crown behind it, and they'll build the ships in a year; or tell them it's folly, and forbid it, and it'll die away by Bealltainn. But say something. You can't leave them hanging on a board in a harbour-house, cutting their names in and out."

Beathag bit off her thread and looked at the patch, and smoothed it with her thumb.

"Do you know what the crown has done, in all the years since Goraidh Mòr?" she said at last. "It has kept the roads. It has kept the granaries. It has judged the boundary ditches and the millers' quarrels and the dues. It has never once sent anyone anywhere. It has never told a fisher where he might fish or a farmer where he might plough. And it has never kept a free man or woman where they did not wish to stay." She looked up. "You want me to begin now? With this? With the sea?"

"I want you to lead."

"I am leading. I am leading them to make up their own minds." Beathag set the shirt aside. "If I command it, every household that goes, goes because the queen said so. And when the sea takes them, and it will take some of them, Mòrag, you know that, their mothers will curse the red hall, and they'll be right to. And if I forbid it, every household that wanted to go and didn't will hate the crown for the rest of their lives, and teach their children to. And the ones who'd go anyway will go in the dark, in whatever they can find, and drown. No. The crown won't send, and it won't forbid."

Mòrag sat very still on the chest. "Then you'll let me go," she said.

It was the first time either of them had said it. Beathag looked at her sister for a long moment, and Mòrag saw her face change: not much, a tightening at the corners of the mouth, the look their mother used to get when a plea went against someone she liked.

"I'll let anyone go," Beathag said. "That's what I've just told you."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what I'm answering." Beathag stood up and went to the window and stood with her back to the room, looking down at the yard where the grooms were bringing in the horses. "I'm the queen, Mòrag. If I say one thing for the whole island and another for my sister, there's no word at all. It's just a queen with favourites. You're free. You're as free as the fisher of Inis àrsaidh who went in first. No freer. No less free."

"And if I go?"

Beathag did not turn round. "Then you'll go," she said. "And I'll be the queen who let her."

## II. Of the Word That Went Down the Road

The word went down the river road three days later, with a rider of the queen's household on a grey horse, in the autumn of {{year:III-0005}}. It was written by Murchadh mac Eòghainn on a leaf of skin and sealed with the queen's seal, and copies of it went to Cuan shean and Ros bheag and Ceann mhòr and Baile chrom and every other harbour of the west, and to the vein-house above Muileann dhearg.

It was not long. The custody-book gives it only in substance: that the crown would send no one across the water, and would keep no one back; that any household of Dia-thìr might go that wished to, and stay that wished to; that the crown would neither help nor hinder the building of ships, but would judge any quarrel that arose from it by the old law, as it judged all quarrels; and that the queen asked the people to think long and choose with their eyes open.

Mòrag rode down behind the rider. She had not meant to. She had meant to go home to Inis àrsaidh in a day or two, by easy stages, and let the word get there before her. But she stood at the gate of the red hall in the grey morning and watched the grey horse go down the road with the leaf in its rider's satchel, and she found she could not bear to be behind it. She called for her pony and went.

She rode the whole twenty-four miles in one day, in rain. The road went down out of the hills along the river and through the wet fields of the lowland, past farms where the barley stubble stood grey and drowned and the cattle stood with their backs to the weather, and then along the edge of the marshes toward the coast; and all the way the rider was ahead of her, a grey shape in the grey rain, never more than a mile off and never less. She did not try to catch him up. At dusk she came down the last hill with the pony blowing, and saw the lamps of Seann Skell below her, and the river pool, and the long dark line of the west quay, and the one lit window of the harbour-house at its head.

## III. Of the Reading at the Door

The rider had reached the harbour-house before her and the reading had begun. Half the town was on the quay. They stood in the rain in a crowd round the door, with their hoods up and their shoulders hunched, and the harbour scribe Calum mac Nèill stood in the doorway with the lamp behind him and the queen's leaf in his hands, and read it aloud.

He read it twice, because the people at the back had not heard. Mòrag sat on the pony at the back of the crowd and heard it the second time, the plain words in the scribe's dry voice, and watched the faces.

She had thought they would cheer. Some of them did, a few young men near the front who had been on the boards from the first week. But most of the crowd did not. They stood in the rain and listened, and when Calum finished and lowered the leaf there was a long silence, and then a low sound that went through the crowd like wind through barley, and she understood that it was not joy. They had wanted the queen to decide for them. And she had handed it back to them, every household of them, to decide for themselves.

A woman near Mòrag said, to nobody, "Well, that's no help," and went home.

But the crowd did not go home, most of it. It stayed on the quay in the rain, talking in knots, well after Calum had taken the leaf in and hung it on a peg beside the roll. And someone in one of the knots began to say the word over, in a sing-song, as the fishers of the west turn anything into a song if they say it often enough; and someone else took it up; and by the time Mòrag had led her pony down to the water's edge to drink, there were a dozen voices on the quay saying it together, not quite in tune:

> *Cha chuir mi duine thairis, agus cha chùm mi duine air ais.*
> I will send no one over, and I will keep no one back.

The queen had never said it in those words. The harbour songs of Seann Skell have said it so ever since, and they are younger than the custody-book and less careful, and nobody on the west coast has ever wished to hear it any other way.

## IV. Of Aonghas at Inis àrsaidh

She did not go into the harbour-house that night. She rode on the three miles to Inis àrsaidh in the dark and came into her own house soaked to the skin, and Aonghas was up, sitting by the fire, as he always was when she was away.

He had heard. Everybody had heard by then; the word had run down the three miles of the shore road faster than a pony. He looked at her as she stood dripping in the doorway, and got up and took her cloak, and put her in the chair by the fire and put a cup of hot ale in her hands and knelt and pulled her boots off, and only then said, "Well?"

"She won't send and she won't forbid," Mòrag said.

"I heard."

"She won't say anything to me that she doesn't say to the fisher." Mòrag drank. Her hands were shaking with cold. "I asked her straight. I asked her, if I go. And she said, then you'll go."

Aonghas sat back on his heels and looked up at her in the firelight. He was a big fair man, slow-spoken, a farmer of the good land up the river behind the town, who had married the queen's sister to the astonishment of the whole west and had never once, in five years, behaved as if it were anything remarkable.

"So," he said. "Do we go?"

She looked at him. She had lain awake over this for a year and a half. She had rehearsed every argument, every way of saying it, how she would put it to him and what he would say and what she would answer. She had been ready for a fight.

"I haven't finished," she said. "I was going to ask you. It was all ready. The oak, and the keel, and how nobody knows, and how somebody will have to learn, and that the queen won't stop us, and that we'd go together or not at all, because the harbour scribe writes a household whole or not..."

"Yes," said Aonghas.

"...and that I know it's your father's land, and your mother's grave behind the house, and..."

"Mòrag," said Aonghas. "Yes."

She stopped. He was still kneeling on the hearth with her wet boots in his hands, looking up at her with his slow patient face, and she saw that he had decided long before, perhaps on the night by this same fire when he had told her about the oaks of Doire fhionn, and had been waiting, as he always waited, for her to catch up.

She put the cup down and began to laugh, and then to cry, and he got up off the hearth and held her.

## V. Of the Knife

She went into the harbour-house the next afternoon, alone. Aonghas had offered to come. She had said that the scribe would know him, and that the household was the household whether one of them stood there or both, and that she wanted to do this herself. He had understood.

It was raining again. The harbour-house was full, as it was every day now, and the bench by the door was full of old men, and the floor was full of people reading the boards, and at the far end under the window Calum mac Nèill sat at his board with his knife. She came in out of the rain with her hood up, and some of the old men on the bench looked at her and did not know her, and then one did, and nudged the next.

She stood in front of the board. Calum looked up. She saw him know her, before she had lowered her hood; she saw the knowing go through his face and out again, and his face go smooth and careful.

"Lady," he said.

"I wish my household entered," said Mòrag. "The household of Aonghas mac Fhionnlaigh, of Inis àrsaidh. Two of us. Aonghas mac Fhionnlaigh, and Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill."

The harbour-house had gone quiet behind her. Without looking round, and without sitting, though there was a stool, she stood and watched Calum take a fresh board from the rack, and plane it, and set the knife to it. He cut slowly, and very well.

She spelled it out for him. She knew he did not need it. Every scribe in the west knew how to spell the name of the queen's sister; it was on half the leaves that came down from the red hall. But she spelled it all the same, letter by letter, in a clear voice that carried to the back of the house, *M, ò, r, a, g*, and watched every letter go into the wood, and then *nic Dhòmhnaill*, all of it; and then her husband's name above it, because he was the head of the household on the board; and then the line down the left side that joined them. It seemed to her that the knife was a long time about it. When the last stroke was cut and the shaving had curled away and dropped to the floor, she let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

"Thank you," she said.

Calum looked up at her. He did not say anything. Whatever he thought, he kept it behind his careful face.

She pulled her hood up again and went out into the rain. Behind her, as the door shut, she heard the harbour-house begin to talk.

He wrote nothing beside the entry. There was no rule that said he should, and he was not a man to make one. The chroniclers have written a great deal about it since, and none of them was in the room.

## VI. Of the Vein-House above Muileann dhearg

Sixty miles east, up in the mountain country, the copy of the queen's word came to the vein-house by the coal-road, three days after it was read at Seann Skell, and the custodians sat down to think what it meant for them.

The vein-house stood on a shoulder of the hills above the diggings at Muileann dhearg, where the galleries went into the mountain. It was a long hall of dark stone, older than the queen's line, with store-houses round a yard and the carriers' gate on the downhill side. In its inner room, in a niche lit by a lamp that was never let go out, stood Crom's stone, the slab that had lain on the first break of the vein. In the room beyond it, on a table of black oak, lay the custody-book, which had been kept since the Holy Age, and in which every hearth on the island that held the right to the coal was written. The vein-house was heir to the Hall at Dùn ìseal, where Brìde's flame had first been bound; the Keepers still kept the flame there, and the stone cup on the threshold, but it was here, above the galleries, that the handful was measured and the book was kept, and it was here that the custodians met.

The master of the vein-house in those years was Eòghann mac Mhuirich, an old man of the Keepers' blood, dry and thin and very patient, who had spent sixty years in the service of the vein and had never once, the custodians said, been seen to hurry. He read the queen's word through at the table, twice, by the light of the lamp before Crom's stone, and then he folded it and said:

"We had better count."

## VII. Of the Question of the Coal

They did not count at once. First they argued, through the whole of that winter and into the spring, over a question that the queen's word did not touch and that no one at Seann Skell had thought to ask.

The right to the coal was earned. That was the Law as it had stood since the Holy Age. A custodian served his apprenticeship at the vein, seven years under a master, learning the galleries and the colours and the measure; and a hearth that held the right to the handful held it because its people had kept the coal and the Law, generation after generation. The right lived in the hands that had learned it. It was written in the custody-book against the hearth, and the vein-house measured to it.

Then what happened to the right when the hearth went over the sea?

Some of the custodians said it went with them. The right was in the hands, and the hands would be the same hands on the far coast as on this one. Others said that the right was to the vein, and the vein was here; a right to a thing on the far side of the sea was no right at all, and the hearth should be struck from the book on the day it sailed. And some, the old ones mostly, said nothing, but looked at each other; because there was a third question under the other two, and none of them wished to be the first to ask it. If the right lived in the hands, and the hands went away, did the vein lose something? Did it answer fewer?

Fearchar mac Ruairidh was the loudest of those who said the right went with the hearth. He was a custodian of the western diggings, a big, black-browed man of thirty with a laugh that carried through stone walls, who had been apprenticed at twelve and had spent eighteen years in the galleries and could name every colour of broken coal by feel in the dark. He had heard the three tellings from Murchadh's own leaf, the winter before last, and had been talking about them ever since.

"The coal knows the hands," he said, at the long table in the hall, in the moon of Gearras, with the wind hammering on the shutters. "You all know it. You've all felt it. You put your hand on a broken face and it's warm to you and cold to the lad beside you. That's not in the book. That's not in the vein. That's in us. Wherever we go, it goes."

"Then take your hands over the sea, Fearchar," said an old custodian of the southern galleries, "and see if the far coast has a vein in it for them to warm."

There was laughter. Fearchar laughed too. But Eòghann mac Mhuirich, at the head of the table, did not laugh, and after a while the others noticed, and stopped.

"We'll count," said Eòghann. "We'll count first. Then we'll know what we're arguing about."

## VIII. Of the Count Made Twice

They sent to Seann Skell for a copy of the roll. The harbour scribe sent it willingly, board by board, copied onto skin by his son, with a small mark like a flame beside each household whose hearth took the handful. It came up the coal-road in the spring, and more boards after it, for the roll went on growing all that year, and in the high summer, in the moon of Lùghnas, the custodians sat down at the long table with the copy of the roll on one side and the custody-book on the other, and counted.

It took them nine days. They went hearth by hearth, household by household, the roll's name against the book's name, reading aloud and ticking. Eòghann sat at the head of the table and did not count; he listened. Fearchar read the roll aloud, because he had the loudest voice. A young custodian of the capital's diggings read the book. Two clerks ticked.

On the ninth day, in the evening, the clerks added their ticks and looked at each other, and added them again, and gave the figure to Eòghann on a slip of skin.

He read it, and read it again, and looked up at the custodians round the table, who were watching him.

"Nearly one in five," he said.

Nobody spoke. One hearth in five of all the hearths on the island that held the right to the coal was on the roll of the willing. Not one in fifty, which was what most of them had expected; not one in twenty, which was what the pessimists had said. Nearly one in five. Fishers, farmers, weavers, boat-wrights; and custodians, many custodians, whose hands the vein had answered, whose apprenticeship had been seven years at these same galleries; and the queen's own sister.

"That's wrong," said someone.

"Count it again," said Eòghann.

They counted it again. It took them seven more days, because they were faster now and angrier. They changed the readers round, so that no one could say a tired voice had skipped a name. Fearchar read the book this time, and the young custodian from the capital read the roll. The clerks ticked.

On the sixteenth day, on {{date:III-0006}}, they gave Eòghann the second slip. It was the same figure, within a hearth or two.

He sat and looked at it for a long time. Then he took his pen and wrote it in the custody-book, in his own old careful hand, under the day; and beside it he wrote that the count had been made twice, because the first figure had not been believed. By whom, he did not say; there was no need.

## IX. Of Fearchar's Own Name

When he had finished writing, Eòghann laid down the pen and said, without looking up: "Is there anything else?"

There was a silence. Then Fearchar mac Ruairidh stood up from the long table.

He had not said anything for sixteen days that was not a name read aloud. He stood with his big hands flat on the table and his black brows drawn down, and he looked, for once, like a man who did not know how loud to be.

"My household is on the roll," he said. "Fearchar mac Ruairidh, of the western diggings, and his wife and his two daughters. It's on the fourth board. I went down to Seann Skell in the moon of Samhnas and had it cut. I counted my own name twice, reading it, and ticked it, and said nothing." He swallowed. "I should have said before we began, and didn't. The figure was what I wanted first. I wanted to know whether I was one of a handful of fools or one of a great many." He looked round the table. "Now I know."

Nobody said anything. The young custodian from the capital was staring at him. The old custodian of the southern galleries, who had laughed at him in Gearras, had put his face in his hands.

Eòghann mac Mhuirich did not look up from the book. He sat with the pen laid down beside it and his old dry hands folded on the table in front of him, and he looked at the figure he had just written as though it were a stranger.

"Sit down, Fearchar," he said at last, quite gently.

Fearchar sat.

The master of the vein-house turned the leaf of the custody-book, very carefully, so as not to smudge the ink, and wrote the date at the head of the next leaf, and the words *the handful for the season*, in the same hand as before; and began to read the names of the hearths aloud, one after another, as he had read them every season for sixty years, for the measuring. And the custodians round the table, one by one, took up their pens and followed him, and none of them looked at Fearchar, and none of them looked at each other, and the lamp before Crom's stone in the next room burned steady and small, as it always had.
