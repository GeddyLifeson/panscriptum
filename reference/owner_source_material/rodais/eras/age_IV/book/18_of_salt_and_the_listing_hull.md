# Of Salt and the Listing Hull

*LE 344 – 394*

## I. Of the Hull that Would Not Ride True

Ruairidh mac Artair had been a merchant of {{place:burg:55}} four-and-forty years old and prosperous in a small, steady way, buying wool in the western shires and selling it on down the coast, when he first began to say to anyone who would listen that a deep hull, built as the fleet's own hulls had once been built, would carry twice the wool of any sewn-plank coaster and outrun the weather besides, and that a man willing to pay for the building of one stood to make back its cost inside three seasons. He had never himself seen a deep-keeled hull afloat, the last of them long since broken up or rotted at their moorings, but he had heard the old tellings of the fleet from his grandmother as a boy, and he had a merchant's confidence that a thing once done could be done again by any man willing to pay what the doing cost.

He found wrights enough willing to try it, the coast still keeping a handful of men who called themselves shipwrights though none now living had built anything deeper than a coaster, and he laid out silver and salt and a season's patience on a keel set down on the strand below the town in the manner, as near as the wrights could reconstruct it from the shapes of old broken timbers still lying half sunk in the shallows, of the hulls Alasdair Saor had once laid there himself. What none of them had, and what none of them thought to ask after until the hull stood built and ready for the water, was the particular scarfing of the keel's long timbers end to end that had let Alasdair's own hulls flex a little in a following sea rather than fight it; that lore had gone into the ground with the last of his fellowship's journeymen, unwritten, a thing carried in the hand rather than on hide, and no one now living had the hand it had been carried in.

The hull went down the strand on a morning in {{year:III-0078}} with half the town come to watch, Ruairidh himself standing at the water's edge with his arms folded and his chin up, and it took the water well enough for the length of ten heartbeats before it began, very slowly, to lean. It did not lie down as the Fhaoileag had once lain down in the Abhainn naomh, with men drowning under it; it only leaned, and kept leaning, past what any hull under sail could be trusted to right itself from, until the wrights standing knee-deep beside it called out that she would not ride true loaded nor light, and that was the whole of it. No one died. Ruairidh stood a long while looking at his hull canted over in the shallows like a beast that had lain down to die on its own ground, and then he told the wrights, in a voice that surprised those who heard it by its steadiness, to break her up for the timber, since good oak was good oak whatever shape it had been cut to and he did not mean to lose the whole of what he had spent on her.

One of the wrights, an old man named Coll who had spent forty years building nothing deeper than coasters and had taken the work on Ruairidh's silver more from curiosity than from any confidence in his own hand, stood a long while beside the leaning hull before the breaking-up began, running his palm along the keel's underside where it lay bared to the air, and said, to no one in particular, that the wood was as good as any he had ever cut, and that the fault lay somewhere he could not put a name to, a way of setting one timber against the next that his own master had never had to teach him because no one in his own lifetime had ever asked a keel to do more than a coaster's keel needed to do. He said it again, differently, to Ruairidh himself, when the merchant came to settle the wrights' wages: that a man could see a joint had failed without being able to see, in the failed joint, the shape of the joint that would not have failed, and that this not-seeing was a harder thing to carry away from a season's work than any silver lost. Ruairidh paid him in full and said nothing to that, judging, as he judged most things by then, that there was no profit in an old wright's grief over a craft that had gone out of any living hand before either of them was born.

She came apart over the following fortnight, plank by plank, and the oak of her went into byres and cart-frames and, in the end, a good deal of it into the strengthening of a granary roof at Inis àrsaidh that stood the better for it for years after. Children of the town came down for a season afterward to play among the ribs of her before the last of the timber was carted off, running the length of what had been her spine and counting her frames aloud for the game of it, losing the count each time and starting again, meaning nothing by it beyond the game itself. Ruairidh never spoke of building another. He went back to buying wool and selling it on by coaster as he had before, a little poorer and, those who knew him said, a little quieter than the man who had stood at the water's edge that morning with his chin up, and it was some years before anyone else on the coast thought to try a deep hull again, and longer still before anyone succeeded.

## II. Of the Bridge-Keeper's Boyhood and the Springs on the Flats

Two hundred miles and more to the north-east, on the river above {{place:burg:76}}, a boy named Iain mac Ùisdein kept his father's toll at the stone crossing that the district still called by the name of the mason who had raised it generations before, Drochaid Ros fhionn, though the man's own hearth-sign, cut sharp into the keystone, was by then the only thing of him anyone remembered. Iain was nine years old the first year he was set to sit at the bridge's north end with the tally-stick and watch the carts go over, and he grew up on that bridge the way another boy grows up on a farm or at a forge, knowing the sound of every cart's wheels before he saw them and the particular groan the third plank from the south end gave under a laden ox-cart that it never gave under an empty one.

The district he watched over was a poor one in those years, a scatter of farms and turf-cutters' holdings on the flats above the river, and the bridge itself carried little enough traffic that his father had time, most days, to teach him his letters between carts, holding the tally-book open on his knee and making the boy copy out the day's crossings twice, once for practice and once for the true record. The bridge itself Iain came to know the way another boy knows a field he has walked every day of his life: the particular lean of the north parapet where a flood two generations back had shifted a single course of stone and no mason since had thought it worth the cost of setting right; the swallows that nested each spring under the third arch and would not be driven off however many carts crossed above them; the deep pool below the second pier where the district's boys swam in Teasach and where his own father had taught him, holding him under the arms in water that never quite lost its chill even at the height of summer. He learned to read a carter's face before the cart came into sight, whether the man was one who paid his toll without argument or one who would try, as two or three along that stretch of road always tried, to talk the toll down by a claim that his load was lighter than it looked, and his father taught him to answer such men by walking round the cart once, slowly, and naming its weight within a stone or two by eye alone, a trick that shamed more than one careless dealer into paying what he owed without further words wasted on either side.

It was on the flats below the bridge, in the summer Iain turned fifteen, that a farmer digging a new drainage ditch struck water that came up warm to the hand and tasted, when a curious neighbour dipped a finger in it, unmistakably of salt.

Word of it went first to Ros fhionn's own reeve and from him, within the season, to men who understood such things better than a farmer digging a ditch, and by the following spring, in {{year:III-0079}}, the flats above the town held a dozen shallow pans laid out to catch the brine and let the sun and wind do the rest, tended by the same farmers and turf-cutters who had dug for nothing better than dry ground for their oats. Iain, still sitting his father's bridge most days, watched the first carts of white salt go over southward that same year, and it did not occur to him then, a boy of fifteen with a tally-stick and no thought beyond the next laden ox-cart, that he was watching the beginning of the one thing that would, before he was an old man himself, make the bridge under his feet worth more than any bridge on that stretch of river had ever been worth before.

## III. Of a King's Death Far to the South and a Cloth Worn Nearer Home

The tidings that reached the bridge that same year, carried by a rider bound for the shire seat who stopped only long enough to water his horse and pass on what he carried, concerned nothing nearer than Cathair dhearg: that Uisdean mac Dhùghaill, who had held the red hall since he was a man of four-and-fifty and was himself the son of Sìleas nic Fhearchair who had reigned sixty-six years before him, had died in his two-and-eightieth year, and that his own son, Uisdean mac Uisdein, a man already thirty years old and long past needing any regency to hold the hall for him, had been crowned in his place without dispute. Iain's father set the tidings down in the margin of the tally-book, as he set down anything worth remembering that the bridge had no better place to keep, and the two of them talked of it, father and son, for the length of one evening's supper, and then talked of it no more, the red hall and its business being to a bridge-keeper's household on the northern flats a thing that happened, as the seasons happened, somewhere too far off to be altered by any word either of them might have had about it.

Nearer home, and to Iain's own family a thing of more weight, the weavers of {{place:burg:131}} had by then been some years at a heavy cloth, undyed, woven close enough to shed a season's rain without oiling, that had begun to spread out from Baile gharbh across the whole of the north-east as the common cloak of anyone who worked out of doors in the wet months. Iain's mother had one herself, bought at the Ros fhionn market from a Baile gharbh carter for less than a dyed cloak would have cost and worth twice the price for the rain it turned. It was in that same undyed grey, {{date:III-0080}} tells, that the district first began to mark its mourning, a household that had lost someone wearing the plain wool rather than any dyed thing until the season turned, in the manner, the old women of the flats said, of the white sorrow the whole island had once worn together for the fleet, though none of them now living had seen that mourning themselves and knew of it only as their own mothers had told it to them. Iain's grandmother died that winter, and his mother wore the grey cloak a full season after, and it was the first mourning the boy himself remembered closely enough to carry with him, the particular grey of an ordinary rain-cloak turned, by nothing more than a household's own silence in it, into something else.

## IV. Of the Handful from Each Load

By the time Iain had his father's own place at the bridge, the salt of Ros fhionn had become, as the flats' own saying had it, worth more than its weight in argument, for there was scarcely a load crossed south over Drochaid Ros fhionn in those years that was not salt or bound for the salting of something, and the district's shire seat, casting about for some due it might fairly lay on a trade that had made the flats rich almost overnight, settled at last, {{year:III-0081}}, on granting the bridge's own keeper a handful from every load that crossed, to be kept and sold on the bridge-keeper's own account in place of any coin toll, salt being worth more on that stretch of river than any coin a farmer was likely to carry.

Iain kept the handful honestly, by his own strict reckoning of what a handful was, never grasping a second time at a sack that had already given up its share, and it became known along the whole road that Ros fhionn's bridge-keeper took no more than his due and no less, which was, among carters who had dealt with less scrupulous men at other crossings, a kind of fame. One carter, a heavy-set woman named Beitris who ran salt down to the shire seat twice a season and had once had a whole extra fistful taken from her at a crossing farther south by a keeper who closed his hand quick and opened it slow, took to stopping at Iain's bridge a full quarter-hour longer than her business required, simply, she told him once, to watch him take his handful in the open, in full view of her and anyone else on the road, and count it back into his own box before her eyes, because it did a body good, after the other crossing, to be robbed honestly for once, in plain daylight, by a man who wanted nothing from her but what the shire seat had granted him. He built, in the second year of the toll, a stout box of oak beside his stool at the bridge's north end, iron-bound against damp, and it filled faster than he had thought any box could fill, so that within a handful of years the carters along the road had begun to say, half in jest and half not, that Iain's salt-box held more against a hard winter than some whole villages of the flats could claim to have laid by, and the saying outlived the man who first made it and passed, as such sayings do, into the common speech of the district.

## V. Of the Wells that Cracked at Cill ghlas

In {{year:III-0082}} the earth moved, one night in early autumn, under the hills about {{place:burg:123}}, a small village some way east of the bridge on the road toward the vein-house's own country, and the shaking, brief as it was, brought down a byre and cracked three of the village's wells so that their water ran brown for a season after and had to be carried in from farther off until the ground settled and the springs found their own course again. The custodians of the vein-house, hearing of it within days, as any tremor near their own hill was a thing they made it their business to hear of quickly, sent men down into the galleries below Cill ghlas to see what the shaking had done there, and found, to the relief of everyone from the master of the vein-house down to the least of the diggers, that the galleries stood exactly as they had stood before, not a prop shifted nor a wall so much as scored, the whole force of the tremor having spent itself on the village's own wells and roofs and left the deep workings, driven true and shored well by generations of custodian craft, untouched.

Word of it reached Drochaid Ros fhionn within the week, as word of anything touching the vein reached every corner of the shire sooner or later, and Iain, hearing it from a carter bound for Cill ghlas with a load of the very salt that had made his own box so heavy, thought little of it beyond a passing wonder at how a village's wells could crack while the ground far beneath it, hollowed out by generations of digging, held fast; and it was some years before he heard, from the same carter returning the other way, that the diggers who had gone down that autumn had said among themselves, though never where the master of the store could hear it, that they had felt the shaking reach them even below, a long slow groan through the rock that none of them cared to speak of twice.

The carter, a broad, slow-spoken man who had made the Cill ghlas run for the better part of twenty years, told it to Iain over the second telling much as he had told it the first time, standing his ox at the north parapet while the toll was reckoned and taking his time about the story the way a man takes his time about anything he does not often get to tell to a fresh ear. The village, he said, had lost nothing worse than the byre and the three wells and a season's worry over brown water, and had rebuilt the byre by the following spring with stone hauled, at the custodians' own offer, from the vein-house's surplus, a small kindness that the village had not looked for and did not soon forget, the vein-house's custodians being known along that stretch of the shire for counting a coal-right more carefully than they counted a debt of neighbourliness. Iain took his handful from the carter's own load, as he took it from every load, and thought, watching the man drive on south with his ox leaning into the yoke, that a shaking felt through solid rock by men working half a mile under a hill was a stranger thing to carry home to a wife than any bridge toll he had ever had to reckon.

## VI. Of the Hollow Man, a King's End, and the Fish of Àth fhiadhaich

The shepherds who ran their flocks on the high pastures above Doire uaine, some way north and west of Iain's own bridge, had kept, for as long as anyone on the flats could remember, one custom that no reeve had ever managed to argue them out of: they lit no fire on those pastures after dark, whatever the cold, for the old carvings cut into the rock there told of a thing called Marbh-bheò, that walked the hills without breath and fell upon any who lit a light within its sight. A reeve had been sent up from {{place:burg:76}}'s own shire seat once, {{date:III-0083}}, a sensible man who thought the shepherds' custom a foolishness that cost them dear in cold nights, and had gone up to argue them out of it in person; he came down again before dark the same day, and no one who asked him why ever got a clear answer, only that he had seen enough of the hills by daylight to have no wish to test them by night, and the shepherds went on lighting no fires, as they had before, and as they would after. Iain heard the whole of it from the reeve's own mouth, some years on, the man having taken to stopping at the bridge on his rounds and being, by then, willing enough to talk of it to a bridge-keeper he had known long enough to trust with the telling, though not to any stranger who might carry it back to the shire seat as a tale against his own good sense. He had gone up, he said, meaning only to walk the pastures by daylight and judge for himself whether the carvings were worth the fear the shepherds spent on them, and had found the carvings plain enough, old work, weathered past any clear reading of what they had first meant to show; what had turned him back before dark, he said, was nothing in the stone at all, but a stillness in the whole of that high ground that he had not met on any other hillside he had walked in thirty years of riding the shire, a stillness with no birds in it, and he had judged, standing in the middle of it with the light beginning to fail, that a man's own good sense was worth less on that particular ground than the shepherds' fear was, and had come down. He never went back to argue the point again, and the shepherds, hearing of his single visit and his single retreat, took it for a confirmation of their custom rather than any defeat of it, and lit no fires the more firmly for it ever after.

It was in that same season, the district's own count agreeing near enough with the count kept at the red hall, that word came south along the road that Uisdean mac Uisdein, who had held the hall two-and-forty years since his father's death, had himself died at two-and-seventy, and that his son Aonghas, a young man of two-and-twenty, had been crowned in his place at {{place:burg:19}} with less dispute than any crowning the district's own memory held, being the first king, so the rider who carried the tidings said, taught his letters as a child by a tutor set over him for the purpose, a woman named Mòr nic Nèill, {{year:III-0084}}, rather than left, as kings before him had mostly been left, to pick up his letters as he could between other business. Iain, by then a grown man with sons of his own old enough to sit the bridge in his place on the days his knees would not carry him down to the stool, thought of his own father teaching him his letters between carts on that same bridge, and found something in the tidings of the young king's tutor that pleased him more than the crowning itself, a plain thing he understood better than he understood the business of crowns.

That same year, or near enough to it that the district's own tellers never troubled to separate the two, word reached the bridge that {{place:burg:12}}, a fishing town some way south that had salted its catch poorly and lost more of it to spoiling in a bad season than any town ought to lose, had begun sending carts north for Ros fhionn salt rather than trust its own poorer workings any longer, {{date:III-0085}}, and from that year the mining shires further south, which took their fish salted from Àth fhiadhaich as they had for generations, ate it cured with salt that had first crossed Iain's own bridge, a handful of it left behind in his box each time, though neither the fishers of Àth fhiadhaich nor the diggers who ate their catch ever thought to wonder, buying or selling it, how many hands and how many bridges a sack of salt had passed through before it reached a miner's own board.

Iain kept his father's toll at Drochaid Ros fhionn the whole of his working life, and the box beside his stool, iron-bound and never quite empty, held by the end more salt than any single winter of his own household could use, so that he gave the surplus away each spring to whichever of the flats' families had come off worst in the hungry months, judging, as his father had judged before him, that a handful honestly taken owed something back to the road it was taken from. He never built a deep hull, nor wished to; he had heard, once, as a boy, the tale of Ruairidh mac Artair's hull that would not ride true, carried up the road by some carter or other the way all such tales were carried, and it had struck him even then, sitting his stool with the tally-stick in his lap, that a keel laid without the hand that once knew how to lay it was no better than a bridge built without a mason's true eye for the stone, and that a man did better, on the whole, to keep what his own hands had actually been taught than to reach after what his grandmother's tellings alone could give him. He kept that thought, along with the salt, and passed both down, in the end, to his own sons at the same stool, with the same box between them.
