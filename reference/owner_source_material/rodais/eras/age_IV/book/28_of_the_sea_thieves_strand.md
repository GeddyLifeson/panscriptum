# Of the Sea-Thieves' Strand

*LE 775 – 835*

## I. Of the Boats Off the Small Islands

Coinneach mac Nèill had held the reeve's board at {{place:burg:305}} for eleven years before the first coaster came in short. It had been, until that summer, the post of a quiet man: he weighed the grain dues, he witnessed the marriages, he settled the small quarrels of a small town, and he went home each evening to a wife who teased him, gently, for the care he took over sums that would have troubled no one else in the shire. Baile chrom sat low on the north-west shore where the land ran out into a scatter of small islands, more rock than soil, and the town had lived the whole of its life off the strip of grey water between them: barley from its own thin fields, fish and grey seal-oil traded down the coast, and a peace so old that no one now living could have said when it began.

His father had held the same board before him, and his father's father before that, so far back as the town's own memory ran, and Coinneach had grown up under the low roof of the reeve's house believing, without ever having cause to examine the belief, that a reeve's chief labour was patience: patience with a fisherman who swore his catch had run smaller than it had, patience with a widow who could not pay her dues until the barley was in, patience with the slow turning of a year that asked, on that shore, little more of a man than that he keep faith with the tide and the season and not trouble himself overmuch with what lay beyond the small islands. He had taken a wife from a boat-building family two coves along, Beathag nic Artair, a woman with a dry tongue and a steadier hand at accounts than his own, and the two of them had settled into a life he expected, those first eleven years, to grow old and unremarkable in, much as his father had before him.

The town he kept that tally for was not large even in its best years, a few hundred souls strung along a single street above the strand, with a temple no bigger than a byre and a harbour that held, on a full market day, a dozen boats at most. It asked nothing grand of a reeve, and Coinneach had given it, in return, nothing grand: a fair weight on the scale, an honest word at the boundary disputes, and an ear that did not tire of hearing the same complaints season after season. He had thought, before the boats came, that this was the whole of what the office would ever ask of him.

It ended, so far as anyone in the town could afterward fix a beginning to it, on a still morning when the coaster out of {{place:burg:63}} came into the berth two days late and a third short in her hold, her skipper white about the mouth and unwilling, at first, to say why. She had been boarded off the small islands, he told Coinneach at last, sitting in the reeve's own kitchen with a cup of barley spirit he had not touched, by three boats that came out of a channel he had fished all his life and had never once thought to watch. The men who took her grain wore no mark and gave no name, and they had not troubled to hide their faces, which the skipper found, telling it, stranger and worse than any violence they had offered him, for it meant they did not fear being known.

The skipper's fright stayed with Coinneach longer than the loss did, which the town could and did absorb, a third of one coaster's hold being no great matter against a whole season's trade. He sat that night at his own table turning the strangeness of it over with his wife, who had heard the skipper's account through the kitchen wall and had her own reading of it ready before Coinneach had finished his. Men who hid their faces feared to be known, she said, setting down her needle; men who did not hide them had already reckoned that being known would cost them nothing, and a reeve who wanted to understand what he was dealing with would do better to ask himself why that reckoning might be a sound one on this particular stretch of coast, this particular year, than to ask who the men were. Coinneach had no ready answer for her that night either, and slept badly, turning the question over until the tide had gone out and come in again without his noticing either.

They were known, within the season. Rolla a' Chalaidh of the coast towns began, that year and the next, to set down names against the losses: not the names of strangers off some unmapped shore, as a body might have hoped, but names that half the reeves of the north-west could have matched, without much searching, to a face they had watched grow up. {{date:III-0131}} the first full count of the takings went up to {{place:burg:19}}, and it named among the sea-thieves a man of Baile chrom, a fisherman's son Coinneach had known since the boy was small enough to be carried on his father's shoulders down to the strand. His name was Aonghas mac Ruairidh, and his father Ruairidh had fished the same grounds as Coinneach's own father before either of them was born.

Coinneach went to the boy's mother himself, because he judged it a thing that ought to be done by the reeve and not left to Rolla a' Chalaidh to say for him, and found her at her door with the salt wind working at a strand of grey hair she had given up pinning back. She did not deny it. She said only that a young man who watches the fish thin out of his own home waters, season on season, for reasons no reeve and no priest has ever once explained to him, will in time go looking for a living where the living still is, and that if the living was to be found only in another man's hold, then another man's hold was where a hungry young man would go looking for it. Coinneach had no answer that satisfied either of them, and he walked back to his own house that evening turning over, more than the theft, the plainness with which she had said it, as though she had long since stopped expecting the world to be other than it plainly was.

He asked, that same week, at every house along the street where a young man had gone missing from his own boat without any word to his kin of where he had shipped instead, and found more such houses than he had let himself expect before he began asking. Some of the fathers would not speak to him at all, closing their doors on him with a civility colder than any rudeness could have been. One old fisherman, a man named Eachann who had fished beside Coinneach's own father, told him only that a reeve who came asking after another man's son should first go home and ask his own conscience how many seasons running he had watched the catch fall and done nothing but write it down smaller in his own book each year. Coinneach carried that home too, and found no more of an answer for it than he had found for Aonghas's mother.

## II. Of Half the Houses Standing Empty

The raiding did not slacken, and within a few years the town Coinneach had inherited a quiet post in was a town visibly thinning under his own eyes, the way a fire someone forgets to feed burns down slowly, house by house, to embers, never going out all at once. Families who had fished the same water for generations began, that stretch of years, to load a cart with what it would carry and go inland before the coast road closed for the winter mud, judging a hard life among strangers a smaller risk than a certain one on a shore the sea-thieves had made their own hunting ground. Coinneach counted the going as carefully as he had once counted grain, because it was, after all, the same board he kept the tally on, and by the time he made his report to the crown's own scribes, {{date:III-0132}}, he could set down in his own hand that half the houses of Baile chrom stood empty, their thatch going to moss, their doors shut on rooms no one would sleep in again that year or any year he could then foresee.

He watched the going of one family in particular that stayed with him longer than the rest: a boat-wright named Murchadh and his wife and their three children, who loaded a single cart on a morning of thin rain with everything the cart would bear and stood a while at the edge of the street before they set off, looking back at a house Murchadh's own grandfather had raised. Coinneach walked down to see them off, as he judged a reeve owed at least that much to any household leaving his charge, and Murchadh, shaking his hand at the cart's tail, said only that a boat-wright with no boats left worth building for could build a cart instead, and had, and that he hoped Coinneach would forgive him for not staying to watch the town finish emptying around him. Coinneach told him there was nothing to forgive, and meant it, and watched the cart out of sight along the coast road before he turned back to a street that had, that morning, six fewer feet walking it than it had the evening before.

He wrote the report himself, sitting up two nights over it by a single lamp while his wife Beathag nic Artair sat opposite mending nets that fewer and fewer hands in the town still had any use for, and he wrote it plainly, without the flourishes a lesser reeve might have reached for to make his own trouble sound larger than his neighbour's, because he judged, rightly as it turned out, that the crown's scribes had trouble enough of their own to weigh without a reeve's vanity added to it. He asked only that the grain dues on the houses still standing be lightened, since a town paying dues reckoned for twice its present hands could not long keep bread in the houses that remained, and he sent the report south by the same rider who carried the coast road's other business, not troubling to send it apart, or to mark it urgent, judging that a true thing said plainly would carry its own weight without his help.

The answer came back before the winter closed the road, shorter than his own letter and written, he could tell from the hand, by a scribe who had many such letters that season to answer and little leisure to spend on any one of them: the grain dues of Baile chrom were remitted, from that year forward, to a sum reckoned by the households that stood, not the households that had once stood, and the reeve was thanked for a count taken, the letter said, with care. Coinneach read the letter twice through at his own table and then folded it away in the chest where he kept such leaves, and told his wife only that the town would not starve on the dues it could no longer pay, which was, he judged, as much as any reeve on that coast could honestly claim to have won that year.

## III. Of the Force Asked and the Force Sent

He did not ask for more, not at first, holding to the same plain reckoning that had served him with the dues: a town remitted of what it could not pay might yet feed itself, given time, on what its remaining hands could still bring in from the water. But the boats out of the small islands grew bolder as the coast grew emptier of eyes to watch them, and a season came when they no longer confined themselves to coasters caught alone on open water but put in, twice within the same moon, to strands within sight of Baile chrom's own harbour and took what stood in the drying-sheds before any hand in the town could be raised against them. It was after the second of these, in the same season the dues remission was scarcely settled in the crown's own ledger, that Coinneach sat down and wrote the letter he had put off writing as long as his own conscience would allow him to put it off, asking this time for a force outright, no longer for lightened dues alone, since a reeve with a tally-stick and a handful of fishermen too old or too young to have gone raiding themselves could no longer hold a coast that men of his own shire, grown desperate or grown greedy or grown both together, had made their hunting ground.

The letter went south in the last good weather of the year and the answer, this time, was slower in coming, for the crown had its own troubles to weigh, and a reeve's petition from a thinning shore on the far side of the island was not, Coinneach understood without needing to be told, the first leaf on any scribe's board that season. He waited through a winter and into the spring that followed, and he was still waiting, mending his own nets now beside his wife in the evenings for want of hands enough in the town to hire, when word came at last that a force under a captain named Iain mac Thormoid had been raised and was coming north by sea, sent, the letter said, on the queen's own word.

He read that line to his wife three times over before he set the letter down, less from any doubt of what it said than from a wish, he admitted to her, to hear it said aloud a third time and see whether it still sounded as unlikely to his own ears as it had the first. Beathag told him, not unkindly, that a man who had spent a whole winter fearing his letter had gone into a scribe's chest and been forgotten ought to allow himself an evening of believing it had not, before he went back to his habit of expecting the worst of every answer the capital sent him. He allowed himself the evening, and little more, for the spring brought planting before it brought ships, and a reeve with half his fields untended could not stand idle on the strand waiting for a sail that might yet be a season in coming.

Eilidh Bhàn had held the throne at {{place:burg:19}} two and thirty years by then, crowned a girl of eighteen and grown, in the reigning, into a woman the north-west knew mostly by the letters her scribes wrote in her name, for she had never once, in all those years, travelled so far from the capital as the coast her own force was now bound for. Iain mac Thormoid's ships put in at Baile chrom on a grey morning of driving rain, and Coinneach, standing on the strand to meet them with half the town's remaining hands at his back, found himself, for the first time in eight-and-twenty years as reeve, glad of a thing he had asked for that had actually arrived.

Iain mac Thormoid was a spare, weathered man who asked more questions in his first hour ashore than any capital officer Coinneach had dealt with before him, wanting to know the set of the tides among the small islands, which channels ran dry at the ebb, which coves a boat might lie hidden in through a whole daylight and never be seen from the open water. Coinneach answered what he could and sent for the town's own boat-men to answer the rest, and by the second evening the captain had a chart of the raiders' ground drawn out on the reeve's own table in charcoal, corrected twice over by men who had fished those waters since before Iain mac Thormoid was born.

They found the sea-thieves' own strand within the week, on {{place:burg:305}}'s own fishermen leading the way, for there was not a man left in Baile chrom who did not, by then, know the channel among the small islands as well as he knew his own doorstep. {{date:III-0133}} the boats went in at first light on Eilean dhubh, and Coinneach, who had asked to go and been allowed to, on the plain ground that a reeve who knew the men by name might yet talk some of them down before it came to burning, found when the fighting was over that talking had bought him nothing at all. The landing was over faster than Coinneach had let himself expect, the sea-thieves having kept no proper watch on a shore they had held unchallenged for the better part of a decade, and most of the fighting, such as it was, went to Iain mac Thormoid's own men, better armed and readier for it than Coinneach's own fishermen, who had come along, most of them, to point out a channel and not to bear arms in earnest. Coinneach himself carried nothing heavier than the knife every man on that coast carried for gutting fish, and he used it for nothing that morning but cutting a length of rope from a burning hull's mooring, judging, once the fighting had truly begun, that an old reeve with a fish-knife had better make himself useful at some smaller task than stand about getting in a soldier's way.

Aonghas mac Ruairidh, thirty-one years old that spring and grown into a man Coinneach would have known nowhere except the strand where he had once been a boy, went down with a spear through him defending a hull he had likely never set foot in as an honest fisherman, and Coinneach, kneeling over him on the wet stones with the boats burning at his back, found he had no words ready for the mother he would have to tell of it, though he had had a full winter and spring to prepare some. He held the boy's hand a while, though he judged from the stillness of it that there was no longer anyone left inside it to feel the holding, and one of Iain mac Thormoid's own men, passing, asked him gently enough whether he had known the dead man. Coinneach answered that he had carried him on his own shoulder once, down to this same water, when the boy was too small to walk it himself, and the soldier, who had likely heard harder things said that morning, said nothing further and went on to the next of the fallen.

Uilleam Ruadh, who had led the sea-thieves for the better part of those years and was known, by then, up and down the whole north-west coast by name and by a red beard no disguise had ever troubled to hide, was taken alive off the strand and carried south to {{place:burg:19}} in irons, in the same ships that had burned his boats. He was six and thirty years old, and he was hanged there before the season turned, the crown's own scribes setting the day down without ornament in the rolls Coinneach afterward read at secondhand, a man's whole life closed in fewer words than Coinneach's own report on the emptied houses had run to. Coinneach felt no triumph in the reading of it that he could afterward account for, only a tiredness that sat in him, he told his wife, the way damp sits in a wall after a long wet season, never felt at any single hour, and never afterward quite gone.

## IV. Of the Harvests That Would Not Come

The raiding ended with Uilleam Ruadh's hanging, or near enough to ended that the coast road carried no further tidings of it, and for a handful of years Baile chrom's trouble was, at least, a trouble Coinneach recognised: the slow, ordinary work of a small town trying to fill again the houses a bad decade had emptied. It was not given the years to do it in. The harvests that should have carried that work began to fail, one on the heels of the last, in a run that the oldest hands in the town, asked, could not match to any dearth they themselves remembered, and by the ninth or tenth year of it the shared-meal custom that had always, in Coinneach's own lifetime, carried Baile chrom through a single bad season could no longer be stretched to carry a run of them.

He kept the custom himself as long as any grain remained to keep it with, standing at his own board each market day and dividing what the town's diminished fields had brought in as evenly as his own hand could manage it, and he told those who came to him, in the years the custom still held, that a shared hunger was at least a hunger a man need not carry alone. He remembered, of those market days, one old woman, Mairead, who had buried a husband and two sons to the sea before the dearth ever touched her, coming to his board with an empty basket and telling him, when he weighed out her share, that she had lived through three bad harvests in her own girlhood and had thought, until this one, that she had already learned everything a hungry year had to teach a body. She had not, she said, and neither, she suspected, had he, and she was proven right in that before either of them saw the end of it.

Coinneach kept his own account of the failing harvests the way he had once kept his account of the emptying houses, a column of numbers in the same chest of rolls, and he could have told, had anyone still cared to ask him, that the barley yield of his own charge had fallen by more than half across those years, and the fish catch with it, for the boats that remained had too few hands between them to work the grounds a full crew had once worked. He wrote no further letter south asking for relief, having learned, from the long wait over the force, that a plea for grain from a coast already known to the crown as thinly held and further thinning would travel no faster than the last one had, and judging, besides, that grain itself, unlike soldiers, was not a thing the capital could simply raise more of by the asking. But a hand, however fair, cannot divide what the ground has simply failed to give, and {{year:III-0134}} the last of the households that had held on through the sea-thieves' years began, in ones and twos and then in whole families at once, to load their carts and go, as their neighbours had gone a generation before them, inland and east toward shires that still had bread to share. Coinneach's own wife Beathag died that same hard winter, of a chest complaint the town's one physician judged no different from a dozen such complaints she had seen through in better years, save that a body weakened by two thin harvests running had less in it to fight a fever with than a body fed as it ought to have been fed.

Coinneach buried her on a morning with the ground half frozen and stood afterward at his own door a long while, by his own account to the few neighbours still near enough to hear him say it, working out for himself, he said, not whether he ought to follow the others east, which he judged by then past deciding, but only how long a man owed a post before the post no longer owed him anything in return. He held the board a further year, more from a settled habit of duty than from any hope left in him that the holding served a purpose the town still needed served, keeping the tally of a shire that had, by then, scarcely enough hands left in it to need a tally kept at all. Old Eachann, who had spoken hard to him once about a fallen catch and a reeve's slowness, came to him that year and told him, gentler than he had ever spoken to him before, that a man could grieve a wife and hold a board both at once for only so long before one or the other of them began to suffer for it, and that Eachann, for his own part, had watched enough men on that coast try to outlast a grief with work alone to know how the trying generally ended. Coinneach thanked him for the saying and did not, that year, act on it, telling himself there was still a use in him yet.

## V. Of the Roll Recopied and the Market at the Ford

The crown's own business went on, in those same years, at a distance the north-west shore had long since stopped expecting to feel. {{year:III-0135}} the queen's scribes at {{place:burg:19}} took down the old Clàr nan Rìghrean from its resting place and copied it afresh onto new hide, adding to it, as the old roll had never troubled to, every ruler since the fleet had gone, and setting the new copy's beginning at An Roghnachadh rather than at the sailing, a small labour of a few scribes in a hall the width of the island away from any door a Baile chrom hand had ever knocked on. And some years after that a market grew up on the strand at {{place:burg:113}}, where the coast road crossed the clear river, trading north-coast fish and the oil of stranded whales to a country that had, by then, stopped looking for either from the harbour Coinneach had spent his own working life keeping. Àth fhionn's strand grew loud with barter on market days in a way Baile chrom's own strand had not been loud in years, and the fishers of the north coast, Coinneach heard it said by a rider passing through, no longer troubled to carry their catch the longer road south to {{place:burg:17}} at all, having found, in the new market, a nearer and readier hand to sell it into.

He heard both tidings the same way he had heard most tidings in his last years at the board, at secondhand, from travellers who stopped to water a horse and talked while it drank, and he set down neither of them in any report, judging, by then, that a reeve with fewer households under him than the roll now carried names for had little left worth reporting. It struck him, hearing of the market at {{place:burg:113}}, that he had spent eleven good years and a score of hard ones keeping a harbour that a coast now further along than his own had simply grown past the need of, the way a man grows past the need of a coat that once fitted him, without any unkindness meant by the growing and none taken; and it struck him too, though he did not say this aloud to the rider who brought the tidings, that a market on a strand he had never once visited would go on trading fish and whale-oil for years after Baile chrom's own harbour stones had nothing left on them but gulls. He held the board through one further harvest, thinner than the one before it, and found, come the following spring, that he had at last worked out for himself the answer to the question he had stood at his own door asking on the morning he buried his wife: that a man owed a post exactly as long as the post still asked a true thing of him, and no longer.

He went from house to house that had a soul left in it, the morning he chose to go, and said what there was to say to each, which was little enough after so many years of saying it in smaller pieces across a market board. Old Eachann was among the last he called on, and told him only that he had been right, a full year late, and that being right late was still a kind of being right. Coinneach said he supposed it was, and left him with the last of the dried fish from his own stores, since a man who no longer had a household to feed had no further use for it and a man who still did might.

He set the reeve's own chest of rolls and tallies in order, for whoever might come after him, though he no longer much believed anyone would, setting the grain counts and the boundary leaves and the long, thinning column of households each in its own place, as though a careful ordering of a record no one would likely ever open again were still, somehow, worth a morning's labour. The hearth he swept clean himself, which his wife had always done before him, telling the one neighbour who watched him do it that a man ought to leave a room as he would wish to find one. Last of all he took down the strip of netting Beathag had left half mended in her own basket, and folded it, and set it on top of the chest, unable to bring himself either to finish it or to leave it lying loose upon the floor.

Then he shut his own door behind him, and set the road east under his feet, an old man by then in everything but the years any roll had ever troubled to count against his name, and did not look back at {{place:burg:305}} again before the road bent and the strand went out of sight behind him. It is told at the road-houses along that stretch of the eastern way that a grey-haired man walked in that year asking after no kin and no trade, only after a shire that would still have a use for a hand that had once kept a tally, and that when he was asked, as a stranger on that road is always asked, what town he came out of, he answered plainly, and then said nothing further about it that any who heard him thought worth carrying on.
