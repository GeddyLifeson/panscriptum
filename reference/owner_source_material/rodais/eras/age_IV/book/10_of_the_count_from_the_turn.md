# Of the Count from the Turn

*LE 40 – 46*

## I. Of the Entry a Day Too Late

The custody-book of the vein-house above Muileann dhearg was kept in a hand steadier than most, and the hand that kept it in the autumn after the sunwise turn belonged to a clerk of four-and-twenty named Donnchadh mac Thormoid, who had been given the leaf and the ink-horn two winters before by a master who was dead within the year of the giving, and who had gone on keeping them since because no one above him had troubled to say he should stop.

He was not the man who had counted the hulls; that had been a boy on a harbour wall sixty miles off, with a hazel stick and a knife, and Donnchadh had never met him and never would. What came to Donnchadh, in the ordinary course of the vein-house's business, was a leaf carried up from Seann Skell by a rider who did not know, or did not say, why the leaf had been so long on the road, and it was Donnchadh's office, not his curiosity, to enter what the leaf told him into the great book of the custody and ask no more of it than the book required.

The leaf told him that the fleet of the willing had gone once round the river pool at Seann Skell sunwise, as a man walks round a well for luck, and had gone down the river after and out past the north cape, and that this had happened, by the harbour scribe's own hand, on a day the leaf gave plainly enough. What troubled Donnchadh, turning the leaf over by the light of the vein-house's lamp with his master's old rule laid open beside it, the rule by which every entry in the custody-book was dated against the feasts of the turning year, was that the day the leaf gave fell after Samhain, and by every account he had ever had of the sailing, of the last stores laid in and the last measures sealed and the three tellings that had first brought word of a far shore to the queen's own board, the fleet had gone out long before that, in a warmer month, with the harvest not yet fully in.

He was a careful clerk, and carefulness in a clerk is a kind of honesty that asks no questions it cannot answer. Turning the leaf a second time and a third, holding it near enough the lamp that the pitch-smoke stung his eyes, he found in it nothing that explained the gap between the day the fleet had gone and the day the vein-house was told of the going: no word of a delay on the road, no note that the first leaf had been lost and this one written after in its place, no apology from the harbour scribe for a silence of half a year that no one at Seann Skell, so far as the leaf showed, had thought worth accounting for. A rider might have been sent back down to Seann Skell to ask. None was. It was, he told himself, setting the ink-horn to the page, a clerk's business only to copy the leaf faithfully into the great book, gap and all, and let the book stand as the book had been given to him.

So he wrote it: the fleet's sunwise turn, entered under the day a full turning of the moon and more after the feast of Samhain, though every other account he had of the matter set the sailing in a warmer season entirely. He signed the entry with his own mark, as his master had taught him, closed the book, and banked the vein-house's night fire himself, because the boy whose office that was had gone home early with a fever, and thought no more of the gap that night than a man thinks of a stone he has stepped around a hundred times on a familiar road.

## II. Of the Years of the Diosal

Four years passed at the vein-house much as the four before them had, in the reckoning of hearths and the sealing of measures and the slow correspondence, letter and counter-letter, between the custodians of Muileann dhearg and the red hall at Cathair dhearg over matters that the histories of great houses call small and the households they touch call everything: a gallery's roof timbered too thin, a widow's right to her dead husband's handful, a boundary stone moved a foot by a neighbour's ox-sled and moved back by night.

Donnchadh kept the custody-book through all of it, and kept also, by then, a second book that was his own and answered to no one, a plain thing of scraped hide bound with cord, in which he set down, for his own convenience and no other man's, the sums and the debts and the running totals that the great book's formal hand had no room for. It was in this private book, in the fourth year after the turn, that he first found himself wanting a single fixed point from which to count forward, because the island's own way of dating things, by the reign of a queen or the year of a feast or the season a gallery was opened, served the custody-book well enough but served his own sums badly, requiring him each time to look up how many years a given reign or a given gallery had already run before he could set two figures against each other at all.

He chose the turn. It was, of all the fixed points he had to hand, the nearest and the strangest, a day already written into the great book under a date that fit no other account of it, and it had at least the merit, in a clerk's eye, of being an ending rather than a beginning, a single day after which nothing further needed to be asked of the fleet, whatever might yet be asked of everything else. He began to write, in the margins of his own book only, the year of the Diosal beside the year of the reign, first for the year then running, which he called the fourth of the Diosal, and then, working backward with a patience that cost him the better part of an evening, for every year in his own book since the turn, so that the private ledger of a clerk of four-and-twenty came, without ceremony and without anyone above him ever asking that it should, to be the first place on the island where the years were counted from an absence rather than a reign.

He told no one. It did not occur to him that the habit was worth telling, being no more, in his own reckoning, than a clerk's shortcut for his own sums, and if any other hand at the vein-house noticed the odd figures in the margin of his private book, no one troubled to ask him what they meant, and he was not a man who offered explanations that were not asked for.

There was, besides, a plainer reason he kept the habit to his own book and no further, one he would not himself have put into words if asked, though it sat behind the choosing all the same: a reckoning that began from the turn was a reckoning that began from a loss, and Donnchadh, who measured hearths and galleries for his trade and had learned early that a measure is only as good as the men willing to trust it, did not know whether the custodians above him, who had lost sons and daughters and whole households to that same turn, would thank a junior clerk for fixing their years to it in a book that anyone might read. His own book no one read but himself. He kept the count there, and there it stayed, a private arithmetic run alongside the vein-house's public one, the two agreeing in every figure that mattered and differing only in the one thing that, to Donnchadh's mind, mattered to no one but him: from what day the years were said to run.

He found, working the count backward and then forward again through that fourth year, a small and useless satisfaction in the plainness of it, that a thing so large as the going of a fifth of the coal-right's households could be made to sit, in a clerk's own ledger, beside the price of a barrel of sealed measures or the wages owed a mason for a season's work on the vein-house wall, one figure among many, asking no more of the page than any other figure did. It was not, he would have said if pressed, that the going meant no more to him than a barrel's price; only that a ledger has no way of saying which of its figures broke a household and which merely counted one, and he had long since made his peace with a trade that could not tell the difference.

## III. Of the Tidings that Reached Seann Skell

That same year, word came to Seann Skell of the fleet, though by what road it came, or in whose mouth, or aboard what small boat put in at the old stones below the harbour-house, no one who was there that autumn ever afterward agreed well enough to say.

It fell to Calum mac Nèill to receive it, as it fell to him to receive most things that came to the harbour-house wearing the shape of news, and he was an older man by then than he had been when the boards were first hung on their pegs, grey through the beard and slower in the hand, though his hand, when it came to the actual forming of letters, had not yet begun to fail him. His wife Seonag had taken to saying, of late, that he sat too long over the boards for a man his age, and he had taken to answering, when he answered at all, that the boards had had a keeper his whole life and would want one after him, and that there was no sense training a new keeper's hand while the old one still held its line.

The word, when it came, came as broken speech rather than as a leaf already written, a telling passed from a boat that did not stay to be questioned to a fisherman of the strand who could not himself say afterward which of the boat's crew had spoken or in what tongue, and it fell then to Calum to set that broken speech down in the harbour-house's own hand before it broke further in the passing. He did it that same evening, by lamplight, with his hand, his wife noted afterward, less steady over that leaf than she had seen it over any other in twenty years of watching him write, so that the letters toward the leaf's foot ran smaller and closer together than at its head, as a man's hand will when he is hurrying to set down what he fears he may otherwise lose before he has it whole.

What the telling gave him, so far as his shaking hand caught it, was this: that the fleet, or some good part of it, had come at last to a shore across the open water, and that it had come to two shores and not to one, two separate landfalls reached by hulls that had not, it seemed, all held together the whole of the crossing, though whether storm had parted them, or purpose, or some quarrel among the crews that the telling did not touch, Calum's leaf did not say and Calum did not pretend to know. The telling ran on a little further, naming, or seeming to name, some particular of the second shore that might have settled the matter one way or another, and it was there, at the leaf's very foot, in the smallest and most hurried of all the hand's letters, that it simply stopped, with no mark of an ending, as though the teller had been called away, or the listener's patience had failed, or the leaf had been torn and the torn part lost before ever it reached the boards.

Calum read it over three times before he judged it fit to send on, and found, each time, that it told him no more at the third reading than it had at the first, and that the not-knowing sat worse with him than a plain bad tiding would have done, because a plain bad tiding, however hard, is a thing a man can grieve and be done with, and this was a thing that asked to be carried unfinished a good while longer, by him and by whoever received it after him.

He did not sleep well that night, and Seonag, waking once to find him sitting up with the lamp still lit and the leaf still before him though he had long since finished the copying of it, asked him plainly what more there was in it that he had not yet set down. There was nothing more in it, he told her; that was the whole trouble with the thing, that it had stopped where it had stopped and left him with no more right than any fisherman on the strand to guess at what lay beyond its torn foot, and yet he was the one whose hand had carried it, and a hand that carries a tale unfinished, he said, feels the weight of the unfinished part as much as of the part it actually holds. Seonag, who had kept house with a harbour scribe long enough to know when an answer was the whole of what she would get, blew out the lamp herself when he would not, and left him to sit a while longer in the dark with the leaf under his hand, which he did, by his own account afterward, until the sky over the water had begun to grey.

In the morning he read the leaf once more before he sealed it for the rider, and it was then, turning it over a last time by the grey early light, that he noticed what he had been too tired the night before to mark: that the hand which had first spoken the broken tale to the fisherman on the strand had used, in the one clear phrase Calum's own leaf preserved whole, a word for the second shore that the island's own tongue did not commonly use of any coast it had seen with its own eyes, a word more often kept for a shore known only by report. He set that observation nowhere in the copy he sent on, judging it, on reflection, too slight a thread to hang a further guess from, and it went with him, unwritten, to his grave some fourteen years after, one more thing the harbour-house's own boards would never show.

## IV. Of the Hearths of Inis àrsaidh

The telling did not stay long behind the harbour-house door. It went out, as such things do in a fishing town smaller than its harbour, along the strand and up the lanes before the ink on Calum's leaf was fully dry, carried by the fisherman who had first heard it and by every mouth that heard it from him after, each mouth losing a little of the whole and adding a little of its own, until the tale that reached the hearths of Inis àrsaidh a day's walk up the coast bore only a family likeness to the leaf Calum had written.

It was heard hardest at Inis àrsaidh because it was from Inis àrsaidh, more households than from any other single place on the roll, that the fleet had carried its people, and there was scarcely a hearth in the fishing town that autumn without some cousin or son-in-law or a whole young family gone in one of the nineteen hulls or one-and-twenty, the number no one there had ever settled either. An old net-mender of the strand, who had given two grandsons to the roll and kept, since, a lamp of her own on her sill though the custom had not yet spread beyond Seann Skell's own waterfront, was heard to say, when the tale of two shores reached her, that two shores were better than none, and was heard also, not an hour later, by the same neighbour who carried her the tale, to say that two shores were worse than one, since a man does not know, now, which of the two to grieve toward or which to hope toward, and must do both at once with a heart no bigger than it was before.

Nothing in the tale, as it reached Inis àrsaidh, told anyone which of the two coasts held which household, and the town did what towns do with a tale too broken to answer the one question every hearth in it wanted answered: it kept both halves of the tale alive at once, telling it one way at one hearth and another way at the next, until the not-knowing became, in a manner Calum would have understood better than most, the whole of what there was to know.

## V. Of the Copy that Went by Two Roads

Calum's leaf, once it was judged fit, did not stay at Seann Skell. A fair copy of it went by rider up the river road to Cathair dhearg, where the queen's own scribes kept the roll of what the crown was told, and a second copy, made from the first by Calum's own son Dòmhnall in a hand less hurried than his father's, went the sixty miles east to the vein-house, where it came, in the course of things, to the same board at which Donnchadh mac Thormoid had four years before entered the turn under its unaccountable date.

He read it standing, the custody-book still open on the board before him from an earlier entry, and set the two leaves side by side without quite meaning to, the old entry of the turn and the new copy of the tidings, and saw between them a silence of four years that neither leaf so much as gestured toward, the fleet gone out one grey morning and nothing more heard of it, so far as the vein-house's own book showed, until this. He entered the tidings as he had entered the turn, faithfully and without comment, copying Calum's broken telling into the custody-book's formal hand exactly as it stood, torn end and all, resisting, as he had resisted before, any temptation a lesser clerk might have felt to smooth the gap where the leaf gave out or to guess, in the book's own voice, at what the missing end might have said.

At Cathair dhearg the other copy went, as such things did, before the queen's own council in the red hall, and there it fared rather less quietly than it had at the vein-house's plain board. The council of Dia-thìr had never yet had to weigh a piece of news it could neither confirm nor disprove, and it did with this one what councils do with such things: it argued. One custodian of the old houses held that two shores meant the fleet had prospered, having found land enough for all its hulls though not all in the one place; another held the opposite, that two shores meant the fleet had failed to hold together at all, and that a fleet scattered on a strange sea was a fleet in worse case than one that had never sailed together to begin with. A third asked, and was not answered, which of the nineteen or one-and-twenty hulls the tale meant by its second shore, and whether the queen's own sister's hull, Mòrag's own Ròn Glas with the pilot of Ros bheag at its steering oar, had held to the first landfall or been carried to the second, and it was at this question, more than at any other put that day, that the whole board turned, as one body, to look at the queen.

Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill sat at the head of the board through the whole of the argument with her hands folded on the wood before her and said, by every account of that day that came down afterward, nothing at all. She had the leaf brought to her once more when the council's voices had run themselves out, and read it over herself, alone, by the light from the hall's high windows, and set it down again exactly where it had lain, and gave no order that it be answered, denied, or sent further, so that the council, having nothing else to do with a silence from its own queen, let the matter drop where it lay and turned to the business the day had actually brought it: a dispute over a ditch between two farms on the river, no different in its particulars from a hundred such disputes the hall had settled before and would settle again.

She kept the leaf by her a good while after the council had gone on to its ditch, longer than the ditch took to settle, and it is told that she had it copied once more in her own hand that same evening, a thing she did for no other piece of business that year, and that the copy she made agreed word for word with the one Calum had sent, down to the place where the telling stopped, so that whatever she had hoped to find by setting the words down again herself, in her own letters, she did not find it, and put the copy away with the rest of the year's papers where it lay, so far as anyone at the red hall ever afterward saw, unread again. In the council's hearing she had asked nothing of which shore her sister's hull had reached, though it was known to every custodian at that board that the third man's question was the one that concerned her before all the rest, and it was said of her by those who served her closest that a queen who forbids herself a question in front of her own council does not therefore stop asking it once the council has gone.

## VI. Of the Grain Ruling and the Bridge

The two years that followed brought the red hall the ordinary weather of a kingdom's business, the kind that fills the greater part of any council's year and the smaller part of any chronicle that troubles to be honest about it. A dispute over the grain owed by the upland shires to the crown's own granaries, long argued and longer delayed, was at last settled in the second of those years in the queen's own favour, the upland reeves agreeing to a measure they had refused for three seasons running once it was put to them, as it had not been put before, in the queen's own hand rather than a steward's. In the same season the bridge at Àth shean, whose middle pier had stood cracked and shored with timber since a flood two winters before, was at last mended in stone by masons sent down from the capital, the last of the timber props pulled clear on a morning cold enough that the masons broke the ice on the river to set their final course.

Between the grain ruling and the mending of the bridge, in the ordinary order of the council's business, with no greater weight given to it than to either, the queen made one further entry in the roll of the house of Dia-thìr, an entry that her own scribes wrote, when the day came, in the same plain hand they had used for the grain and would use for the bridge, no larger, no darker, set apart by no mark that a reader turning the leaf idly would have paused at.

## VII. Of the Name Written the Ordinary Way

It was Murchadh mac Eòghainn who held the pen that day, as he had held it since before the three crews first walked up the river road with their tale of a far shore, an old man now, older than any other hand still serving at the red hall, whose eyes had begun to want more light than the hall's high windows gave even at noon and whose fingers, in the cold months, had to be worked a while by the fire before they would close properly round a quill.

The queen gave him the words herself, standing at his shoulder rather than sitting, as she did for no other entry he had written that year, and the words, when he had them whole, struck him, though he gave no sign of it and asked no question, as being fewer than the matter seemed to want and plainer than any matter he had ever set down at that board before: that Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, and the children of Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, were struck from the order of succession to the crown of Dia-thìr, as persons no longer on Dia-thìr.

He wrote it as he had been given it, in the ordinary hand, and it was only in the forming of the name itself, Mòrag's own name, that his pen slowed by the smallest degree a watcher might have caught, not faltering, not blotting, only slowing, the way an old man's step will slow over ground he has walked a thousand times before without ever once, until that day, having cause to feel its particular shape beneath his foot. He wrote the name in the same letters he had given it every time he had written it before, in the roll of the first tellings, in the entry of Mòrag's own household onto the harbour boards, in a dozen ordinary mentions of the queen's sister across four decades of a scribe's ordinary business, and it was, in the end, exactly that, the name written the ordinary way, that seemed to trouble him longest after the ink had dried.

The queen did not stand over him again once the entry was made. She read it once where it lay, gave him no word of thanks and no word of anything else, and went out of the hall by the same door she had come in by, and it was left to Murchadh, as it was left to no one else in that hall or any other, to close the book on a name he had written a hundred times before and would, so far as the roll of the house of Dia-thìr was ever afterward concerned, never write again.

## VIII. Of the Leaf Sanded

He sat a while after she had gone, the pen still in his hand though there was nothing further that day for it to write, and did at last what he did at the end of every entry he had ever made in that book, no more and no less: he took the fine sand from the box that had stood at his elbow at that board for forty years and more, and shook it evenly across the wet ink, and let it lie a moment, and tipped it off again into the box for the next leaf's use, an old man's hands moving through a motion so long practised that they needed, by then, no more of his mind's attention than his own breathing did.

The hall was near empty around him by then, the day's business done and the light going long and low through the high windows, and if any of the few who remained thought to watch him at it, they saw only what they had seen him do at the close of a thousand ordinary days: an old scribe closing his book, sanding his last leaf, and setting both aside for the morning, in a hand that gave away, to any eye that did not already know what the leaf held, nothing at all of what had just been written under it.
