# Of Catrìona Bhuadhach

*LE 561 – 604*

## I. Of the Wound That Would Not Close

Word came to Catrìona nic Aonghais in Faolach, twelve days after the field below {{place:burg:354}}, that Dùghall Garbh was dying at {{place:burg:273}} of a cut he had taken in the fighting and had told no one, that day or for a week after, was more than a graze.

She had not seen the wound herself. She had seen him ride from the field with his standard up again in another man's hand, and had taken that, as everyone in her own camp had taken it, for a sign of nothing worse than a hard day; men lost standards and found them again in a battle without losing much else. It was only afterward, from the mouth of a rider sent ahead of the litter, that she learned the cut had gone in under the arm where the mail gapped, and that Dùghall had bound it himself with a strip of his own shirt and said nothing of it to his own physicians until the fever was already three days old.

"He would not send for you," the rider told her, standing in the doorway of the house she had taken for her lodging, snow melting off his shoulders onto the floor. "He said there was nothing to send for."

She went anyway, and was three days on the road behind him, and arrived at Baile Mòr dhomhain to find the thing already over. Dùghall Garbh had died in the night, in the same low stone hall where his father and his father's father had held their own court apart from the crown for three generations, with his own physician asleep in a chair beside him and no one else in the room. They told her, those who had been in the house, that he had asked once, near the end, whether the vein-house gates were open, and been told they were, and said nothing further that anyone had thought to write down.

She stood a long while over him before they closed the hall to strangers, a man she had fought six years to beat and then one season to save, grey through the beard as she remembered him from the camp-fire where he had first come to her with six men and no herald, older now only by the stillness. She had not loved him, and had not, she thought, even wholly trusted him, not in the season they marched together, watching each night to see which of his captains still eyed her own with the old war's suspicion. Yet she had believed him, in the one thing he had said to her that had mattered, that a crown enforced by cold hearths was a crown that belonged to no house at all, and looking down at him she understood that he had believed it to the very end, past the point where believing it cost him anything but his own life.

She sat with him a night before the hall was closed, though there was no longer anyone to sit with, only a body laid out in the manner his own house kept, feet to the door, a cup and a straight knife set beside him that no one had thought to take away though he had no more use for either. A servant of the house brought her a bowl of broth she did not eat and a blanket she did not use, and stood in the doorway a while as though waiting to be dismissed before she understood that the queen meant to sit the whole night through and left her to it. Catrìona found, sitting there, that she could not settle her mind on grief exactly, nor on triumph, though either would have been a simpler thing to carry out of that room than what she did carry, which was closer to a kind of exhausted gratitude, that the war had found an ending that did not require her to be the one who ended it by killing the last man who might have carried it on.

His captains came to her camp within the fortnight, not as men surrendering — there was no field left to surrender on — but as men arriving somewhere they had already decided to be. Their spokesman was an old man named Artair mac Lachlainn who had fought at the ford against Raghnall's host and lost a son there, and he laid no terms before her at all, only said that Dùghall Garbh had told them, before Muileann chrom, what he meant to do if the battle went their way, and that he saw no reason a dead man's word should bind them less than a living one's. In the weeks after the field she had expected to spend a season yet gathering in a house whose lord had died without naming who should have his men, not to spend it, instead, sitting in a borrowed hall listening to captains who had killed men of hers not two winters before tell her, plainly and without much ceremony, that the war they had all three been fighting was over, and had been over since the morning the vein-house gates opened from within.

She rode to {{place:burg:19}} in {{year:III-0112}}, to the red hall her uncle's line had held since before her birth, and was crowned there in a ceremony she remembered afterward, when she thought of it at all, chiefly for how little of it there was. No old king sat in the hall to hand her the crown as his own had been handed to him; the crown itself had lain fifteen years in a strongbox in the vein-house's custody, no one quite sure whose to keep it was until the custodians themselves sent it down under guard with a note, in a clerk's hand, that the crown of Dia-thìr was the crown of Dia-thìr whoever wore it, and the vein-house claimed no more of it than that. The heads of the seven houses swore to her one after another, in the order the old rolls gave them, and she watched their faces as they knelt and understood, better than she let herself say aloud that day, that not one of them knelt to her because they believed her the rightful queen. They knelt because the war had gone on ten years and killed enough of their own sons that none of them, not even the houses that had held for Raghnall to the last, could find in themselves the appetite to ask whether a fourth claimant might do better.

She was called Catrìona Bhuadhach from that year, Catrìona the Victorious, and she came, in time, to find the name harder to carry than the crown. A victory won because the losing side had exhausted itself first was not, she thought more than once in the quiet of the years that followed, a thing a person ought to be praised for as though it were a feat of arms; and yet the name stuck to her the way names do, said so often by so many mouths that in the end even she began to answer to it as though it were simply hers, the way a scar becomes simply part of a face.

At {{place:burg:491}} that same winter, Eòghann mac Dhùghaill kept the store as he had kept it through the whole of the refusal, and did not scrape clean the board on which Raghnall Ruadh's list still stood, face to the wall in the storeroom where no visitor saw it. When word of the coronation reached the mountain he sent down, with the season's ordinary account, a single line that the new queen's household never saw and that he never explained to anyone who asked: that the store had gone one whole season without doing the one thing the law had ever asked of it, and would not do so again while he held the beam.

## II. Of the Sentence in the Yard

Three years a queen taught her more of the shape of her own kingdom than ten years a claimant had. She rode the shires that had gone cold in the refusal and found some of them slow to trust a coal that had once been taken from them by a man wearing the same office she now wore; she rode the shires that had stood by Raghnall and found some of them slower still to trust a queen who had beaten their lord in the field. What she came to understand, riding between the two, was that the war had not truly ended at Muileann chrom. It had only stopped being fought with hosts. It went on being fought, in a smaller and more patient way, every time a reeve in a formerly hostile shire wondered aloud whether the new queen might, in a hard winter, do to his hearth what the old master of the vein-house had done to another's.

She went up to {{place:burg:491}} in {{year:III-0113}} to put a stop to the wondering, and found the seven heads of the houses less willing than she had hoped to have their own power bound in the same stroke that bound the crown's.

They met in the same yard where Eòghann had stood seven years before with the list in his hands, because Catrìona had asked for it to be the same yard, and had said, when one of the heads suggested the hall would be warmer, that the law they meant to write belonged to the place where it had been broken, not to a fire and a bench. It was a cold morning even for the mountain, the beam and the trestle still standing where they always stood, the measures hung on their pegs, and Eòghann himself sent for and set to stand at her shoulder, not to speak unless spoken to, only to be seen standing there while the houses argued over words.

The argument went three days. Every head agreed, in the first hour, that no crown ought to be able to do again what Raghnall Ruadh had done; agreement on that much cost nothing, since Raghnall was dead and none of the seven wished to be thought his heir in spirit. It was the second half of the sentence that cost something, and Catrìona had known, before she rode up the mountain, that it would. A law that bound only the crown left every house free to do, within its own galleries, exactly what Raghnall had done from the vein-house's own store; and three of the seven, when she said so plainly, went quiet in a way she had learned by then to read as houses doing arithmetic about their own galleries rather than about the law.

"You ask us to bind our own hands," said the head of Clann Ìomhair, an old woman named Beathag nic Uisdein who had held for Raghnall to the last field and lost two sons doing it, "for a wrong none of us has done."

"None of you has done it yet," Catrìona said. "Raghnall had not done it either, the morning before he did it. He had kept the store honestly for years before that morning. I do not ask you to swear you would never become him. I ask you to write it down so that if any of you ever does, the writing itself will already be older than the wrong, and no one will be able to say the law only learned to forbid it after the fact."

They wrote, and struck out, and wrote again, through the better part of three days, a clerk of the vein-house setting each draft on a fresh board and the seven heads picking at it word by word the way Catrìona had once seen carpenters pick at a joint that would not sit true, testing every phrase for the gap a future Raghnall might slip through. Eòghann was asked, on the second day, to say plainly what Raghnall had said to him in the yard, and did, in the same flat voice he might have used to read out a season's weights, and something in the flatness of it did more to settle the argument than any of the queen's own words had. A man who had stood where he stood, and could still say only "he gave me a list, and I measured no lie into the beam before that morning, and none after," was harder for any house to argue against than a queen with a claim to press.

What they set down at the end of the third day was one sentence, no longer than the one Raghnall had spoken in the yard seven years before, and Catrìona thought, hearing the vein-house's clerk read the final draft aloud in the cold, that there was a kind of justice in that, a wrong undone in exactly as many words as it had taken to do it: that no claimant, house or king might withhold the measured handful from any lawful hearth. The seven heads set their marks to it one after another, Beathag nic Uisdein last and longest, her hand steady enough on the board but her face, Catrìona thought, that of a woman signing away something she had not fully decided she wished to give up. It was the ruling of the hearth, the first law the record kept that bound the custodians themselves and not only the households that came to them, and it went into the custody-book beside the Small-Burning Law it was written to guard, in the same hand, on the same page, as though the two had always belonged together.

Eòghann kept his own copy of it, in his small cramped store-hand, and hung it on the peg beside the board that still faced the wall, and told no one, then or later, which board he meant a visitor to read first if ever he showed them the room.

Catrìona rode down from the mountain that third evening in a snow that had begun again in the afternoon and did not let up before she reached the low country, and found herself turning the sentence over in her own mind the whole way, testing it the way the houses had tested it, for the gap she might herself have left in it without meaning to. She had wanted, going up, a wall no future Raghnall could climb; what she had instead, she understood by the time the lights of the first shire town showed through the snow, was a fence with seven gates in it, one for each house, each gate held shut for now by seven heads who had watched what the withholding did to a mountain full of men no better and no worse than their own tenants. A fence was not a wall. She thought, riding through the dark, that it would have to serve, since no queen who had spent ten years learning what a crown could and could not compel was fool enough to believe a wall could ever truly be built at all; the most any law could do, she had come to think, was to make the breaking of it loud enough that the next generation would hear the noise before it happened again, not merely read of it after.

## III. Of the Clerk and the Road

A girl of seventeen came to the red hall the following year, sent up from {{place:burg:23}} by a Macha priest who had taught her her letters on the strength of nothing but her own asking, and stood in the yard a whole morning before anyone thought to ask her business. Her name was Seònaid nic Coinnich, and her father, Coinneach mac Ailein, a farmer of {{place:burg:316}}, had come through the two winters of the refusal with a cough he never afterward lost; she did not tell the queen this outright, that first morning, only that she could read and write in a fair hand and had walked from Cathair mhòr on the strength of a rumour that the household of a queen who had fought for the cold shires might have use for a clerk who remembered what the cold shires were.

Catrìona took her in without much ceremony, partly because the household's old clerk was failing and partly, she admitted to herself only once the girl was already settled in the scribes' room, because there was something in being served by a daughter of Doire mhin that felt to her like a debt being paid rather than a favour being given. Seònaid proved quick with a pen and quicker with a question, and within a year had made herself useful enough at the ordinary business of the household — tallies, petitions, the endless small grain of a court's paperwork — that no one thought any longer to remark she had come up from a burned granary with nothing but her letters.

The queen's own household, in those years, held its griefs quietly and in order, the way a well-run house holds everything. Her husband, Iain mac Eachainn, whom she had married in the second year of the war more for the alliance his house brought than for any great warmth between them, and had come in the years after to be genuinely fond of, in the unhurried way two people grow fond of each other across a shared table over enough winters, died in {{year:III-0115}} of a wasting in the chest that no physician in the hall could name or slow. He had never much liked to be spoken of as a king; he had kept, all his years in the red hall, to the smaller business of the household, the granaries, the stables, the endless small disputes among the servants, and left the larger business of the crown entirely to her, and she found, in the months after his death, that she missed most precisely that smallness, the sound of him settling some trifling quarrel between two grooms in the next room while she sat with a petition she could not settle at all.

Seònaid was not the only one of the cold shires' children the household took in during those years, though she was the one who stayed longest and rose highest; two others, a boy from Cathair mhòr and a girl from a burned granary near the ford, were given places among the household's servants and left again within a few years for households of their own, and Catrìona, who kept a rough tally in her own head of how many such places she had filled since her crowning, thought of it less as charity than as a kind of quiet accounting, a debt the crown owed the shires whose hearths a claimant's war had once cost, paid out a place at a time rather than all at once. Seònaid herself never spoke of the debt in those terms, not to the queen and not, so far as anyone in the scribes' room ever heard, to herself; she asked, in her first winter at the hall, only to be shown the custody-book's oldest entries, and spent whatever hours the ordinary work of the household left her copying out, in her own fair hand, the tallies of the refusal from the winters her own family went cold, a labour no one had asked of her and that she never fully explained, though the queen, coming on her at it once by lamplight, asked no questions either, only let her be.

She threw herself, that year and the years after, into the one piece of work that felt to her like it might outlast her own reign in a way no ruling of a court ever quite did: a road. Her own town, {{place:burg:166}}, lay a hard two days from the capital by the old cart-track, a road that turned to a mire from Bealltach to Lùghnas and stranded carts to the axle every wet spring, and she had grown up hearing her own kin at Inis thais complain of it the way people complain of weather, as a fact of the world rather than a thing that might be mended. She had the stone laid herself, in the years between {{year:III-0113}} and {{year:III-0114}}, south from the capital by a market town on the old track and past Am Buabhall Fortanach at {{place:burg:324}}, the old hostel Eilidh nic Chaluim had rebuilt in stone four centuries before, all the way down to her own gate at Inis thais; and she rode the whole length of it herself twice while it was building, in a cart with iron-shod wheels she had had made for no other purpose than to feel, under her own body, whether the stone held.

It came to be called Rathad na Banrighinn, the queen's road, though she had never asked for the name and could not, when a courtier first used it to her face, remember having given anyone leave to use it. The first carters to run the whole length of it did so within the year the last stone was laid, salt-carts and grain-carts that had once taken three days where the road now let them do it in two, and one of them, an old man who had driven the mire road for forty years before the stone came, told a rider of the household, in words that were carried back to her and that she kept, in her own way, longer than she kept most compliments, that he had driven the new road with his eyes shut half a mile at a stretch, only to feel the cart go steady under him the whole way, a thing he had never once been able to do on the old track without losing a wheel to it.

The building of the road cost her more of the treasury than any single work of her reign, and more than one head of house asked her plainly, in council, why a queen should spend so much stone on a track to her own town when the mire road had carried grain and salt well enough for three generations before her. She answered them, the one time the question was put to her outright, that she had not built it for her own town at all, though she did not trouble to hide that she was glad it reached her own gate; she had built it because a kingdom bound together by a law written in a cold yard on a mountain ought to be bound together, too, by roads that did not fail every spring, and that a house which could not be reached in the wet season by anything heavier than a laden mule was, in every way that mattered to a hearth in winter, as far from the crown as the far coast the fleet had gone looking for. Whether the council believed her reasons or only tired of arguing against a queen who had already begun laying the stone before the question was put to her, the road went on being built, mile by mile, through the years the vein-house's ruling was still settling into the custody-book's habit.

To {{place:burg:64}}, that had shut its own gates against all three claimants and been besieged twice for the shutting and taken by neither, she granted, in {{year:III-0115}}, the right to keep its own gates and take its own tolls, a thing no other town on the island held of the crown; and when the grant was cut over the landward gate in stone letters a mason's apprentice had spent a season shaping, she rode down to see it done and stood a while reading her own name in a hand not her own, thinking, as she had thought at her own coronation, that there was a kind of victory in a town's stubbornness that deserved more honour than her own had ever quite earned, since Seann Warr had held to nothing but its own gates, and never once had to be forgiven for choosing wrongly.

## IV. Of the Single Day

She grew old at the red hall the way her uncle Tormod Bàn's line had grown old before her, without the long unnatural years the first children of the island's oldest houses had once been given and had, one by one, been made to give up; she counted six-and-thirty years at her crowning and eight-and-seventy at her death, and never once, in all the years between, heard anyone remark on the length of her reign as though it were a wonder, only as though it were simply what a long life looked like, now, on Dia-thìr.

She died at {{place:burg:19}} in {{year:III-0116}}, in the same hall where she had been crowned with so little ceremony, and left behind her a son, Lachlann mac Iain, not yet forty, who had spent the better part of his own life at the edges of her court learning a trade he had never much sought and could not now refuse. He had been, by his mother's own choosing, kept away from the vein-house's business until he was old enough to understand what it had cost his mother's generation to bind it, and he came to the crown, in the weeks after her death, with a caution Catrìona herself had never had the leisure to learn slowly; he had learned it, instead, all at once, from watching what the writing of one sentence in a cold yard had taken out of his mother.

She had told him once, the only time he ever asked her outright what it had cost to write the ruling of the hearth, that the hardest part of the three days on the mountain had been the waiting afterward far more than the arguing, the years spent not knowing whether seven marks on a board would hold when the next hard winter came and tested them; and that a king who inherited the ruling already made would never know that particular waiting, which she counted, in her own way, as a mercy she had bought him at some cost and was glad to have bought. He remembered the saying, standing at her grave in a cold that reminded more than one of the older mourners of the yard at the vein-house forty years before, and thought that he understood it a little better now than he had when she first said it, though he would go on, in the quiet way of a man newly come into an office too large for him, understanding it better still for years afterward.

The seven heads swore to him within a single day of her burial, a thing the household's clerks remarked on at the time and the custody-book set down afterward as worth remarking, since no crown in living memory had passed so quickly or so quietly from one hand to the next. Beathag nic Uisdein, grown very old herself by then, was the first to kneel, and was heard by more than one witness to say, low, as she rose, that she knelt this time to the son of the woman who had made her put her own mark to a law she had once thought bound only her enemies, and had come, in the years since, to be glad of the binding.

Seònaid nic Coinnich stood at the back of the hall that day, no longer the girl who had walked up from Cathair mhòr with nothing but her letters but a woman near sixty herself, grey and steady, kept on through three changes of clerk beneath her because no one in the household could be found who knew the old rolls half so well. She had spent the two years before the queen's death gathering, at Catrìona's own asking, the memories of everyone still living who had fought in the war of the three claims — an old man who had stood at the ford, a store-master's son, a woman of Doire mhin who still remembered the cold with a plainness that made even Seònaid, who had lived it too, wince to hear it set down; and she stood in the hall that day of the swearing with the beginning of that work already half written in a chest in the scribes' room, and thought, watching the seven heads kneel one after another in the space of a single afternoon, that she was watching the last event her book would need before she could call it finished, though she did not yet have the last of its words.

It is told that she counted the swearings as they happened, without meaning to, the way a clerk counts anything set before her in order; that she reached seven and found herself still standing, listening to the new king's voice give its first answer in a hall that had heard four changes of ruler go far less smoothly than this one had; and that she went back to the scribes' room that same evening, before the feast that marked the day was even lit, and wrote at the top of a fresh leaf the opening words of the history of the war, in a hand she meant to be steady enough that no one who read it after her would doubt a single line of it.
