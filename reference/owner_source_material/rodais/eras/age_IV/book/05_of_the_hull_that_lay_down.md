# Of the Hull that Lay Down

*LE 6 – 9*

## I. Of the Launching of An Fhaoileag

Through the rest of that fifth year, and into the sixth, the frames on the strand below Seann Skell filled in. The town that had come down to stare at the bare ribs came down again to watch the planks go on, one strake and then the next, pegged to the frames with treenails of heart-oak and caulked with tow and pitch in the old way, though nothing else about her was old. Alasdair mac Iain walked her length every evening still, his palm on the wood, and by the moon of Gearras she had a deck, and a mast stepped through it into the keel he had laid with his own hands, and rigging that the rope-walks of Cuan shean had sent west by cart, coil on coil.

They called her An Fhaoileag, the gull, because she rode high and pale on the water when she was floated off the blocks, and because a boy on the strand said she looked like a bird about to go up. Alasdair had wanted no name at all, or a plainer one, but the wrights had built her and the wrights named her, and he did not argue it.

The mast went in on a cold, still morning with the whole fellowship standing round the step and Coinneach mac Fhearghais, oldest now of the first apprentices though he was not yet twenty, holding the foot of it steady while four men walked it up on sheer-legs of borrowed spruce. It went home clean, no binding, no easing with a maul, and the wrights took that for a good sign, the way wrights on the strand had always taken such things for signs, though none of them could have said what god or luck they meant by it. Rigging came down from Cuan shean in coils on a cart that took two days on the road, hemp still smelling of the fields it had grown in, and the strand spent a fortnight reeving it through blocks that had never held a rope so heavy, learning as they went which of their old coaster's knots would bear the strain and which would not.

On the morning of her launch the whole strand and half the town stood along the harbour-house wall to see her go down. A custodian of the vein came, at Mòrag's asking, and set a coal from Taigh na Fèithe in a clay dish at her step, as was done for a house's first hearth, because a ship going out onto open water for the first time was thought to want the same blessing as a house standing up for the first time. Calum mac Nèill wrote in his roll that day that the harbour-house had never held so many people at once, not even for the reading of the queen's word.

She took the water clean, without a shudder, and rode the wash of her own launching like a thing that had done it a hundred times. Alasdair stood at the harbour wall with his hands under his arms, as he had stood since he was a boy watching other men's boats, and for the length of that morning he let himself believe the thing was finished.

It was not finished. It had not yet begun.

## II. Of the Following Sea

A trial crew of fourteen took her down the Abhainn naomh three days later, to try her in open water before ever a household's goods went aboard her. Alasdair would not sail in her himself; he never went to sea in any hull he had built, and no one on the strand thought to ask him why. He walked instead along the north bank of the river with the tide, and came out at last on the headland above Ros àrsaidh, the point where the boats of Inis àrsaidh had always put out to the open sea, and stood there with his hands jammed under his arms in the wind off the water.

His sister's boy Tormod Beag was aboard her. He was fifteen that year, all wrist and elbow, and he had begged a place on the trial crew for a month before Alasdair gave it to him, more to be rid of the begging than for any other reason. Alasdair had told him to keep low and hold on and do nothing that was not asked of him. Tormod had grinned and said he would.

She came down out of the river mouth with the ebb under her and turned her head into open water, and for a while she went as well as any hull Alasdair had ever watched, better. The wind was out of the south-west, no more than the sea took every day of that season, and she carried it easily, heeling a little and coming up again. Two coasters out of Inis àrsaidh stood off to watch her pass, their crews idle on the oars, and one of the men called across the water that she went like something that had done it before, and the trial crew laughed and called back that she had not, not once, and the laughing carried clear to the headland where Alasdair stood. Alasdair began, against every rule he set himself, to be glad of her.

Then a sea came up astern of her, bigger than the ones before it, the kind that gathers itself out of nothing on a coast that has been building weather all week without quite letting it fall. He saw her stern lift to it. He had seen a hundred small boats lift to a following sea and come down again, and he waited for her to come down.

She did not come down soon enough. Her stern rose and her bow went under, and the water that should have run off her deck did not run off fast enough, because she was deeper and heavier and slower to shed a sea than any boat the island had ever built, and no one, not even Alasdair, had known that until that morning. She hung a moment with her bow buried and her stern high, and then she went over on her side as a tired animal lies down, not fighting it, folding into the water she had ridden so well an hour before.

There were fourteen heads in the sea and then there were fewer. Alasdair went down off the headland at a run, over ground he did not remember afterward crossing, shouting to the strand before he was near enough for shouting to do any good. Men came off the headland and off the strand at Inis àrsaidh in every boat that could be got into the water in time, oarsmen still buttoning their coats as they ran the boats down the shingle, and they brought back three living and the bodies of eight of the drowned; three more were never found at all, and the sea gave up nothing more of them that year or any year after. Tormod Beag was among the eight brought back. Alasdair carried him up the beach himself, though the boy weighed nothing at all by then, nothing a man his age should have been able to feel as light, and he did not let anyone else take the weight from him the whole way up to the boat-shed.

## III. Of the Names Cut at Inis àrsaidh

Inis àrsaidh kept no roll of its own like the harbour-house at Seann Skell; it had no need of one before that week. But it kept a board, as every fishing town on that coast kept a board, for the names of those the sea did not give back whole, and the old woman who kept it, whom the village called the roll-keeper though the office had no other name, brought it out and set it against the wall of the boat-shed and cut eleven names into it that night by the light of two lamps, working slowly because her hand was not steady and because there had never before been eleven names cut on one night.

The village sat up with her while she cut them. That was the custom, though no one could have said when it began: the cutting was not to be done alone, in case the knife slipped, or in case grief slipped, and there was no telling the two apart in the dark. Alasdair sat with the rest, on an upturned creel, and watched his nephew's name go into the wood letter by letter, and said nothing, because there was nothing he had any right to say that would not have sounded like an excuse.

They keened the eight that were found in the old way, the women's voices going up together and falling together, on the shingle where the boats were drawn up, with the bodies laid out under sailcloth and a lamp at each head. Alasdair's sister could not keen for her own son; she sat by him dry-eyed and silent while the other women's voices rose around her, and it was an aunt of the boy's father, come over from Ros bheag, who keened in her place, because the custom held that the loudest grief in a house is not always the one closest to the loss, and someone must carry it who still has a voice to carry it with. For the three that were not found there was no body to keen over, and the village did what was done in such a case: it lit a lamp on the shore all the same and let it burn down alone, and no one sat with that one, because there was no one to sit with.

Alasdair's sister did not come near him that night, and he did not go near her. He learned afterward, from his sister's husband, that she had said only one thing when the word reached her, which was that the boy had never once, in fifteen years, been given anything he had to ask for twice; she had thought, she said, that the asking might have been a kind of protection, and it was not. Alasdair carried that sentence a long way, and it did not get lighter with carrying.

He stayed at Inis àrsaidh three days, doing whatever was asked of him and nothing that was not, mending a net, hauling water, standing where he was told to stand. On the third day the roll-keeper found him sitting by the board in the daylight, reading the eleven names as though he had not already read them a hundred times in the dark, and she said to him, not unkindly, that a board could be read by a man once and remembered, and that reading it again and again was a way of staying somewhere a person had already left.

He went back to Seann Skell that evening, by the inland path rather than the road along the strand, where the second frame stood waiting for him under its canvas, so as not to see it until he was ready.

## IV. Of the Fire on the Strand

Eleven days passed before he was ready. On the twelfth, at night, with no one told he was going, he walked down to the tide-line below the blocks and built a driftwood fire, and sat by it with a stick, and began to draw a keel in the wet sand.

He drew the first one as he had built it: straight, deep, true along its whole length, a spine with the ribs rising square off it on either side, drawn on the day he laid it as the only honest line a keel could carry. He looked at it a long while. Then he rubbed it out with the flat of his hand and drew it again, and this time he took the straightness out of the forward third of her, where she met the sea first, and let it curve up and in, hollowing what had been full and filling what had been hollow toward her middle, so that the water coming at her from ahead would be turned aside along her sides instead of gathered up under her counter and carried aft to break her stern down as it had broken An Fhaoileag's.

He could not have said, that night, in words another wright could have taken away without watching him do it, what exactly he had changed. No single measurement carried it. The whole run of her forward body carried it, an inch here and half an inch there, multiplied along thirty feet of timber until the sum of it was a different animal. He rubbed it out a third time and drew it once more to be sure his hand had not lied to him the first two times, and it had not.

He sat by the fire until it burned down to nothing and the tide came up and took the sand smooth again, and by the time it did that he no longer needed the drawing. It was in his hands the way a tune is in a fiddler's hands after he has played it enough times to stop thinking about the notes, and it would stay there, and he would teach it to no one by writing it down, because there was no way he knew to write it down, only to show it, plank by plank, to the men who worked beside him until it was in their hands too.

## V. Of the Second Keel

The second keel was laid before the spring turned, on the same bed of oak blocks above the tide-line where the first had lain, cut from a timber of the Doire fhionn oaks that had been kept back, against Alasdair's own advice at the time, as too short for a deep keel and fit only for framing. It was not too short for what he needed of it now. The new run forward took less length to cut than the old, because the fault he was correcting lay in her lines and not in her size.

He would not let the wrights call her anything until she was planked. Some on the strand wanted to call her for Tormod Beag; Alasdair heard of it and said, through Coinneach mac Fhearghais, that the boy's name was not going onto a hull until a hull had been built that would not need to earn forgiveness for it. No one argued with him. Coinneach, who was seventeen that year and the steadiest of the young wrights, took over much of the day-to-day setting of frames that season, because Alasdair was often at the shed with the drawing rather than on the blocks, going over the new run again and again with a batten and a straightedge before he would trust his own hands a second time.

Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill sent silver down from Inis àrsaidh again that spring, as she had for the felling of the oaks, though no one had asked her a second time. Aonghas mac Fhionnlaigh carried it himself, and stood a while on the strand watching the new frames go up before he said anything of why he had come. He told Alasdair, plainly, that his wife had heard of the drowning within the week and had slept badly since, her own household being one that meant to sail aboard some hull of this same fleet, and that the silver came as a bargain rather than a charity: build her right, and build her sound, and Mòrag would answer for the timber and the cordage for as long as the fellowship needed either. Alasdair took the silver and said less than he was asked, which the strand had come to know as his way of saying yes.

There were wrights who doubted the new run before they had planked a foot of her, men who had built sewn boats all their lives and trusted a keel that lay straight the way a man trusts a rule he was taught as a boy. Alasdair did not argue with them either. He set two of the doubters to plank her forward sections, where the change ran deepest, and let the wood make the argument for him; by the time the garboard strake was hung and fair along her new run, fitting the frames without the wedging and forcing that a poor line always asks for, the doubt had gone quiet, though it had not, in every man, gone away.

By midsummer An Dara Fhaoileag stood in frame on the strand, lower in the bow than her sister had stood, fuller amidships, narrower where she met the water first. The town, which had come down twice before to stare at the ribs of a beast, came a third time and said less about it. They had learned, in the space of one season, that a hull on the strand was not a promise.

## VI. Of Those Who Struck Themselves Out, and Those Who Entered

Word of the drowning reached the harbour-house at Seann Skell before the bodies had finished coming ashore, carried by a man of Inis àrsaidh who had run most of the way. Calum mac Nèill wrote it into his roll that night in four lines, dated, and under it, because he did not know what else to do with a thing that size, he ruled a line across the whole leaf.

In the weeks after, eleven households struck themselves from the roll. None of these were the households of the fourteen who had sailed, most of whom had never been on the roll at all, being crew hired for the trial and no more than half-committed to the crossing; these were households from up and down the coast who had heard the telling and found that what had been a season's talk was now a thing that could happen to a son sent out to try a boat that was not even the ship that would carry him across the water. Calum cut the strokes through their names himself, as the rule required, and left the names standing to be read, as the rule also required, because the net-maker Iain's household had struck itself twice and been read, and the rule did not bend for grief any more than it had bent for anything else.

But in those same weeks eleven other households entered, the number was remarked on at the time and is remarked on still, though no one has ever found a reason for it beyond the coincidence of the count. Among them was a fishing household of Inis àrsaidh itself, kin of one of the three men brought back alive, who came to the harbour-house and gave their names and said, when Calum asked them why now of all times, that they had watched the sea take what it wanted once already and saw no more reason to fear it for waiting. A widow of Ros bheag entered too, a woman with no husband left to lose and two grown daughters who had asked to go before the drowning and been refused; she told Calum only that a mother who forbade the sea one week and could not answer for it the next had no business forbidding anything, and gave the names of all three.

Calum wrote both counts, the eleven struck and the eleven entered, on facing pages, and drew no line connecting them, and no comment under either. It is the one page in the whole roll where a later reader can see the shape of the thing without being told what to think of it.

## VII. Of the Rope-Walks of Cuan shean

At Cuan shean, a dozen miles down the coast, the news reached the harbour front within the day, and when it did it changed nothing that Iain Ròpaire could see in the work of his rope-walks, which had been going on from first light to last since the queen's word first came down.

Cuan shean had given over its hemp fields to the fleet the year the keel was laid, and by the sixth year of the age its harbour front was three long rope-walks end to end, each the length of a good cable, where the spinners paid out hemp from bundles at their waists and the walkers went backward down the walk with it, twisting the strands together at a steady pace that a man could keep up from dawn to dark if he did not think too hard about it. The walks ran from first light because hemp twisted best with the morning damp still in it, and by noon the whole harbour front smelt of it, green and sharp, a smell the older fishermen of the town said they would carry to their graves and be glad of. Iain had walked a rope-walk himself since he was younger than Tormod Beag had been, and he was master of all three now, a broad, slow-spoken man who counted his own steps under his breath without knowing he did it, and whose hands, from thirty years of the hemp, were harder across the palm than the sole of a boot.

The trouble, when it came, had nothing to do with the hemp; it was a trouble of ignorance. A rope-maker at Cuan shean knew his cordage the way Alasdair knew his timber, by hand and by eye, but he had never seen a keel or a frame or a mast-step, and he made his rope to lengths and weights that a written order gave him, without knowing what the rope was for or where the strain would fall on it. Two shroud-lines sent west that spring parted at the deadeye within a season, and the wrights sent word back up the coast, not a complaint exactly, more a puzzlement, asking why rope that had held on a coaster for ten years should fail on a hull that had not yet crossed open water once.

Alasdair sent two of his wrights north to Cuan shean that summer to answer it, Coinneach among them, and they did what neither side had thought to do before: they walked the rope-walks themselves, end to end, and named the parts of a hull to the spinners and the walkers as they went, stem and sternpost, garboard and futtock, the standing parts and the running, what took the ship's whole weight and what took only the wind's. Iain Ròpaire, who had never in thirty years thought of his trade as needing another man's trade explained to him, found that once he knew what a shroud held up he made a different rope for it without being told a different measure, and that the walkers, who had always kept their pace by nothing but habit, began without anyone teaching it to them to set the hull's own words into the rhythm of their feet, stem, stern, garboard, keel, over and over down the length of the walk, so that the work went no faster but somehow sat easier in the hand.

Iain never wrote any of it down. He had no cause to. But he took to walking the middle rope-walk himself again that summer, though he had not walked one in years, master's work being mostly standing and watching by then, because he wanted the words in his own feet before he trusted them to anyone else's. His wife asked him why, at his age, he was coming home with rope-burn on his palms like a boy's, and he told her only that a man who gave an order ought to know what it cost to carry it out. The households of Cuan shean, when they told each other years afterward how the town had come to make the fleet's best cordage, told it as the year the rope-walks learned the names of a ship.

## VIII. Of the Sail-Cloth of Baile àrsaidh

A little north of Cuan shean, in the shire of Baile àrsaidh, the levy on woven cloth had been running since the year the second household struck itself from the roll at Seann Skell, though it had no name yet beyond what the shire seat's tally-keeper called it in his own book: a length, close-woven, from every household that wove, owed once and reckoned against nothing, neither rent nor tithe.

He kept it on tally-sticks in the old way, a notch for a length delivered and a second cut for a length short, and by the ninth year of the age he had cut more notches than he had thought the shire held households that wove at all, because women who had never sent so much as a hank of thread to the shire seat before sent their close-woven lengths that year, some of them households with no one on the roll and no thought of ever being on it. He asked one such woman, an old weaver of a hill farm a long day's cart-ride inland who had not seen the sea since she was a girl, why she troubled to send cloth for ships she would never sail in and never see. She told him that her granddaughter had married into a household that had gone, and that a sail was the one part of a ship a weaver's hands could put something of herself into, and that she would rather her granddaughter went dry than went in a boat with a stranger's thread in her sail.

He cut her notch and said nothing to that, because there was nothing in his book for it, only a length and a date. He kept the sticks in a chest at the shire seat, one for each year of the levy, bundled with a thong and a wooden tag scratched with the year, and by the ninth year the chest wanted a second lid to close over the first. The tallies survive; how many households paid, and how many like the hill weaver paid for reasons the notch does not carry, is not now to be known, and the tally-keeper never thought to ask a second time, having learned from the first that the answer would not fit on his stick.

## IX. Of the Hull that Stood Beside Her Sister

By the ninth year of the age An Dara Fhaoileag was planked from keel to sheer and stepping her mast, and Alasdair, who had not let the wrights name her, at last let them, and they called her An Dara Fhaoileag, the second gull, because no better name came to hand and because the strand had grown used, by then, to naming its hulls for what they were rather than for what was hoped of them.

She stood on the strand where the first had stood, lower forward, fuller in the belly, narrower at the bow than any hull the island had built before her, and every wright who worked her hull now worked to the pattern that had been drawn once in wet sand and rubbed out twice before it was trusted. Alasdair walked her length in the evenings as he had walked the first, his palm on the wood, and if he still would not sail in any hull himself, no one asked him why any longer.

The rope for her rigging came down from Cuan shean already knowing, in the walkers' hands if nowhere else, what strain it would be asked to bear. Her sailcloth had come, close-woven, from households up and down a shire whose weavers had never seen a mast go up. Eleven names stood cut into a board at Inis àrsaidh that the tide never reached and the weather was already beginning to soften at the edges, and would go on softening for as long as anyone thought to keep the board upright. On the harbour-house rolls at Seann Skell, eleven strokes lay across eleven names on one page and eleven fresh names stood uncrossed on the facing one, and Calum mac Nèill had still found no comment that belonged under either.

Alasdair stood at the harbour wall in the last light of that ninth year and looked at the hull standing there, lower and fuller and, he believed, honest in a way the first had not been, and did not let himself be glad of her, not yet, not the way he had let himself be glad once before. He only trusted her, which was a smaller thing and, he had learned, a safer one, and it would have to do until the sea had had the chance to prove him right or wrong a second time.

Coinneach mac Fhearghais found him there after the light had gone and the strand had emptied, and asked him, since he had never once been answered the first time he asked it, three years before, whether the fleet would truly be finished within his own lifetime, or whether it was a thing being built for other men's sons to sail. Alasdair looked a long while at An Dara Fhaoileag, and past her at the black shape of the shed where the third already stood framed under its canvas, and said only that he did not know, and that not knowing was the one honest answer he had given anyone about any of it since the day he first read the roll in the harbour-house. Coinneach asked nothing further, and the two of them stood together at the wall a while in the dark, listening to the tide work at the stones below the new quay, until the cold sent them both in.
