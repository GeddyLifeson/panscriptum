# Of the King the Houses Made

*LE 125 – 211*

## I. Of Two Lines in One Book

At {{place:burg:19}} in {{year:III-0051}} King Raghnall mac Raghnaill died in his eightieth year, and the custody-book gave him, as it had given his father before him, a single line: the name, the year, and no more. His son Ìomhar mac Raghnaill was crowned in the same hall the same season, three-and-thirty years old and already the father of a boy of his own, and it is told that the old men of the court, remembering the father's crowning three-and-thirty years before, said there was little to choose between the two days beyond the faces, the same silver cup passed from hand to hand and the same oath said in the same words over it. The reign that followed was long and it was quiet, and a quiet reign, as the harbour scribes of the west coast had already learned to say of Raghnall mac Uilleim's, is the kind that leaves the least for any book to hold.

Yet the years were not empty, only unhurried, and a teller who wishes to be honest with them must find room for what filled them, small as it was beside a crowning or a death. At Muileann chiar, where a seam of silver had been worked since the Holy Age within four families who called themselves the smiths of the seam, {{year:III-0052}} saw the fellowship's oldest craft turned at last to a use beyond its own trade: rings of the pale metal, broad enough to sit close about the throat, beaten out for the heads of the seven houses of custody to wear at the vein-house and at the red hall alike, so that a stranger new to the court might know a custodian from any other lord by the collar at his neck before a word was said. Inis mhòr in the north, in those same years, put barges on the Abhainn ghorm to carry its grain down to Caol bheag and along the coast to Cnoc bheag, sewn-plank boats of the old pattern that never lay a night out of sight of land, for no one on the island since the fleet had wished to be out of sight of it; and it is told that a boy of Inis mhòr, watching the first barge loaded, asked his father why a boat so laden should not simply go the shorter way across open water, and got for his answer only a look, and no word at all.

In {{year:III-0054}} a coughing sickness ran through Caol fhiadhaich on the south-west coast and killed some ten of its people before it burned itself out, fewer than a hard winter alone might have taken, and the reeve of that shore held, afterward, that it was the shared meal kept through the whole of the sickness, every household eating at the one board rather than shutting its door against its neighbours, that had kept the number so small. It is told that the reeve's own wife was the first to fall sick, and that she herself would not let her own door be barred against the board, saying that a household that ate apart out of fear would go on eating apart long after the fear had any cause, and that a shore so small could not afford the habit. She lived, and the custom lived after her: the board was still shared at Caol fhiadhaich, sickness in the shore or none, when the cough was remembered by no one but the reeve's own book, and it is told there, in a hand grown old, that the meal had by then outlived the memory of why it was ever begun.

Whether the meal or the mercy of the season deserved the credit for how few the cough took, no one at Caol fhiadhaich troubled to settle, holding that a shore glad of its own dead being few had better things to spend its arguing on than the reason for it.

It is in the leaves of the vein-house, not the red hall's, that the fullest word of those years is kept, for Ìomhar's own reign left the crown's scribes little to enter beyond the ordinary turning of the shires, while the custodians of Muileann dhearg went on measuring the handful, hearth by hearth, exactly as Fionnlagh Dall had first measured it, the Small-Burning Law untouched by a change of king any more than the tide is touched by one. The scribe who kept those leaves after old Donnchadh mac Thormoid — a younger cousin of his own line, trained at Donnchadh's own elbow in his last years and given the boards on his death — wrote of King Ìomhar chiefly that he came up to the mountain twice in every year to see the galleries walked and the measures sealed, as the kings of his line had done since Beathag's day, and asked, each time he came, after the health of whichever house's head happened to be standing nearest him, a small courtesy that cost him nothing and that more than one house remembered of him long after they had forgotten anything else he had ever said or done.

He is remembered at Muileann dhearg, too, for one habit no king before or after him is recorded as keeping: he would stand at the mouth of whichever gallery was being walked that day and ask the youngest hand present, whether a bound child of seven years or a journeyman of twenty, to name for him the parts of the working he could see from where he stood, the prop, the cut, the tally-mark on the wall showing how far that season's measure had already gone. Some of the custodians thought it a king's idle amusement and no more; the vein-house's own scribe, who watched him do it a dozen times across as many years, wrote once that it seemed to him less amusement than a kind of counting of his own, a king who had no child to leave the hall to making certain, gallery by gallery, that the hands who would go on working it after him at least knew what they held in them. He never said as much himself, and no one thought, while he lived, to ask him.

## II. Of a Boy at Baile Mòr dhomhain

Fearchar mac Lachlainn was born in the house of Clann Fhearchair at {{place:burg:273}}, the elder son, and yet the one who, by every reckoning his own family made in his boyhood, was least likely ever to be asked to carry anything heavier than his own name. His younger brother Iain was the one their father set, from the time either of them could walk, to keep the house's hall when he should be gone; Fearchar was given instead to the vein-house's own custom, a boy's seven years bound to a custodian's household to learn the galleries and the measures as every child of the seven houses was meant to learn them, whether or not he was ever to sit at the head of his own table. He came home from that binding at fourteen knowing the weight of a sealed measure by its heft alone and the sound a gallery makes when its timbering has begun, quietly, to fail, knowledge that served him, in the years that followed, rather better than anyone in his own house had thought to expect it would.

He kept, into his own old age, one memory of those seven years that he told often enough to his own children that it passed down in the house of Clann Fhearchair as a small story of its own: a night in his eleventh year when the custodian he was bound to woke every hand in the gallery, the boy among them, because a timber had begun to sing, a low steady note none of the older men could place until the custodian herself set her ear against the wood and named it, and had the whole gallery cleared before the prop gave way an hour later with no one beneath it. Fearchar remembered standing outside in the cold with the other bound children, none of them told plainly what the noise had meant, and understanding all the same, from the set of the grown faces around him, that the vein answered to more than a measure and a season, and that a house's right to the coal was worth exactly as much as the care the house put into listening for what the coal had to say. It was a lesson, he used to tell his own sons afterward, that no crown he ever wore taught him any better than a mountain gallery had taught a boy of eleven standing in the dark.

He was seven years old, by his own later telling, when word came to Baile Mòr dhomhain that Am Buabhall Fortanach, the old road-house on the mountain road, had burned to its footings in {{year:III-0055}}, and that its keeper Eilidh nic Chaluim meant to raise it again in stone rather than in the timber it had always stood in before. He remembered, or believed he remembered, riding out with his father to see the new walls going up, and being given, by Eilidh herself, a bowl of the jugged buffalo the house was already known for before its own roof was finished, and thinking, as a boy of seven thinks of such things, that a house built to outlast fire was a fine thing for a man to leave behind him, better than a crown, which he had not then any reason to think about at all.

Thirteen years old, Fearchar heard his father's letters tell the house of Clann Fhearchair that King Ìomhar's only son, Raghnall Beag, had died of a fever at {{place:burg:19}}, a boy not much older than himself, and he remembered his father saying, low, over the letter, that a king with no other child was a house with one wall standing, and that the wind would find it sooner or later whatever anyone did. Fearchar did not understand, at thirteen, what his father meant by it, and did not ask, judging correctly enough that a boy who asked too many questions of a letter that troubled his elders was likely to be sent from the room before he got an answer.

The spirit-house the keepers of the Old Spirits raised at {{place:burg:315}} in {{year:III-0056}}, where the dead of the eastern shires had their names cut new on wooden posts each generation as the old posts rotted away, was a thing Fearchar came to know only at second hand, for Baile Mòr dhomhain kept its own dead by its own older custom and sent no names east to be cut on another shire's posts; but it is told that he visited it once as a young man, on a journey with his father to the eastern coast, and stood a long while before the posts reading names he knew nothing of, and said afterward only that a shire that cut its dead's names in wood rather than stone must trust its own memory further than he would care to trust his.

He was three-and-twenty, newly married and settled to what he believed would be the whole of his life, keeping his own small share of his house's galleries and answering to his brother Iain in every matter that touched the hall, when word reached Baile Mòr dhomhain in {{year:III-0057}} that King Ìomhar mac Raghnaill had died at {{place:burg:19}} with no living child, the line that ran back through Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill and the queen who had neither sent her sister nor kept her ended in a single sentence in the custody-book, and that the heads of the seven houses were summoned to the capital to choose, for the first time since the sunwise turn, a king the crown's own blood had not simply handed down to them.

## III. Of the Silences in the Yard

Iain went up to {{place:burg:19}} as the head of Clann Fhearchair's own house, for their father was three years dead by then and had named him to the headship, and Fearchar went with him only as an elder brother who kept the galleries and had no more claim on the meeting than any other custodian. He remembered the yard of the red hall as smaller than he had pictured it from his father's tellings, and colder, the seven neck-rings of Muileann chiar catching what little sun there was as the heads stood in a loose ring about the well and said, for the better part of a morning, nothing at all that any of them could afterward be brought to repeat in full.

It was the head of Clann Ìomhair who broke the silence first, saying only that the line of Beathag had held the hall a hundred years and more and that no house among the seven had any better claim to it than another, which was less an argument for any one name than a plain statement that the argument, once begun, would not end quickly; and it was the head of Clann Mhuirich, the oldest of the seven present and the one whose own house had given most of the Holy Age's Keepers, who said, after a further silence, that a hall so long kept by inheritance might do better kept, this once, by choosing, since a house chosen rather than born to the crown would know from its first day what it owed the six that had chosen it. No one answered her directly, but the silence that followed her words was of a different kind than the one before it, and Iain, standing at his brother's shoulder, felt the meeting turn, though he could not afterward have said on what hinge.

They spoke Iain's own name before the day was out, as head of a house whose galleries ran deep and whose dealings with the other six had never once, in living memory, come to open quarrel, and it was Iain himself who turned it aside, saying plainly that a man who kept one house's hall well enough might yet keep a kingdom's poorly, and that he for one would rather be remembered as a fair head of Clann Fhearchair than a middling king of Dia-thìr. A murmur went round the ring at that, less agreement than the sound seven tired people make when a hard morning has at last produced one sentence plain enough to hold onto, and it was into that murmur that Iain named his own brother, standing beside him in the yard with nothing to say for himself, a custodian of no particular note who had spent his own seven years among the galleries and knew, better than any man born to a hall, what the vein-house asked of the houses that answered to it.

Fearchar felt every face in the ring turn to him at once, and remembered afterward that his first thought, before fear or refusal or any grander feeling had time to reach him, was a plain wish that he had worn a better coat, having ridden up from Baile Mòr dhomhain expecting nothing of the day beyond standing at his brother's shoulder and going home again unremarked. The head of Clann Raghnaill, a broad man with a scar across one brow who had said nothing at all until then, asked him outright, in the flat way of a custodian asking after a season's measure, whether he could read a gallery's timbering by ear, and Fearchar, too startled to judge whether the question was mockery or something else, answered honestly that he could, or had been able to once, as a bound child, and had had no cause since to test whether the ear for it stayed with a man once his seven years were done. It is told that the head of Clann Raghnaill said nothing further to that, only nodded once, as a custodian nods when an answer has satisfied a question he did not trouble to explain the reason for asking.

Fearchar's own account of what followed, given years later to his son and kept in the family's own memory rather than in any leaf of the vein-house, was that he tried three times to refuse before the heads would hear him refused, saying first that he was a younger son with no training for a hall, then that his own house had a better claim in his brother, and last, when neither answer moved them, that he did not want the thing, which he judged, and rightly, to be the one argument likeliest to move seven heads who had spent a whole morning weighing a crown too heavy for any one house to want alone. It was the head of Clann Mhuirich who answered that last refusal, saying that a man who did not want the crown was, of the men in that yard, the only kind she would trust to wear it, and it is told that no one spoke again after she had said it, the seven neck-rings catching the last of the light as the heads, one after another, bent their own heads to a man who had not asked to be chosen and could give them no better reason to choose him than that he had not asked.

He was crowned at {{place:burg:19}} in {{date:III-0058}}, five-and-twenty years old, the first king in the custody-book's own memory set on the hall by seven houses standing in a cold yard until their silence turned, of its own weight, into a name, rather than by the blood of the hall before him. No silver cup was found ready for the rite, the last having been put away at Ìomhar's death with no clear order for its keeping, and it is told that the head of Clann Mhuirich sent a servant running to her own lodging for the cup her own house kept, a plainer vessel than the crown's own would have been, so that Fearchar mac Lachlainn drank his first oath as king from a cup that belonged, strictly, to none of the seven houses and to one of them alone, a small confusion no one thought worth correcting once the oath was said.

## IV. Of the Double Court

He kept the crown nine-and-twenty years, and it is told at Baile Mòr dhomhain, though seldom at the red hall, that he never once in all those years stopped thinking of himself as a custodian who happened to sit a throne rather than a king who happened to know the galleries. He divided his court from the second year of his reign, keeping the greater part of each year at {{place:burg:19}} as every crowned ruler before him had done, and a lesser part at {{place:burg:273}} among his own house and within an easy ride of the vein-house, so that no matter that touched the coal need wait on a rider's journey to reach him. The double court settled, by {{year:III-0059}}, into a custom his own house kept for as long as it held the crown, and it was Iain who kept Clann Fhearchair's hall at Baile Mòr dhomhain whenever the court was away at the capital, a headship within a headship that suited him, by his own account, a great deal better than a crown ever would have. The two brothers wrote to one another through every season of the division, letters that a later hand at Baile Mòr dhomhain gathered and kept as one leaf-book rather than let scatter through the house's papers, and it is from those letters, more than from anything the red hall's own scribes set down, that the plainer texture of the reign is known: a dispute over a boundary stone between two shires settled by Fearchar in three lines and referred back to Iain to see enforced; a petition from a widow of Muileann dhearg for a second season's measure after a bad year, granted; a complaint, more than once repeated in Iain's own hand, that the capital's cooks put too much salt in a dish that had never wanted it at Baile Mòr dhomhain, over which the two brothers seem to have corresponded at greater length than over any single matter of the coal in the whole of the reign.

Fishers of {{place:province:57}}, on the eastern coast, joined in one fellowship in {{year:III-0060}} after a season of losses no single household could carry alone, three boats lost in one autumn's weather and two households left with neither boat nor the timber to build another, sharing boats and nets thereafter among families who had fished the same waters apart from one another for longer than any of them could say why. A fisherman of that shore, sent up to Baile Mòr dhomhain the following spring on some ordinary errand of tribute, was brought before the old king almost by accident, the household he lodged with having mentioned, in passing, that he came from the coast where the new fellowship had lately been made, and Fearchar kept him a full hour asking after the manner of it: how the boats were shared, whose name a catch was entered under, what was done when one household's boat failed and another's held. The fisherman, by his own account given, years afterward, to his own grandchildren, had expected a king's questions to be grander than a fellowship's own accounting, and found himself instead answering much the same questions any custodian might have asked of a gallery's sharing among the seven houses, and it was only riding home that it came to him what the old king had truly been asking after all along: the fellowship, and whether a thing built from loss could be trusted to hold once the loss was forgotten.

The founding was read out, when word of it first reached Baile Mòr dhomhain, as a small thing beside the coal and the crown, though Fearchar himself is said to have remarked, after that hour with the fisherman, that a fellowship made from loss rather than plenty was likely to outlast one made the easier way, having already proved, before it was a year old, that its members would stand by one another when standing by one another cost something. It was a saying his own house kept after him, said sometimes of the coal-right when the seven houses' own dealings grew strained, though never, so far as the leaf-book records, in his own hearing.

He grew old at the work slowly and without complaint, riding between his two halls until the riding grew hard, and in his last years he kept, more than any other habit of his reign, the one he had carried up from his own boyhood among the galleries: he went on asking the youngest hand at any working he visited to name what stood before him, prop and cut and tally-mark, though by then his own knees would no longer let him crouch at a gallery's mouth to hear the answer, and a custodian would crouch for him and repeat the child's words up into the light. His own son Fearchar Donn, brought to the mountain young as his father had been, was asked the same question at seven years old as every other bound child was asked it, and gave, by the account kept at Muileann dhearg, an answer no better and no worse than any other child's, which the old king is said to have called, laughing, the first honestly ordinary thing his own house had produced in two reigns.

He died, in the end, at {{place:burg:273}} in {{year:III-0061}}, among his own house rather than at the red hall, four-and-fifty years old, his brother Iain at his bedside and his own son, called Fearchar Donn for the colour of his hair, already a grown man ready to take the hall without dispute from any of the seven. He asked, in his last days, that no coal be burned beyond the ordinary handful for any rite made over him, saying that a custodian who had spent a lifetime measuring the vein for other households had no wish to be measured out of it more generously than any tenant of his own galleries would be, and the vein-house kept the asking to the letter, so that the fire lit for the old king's own rite was, by every account, no larger than the fire any house of the seven lit for its own dead that same season. They buried him, as his own father had been buried, in the ground of Clann Fhearchair rather than in any royal tomb at the capital, and it is told that the seven houses sent their heads to stand at the grave, each still wearing the neck-ring of Muileann chiar his own house had worn since before Fearchar was crowned, and that not one of them, in the whole of the burying, was heard to say that the choosing made in that cold yard nine-and-twenty years before had been anything other than the right one. Fearchar Donn was crowned at {{place:burg:19}} within the season, without dispute from any house, the first son to follow a father the houses themselves had made, and so the crown, having once been chosen rather than inherited, went back, as if it had never left it, to the plainer road of a son following a father into the hall.
