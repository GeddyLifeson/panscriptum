## I. Of the Queen on the New Stones

Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill came down to Seann Skell in the moon of Sìolach, when the barley stood green and short in the strips above the river and the last of the fleet's rigging was going up on hulls that had, the year before, been only frames on the strand. She came with a small household, six riders and a groom and no more, and she had told no one at the harbour-house to expect her; the first word Niall mac Dhòmhnaill's father had of the queen's coming was the queen herself, standing in the door of Taigh a' Chalaidh out of the rain with her hood still up, asking whether the harbour scribe was within.

He was. Calum mac Nèill had kept the boards of Seann Skell for thirty-nine years by then and had stood before three kings and one queen at An Talla Dearg in that time, and he still, his son said afterward, went white to the lips when he saw who stood in his own doorway. He gave her the chair kept for the reeve when the reeve came to check the rolls, and stood himself, and she let him stand, because there are things a queen does not trouble to change in another man's house.

She did not ask to see the boards. She had had copies of the roll sent to Cathair dhearg every season since the boards were first hung, and she knew, better than most men at An Talla Dearg, how many households had gone onto them and how many had come off again; there was nothing in the wood that the copies had not already told her. What she asked for, when Calum had got his voice back enough to ask what she wanted of him, was to walk the quay.

He walked with her himself, a half-step behind and to the side as the custom of the harbour-house had it, past the fish-boats at the old stones and out along the new dressed work the masons had laid nine years before, and the hulls of the fleet lay against it the whole length of the walk, nineteen or one-and-twenty of them, riding a little high yet for want of their full stores, their new-cut timbers pale against the darker, salted wood of the coasters moored further down. Beathag stopped at no one hull longer than another. She walked the length of the quay once, from the fish-stones to the western end where the water ran deep enough at low tide for the largest of the keels, and stood there a while with the wind off the sea working at the edge of her hood, and said, to no one in particular, that she had not thought, when the first crew came up the river road with its tale of a far shore, that she would live to see so many hulls built on an island that had never built one.

Calum said nothing to that. It did not seem a thing that wanted an answer.

She found her sister on the way back, though whether she had come down to Seann Skell to find her, or had only known she would, Calum could not afterward have said, and did not ask. Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill was on the strand below the new quay with a coil of rope over one shoulder, helping a rope-maker's boy stow gear against the weather that was coming in off the water, her sleeves rolled and her hands as roughened, by then, as any fisherwoman's on that shore; and when she straightened and saw who stood above her on the stones, she came up the slip at her own pace, neither hurried nor slowed, wiping her hands on her skirt as she came, the way she might have come up to greet any traveller who had asked for her at the harbour-house door.

The two sisters stood together on the new stones a little apart from Calum and from the queen's household, and what passed between them there, no one who stood by ever told, not Calum, who did not go close enough to hear, and not the two riders who stood at the queen's back with their eyes on the weather and not on the queen. It was not a long conversation. Leabhar a' Chùraim, when it comes at last to speak of that day at all, gives it in one line: that the queen came to Seann Skell in Sìolach and was seen upon the quay with her sister, and went home again before the sailing, as her word of long before had bound her to do.

It is told, though not in Leabhar a' Chùraim, that Beathag put her hand once to the back of Mòrag's head, the way an elder sister will to a younger one who has not stopped being the younger one no matter how many years or how many miles come between them, and that Mòrag let her, and that neither of them wept where Calum or the riders could see it, whatever was done with the weeping once the queen's household had ridden back up the river road and Mòrag had gone down again to the rope-maker's boy and the gear that wanted stowing before the weather came in.

Beathag did not stay the night. She had told Calum, on the walk out to the western stones, that she would not be at Seann Skell for the sailing, whenever it came, because a queen who has sworn to send no one over and keep no one back has no business standing on a quay to watch her own sister taken from her by a promise she made herself; and Calum, who had thought a great many things about the queen's word in the thirty-seven years he had kept it written above his own door, thought that the truest thing he had yet heard said of it. She rode north before dusk, in the rain that had held off all day and came down at last as her household cleared the last houses of the town, and Niall, who was ten that spring and had watched the whole of the queen's visit from the harbour-house step with his grandfather's hand on his shoulder to keep him from getting underfoot, remembered afterward chiefly the sound of the horses going away up the river road in the wet, and his grandfather standing a long while in the doorway after the sound had gone, looking at nothing that Niall could see.

## II. Of the Lamps on the Sills

The fleet's full stores went aboard through the whole of Bearnach, the gap-moon between the last of the winter's keep and the first green of the year, when the granaries stand at their lowest and every household on the island counts what is left in the barrel and reckons how far it will stretch. Niall was eleven that Bearnach, and had grown, in the two years since he had run the quay counting hulls and losing the count, into the age at which a harbour scribe's grandson is given work rather than only allowed to watch it; and the work his grandfather gave him, on the last evening before the sailing, was a hazel stick shaved clean along one face and a small knife with a worn horn handle, and the instruction, given once and not repeated, that he was to sit on the wall of the west quay when the fleet went out in the morning and cut one notch for each hull as she passed, and no talking.

He had heard, by then, every telling there was to hear of why the count mattered and why no one trusted a single teller's word for it alone; the harbour-house kept three tallies of everything that passed its boards, his grandfather had told him more than once, because two men's memories never agreed and a third was needed to settle which of the first two lied, though neither, as often as not, had lied at all, only remembered differently. Niall took the stick and the knife with both hands, the way he had been shown to take anything his grandfather gave him that was not a toy, and did not ask what would be done with his tally once it was cut, because a boy of eleven who has grown up in a harbour-house learns early that some questions get no answer and are better not asked twice.

That evening, as the light went, every house along the Seann Skell waterfront set a lamp on its sill, and kept it burning. No one had told them to. It began, so far as anyone could afterward say, at the fish-stones end of the quay, where a widow whose only son was going out with the fleet lit her own lamp early against the dusk and left it burning past her own bedtime because she had nothing else that evening to do with her hands; and it went on from house to house along the strand the way a rumour goes, without any one person carrying it, until the whole curve of the harbour was pricked with light from the fish-stones to the new western stones where the fleet lay. Niall's grandmother Seonag lit their own lamp at the harbour-house window without being asked, and when Niall asked her why, she said only that a lit window was a thing a body understood without being told what it meant, and that some things were better left at that.

Niall went down to the quay before the lamps had all been lit, because he could not sit still in the house that evening and his grandfather, for once, did not tell him to. The hulls lay close against the new stones, so close in places that a boy could have dropped a pebble onto a deck without much trying, and the whole length of the quay smelled of pitch and wet hemp and, under both, the tallowy reek of the sheep's fat the crews had been rubbing into the blocks and running-gear all that week against the salt to come. Somewhere aboard one of the nearer hulls a woman was singing under her breath, some working song with no words Niall could catch, and a man's voice told her, not unkindly, to hush, and she did, and started again a little later when he had gone forward.

He found his aunt Mairead's household aboard the third hull from the western end, a low broad-waisted ship named, so far as anyone had told him, for nothing he had heard, her deck crowded with barrels lashed down under oiled hide and her people moving about the business of a last evening with the particular quiet haste of those who mean to be done with the loading before dark. Mairead saw him on the quay wall and came to the rail, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a smear of pitch on one wrist, and asked him, in the ordinary voice she had always used to him, whether his grandfather had given him a proper task for the morning or meant to leave him idle on the wall like any other spectator.

He showed her the stick and the knife. She looked at them a moment longer than the showing needed, and then she said that he was to cut a good deep notch for her hull when it came, whichever number it fell at, so that whatever else the tally lost or kept, hers would be cut clean; and she reached down over the rail and put her hand once, briefly, on the top of his head, the same gesture, though he had no way of knowing it, that the queen had used on her sister on the new stones two moons before. Then her husband Dùghall called her back to the lashings, and she went, and Niall sat on the wall a while longer with the stick across his knees, watching the lamps come out along the whole curve of the strand behind him, one and then another, until there was no dark house left between the fish-stones and the western end.

His grandfather found him there past full dark and did not send him home. Calum stood beside him a long while without speaking, his eyes on the lit windows behind the hulls rather than on the hulls themselves, and it was Calum, in the end, who spoke first, saying only that he had kept the boards of that harbour for near forty years and had thought, when the first name went onto them, that he understood what the roll would cost the town; and that he had been wrong about the size of it, though not, he thought, about the shape. Niall did not understand all of what his grandfather meant by that, and did not ask him to explain it, because the night did not seem to want explaining. They sat together on the wall until the cold drove them in, and behind them, the whole length of the waterfront, the lamps burned on into a night that no one along that shore, by every later telling, slept much through at all.

## III. Of Mòrag's Last Walk

Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill walked the waterfront of Seann Skell alone that same night, from the fish-stones to the western stones and back again, twice over, though no one had asked her to and she told no one she meant to do it.

She had spent the whole of that Bearnach at the harbour, seeing her own household's gear stowed and her husband Aonghas's and, in the last days, the gear of her grown daughter Beathag Òg's household besides, for Beathag Òg had married into a Ros bheag family two years before and had brought her own name onto the roll within the year, mother following daughter as often, by then, as daughter followed mother; and Mòrag had not, in all that season's labour, found an hour to walk the quay simply to look at it, the way a person looks at a room she means to leave the following morning and will not see again.

She did it that night instead. The lamps were lit along every sill by the time she started out, and she walked past them slowly, naming the households to herself as she passed each house. She needed no reminding who lived where; it seemed to her, she told her husband afterward, a thing worth doing once before she could no longer do it: to walk a street she had known the whole of her life and put a name to every lit window on it, as though the naming were itself a kind of leave-taking that the sailing in the morning would not have room for.

She found Catrìona nic Iain on the deck of An Ròn Glas, the hull that would carry Mòrag's own household and Aonghas's and a dozen more of Cuan shean's people besides, coiling a line at the foot of the steering oar with the particular unhurried care of a pilot who has done the same work a hundred times and means to do it once more, correctly, before it matters. Catrìona had been made pilot of that hull at Seann Skell the year before, on the quay, the same evening she had first named Reul na Stiùire to Mòrag by its Dia-thìris name, and the two women had not, in the year since, had much cause to speak beyond the ordinary business of a hull being fitted for sea; but Catrìona straightened when she saw who stood at the rail, and said, without being asked, that the oar answered well and the ship rode true in the little chop of the harbour, and that whatever else the morning brought, it would not bring a fault in the steering.

Mòrag thanked her for it, and stood a while at the rail looking down the length of the deck where her own people's gear lay lashed among Cuan shean's, and asked Catrìona, once, whether she was afraid. Catrìona thought about it before she answered, which Mòrag afterward said she had liked in her more than any answer the girl might have given straight off, and said at last that she was less afraid of the sea than of the not-knowing what lay past it, and that she supposed those were, in the end, close to the same fear worn two different ways. Mòrag said she thought that was true of most of them on the roll, and left it there, because there did not seem, that night, to be a better thing to say about it than the plain truth already said.

It was on her second pass along the strand, going back toward the fish-stones in the last of the evening light, that she saw the man standing by his cart at the head of the slip below the harbour-house, a tall man in a carter's coat with a horse still in the traces beside him, neither loading the cart nor unloading it, only standing, the way a man stands who has come to a place to decide something and has not yet decided it. She knew him, a little, the way a household of the roll comes to know the carters who bring goods down to the quay for those still packing; his name, she had heard once and did not forget, was Fionnlagh, and he had brought loads down to the western stones three times that season without, so far as she had ever seen, putting his own name to the boards.

She did not go to him, and he did not come to her, only passed within a stone's throw of where he stood, close enough to see that his cart was empty, and that he watched the lamps along the strand with the same stillness with which she had, herself, watched Catrìona's hands on the coiled line. Something in the set of him, she said to her husband that same night, told her plainly enough that here was a man who had already made his choice and stood at his cart only to be certain of it one more night before the boards closed for good. It did not seem, then, a thing a stranger should interrupt, so she walked on without speaking to him; and in the morning, when the fleet went out, his cart stood in the same place, and he stood beside it still, and did not go aboard any hull that passed.

Mòrag went back to the house she had taken near the fish-stones as the last of the light went, and sat up late with Aonghas over a fire that neither of them let go out, and said little of what she had seen or felt on her two walks along the strand, because there did not seem, between two people who had already said everything that could be said about the morning to come, to be much profit in saying it twice.

## IV. Of the Sunwise Turn

On {{date:III-0023}}, in the grey before sunrise, with the lamps along the waterfront burned low but none of them yet gone entirely out, the fleet of the willing went out from Seann Skell.

Niall was on the wall of the west quay by the time the first grey showed over the eastern hills, the stick and the knife in his lap and his knees drawn up under his chin against the cold that comes off water before the sun is properly up. The town had gathered along the whole length of the strand behind him, more people than he had ever seen crowded onto that stretch of stone at once, and yet, he remembered afterward, it was a quiet crowd, quieter than a market day or a feast day, as though everyone on the waterfront had agreed, without a word passed between them, that there was nothing that morning that wanted shouting about.

The first hull came off the quay a little before the sun cleared the hills, warped out from the stones on lines that a dozen hands ashore paid out together, and swung her bow round, away from the straight run down the river toward the open water, toward the head of the pool where the Abhainn naomh widened below the town before narrowing again to the sea. Niall had heard his grandfather say, more than once in that last week, that the fleet meant to go out sunwise round the pool for the luck of it, the way a man walks sunwise round a well he means to drink from, or round a house new-built before he sleeps his first night in it; but seeing it done was another thing from hearing it told, and he watched the first hull swing wide and slow with something closer to wonder than to the grief he had half expected of himself.

He cut his first notch as she passed him, close enough along the quay wall that he could have named half her crew by sight, and did not look long at any one face among them, because his grandfather had told him, the one instruction given twice that whole week, that a counter who looks too long at faces loses his count, and Niall meant, that morning above every other, not to lose it.

An Dara Fhaoileag came after her before the first had gone half round the pool, and the third close on the second, until the whole fleet was strung out across the water in a long curving line, nineteen hulls or one-and-twenty, going round the pool sunwise in the grey light with the town silent along the strand behind them. Niall cut his notches steadily, one, two, three, murmuring the count under his breath the way his grandfather murmured a leaf's entries while he wrote them, and did not let a gull's cry or a call across the water turn his eyes from the stick and the passing hulls the way it had turned them, two years before, when he had tried the same count for no better reason than his own idleness. He knew An Ròn Glas when she came, by the grey seal cut into her stem-post, and cut her notch no deeper and no shallower than any other's, though he looked, for the length of one breath only, at the woman standing at the steering oar with her hand steady on the loom, and thought that he had never before seen anyone look so entirely at ease doing a thing he himself could not have begun to do.

His aunt Mairead's hull went by third from the last, low in the water with her full stores aboard, and Mairead stood at the rail with Dùghall and their two children and lifted a hand to the quay, not waving exactly, only holding it up a moment the way a person does to be seen rather than to signal anything in particular, and Niall, remembering what she had told him the evening before, cut her notch deep, deeper than his grandfather's instruction strictly wanted, and did not think, that morning, that anyone would ever come to check the depth of one notch against another on a stick kept, in the end, for no purpose he was ever told.

At the foot of the pool the fleet straightened out of its long curve and turned down the Abhainn naomh toward the sea, hull after hull falling into the line, and it was there, his grandfather told him afterward, that the harbour-house's own written account of the sailing came to its end, because a scribe can write only what passes before his door, and the river beyond the pool's foot passed beyond any door Seann Skell had. Niall kept his count a while longer, watching the sterns go small down the river between the willows, and could not, in the end, have said with any certainty whether his own stick agreed with his grandfather's or with the tallies any other counter along that strand was cutting that same morning; only that he had cut one notch for every hull he had seen pass, and had not once, that morning, let his eyes wander long enough to lose the number.

When the last hull had gone from sight round the first bend below the pool, the crowd along the strand stood a while longer looking at the empty water before it began to disperse, and it was some little time before anyone on that whole long strand moved to go home. The harbour songs, sung on later anniversaries of that morning by people who had not themselves stood on the quay to see it, tell that a mist came down on the water behind the last hull as she cleared the river's mouth, and did not lift again until she was gone from any watcher's sight; whether that mist stood there in truth, or only in the songs that were made afterward to close the telling properly, no one who was on the quay that morning ever afterward could say for certain, Niall least of all, who had kept his eyes on his stick and not on the weather.

He put the stick inside his shirt, against his skin, before he climbed down off the wall, though no one had told him to keep it rather than hand it over, and no one, that day or afterward, ever asked him for it back.

## V. Of the Boundary Ditch

At Cathair dhearg that same morning, in An Talla Dearg above the river, Queen Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill sat the ordinary court of the day and heard, among the first matters brought before her, a dispute between two farmers of the shire nearest the capital over a ditch that marked the boundary of their two holdings, which one of them held had shifted its course in the spring floods and the other held had not shifted at all, only been dug a little wider by a neighbour with more land than sense.

It was not a great matter, and the court did not treat it as one. The queen heard both farmers out in turn, asked the elder of the two clerks present what the boundary stones of that parish showed, and gave her ruling before noon, that the ditch should be walked and marked again by the parish reeve within the month and the stones set where the walking found them, whatever either farmer might say of floods or neighbours; and the two men, neither wholly satisfied nor wholly aggrieved, thanked her in the manner the court required and went back out to the yard to find their horses.

No rider had yet come up the river road from Seann Skell that morning, nor would one come for two days more, for the distance was four-and-twenty miles and no rider rode it faster than a long day's going even with the best of horses under him; and so the queen who had stood on the new stones at Seann Skell in Sìolach and taken her leave of her own sister without ceremony, as her own word had bound her to do, sat that morning in her own hall and heard a boundary dispute between two farmers she had never met and was not likely to meet again, and gave it the same weight and the same care she had given every such dispute brought before her in all the years of her reign, because a queen who has sworn to send no one over and keep no one back has, on the morning her own sister goes over, no other work to do that the day will let her leave undone.

It is told that she asked, once, near the end of that day's court, whether any word had come up from the coast, and that the clerk at her elbow, who knew as well as she did that no word could yet have come, told her only that none had, and that she said nothing further about it, and turned to the next matter on the roll.

That evening the harbour scribe of Seann Skell walked the length of the west quay alone, from the fish-stones to the new western stones where the fleet had lain the night before, and found the berths standing empty along their whole length for the first time since the masons finished them, the water lapping at stones that had known nothing but hulls; and it is Calum's own words, given to his son Dòmhnall that same night and kept afterward in no book but the family's own memory, that a harbour is a strange thing to walk empty, being built for nothing else but to be full, and that he did not think, walking it that evening, that he would ever again see it as anything but a place where something was missing, whatever ships came afterward to lie against those same stones.
