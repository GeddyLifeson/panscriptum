# Of the Eleven and the Thinning Mist

*LE 1,800 – 1,819*

## I. Of the Eleven at Cidhe an Diosail

The book Eòghann mac Nèill kept at Cidhe an Diosail was not Leabhar a' Chùraim, and no vein-house clerk had ever asked to read it, but it had passed from scribe to scribe at that quay for longer than any man living could reckon without a rod and a rule, and Eòghann, who had held it eleven years by the winter of {{year:III-0238}}, had long since stopped wondering whether the hand before his own had felt, on a grey Là an Diosail, anything like what he felt now, setting the board on his knee and wetting the reed.

The stones of the old quay ran green at the foot, where the river had worked at them since the fleet went down it, and the green did not wash off in any season, wet or dry; Eòghann had asked an old woman once whether it had always been so, and she had said only that the stone remembered the water better than the water remembered the stone. He stood a little back from the rite that year, as the scribe's place required, and counted the people on the quay the way he counted anything that mattered to Taigh na Fèithe's own reckoning: once by eye, and again by touch, a thumb moved along the tally-notches cut into the board's edge so that the eye could not lie to itself twice running. Both counts came to the same number. Eleven.

It was not so few as it had been the winter before, when a wet Gearras had kept half of Seann Skell indoors and only nine had stood the rite; nor was it so many as the year Eòghann had first held the book, when a full score had come down to the water, most of them the harbour priests of the old order and their households and a handful of the town's oldest families who kept the rite the way a house keeps a debt long unpaid to anyone living, since a debt let lapse has a way of being called in unlooked for. He wrote the number first, as the custom of the book required, and then the weather, a grey morning with no wind off the water and the tide at half ebb, and then, because Manannan's own priest had said the prayer that year at the same stones and at the same hour as the rite, as had become the way of it these ninety years, Eòghann noted that too, in the same hand and the same ink, though the two rites had never once, in his own years at the quay, agreed to be called one thing.

He sat a while after the last of the eleven had gone up from the water, the reed still wet in his hand, and thought, as he had thought more than one year running now, that there was more he might have written and did not: that the old woman who had told him the stone remembered the water was not among the eleven that year, having kept her bed since Samhnas with a cough that would not leave her chest; that the priest's prayer, said each tide now and not only on the rite's own day, had begun to sound in the ears of Seann Skell less like an asking and more like a habit a town keeps going out of fear of what silence might mean if the asking stopped; that no one under thirty years old had stood on the quay stones that morning at all, of the eleven or of anyone else. None of it went into the book. The book was for the number, the weather, and the tide, and had been for as long as it had been kept, and Eòghann mac Nèill, who had never in eleven years added a line the book did not ask of him, was not the man to begin the year the count came to eleven.

Eòghann closed the board and carried it back up the harbour-house stair, past the window where a lamp had stood every dark of the year since before he held the office, and did not need to look at the sill to know the lamp was not lit; it had not been lit in three winters, since the old woman who had kept it, Ealasaid nic Iain, took to her own bed at the far end of the same street and never rose from it again. He had gone to her burying himself, one of a bare dozen who did, and had heard it said over her grave, by a niece who had never once climbed the harbour-house stair in her own life, that Ealasaid had kept the lamp because the town would not, and that the town, now she was gone, would have to find some new reason to keep it or else own that it had none. No new reason had been found. The window stood dark that winter and every winter after, and Eòghann, passing it each Là an Diosail with the board under his arm, had come to think of the dark glass less as an absence than as a kind of company: the one other thing on that street, besides himself, that had gone on doing its office to the end without once being thanked for it.

He had not always meant to keep the harbour scribe's book so long, nor so close to the letter of it. He had come to the office at nine-and-twenty, a fisherman's son with a fair hand and no kin among the old harbour families, taken on because the man before him had died of a fall on the very stair Eòghann now climbed and left no son of his own trained to the work; and Eòghann, in his first winters at the board, had thought the office a thin thing to give a life to, a count and a weather-note and a tide, set down for no one he could name and read by no one he had ever met. It was only slowly, and never all at once, that he had come to feel what the scribes before him must also have felt, standing where he stood: that a book kept faithfully once no one still troubled to ask what was in it becomes, by the keeping alone, a kind of company of its own, and that a man who breaks that faith to say something truer than the book allows has broken the one thing the office was ever for.

## II. Of the Petition Laid Aside

Word reached Seann Skell late, as word from Cathair dhearg mostly did, that Aonghas mac Lachlainn had died in his own bed in the spring after the rite of the eleven, four-and-eighty years old and the last of the council that had sat when Athchuingean an Airgid was refused. He had turned his own vote against the smelt at Muileann chiar by a single voice, eight-and-twenty years before, and Leabhar a' Chùraim had written down his name and nothing of his reason, and in all the years since, at every fair and every ford where the tale was told, someone had always claimed to know at last why a man of Clann Lachlainn, whose own kin stood to gain by the silver as much as any house on the coal-right, had turned against his own house's asking. No two of the tellings agreed. A kinsman of his, sitting by him in the last week, is said to have asked him plainly whether he would not, now that it could cost him nothing, say what had turned his hand that day; Aonghas is said to have looked at the kinsman a while, and to have answered that a man who explained a thing he had done for good reason usually did it because the reason had stopped seeming good to him, and that his own had not, and he saw no cause to give it up now for the asking. He died within the week, and whatever he knew of his own vote went into the ground at Cathair dhearg with him.

Aonghas was carried to Cathair dhearg's own burying-ground under the ordinary rite of his house, with no more said over him than was said over any man of four-and-eighty years who had held no crown and started no war, and Leabhar a' Chùraim's clerk who entered his death wrote his name, his age, and the year, and nothing further, having no more to go on than the town did. A rider from Muileann chiar came down for the burying, sent by the silver guild there on the strength of an old courtesy and no closer tie, and stood at the back of the small crowd through the whole of it without once being asked, by anyone present, why a silversmith's rider had troubled to come so far for a man who had cost his own guild a fortune it never got to make. If he had an answer ready, no one at Cathair dhearg thought to want it.

Clann Choinnich, who had brought the petition twice and been refused twice, did not bring it a third time. The house had no want to test a new council on a question the old one had settled by so slight a margin, with the one man who had settled it now beyond any second asking, and Leabhar a' Chùraim's own note on the matter, written some years after by a clerk with no memory of the day, says only that the petition lay unbrought and unanswered, and that Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig, which had measured the hearth's own handful since Fionnlagh Dall's day, was kept as it had always been kept. No house is known to have grieved the silver it might have made. Muileann chiar's smelters went on working the ore they had always worked, at the heat charcoal gives and no more, and if any among them thought the coal beneath their own hills might have served them better, Leabhar a' Chùraim does not trouble to say so, and neither, in all the years Eòghann kept his own book at Seann Skell, did anyone who came through the harbour say it to him either.

Of the queen, in these same years, there was less and less to write, and at last nothing. She had gone up to Taigh na Fèithe eight winters before the rite of the eleven and had not come down to An Talla Dearg again, and no rider came from Muileann dhearg to say why, nor any word at all beyond the plain fact of her absence, which the council at Cathair dhearg kept from growing into a question by the simple means of never once asking it aloud in any hall where a clerk might be minded to write the asking down. Eòghann, at his own remove on the coast, heard of it the way he heard of most things from the capital: late, and thin, and by the mouth of a carter who had it from a carter who had it from someone nearer An Talla Dearg than either of them had ever been. He entered none of it in his own book, telling himself it was no business of the harbour scribe's what the council at Cathair dhearg chose to leave unsaid, though he noted, privately and only to himself, that a crown gone quiet in that particular way asked a great deal more of the men who kept the record after it than a crown that had simply died and been buried in the ordinary manner, with a date and a name and an heir to write down after both.

## III. Of the Chair Left Empty

Na Seachd Taighean had sat at Cathair dhearg in the queen's place since the winter after she went up to the burning, and it was in the seventeenth winter after her going that they came to the matter of a name; no one of them had called that sitting first, but each had by then sent its own rider up the same road on its own business, and the riders, finding one another at the same door on the same day, had gone in together rather than wait apart for a summons that no one left at Cathair dhearg had the standing to send. An Talla Dearg in those years held perhaps five hundred souls in all its lanes and closes, fewer than a single one of Na Seachd Taighean' own tenants counted in a good year, and the board where the seven sat was the same board Catrìona Bheag had sworn them to Riaghailt an Teallaich over, twelve generations before, its grain gone dark with the hands that had rested on it since.

No new crown was proposed. Clann Raghnaill's own head, an old woman by then and no friend to haste, said first what more than one of the seven had thought and none had yet spoken: that a crown with no one to wear it was no crown at all, whatever Taigh na Fèithe's rolls might go on calling it, and that Na Seachd Taighean sitting at one board with no voice among them answerable for what the board decided was a slower kind of ruin than any war the record kept. She proposed no new crown. She proposed only that the seven, sitting as they now sat, should call themselves by some plain name for what they in fact were, custodians of the coal-right and, in the queen's long silence, of a good deal more besides, and that one among them should speak for the seven when a single voice was wanted, at Taigh na Fèithe or on the road or before any stranger who came asking who ruled at Cathair dhearg now.

They named themselves, that same sitting, Comhairle a' Chùraim, a name near enough to Leabhar a' Chùraim that no clerk in any hall would need the thing explained to him twice. For the one voice, they turned to Tormod mac Ailein, eight-and-thirty years old and the youngest man at the board by a full generation, a custodian's son of no house among the seven who had kept Taigh na Fèithe's own accounts at Muileann dhearg since he was a boy and was trusted, by every house there for reasons each gave differently, to want nothing for himself that the other six had not already agreed he might have. They called him Ceann a' Chùraim, and he took the name the way a man takes a coat handed him against weather he had not himself chosen to walk out in, gravely, and without much show of being glad of it.

Clann Choinnich alone, of the seven, asked whether a council so newly named might not also, in the same sitting, be asked again about the silver; the head of that house put the question mildly, and mildly it was set aside, the board's temper being for settling one uncertainty at a time and no more, and the silver, as every house at the board well knew, being the one uncertainty the last council had settled by a single voice now in the ground. Nothing more was said of it that winter, nor at the sitting after.

The chair at the head of the board, the queen's own chair, they left standing empty. No one proposed that Tormod sit in it, and Tormod himself, asked once by a clerk which chair he meant to take at the next sitting, said only that his own place at the board's foot had served him well enough thus far and he saw no cause to move up it now. The chair stood empty at every sitting after, and moving it would have wanted an explaining nobody at the board was minded to give. The council's own letters, from that first winter on, went out from Cathair dhearg sealed with Taigh na Fèithe's own trellis and hollow stone and no name written above the seal, a custom An Talla Dearg's clerks had already begun in the queen's last silent years and saw no reason, now, to give up merely because the silence had at last been given a name of its own.

## IV. Of the Fisher of Ceann mhòr

At Ceann mhòr, twenty miles up the coast from Seann Skell where the fishing was thin and the houses fewer with every count the crown's reeves had ever troubled to make, a fisher named Oighrig nic Eachainn kept her father's boat after him, one of perhaps a dozen boats still put out from that shore at all, the rest laid up above the tide-line the way the last barge of Inis mhòr had been laid up long before, less because anyone had decided the sea was done with than because there was no longer hand enough left on that coast to man them. Oighrig went out before first light most mornings the winter after Na Seachd Taighean named themselves at Cathair dhearg, though word of it did not reach her for the better part of a year; a fisher of Ceann mhòr had small cause to trouble herself with what An Talla Dearg called its own council, so long as the fish still ran and the priests at Seann Skell kept their own rule of sight-of-land, which was the only rule of the crown's that had ever reached so far up the coast in any form a fisher needed to remember.

That rule she had known from a child, the way she had known to reef a sail before her father ever let her hold the tiller: a boat went out as far as the eye could keep the headland, and no further, for beyond the headland lay Manannan's own mist, and no boat that had ever gone into it, in all the tellings the coast still told, had come back to say what lay past it. Mànas mac Dhùghaill, the priest who kept Manannan's rite at Seann Skell those years, had taken, on {{date:III-0239a}}, to writing into his own book of the rite a thing the older priests before him had said only at the hearth and never on paper, that the grey out past the headlands stood thinner each year than the year before, so that a morning that would once have shown a fisher nothing beyond an arm's length of grey water now sometimes showed her the grey itself, thin as a held breath, with something beyond it that was not mist at all. He said the prayer at every tide by then, not only at the rite's own day, the same words the synod had given the priests two centuries back, Manannan, keep the sea shut, and he wrote in his own book that he did not know whether the saying of it more often was holding anything back or merely making plainer, each time it was said, how little the saying now held back.

Oighrig rested on her oars one still morning that same winter, further out than the rule strictly allowed but not so far that any priest watching from the shore could have called it a true crossing of the line, and looked north into the grey the way she had looked at it every morning of her life without ever once, before that morning, seeing anything in it but grey. The water lay flat and dark under her hull, and the mist stood off the bow the way it always stood, a wall with no far side that the eye could find. Then, for the space of a few breaths only, before the grey closed again the way it always closed, she saw a line beyond it, hard and dark and level, ruled clean across the edge of the world where the water met the sky and went on past where any headland could account for it. It was there and it was gone, and she could not afterward have sworn, put to it plainly, that she had not simply willed herself into seeing what every fisher on that coast had half hoped and half dreaded to see since the priests began saying the prayer at every tide instead of once a year.

Her father had drowned off that same headland eleven years before, in weather no worse than a dozen he had come through safely in his own life, and Oighrig had grown up hearing the coast agree, without ever quite saying so to her face, that the sea took a man in the end for no better reason than that it had waited long enough; she troubled little enough with the thought on an ordinary morning; that morning, with the hard line still standing before her eyes though the mist had long since closed over it again, she found herself wondering, for the first time in eleven years, whether her father had ever, on some grey dawn like this one, rested on his own oars a moment past where the rule allowed and seen the same thing she had seen, and said nothing of it either, for the same plain reasons she meant to say nothing of hers.

She turned for home as the rule said a fisher must, and did not speak of it at the shore, not that morning nor for some while after, telling herself there was little enough good that came of a fisher's tale believed too readily and a good deal of harm that came of one believed and then found false. When she did speak of it, months on, it was to no priest and no reeve but to her own mother, mending a net by the one lamp their house still burned after dark, and her mother heard her out and said only that a woman who had seen a true thing and kept it to herself a season had already shown better sense over it than most who saw such things at all, and that she might do worse than go on keeping it a while longer yet. Oighrig did. It went into no book at Seann Skell and no roll at Cathair dhearg, and the only record that she ever spoke of it stands in her own mother's memory and, after that, in no record at all.

## V. Of the Last Winter

By the fifteenth winter after the rite of the eleven, the harbour-house at Seann Skell had stood so long with its lamp-window dark that a stranger asking after the custom of Oidhche nan Lòchran would have found no one on the waterfront under sixty years old able to say from their own memory what the night had once looked like, lit. Eòghann mac Nèill, who had kept the harbour scribe's book at Cidhe an Diosail for six-and-twenty years by then, still walked down to the stones each Là an Diosail with the same board under his arm, and still counted, and still wrote the number and the weather and the tide in the same plain hand the book had always asked of him, though the number by then had not risen above a dozen in longer than he cared to reckon, and one bitter year had come down, by his own careful count, to seven.

That last winter the record tells of, the count is not written, for the book, if Eòghann kept it to the end, does not survive to say what he wrote in it; what is told of that season comes from elsewhere; from Mànas mac Dhùghaill's own book of the rite at Seann Skell, which does survive, and which sets down, in a hand grown old and careful by then, that the priests said the prayer at every tide that winter as they had said it every winter for thirty years, and that on more than one still morning the line beyond the mist stood plain enough from the harbour wall that a man need not put out in any boat at all to see it. And from Leabhar a' Chùraim, which notes, in the same dry hand that had entered every winter burning since Fionnlagh Dall's day, that the council of the custody sat at its board in An Talla Dearg through the dark of that year as it had sat through every year since the seven had named it, and sent its letters out sealed as it had always sent them, with no name above the seal, and no word in any of them of the chair that stood empty at the board's head, which by then no clerk living had ever seen anyone sit in at all.

On a still morning near the year's dark heart, with no wind on the water and the tide at the ebb it kept at that season, the priests of Manannan said their prayer at Seann Skell as the sun came up grey behind the mist, and further up the coast at Ceann mhòr the few boats still fishing that shore put out before light as they always had, and one or two of the older hands among them, resting a while on their oars past where the rule strictly allowed, looked north a moment longer than the rule strictly asked of them either, before they turned, every one, for home. At Cathair dhearg the council sat at its board in An Talla Dearg with the chair at its head standing empty as it had stood three-and-twenty years, and wrote its letters, and sealed them, and sent them out by riders down roads Taigh na Fèithe had kept in good repair since before any house now sitting at that board had held the custody at all. And on the waterfront at Seann Skell, where a lamp had once stood in every window on Oidhche nan Lòchran, the one window that had kept a lamp longest of all stood dark, as it had stood every winter since the woman who kept it went into the ground, and no one that year, nor any year after within this record's telling, set a light there in her place.

The mist stood on the water as it had stood since the fleet went down the river into it eighteen centuries before, and it stood, that winter, thinner than any priest at Seann Skell could remember it standing, and no boat put out to try it. The prayer was said at every tide. The council kept its board. Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig measured the hearth's own handful, hearth by hearth, as it had measured it since Fionnlagh Dall first laid down the rule, and three houses of the seven held between them more of the right than the other four together, and the petition that might once have changed it lay where it had lain twelve years, unbrought and unanswered, in Taigh na Fèithe's own inventory. Of the crown, nothing further was written. The lamp-window stood dark on the waterfront through that winter and the winters after it, and the mist, each morning the tide allowed a boat to look, lay a little thinner on the water than the morning before.
