# Of the Swallowing Cough

*LE 1,075 – 1,159*

## I. Of the Cough at Muileann dhomhain

Beathag Fhionn was thirty-nine years old and had sat at her mother's right hand in the red hall for eleven of them, hearing boundary pleas and grain rulings and the slow ordinary business of a settled island, before she heard the word Muileann dhomhain spoken in a voice that made her set down the tally she was reading. The rider who brought it had ridden the eighteen miles from {{place:burg:466}} without stopping to eat, and he stood in the hall's doorway swaying a little on his feet while he told it: that a sickness had come into the town that closed a man's throat until he could not swallow his own spit, still less bread or water, and that it had come quickly, house by house along the lower street first and then up into the hill lanes, and that the town's own healers, who had buried plagues before and thought they knew the shape of a hard season, had never seen a dying so fast nor a throat shut so tight against a cup held to it.

Her mother, Mòrag Ruadh, was seventy-nine years old that year and had worn the crown sixty years, since she was nineteen, and she heard the rider out in full before she said anything at all, which Beathag had long since learned was her mother's way with any tidings that frightened her, the silence buying her time to decide what a queen ought to feel before she let herself feel it. When she did speak it was only to ask how many, and the rider said he did not rightly know, that the healers had stopped counting somewhere past a hundred and had gone back to nursing rather than tallying, which was, he said, an answer of a kind. It is told at Cathair dhearg that the queen sent grain and dried fish south that same week, and priests of Macha's wood-watchers to sit with the dying where the town's own could not, and that she sent nothing else, because there was nothing else on the island that a cough could be answered with.

Beathag rode south herself before the season was out, against her mother's own wish that she stay clear of the sickened town, and stood a morning at the edge of the lower street with a cloth held to her own mouth as the custodians had asked of her, watching two men carry a hurdle between them with a third man on it who could no longer swallow so much as the water they tried to give him. She did not go nearer than that edge of street, and she said afterward to no one but her own scribe that she had gone south chiefly to see with her own eyes what a rider's telling could only ever half give her, a thing she judged, looking back on it in her own age, to have been more for her own sake than the town's, since the town had no need of a queen's presence and every need of grain, fish, and priests, which had already been sent before she ever mounted a horse.

By the turn of the season the town had lost near a third of its people, the healers' own best count when the dying finally slowed enough to be counted, and the losing fell heaviest along the lower street where the houses stood close and shared a well, whole households going into the ground within a fortnight of one another, while houses two streets up, set apart on the hill with wells of their own, lost no one at all. Beathag heard this reckoning read aloud in the hall the following winter and thought, though she said it to no one, that the closeness of the houses, and not any fault in the blood, had done the killing, a thought she kept turning over long after the telling had moved on to other business, for she could see no way to prove it and no one in the hall who thought it worth the proving.

Two years on, the cough came down the coast to {{place:burg:110}}, and Seann Bhral acted where Muileann dhomhain, taken by surprise, had had no chance to act at all: the town shut its own harbour before a single ship from the sickened towns could put in, and posted its own reeves at the gate to turn back any traveller on the coast road, and kept the shared meal, which had always been carried house to house by hand, by leaving baskets of bread and dried fish at the town gate instead, for whoever within needed them, so that no one from outside the walls need come near enough to catch a dying man's breath. It held. Seann Bhral lost fewer in that whole hard season than Muileann dhomhain had lost in a single fortnight, and the harbour towns down the coast, hearing how the shutting had been done, began that same year to talk among themselves, in a way they had not needed to before, about which of a town's own doors it might be wisest to close, and when, and who among their own people would have the stomach to keep a gate shut against a neighbour's own cart.

A reeve of Seann Bhral, an old woman who had kept the town's grain store for thirty years and was given the gate to keep as well once the shutting began, is said to have told a rider passing through, long after the sickness had gone by, that the hardest part of the whole season had not been the turning-away of strangers, who took a refusal at a strange gate as no more than the ill luck of the road, but the turning-away of the town's own folk who had gone out before the shutting to visit kin along the coast and came home to find the gate closed against them too, the rule allowing no house, however dear, an exception once the order was given. She had stood at that gate herself, she said, and told a fisherman she had known from childhood that he could leave food and word for his own wife within, but that his own body could not pass the gate until the season turned, and he had gone away up the road without answering her, and she had not slept easily for a week after, though she never once doubted the order had been the right one.

## II. Of the Reeve at the Ford

Beathag Fhionn was crowned in the same hall her mother had held for sixty-three years, forty-two years old that spring and grey already at the temples, and the first act her own scribes set down under her own seal, before a grain ruling, before a boundary plea, before anything the hall's ordinary business might have offered her instead, was the closing of the coast road between Seann Bhral and Muileann dhomhain. It is told that she gave the order within the month of her crowning, before the cough had shown any sign of moving further west along that same road, judging, as her scribes later wrote it down, that a queen who waited for a sickness to prove itself before she acted against it had already waited too long to act at all.

The order she sent was plain enough for any reeve on that road to carry out without asking her leave a second time: that no traveller, cart, or beast was to pass between the two towns until the queen's own word released the road again, and that a reeve was to be set at every ford and every crossing of the coast road between them to see the order kept, day and night, through whatever weather the season sent. Six fords lay along that stretch of road, some no more than a stepping-place where the coast road crossed a stream too narrow to trouble naming, and to each of them the crown's scribes sent a reeve, chosen mostly from the towns nearest each crossing, men and women who knew the road, and were known on it, in turn.

To the ford nearest Muileann dhomhain, where the road ran closest to the sickened town and where, if the cough meant to travel west at all, it would first have to cross, the queen's scribes sent a young man of the town's own kin, Seumas mac Coinnich, thirty-two years old, who had held no office before that spring beyond keeping his own family's small herd of goats on the hill above the lower street where the dying had been thickest. He had lost, in the sickness's first passing, an aunt and two cousins and had watched, from his own high ground, smoke go up some mornings from more than one house on the lower street at once, and it was for that reason, the scribe who chose him is said to have written, and not for any skill the office required, that he was thought the surest hand to set at that particular crossing: a man who had already seen, at close hand, what the road behind him was closing against.

The other five reeves along that same stretch of road were of an ordinary sort, chosen for steadiness rather than for grief: a miller's widow at the ford above Caol dhearg, a shepherd who had never before left his own hill in a working life of forty years, a young clerk lent from the crown's own household at Cathair dhearg who found, within a week, that a ford asked patience of a kind no clerk's stool had ever asked of him. Between them the six held the whole of that road's crossing points, and it is told that not one of the six let a single traveller through in the three years the order stood, whatever the traveller's reason given, a plain and stubborn faithfulness the crown's own scribes remarked on afterward as owing less to any fear of the queen than to a thing harder to put a name to, which the scribes, wanting a word for it, called only the road's own discipline, as though the shutting itself had taught the six who kept it a single lesson none of them had known before that spring.

He held the ford through that first summer without much trouble, turning back carters who had not yet heard the order and travellers who had heard it and thought it need not apply to them, a labour that asked little more of him than a plain word said firmly and, once or twice, a hand kept near the staff he had been given, though he never once had to raise it. The travellers who came to his crossing in those first weeks were mostly strangers to him, or near enough, and a stranger turned back at a ford, however aggrieved, was a small enough thing to carry home at evening and set down.

It was near the season's end that his own brother came to the crossing, a cart behind him loaded with what looked, from the ford's near bank, like the whole of a household's goods, and called across the water before Seumas had properly seen who it was that their mother lay dying on the far side of the closed road, in a house two miles past the ford, and that he meant to go to her whatever any order said, closed road or no. Seumas knew the voice before he knew the face, and he is said to have stood a long moment at his own side of the ford with the staff loose in his hand, saying nothing, while his brother waited on the far bank for an answer that was, by both their own tellings ever after, longer in coming than either brother thought it ought to have been.

He turned him back. It is told, though Seumas never once spoke of that afternoon again in the whole of his life, that he said only that the order held for every traveller on the road or it held for none, and that a reeve who let his own kin through a ford he had sworn to keep shut had, by that one letting, already broken every other turning-back he had made or would make after it, whatever the reason given him. His brother called him hard names across the water, and then, when the names brought no answer either, turned the cart and went back the way he had come. It is told at Muileann dhomhain that their mother died within the week, and that the two brothers did not speak again for the better part of five years, until the road was long since opened and the cough forgotten by every household the sickness had not touched.

The road stayed closed the better part of three years, and the cough, whatever carried it, did not once cross west of the fords the reeves held; Muileann dhomhain buried its own dead and Seann Bhral kept its gate, and the rest of the coast, watching the two towns from a safe distance, learned a lesson it did not soon forget: that a shut door, held firmly enough and long enough, could hold back what no priest and no healer had found any other way to answer. It is told that Seumas mac Coinnich kept the crown's reeve-board at that same ford for the rest of his working life, near thirty years, until his own death in his sixtieth year, and that no other order he was ever given afterward asked so much of him again, nor was any other, in the town's own telling of it, ever kept so faithfully.

## III. Of the Houses Taken by Lot

Muileann dhomhain was slow to fill again. For twenty years and more after the road reopened the town stood at less than two in every three of the households it had held before the sickness, whole streets of the lower town given over to houses with no one left to claim them, their thatch going grey and their doors swinging on hinges no one troubled to mend, and the reeve who kept the town's own roll in those years, an old man who had lost a wife and a son to the cough, is said to have walked the lower street once a season and written down, without much comment, which roofs had fallen in since his last walking.

It was families from the hill country above the town, land too thin and too far from any market to hold a growing household easily, who came down in the end to take the empty houses, a handful each year rather than all at once, drawn by land already cleared and walls already standing, however far gone, and cheaper by far than the work of raising a new house from nothing on a hillside. Three brothers, sons of a hill farmer of Caol dhearg, walked down with their wives and children in a single autumn and drew, between the three of them, three houses standing side by side on the lower street where an entire family of the old blood had once kept a smithy, and it is told that the eldest of the three, Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, stood a long while before the middle house before he would go in, the smell of another family's hearth still on the walls after twenty years empty, before he said only that a house left standing was a house left standing, whoever had raised the roof-tree first.

The town's remaining custodians, judging that an empty house served no one and that a filled one, however filled, served the whole town better than a street of fallen roofs, opened the houses to lot rather than to purchase, a stone drawn from a bag for each household that asked, so that no family of the hill country could claim to have bought its way past a neighbour with a fuller purse, and no family of the town's own old blood could claim a house left standing by right of who its people had once been.

By the year the last lot was drawn, near thirty years after the sickness had first come down the lower street, the older families of the town, those few whose houses had never emptied at all, said afterward that they no longer knew, walking their own streets, half the faces they passed, and that a town which had once been able to name every household back three generations came, that generation, to name its own streets by house rather than by kin: the goat-herd's house, the old weaver's house, without much certainty any longer of whose hands had built them first. It is not told that this troubled the town greatly. The houses stood filled again, and the well that had carried the sickness fastest along the lower street was by then two wells, dug wider apart at the custodians' own asking, and Muileann dhomhain, by every measure a reeve's roll could set down, was once more a living town and not a street of empty roofs.

## IV. Of the Bridge Built Anew

Drochaid Ros fhionn had carried the salt carts of {{place:burg:76}} across the Abhainn bheag for the better part of seven centuries by the year its own arches, worn thin by cart-wheel and flood alike, began to shed stone into the water below them faster than any mason thought safe to let pass unmended. A carter's mule went into the river with its whole load one wet spring when the road bed gave way beneath it, the mule drowned before anyone could cut it free of its traces and the carter left standing waist-deep in the current holding a dripping harness, and it was that one loss, small as it was against a season's whole trade, that the salt-masters afterward pointed to as the thing that had finally moved them to act rather than to wait another year on the crown's own masons.

The salt-masters of the flats, whose whole trade crossed that one bridge on its way to every market the island kept, met among themselves rather than wait any longer, and it is told that they raised, among their own number, the whole cost of the arches' rebuilding out of a season's trade, a thing no guild of salt-carriers had ever before been asked or offered to do for a bridge that was not, in any strict reckoning, theirs to keep.

The new arches, when they were finished, carried the salt-masters' own names cut into the keystones, each master's name set into the stone above the arch his own purse had paid for, a vanity the crown's reeve who came to see the work done is said to have remarked on, not unkindly, to the eldest of the masters present; and the old man answered him, the reeve's own letter to Cathair dhearg records, that a name cut in stone over running water would outlast any name set on a tally of silver, and that a salt-master who had spent his whole life watching his trade pass unremarked over another man's bridge might be forgiven, once, for wanting his own name to be the thing that held the road up.

Beathag Fhionn, by then in the thirty-seventh year of her reign and growing heavier in the years than she liked to feel herself grow, sent word that the crown would meet whatever the salt-masters' purses could not, and that the bridge, however it was paid for, remained the road's own bridge and not any one guild's to close or open as it pleased; but it is told that no crown's silver was in the end asked for, the salt-masters' own trade having carried the whole cost between them before her letter reached the flats, and that she let the matter stand as it had settled itself, judging, her own scribes later wrote, that a bridge mended without her silver was no less mended for the lacking of it.

## V. Of the Queen's Death

She died at {{place:burg:19}} at nine-and-eighty years old, in the same red hall where she had sat at her own mother's right hand and closed a road against a sickness within the month of her crowning, and it is told that she was clear in her mind to the last day, dictating a grain ruling to her scribes on the same morning she took to her bed and did not rise from it again. The scribe who took down that last ruling, a young woman new to the hall's service, said afterward that the queen had asked her, once the ruling was finished and set aside, whether the road between Seann Bhral and Muileann dhomhain still stood open, a question the scribe had no ready answer for, the road having been open more than forty years by then and given no cause to be spoken of in the hall in all that time; the queen, told that it did, said only that she was glad of it, and closed her eyes, and did not open them again to speak of any other matter.

Her son Ailean was crowned within the season, fifty-nine years old himself and no longer a young man by any measure the hall could offer, the first king of that name in the house of Raghnall, and it is told that he wept once, briefly and without shame, at his own crowning, and that no one in the hall thought the less of him for it, his mother's reign having been long enough that half the hall had known no other queen in the whole of their own working lives.

## VI. Of the Fading Things

It is told at {{place:burg:3}} that the last dyer who knew the true mixing of Baile ghorm's own blue died in those same years without ever having taught it whole to either of the two apprentices she had quarrelled with and sent from her house in turn, so that the blue the town's weavers dyed thereafter, though it answered to the same old name, was in truth no more than the common blue any dyer on the island could mix, and it is said that the older weavers of the town, holding a length of the new cloth up against a length kept from before the old woman's death, could tell the two apart at a glance and never once, in the whole of their own lives after, stopped mourning the difference aloud to anyone who would listen.

Lightning found the ancestor-posts at {{place:burg:315}} on a summer night some twenty years after the queen's death, and burned them to their footings before the keepers could carry so much as a bucket from the well, and it is told that the keepers, setting new posts in the old ground the following season, cut the names again as best their own memory and the custody-book together could recover them, and owned plainly, in the roll they kept of the cutting, that a handful of the names so cut were guesses only, the old carving too far gone in the years before the burning for even the oldest keeper living to swear to every letter of them. No one is told to have thought the less of the new posts for the owning. A name guessed at in good faith and cut with the same care as a name known outright, the keepers held, honoured the dead it stood for no less than certainty would have, and the roll's own plain admission of the guessing was kept alongside the new posts, for any who came after to read and judge as they pleased.

The Abhainn bheag silted at {{place:burg:76}} in the years that followed, a shifting bar building itself below the salt flats faster than any dredging the salt-masters could set their own hands to clear it, until the loaded barges that had once run the river to the sea on every tide found themselves grounding, one after another, a bowshot short of open water, their pilots standing in the shallows and cursing a bar that moved a little further out with every season's flood, no two years' soundings ever quite matching the last. The salt-masters, whose own fathers had cut their names into Drochaid Ros fhionn's keystones not forty years before, took their trade at last to {{place:burg:443}}'s own harbour along the coast, where deeper water asked no such patience of a laden barge, and it is told that the flats above Ros fhionn went on making salt as they always had, the brine springs caring nothing for a river's silting, but that the barges that had once carried it out grew fewer year on year, until a pilot standing at the old bar on a still morning might watch a whole tide turn and go out again without a single loaded hull crossing it, and count himself, by then, not much surprised.
