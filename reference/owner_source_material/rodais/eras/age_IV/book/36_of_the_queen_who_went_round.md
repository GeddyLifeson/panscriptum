# Of the Queen Who Went Round

*LE 1,440 – 1,629*

## I. Of the Oath of the Seven

Queen Catrìona Bheag called the heads of the seven houses to Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0197}} to swear again to the ruling of the hearth, Riaghailt an Teallaich, as no queen had asked of them since Catrìona Bhuadhach set the ruling down. None of the seven had ever refused it; none of them, either, had been asked to swear it again in three generations, and there is a difference, the red hall's own clerk noted at the foot of his roll, between a promise a house keeps because it was never tested and a promise a house is called to make a second time before witnesses. Clann Mhuirich swore first, as their custom was from the founding of the seven, and Clann Fhearchair and Clann Ìomhair and Clann Raghnaill and Clann Thormoid after them each in turn, a hand laid flat on the vein-house's own hollow stone that a rider had carried down from Muileann dhearg for the day and would carry back again before the moon was out.

Clann Choinnich swore last. The head of that house, an old woman who had held it thirty years and buried two husbands in the holding of it, stood the better part of an hour at the foot of the hall while the other six spoke their oath and sat again, and when her turn came at last she stood a while with her hand still at her side and asked the queen plainly, before the whole hall, whether the swearing changed the measure her house held or only the manner of swearing to it. Catrìona Bheag answered that it changed nothing but the manner, and the old woman said that a manner asked twice in three generations was worth asking after, and swore then, her hand flat on the stone the same as the other six had laid theirs, no louder and no softer. The clerk who kept the red hall's roll wrote that all seven had sworn and that one had asked a question first, and thought it worth the extra line, though he could not himself have said what he expected any reader after him to make of it.

The stone had come down from Muileann dhearg two days before under a rider's own care, wrapped against the weather in the same hide it had travelled in the last time an oath of the seven was sworn, and it went back up the same road the morning after, carried by a different rider on a different horse, so that no single house of the seven could ever afterward claim to have held it longer under its own roof than any other house had. The custodians who kept it at the vein-house between such swearings let no one handle it but the rider charged with the carrying, and are said to have counted, on its return each time, the small chips at its edge against a tally kept for that purpose alone, a caution against nothing anyone could name and against everything, at once, that fourteen centuries of careful custody had taught the vein-house to guard.

At Cathair mhòr that same decade the priests of Macha finished the floor of their great house, black and white stone laid in a pattern none of the masons who set the last of it had themselves begun, for the floor had been eleven years in the laying and every hand that had cut the first courses was long dead or long retired by {{year:III-0198}}. It stood, when it was done, the largest roofed building on Dia-thìr, and the priests who walked it barefoot on the day of its consecration said afterward that the black and the white ran so true underfoot that a man could close his eyes on it and still tell, by the cold of the stone alone, which colour he stood on.

## II. Of the Stair Found Again, and the Dry Years

Charcoal-burners working the eastern woods in {{year:III-0199}} broke through into a cut stair going down under the hill, the same shape and the same worked stone as the stair the quarrymen of the west had walled up five centuries before and never spoken of again. Word of it reached the vein-house within the season, and the custodians who came to look at it did what their fathers' fathers had done at the first one: they had it walled, the new stone set flush with the old rock so that a stranger walking the wood afterward would find nothing but a hillside grown over with bramble. The scribe who kept the custody-book bound the two entries to one another with a line in the margin of each, the eastern find to the western, though he wrote nothing of what either stair went down to, for nothing had ever been written of the first and no one saw reason to begin with the second.

The shires about Achadh shean had the worse of it in those same years. Three seasons running, from {{year:III-0200}}, the rain that the eastern hills had always caught for the wells below them failed to come, and the wells themselves ran low and then dry, one after another, until the reeves of the shire sent word to Cathair dhearg that the shared meal alone would not carry them through a fourth dry summer. The crown carted grain in from the south by the mining road, ox-team after ox-team through a country too parched even for the carts to raise the dust they usually raised, and the shire held, though it held thin. When the rain came back the reeves did not trust it to stay, and in {{year:III-0201}} they set down on a board at the well-head how the deep wells would be shared among the households by lot and by turn, so that no single dry year again could be answered only by whichever family reached the water first. The board was cut new each generation as the old one wore, the names on it changed with the households that held the shares, but the lots themselves were never drawn again; the first sharing held, by habit more than by law, for as long as the shire kept any memory of the dry years at all.

It was at Àth shean, not many years after, that a young clerk newly set over the granary's accounts found, going back through the ledgers for a reason he could no longer afterward name, that the firlot the granary measured its grain by had worn smaller than the standard kept at Cathair dhearg, and had been worn so, by the plainest reckoning he could make of the old entries, for longer than any single reeve's term of office could account for. He carried the matter up rather than let it stand, and in {{year:III-0202}} the reeve had every account the granary held corrected as far back as the ledgers would allow the reckoning to run, a labour of a whole winter's evenings that satisfied no one so much as it satisfied the clerk himself, who is remembered to have kept, for the rest of his own service, a private habit of weighing his own hand's firlot against the standard each Samhnas whether any question had been raised against it or not.

## III. Of the Last of Clann Mhic Phàil

Muileann gheal had been known the length of the eastern road for the grey-green glaze its potters set on the custodian houses' own tables, a colour no other kiln on the island had ever matched and no potter of the town had ever fully explained even to the sons and daughters who grew up firing the same kilns. The secret, so far as it was a secret and not simply a knowledge no one outside one family had troubled to learn, had stayed inside Clann Mhic Phàil for as long as the town's own memory ran, passed from a potter to whichever child of the house showed the surest hand at the wheel and the wet clay, and by the time Iain Mac Phàil came to the kilns as a young man there was no one left in the family but himself to pass it on to and no child of his own yet born to take it.

He tried, in the last years of his life, to set the glaze's making down in some form another hand might follow, and failed at it in a way that seemed to surprise him more than it surprised anyone who watched him try: the mixing he could describe well enough, the grinding of the stone and the ash and the water measured against each dish's own weight of clay, but the firing, the moment at which the kiln's heat had to be let fall rather than pushed higher, was a thing his own hands knew and his own words could not carry over to another man's hands however carefully he chose them. Two apprentices he took in those last years, both quick enough at the wheel, and both came away from their years under him able to throw a passable dish and unable to glaze it grey-green rather than the ordinary brown every other kiln on the island already knew. He died in {{year:III-0203}} with neither apprentice yet trusted to try the firing alone, and for a generation after him the potters of Muileann gheal tried, by every method any of them could reason out or borrow, to find the measure of heat and the moment of its falling that Iain Mac Phàil had carried out of the world with him, and none of them came near it. The custodian houses kept the dishes they already held, and are said to have prized a chipped one, thereafter, above a whole dish of any other glaze the island's kilns could offer.

Baile Mòr ruadh, in the same decade, found a smaller kind of fortune in a cattle fair its own market had held for years without any particular note being taken of it, until drovers from as far as Àth chiar and the eastern hills began, in {{year:III-0204}}, to bring their beasts down for it every autumn rather than sell nearer home. It became, within a few more years, the greatest fair in the east, and the town's reeve, who had inherited a market of modest size and modest reputation, is remembered chiefly for having done nothing at all to bring the fair's new fortune about beyond widening the ground it was held on, a plain honesty he himself is said to have insisted on whenever anyone tried to praise him for foresight he had never claimed.

## IV. Of the Queen Who Went Round

Sìleas Òg was crowned at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0205}}, twenty-four years old and the first of her line in three reigns to come to the throne with both her parents still living to see it, though neither lived to see much of what she made of it. She had grown up, by her own account given years later to a clerk who asked her plainly what had put the notion in her head, hearing the shire seats named at her father's own table as places on a map and nowhere else, a string of names a reeve's report might carry to the red hall once a year and no oftener, and she had thought, young enough still to say so aloud without anyone troubling to correct her, that a queen who had never set foot in Achadh shean or Baile Mòr ruadh or Doire fhionn knew those places the way a child knows a story told at the hearth: well enough to repeat it, not well enough to have stood in the rain there.

She began her round within two years of her crowning and kept at it, a shire seat or two each season as the roads and the weather allowed, until in LE 1,535 her circuit had carried her, by the vein-house's own reckoning of her comings and goings, to every seat the island's shires held, the first ruler of Dia-thìr of whom that could be said. It cost her nothing so grand as the reeves at each seat feared it might when word of the coming visit reached them first: she asked for no feast beyond what the house already kept, sat at the ordinary board rather than any board raised for her, and asked, at every seat alike, the same plain question of whoever the town set before her, what the shire lacked that the crown might supply and what the crown asked of the shire that the shire could not in truth give. She reached the last seat of her round at last, on the bare northern stretch few queens before her had troubled to visit at all, some half-dozen years into her reign, and is remembered to have said, of the whole labour of it, only that she had at last stopped repeating a story and started remembering a place, a distinction she never explained further to the clerk who wrote it down and that he never asked her to.

At Achadh shean, on the leg of her round that carried her furthest east, she stood at the well-head and had the reeve read her the board where the shares were cut, name by name, and asked him why the lots had never been drawn a second time in all the years since the dry seasons. The reeve, an old man who had held the office longer than the queen herself had been alive, answered that no one had ever wanted to be the household that asked for the lots to be drawn again, for the asking would have said, to every other household on the board, that the first sharing had been unfair, and no shire wanted that said of it over a matter of water it had, in the end, survived. Sìleas Òg is remembered to have looked at the board a long while before she answered him, and to have said only that a shire that would rather keep an old unfairness than name it was a shire she understood better for having stood at its well than she ever could have from a reeve's yearly report, and she rode on the next morning without asking the reeve to change anything at all.

It was on the strength of what her round had shown her, more than any single report a reeve had ever carried to the red hall unasked, that she commanded, once home again, a new count of every household holding the right to the coal. The count came back in {{year:III-0206}} smaller than any before it, the right narrowed by two centuries of house joining to house and holding swallowing holding until fewer families held more of it between them than had ever held any part of it before, and Sìleas Òg, reading the figure the vein-house's scribes laid before her, had it entered in the custody-book without a word of comment either way, a silence the scribes themselves are said to have found harder to read than any word she might have spoken over it.

Ceann mhin burned a second time in {{year:III-0207}}, and this time no war stood behind the fire for anyone to blame it on; a spark from an ordinary hearth, in an ordinary wind off the water, was all the town's own reeve could afterward find to set against the loss of half its waterfront a second time in living memory. He gave the order himself, before the ash had cooled, that the town be rebuilt with gable walls of stone standing between each house and its neighbour, so that whatever caught next would have to cross stone rather than thatch to reach the house beside it, and Ceann mhin was rebuilt that way within the decade, the only town on the coast, for a generation after, that a stranger could tell from its own neighbours by the stone standing plain between one roof and the next.

## V. Of the Silver Refused and the Steel Lost

Clann Choinnich brought a petition to the queen's council at Muileann dhearg, drafted by the house's own clerk after three winters of the head's own arguing at her own table that the coal, burning hotter than any charcoal the smiths of the east could otherwise buy, might be spared beyond the measured handful to smelt the silver of the seam at Muileann chiar. It was the first such asking the vein-house's custody-book had ever recorded, for the coal until that year had been burned only for worship, the small hearth-measure of the Small-Burning Law and no more, and the council that heard the petition in {{year:III-0208}} sat the better part of a day over it before it refused, by what the clerk's own record calls a narrow voice and does not further number. Sìleas Òg, presiding, is said to have given no ruling of her own beyond confirming the council's, and to have told Clann Choinnich's head, privately and afterward, that a law kept since before the fleet sailed for one reason was not easily bent to a second reason however sound the second reason was, a courtesy the old woman is said to have accepted without being in any way persuaded by it.

The clerk who had drafted the petition, a younger kinsman of the house who had spent the better part of a year weighing the coal's heat against the charcoal it would spare and setting the figures down in a hand as careful as any the vein-house's own scribes kept, is remembered to have asked, once the council's refusal was final, whether the figures themselves had been wrong. He was told they had not; the council had refused the asking and not the arithmetic, and the distinction, small as it sounds set down plainly, was one the young clerk is said to have carried with him the rest of his working life, keeping thereafter, in every paper he drew up for his house, a line at the foot of it noting which of his figures the council had never in fact disputed.

The smiths of Dùn dhearg, who had made the ceremonial blades of the custodian houses since the walls of that town first stood, lost in {{year:III-0209}} the last man among them who knew how the fellowship's layered steel was folded, a craft that had passed master to apprentice within four families for longer than the vein-house's own oldest custody-book could account for and that died, when the last of those four families' last trained hand died with no apprentice yet far enough along to have learned the whole of it, as completely and as suddenly as Muileann gheal's glaze had died sixty years before. The blades of Dùn dhearg were ordinary blades after him, well enough made and no better than any other smith's on the island, and the houses that had once prized a Dùn dhearg blade above any other kept the ones they already held the way the custodian tables kept their chipped grey-green dishes, a possession valued now for what it had once been rather than for anything the town's own forge could still produce.

The houses themselves, in {{year:III-0210}}, began burying their own heads at Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, among graves older than any name still legible on them, a custom the council settled on for reasons the custody-book gives only as fitting the dignity of the houses and no further. The tenants of the land about the mausoleum, who had buried their own dead where their own families had always buried them, called the new custom unwise to bury the great among graves no one living could any longer read the names of, and went on burying their own kin where they always had, a small disagreement between the houses and the land they held that neither side pressed the other to settle.

Of Sìleas Òg's own last years less is told than of her round or her count, and the king-list passes from her long reign to that of a kinsman of her own house, Ailean mac Raghnaill, with no note of grief or of gladness set between the one reign and the next, the plainest kind of ending the red hall's own clerks ever gave a ruler who had done as much as she had to be known by name in every shire seat on the island.

## VI. Of the Short King and the Shut Sea

Ailean mac Raghnaill, the fourth king of that name, was crowned at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0211}} at nine and thirty years old, and is remembered in the king-list for nothing else at all, for within the year he was dead at forty, the shortest reign the list had ever set down. What ailed him the custody-book does not say, only that his sister Mòrag nic Raghnaill, four and thirty years old herself, was crowned in his place before the same season had turned, and that she went on to reign some forty years more, the king-list's longest run of entries recording, in all that time, nothing graver than good harvests and the keeping of the rite. A clerk who served her the whole of those forty years is said to have remarked, near the end of his own service, that he had written more entries under her reign than under any three reigns before it and had, in all of them, set down no death by war, no famine and no quarrel between the seven houses worth the ink to record it, a run of quiet years the red hall's own roll had not seen the like of since before the sundering fleet had ever gone down the river. Mòrag nic Raghnaill herself is remembered to have kept, of her brother's short reign, one habit only: she had the year of his crowning and the year of his death cut on the same stone rather than on two, a small economy of grief that her own clerks never asked her to explain and that stood, long after her own reign had ended, as the plainest record the king-list gave of the brother she had outlived by forty years.

It was in the first year of her reign, {{year:III-0212}}, that the priests of Manannan gathered in synod at Seann Skell and restated, before the assembled orders of their own house, the rule of sight-of-land that Manannan's priests at Ros bheag had first laid down nine centuries before: that no boat of the faithful was to go beyond sight of the headlands, on Manannan's own word that the mist behind the fleet was shut once and would not be opened a second time for any pilot bold enough or foolish enough to look for it. To the old rule the synod added a new prayer, to be said by the priests at the foot of the river pool where the fleet had once turned sunwise and gone, words the synod itself is said to have argued over through two full days before agreeing on the shortest form of them: *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.* Manannan, keep the sea shut.

Further down the eastern road, in years quieter than any the priests' synod troubled itself with, a ferry family settled at Cidhe Beag on the Abhainn uaine in {{year:III-0214}} and took up the crossing there by canoe, carrying folk and their goods across water that no bridge had ever been thrown over and none, in that stretch of the river, was ever likely to be; they held the landing, father to daughter and daughter to son, for the rest of the age, and travellers on that road came to speak of the crossing the way they spoke of any other fixed thing on the road, a ford, a road-house, a ferry that had simply always been there. And on the slopes above Àth shean, growers who had pressed blaeberry and bramble into a dark wine for their own tables since no one could say when began, in {{year:III-0214a}}, to sell the pressing along the eastern road in casks that kept a full year without souring, and the road-houses of that stretch took to serving it as black wine to travellers who had never before had anything darker than ale set before them at a road-house table, a small trade that outlasted, as small trades sometimes do, a good deal that the age's own reeves and councils had thought far more likely to last.

The priests of Manannan said their new prayer at the foot of the river pool that same year and every year after, at every tide the rite required of them, long after the ferry at Cidhe Beag and the black wine of Àth shean had settled into the ordinary business of the roads and no longer seemed, to anyone who used them, like anything new at all: keep the sea shut, said at the water's edge where the fleet had gone, the one part of that whole age's business with the sea that the island's own priests were still, after so many centuries, careful to say aloud.
