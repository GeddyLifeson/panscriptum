# Of Manannan's Cup

*LE 650 – 699*

> *Is fhada an cuan, is goirid a' chuimhne.*
> Long is the sea, and short is memory.
>
> — kept afterward on the west coast, of the rule that came out of Ros bheag

## I. Of the Preaching on the Quay

Mànas mac Ruairidh had grown up under the shadow of the harbour-court at {{place:burg:489}}, in a house so near the water that his mother used to say the tide kept better time than his father did, coming and going by a clock she never once had to wind and never once caught lying, which was more, she said, than could be said of most clocks or most husbands. His father Ruairidh had sworn the old oath of the quay in his own youth, the sting-oath the western merchants kept among themselves to bind their word in a trade where a man's whole living might ride on another man's honesty and no scale or measure could weigh that; and he kept, all the years Mànas knew him, a strip of tarred rope hung by the door in token of it, though he never once told his son plainly what the oath bound a man to, saying only that it was enough that it bound, the way the tide was enough without a boy needing to know why it rose.

Mànas asked him once, at twelve, sitting on the sill while his father mended a creel by lamplight, what he had sworn on, exactly, to make the mark that never faded from his palm. His father set the creel down, held up his hand so the lamp caught the old scar full on, a mark gone the colour of weathered wood and shaped, if a boy looked at it long enough, something like a fork of lightning striking down into the wrist, and said only that the mark answered better than his mouth ever could, and that a boy who wanted to know what a sting-oath cost a man would do better to watch how carefully his elders spoke, all his life, than to be told it once and think the telling was the same as the knowing. He let that be the end of it, and went back to the creel, and Mànas, watching him work by the small light, understood without being told again that there were questions in that house that a father answered with his hands and never with his tongue.

He grew to a young man who could splice a rope and gut a fish as well as any on the strand, and had, besides, a voice that carried further over open water than most men's carried over dry land, a gift he discovered by accident one wet morning hailing a boat in from a squall that had half the harbour shouting itself hoarse and unheard, and put, for some years after, to no grander use than calling men in from the rocks to their dinner. By thirty, having buried his mother two winters before and watched his father grow slow and stooped over the same creels, he stood up one still morning on the harbour stones at {{place:burg:489}} and said aloud the thing that had been gathering in him, he told those who asked him afterward, since he was a boy on a sill watching a scarred hand answer for a mouth that would not.

He chose, whether by his own design or only because it was the ground he had always stood on, the same stretch of quay where the old sting-creed of the Holy Age had first stood up, longer ago than any living memory reached, and called itself, before any other order on the island, the one true faith; and it was there, on {{date:III-0120}}, with the tide going out and a handful of fishermen mending net within easy earshot, more from idleness that morning than from any intent to listen, that Mànas named the power he had come, at last, to preach. He named Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea, raising his voice only a little, for it carried without needing to be raised: not the stone at Cnoc bheag, not the hawk, not the ancestors under their posts at Dùn ìseal. Manannan's Cup of Truth would not hold a lie spoken over it, he said, and broke, so the old telling had it, in the very hand of the man who tried; and it bore, worked into its rim, the yellow scorpion for its sign, sting and truth being, he said, closer kin than most men supposed. It was Manannan, and no other power under any name the island kept, who had drawn his mist across the eastern sea behind the fleet that had once gone out from this same quay; and what that fleet had done, the going out past every sight of land and star and known shore, must never, while the mist held, be tried again by anyone who called himself faithful to the god whose sea it was.

The net-menders did not stop mending net while he spoke, not at first, and he had sense enough, even new to speaking, not to ask it of them. But before he had finished, one of them had stopped, and it was his own father, who rose from his creel and crossed the wet stones with the rope-mark still plain on his upturned palm and knelt, an old sworn-man on wet stone in front of his own son, and said only, low enough that few beyond Mànas himself ever swore they had heard it, that a mark on a hand was one kind of oath and a thing spoken plain in daylight was another, and that he had waited the whole of his life, without knowing he was waiting, to hear which kind his son would choose to make. Six others knelt after him before the tide had gone out much further, fishermen and net-menders who had known Ruairidh's son since he was a boy hailing boats off the rocks, and it was those seven, the old sworn-man and the six who came behind him, that the harbour afterward remembered as the first of Òrd Mhanannain, the order of Manannan, though Mànas himself, asked afterward who had truly been first, would only say that a father owes his son that much attention and leave the rest of the honour unclaimed.

Word of it did not travel fast in that first season, no faster than any other quay-talk carried on a wet wind, and Mànas kept mostly to preaching where he had always stood, growing neither rich nor grand on it, sleeping still under his father's roof and eating what his father's nets, and increasingly his own, brought in. It was five years on that the first boats were brought up for blessing at the berth the harbour scribes had begun, a generation before, to call Cidhe an Diosail: a dozen coasters brought in one after another on a still morning, their skippers standing bareheaded on the stones while Mànas, no longer so young himself, passed a cup of harbour water over each hull in turn and spoke the words that would, within his own lifetime, harden into the fixed form of the rite. He used no vessel finer that first year than a plain wooden bowl, having no silver and wanting none, and when one of the skippers, a man better off than most, offered to have a proper cup made and gilded for the purpose, Mànas told him that Manannan's own Cup had never needed a smith's hand to make it true, and that a bowl which had held nothing but harbour water and honest words was nearer the god's own Cup than any gilded thing a smith could raise.

The harbour-court, whose own sworn-men had kept the sting-oath at {{place:burg:489}} since long before Mànas's father wore the mark, did not welcome the new teaching as readily as the net-menders had, and for some years after the first kneeling the court's own scribe, a careful man named Donnchadh mac Ailein who had no particular quarrel with Manannan but a settled one with any change to the harbour's old order, refused to enter the order's blessings in the harbour rolls alongside the tallies of hulls and cargoes, saying that a rite was not a hull and had no business on a board kept for hulls. Mànas never pressed the matter, and told his own congregation, when one of them grumbled at the scribe's refusal, that a rite recorded on a board and a rite kept true in a man's own dealings were not the same thing, and that he would rather have the second without the first than the first without the second. It was not until some years after Mànas's own death that the harbour-house began, of its own accord and without any order from the court, to note in its margins when a hull had been blessed at Cidhe an Diosail, a small mark beside the entry that later harbour scribes kept without ever troubling to ask why it had begun.

A month after that first blessing, word came up the coast road that Lachlann mac Iain had died at {{place:burg:19}}, eighty-nine years old and quiet to the last, fifty years on the throne his mother had left him without, so far as the harbour ever heard, doing anything a quay would trouble to remember; and that his daughter Anna, fifty-four years old and long past any waiting for it, had been crowned in the same hall. Mànas heard the tidings the way the town heard most tidings, from a rider who stopped at the horse-trough by the harbour-court to water his horse and talked while it drank, and he is not recorded to have said anything about it at all, which the harbour took, rightly or not, for a kind of answer of its own: that the sea's governance and the crown's were, to his mind, two different powers entirely, and that the death of one king, however long his quiet reign, was the crown's business to mark and not the tide's.

## II. Of the Cup Carried Down the Coast

The teaching went where the fishing went, which was down the coast before it went anywhere else, carried less by Mànas's own feet, which stayed mostly within a day's walk of {{place:burg:489}} to the end of his life, than by the fishing families themselves, who traded catch and net-twine from harbour to harbour and, riding along with them in the same creel, whatever a man had lately taken to believing. By the time Mànas was an old man it had reached {{place:burg:63}} to the south and {{place:burg:282}} to the north, both of them towns that had given their strand and their labour to the building of the fleet in the years before the Sundering and had never, in all the years since, quite stopped listening for word of anything to do with the sea beyond their own headlands; and it was at {{place:burg:282}}, among the rope-walks that had once measured cable enough for keels no one now living had seen launched, that the teaching took hold hardest and earliest of anywhere beyond Mànas's own quay.

An old rope-walker there named Tormod Ciar, who had walked the same length of ground backward twisting strand into cable since he was a boy too young to be paid a man's full wage for it, was among the first of Cuan shean to kneel, and he did it, by his own account, not for any word Mànas had ever spoken to him in person, for the two men never once met in this life, but because a travelling net-mender stopped under the eaves of the rope-shed one wet evening, waiting out a squall, and told the walkers gathered there what the preacher at {{place:burg:489}} had said of the mist and the fleet and the sea that must not be tried again. Tormod, who had spent the whole of his working life laying rope for ships that no longer sailed anywhere beyond sight of their own shore, and had never once, in all his years at the walk, been given a reason for it beyond that the fishing did not need longer cable than the fishing needed, found in the net-mender's telling the first account he had ever heard that made any sense of why his own trade had shrunk, year on year, without ever once, in his lifetime, growing back to what the old wrights' fellowship had once built it for. He swore the Cup before his fellow rope-walkers on the walk itself the following morning, with no priest of the order nearer than {{place:burg:489}} to hear him, cupping harbour rain in his own two hands for want of a bowl, and told them afterward, more than once, that he had sworn it to the sea and not to any man standing on dry ground, and that the sea, unlike a man, had never yet broken an oath it was given honestly.

Mànas himself grew old at the harbour he had never left, and the house the order raised in those years, Taigh Mhanannain, stood not far from the harbour-court his father had sworn to, low and plain and built of the same grey stone as the quay, so that a stranger walking past it on a grey morning might have taken it, at first glance, for one more shed of nets and tar rather than the house of a growing order. He kept the rite the same each year he had strength left to stand for it, the bowl grown at last to a proper cup of plain silver that his own congregation had raised the coin for without his asking, and he is remembered by those who were young when he was old as a man who spoke less as the years went on rather than more, having said, he told one of his own who asked him plainly why an old teacher should fall so quiet, most of what he had ever had to say in the first years of his preaching, and seeing no good cause to say it twice over merely because he had lived long enough to be asked.

In the last winter of his life, when he could no longer stand the whole length of the blessing without a younger man's arm under his own, a woman of the order named Beathag nic Dhonnchaidh, who had kept his house those last ten years and would keep the order's house after him, asked him one evening by the fire whether he feared the mist would lift before the island had properly learned what Manannan had shut it in from. He answered, she told others afterward, that a fisherman who feared every squall before it rose would never once put out from the strand, and that a teacher who spent his last strength fearing a thing that had not yet come was a poor steward of the strength Manannan had given him to spend on the thing that had. He died soon after, at {{place:burg:489}}, in the house he had raised, within sound, his own people said, of the same water he had preached beside for sixty years, and was buried, at his own asking, under a plain slab, cut with no name, set facing the harbour mouth, so that the tide he had spent his whole life speaking of would be the last thing, and the first thing after, to pass over him. Those who came to Taigh Mhanannain in the years right after his death found it kept much as he had kept it, by men and women, Beathag nic Dhonnchaidh foremost among them, who had never once heard him preach with their own ears and knew his teaching only at second and third hand, the way the island, by then, knew most of what it believed.

## III. Of the Rule of the Headlands

Beathag nic Dhonnchaidh, who had kept Mànas's house and, after him, kept the order's, lived to see the teaching carried further than the old man had ever thought to carry it. The priests who rose up after him at {{place:burg:63}}, where the teaching had taken almost as deep a root as at Mànas's own quay, met through the better part of a winter to debate, by all the later order kept of it, not whether the sea beyond the headlands was Manannan's to forbid, which none of them doubted, but how far a boat might go before the forbidding took hold; and it was an old priestess of that house, one who had herself, as a girl, watched the last of the pilots die without finishing what he meant to teach, who argued the debate down at last to a single plain rule, that no boat of the faithful might go where the headland could no longer be seen from its own deck. In {{year:III-0123}} it was laid down so, on pain of standing before the Cup and answering for it, and the rule held on the west coast, once it was made, for longer than any one preacher's lifetime could have hoped to account for. It was not, those who kept it afterward always insisted, a fear of the sea itself, which Manannan's own faithful were taught to hold in a kind of grave and working affection rather than dread, but a fear of what the sea beyond sight of land had already once been made to carry away, and what it might, if the mist should ever thin, be asked to carry away again.

The rule was tested within its own first year, off {{place:burg:63}}, by a skipper named Coinneach mac Sheumais who had fished the same grounds for thirty years without troubling any priest's opinion of where his own boat might go, and who, on a clear still morning with the headland still just visible astern, no more than a low grey line, took his boat a little further out than he had ever taken it before, chasing a run of fish that seemed, that particular day, well worth the chasing. He was turned back before noon by two of the order's own boats, crewed by men he had drunk with his whole life, who came up on either side of him and told him plainly, not unkindly, that the rule made no exception for fish however plentiful, and stood off his bow, oars shipped, until he brought his own boat about and ran before the wind back the way he had come. "I could still see the headland," Coinneach is said to have told them, and the elder of the two boats' skippers is said to have answered only that the rule did not ask what a man could still see, only how far off the land he stood when he saw it, which silenced Coinneach more thoroughly than any longer citing of the rule could have done. He never fished that far out again, and told his own sons, afterward, that he had not minded the turning back nearly so much as he had minded finding out, in front of two men he had known since boyhood, that he had been wrong about how far a headland could still be seen once a man had already made up his mind to go past it.

What the rule cost the west coast, over the span of years it held, was harder to see in the turning back of a single boat than it was seen weighed whole, as those who came after had cause to weigh it. The steering of a course by the stars, already half lost on that shore since the pilots who had carried Seumas Reultach's own teaching west had grown old and died without setting down on hide more than a fragment of what they had once held in their heads, went out entirely where it had never before, in all the years since the fleet's building, had the chance to be forgotten so completely, for there was no longer any voyage sailed on the west coast long enough, or dark enough, or far enough from a known headland, to need it kept. A pilot's grandson at {{place:burg:63}}, a boy named Griogair, was asked one evening by a visiting priest of the order which star his grandfather, a man once counted among the last on that shore who could hold a course by night, had steered by. The boy, who had heard his grandfather's name spoken all his life with a kind of respect he had never fully understood the grounds of, only that the old man had once, before Griogair was born, gone further out on the water than any boat now went and had come home again, could not answer, and stood a while working at the question as though it might yield to being stared at hard enough before admitting, plainly, that he did not know. Nobody in his own house, it came out in the asking, had thought, while there was still anyone alive who remembered it, to write the star's name down or teach it onward, having had, each of them in turn, other and nearer things to teach a boy first.

At {{place:burg:67}}, further inland and further still from any water Manannan's priests troubled themselves to bless, the royal granaries that had stood since before the Sundering, low buildings of timber and old stone that had weathered the tallies of a hundred harvests, were pulled down in {{year:III-0124}} and built again from their own foundations, in dressed stone this time and on raised floors set well clear of the ground, against the rats that had for years been getting into the lower bins faster than any night-watch could keep them out. The tally-house went up new beside the granaries in the same stone, and the reeve who oversaw the rebuilding, an old man named Fearchar mac Phàdraig near the end of his own long service, walked the finished floors once with a lamp held low to the new stone and said only, when he was satisfied, that a granary built to outlast the rats might, with a little luck, outlast the reeve who had seen it built as well, and went back to his tallies without further comment on a labour that had taken the better part of three years and left the shire's grain, that first winter, dry from wall to wall without a single sack lost to damp or gnawing, a thing no man then living at {{place:burg:67}} could remember having happened before.

It is told at {{place:burg:489}}, where the order kept its house and its rite long after the man who had begun both was laid under his plain slab facing the harbour mouth, that a keeper of Taigh Mhanannain in those same years was asked by a stranger passing through, a merchant of no particular faith who had heard the teaching only as one more curiosity of the west coast worth a traveller's idle question, whether the order held that the mist would lie over the eastern sea for ever. The keeper is said to have answered that Manannan's mist had been drawn once, for a reason the order did not claim to know in full, and that it would lift, if it ever lifted, in its own time and by no persuasion of theirs, and that in the meanwhile the Cup asked only truth of those who swore to it and left the sea's own intentions to the sea alone. The stranger asked nothing further and went on his way, and the keeper, watching him go, is said to have thought, though not to have said it aloud to anyone, that a boy at {{place:burg:63}} who could not name his own grandfather's star had already answered the merchant's question, without meaning to and without knowing he had done it, as well as any priest of the order ever could.

The harbour rolls say nothing of the boy Griogair afterward, for a boy who does not follow the sea leaves no mark on a harbour board, and Manannan's own keepers, unlike the harbour scribes, wrote down oaths sworn and boats blessed and little else beside. It is told only, at {{place:burg:63}} among those who kept the priest's question and the boy's silence as a small cautionary tale of their own, that Griogair grew to a man who worked the land above the harbour rather than the water below it, and that when he had sons of his own he taught them, whatever else he judged worth a father's teaching, the names of every headland visible from the shore before he taught them anything of what lay beyond it, having decided, so far as anyone could later say for him, that a thing worth keeping was a thing a man ought to write down or speak aloud the first year he knew it, and not wait, as his own house had waited with his grandfather's star, for someone else's asking to prove the waiting had already cost him everything there was to lose.
