# Of the Stair under the Hill

*LE 944 – 1,015*

## I. Of the Road and the Empty Pastures

By {{year:III-0145a}} a rider going up from {{place:burg:19}} never rode the whole way on one horse. He changed at a road-house every few hours, wherever the coal-roads' own charter had set one, and the keeper of the house had a fresh animal saddled and standing before the old one had fully stopped, so that the word of the council could travel from the red hall to the mountain between one dawn and the next dark without once slowing to a walk. The stabling was paid, every quarter, out of the tolls the roads themselves raised, and a keeper who let a rider wait so much as the time it took to draw a bucket of water heard of it soon enough from the reeve who kept the charter.

A keeper of one such house, a widow named Mairead nic Aonghais who had held her stretch of the northern road for eleven years, kept a tally of her own beside the reeve's: a notch cut for every rider who came through wearing the crown's own badge, and a second, smaller notch for every one of them who thanked her by name rather than simply throwing the reins at her boy and swinging up onto the fresh horse before his feet were properly in the stirrups. She said, to the one traveller who ever thought to ask her about it, that the first tally kept the reeve satisfied and the second kept her own opinion of the office satisfied, and that the two tallies did not always run in step with one another. It became a boast repeated at every road-house table from the capital to the pass, all the same, that a rider of the crown's had never yet gone hungry, thirsty or afoot on a coal-road, whatever else might be said of the years, and Mairead's own notched stick, kept behind her door long after she was too old to saddle a horse herself, was shown to more than one grandchild as proof of it. She kept a third mark too, smaller still and cut with a different knife, for the riders who came through carrying nothing worth the council's own asking, grain prices and boundary pleas of no great weight, and she said once, to a reeve who pressed her on why she troubled to keep so fine a tally at all, that a woman who fed and mounted the crown's own business every day of her working life had earned the right to know, for her own satisfaction if nobody else's, how much of that business was worth the horse it rode on.

It was in {{year:III-0146}} that the last shepherd families came down out of the high pastures of Doire uaine, in the hill country north of the mountain, and settled in the valley villages below. They gave the grazing as their reason, and the grazing was true enough; the grass up there had thinned with every dry summer for a generation, and a flock that once wintered fat on the high ground now came down each autumn with its ribs showing plainer than the year before. But every family that made the move, asked why now and not ten years before, spoke also of the Marbh-bheò, the dead-alive that was said to walk the empty shielings once the flocks had gone down, and not one of them could say exactly when they had first begun to feel that the high ground no longer wanted their company as much as their sheep still wanted its grass. An old woman of that country, the last to leave her own shieling, driving three goats and a lame ewe ahead of her down a track her own mother had walked before her, told the priest who met her at the valley's edge that a place could grow tired of being lived in alone by living things, and that she had felt the tiredness of it in her own bones a good while before her knees ever gave her a better reason to come down. The high shielings stood empty from that year, their turf roofs falling in one by one over the winters that followed, and no family of that country went up to winter a flock on that ground again. A drover passing that way some years on, driving cattle to the lowland market by the old high road because it was shorter than the coast way, said he had ridden the whole stretch of the empty pastures in half a day and had not once, in all that riding, heard a dog bark or seen so much as a wisp of hearth-smoke where a dozen shielings had once stood smoking every evening of his own boyhood. He said the silence of it troubled him more than any wolf or storm the road had ever given him, and that he pushed his cattle on rather than camp there the night, though the light was failing and a camp there would have cost him nothing but the asking of ground nobody was left to grant or refuse.

{{place:burg:76}} kept its salt fair in {{year:III-0147}} as it had kept it every year within living memory, the salters working the pans from before first light and the carters lining up their carts along the flats for a full mile before the gates opened. The reeve who counted heads that day, for no better reason than that the crowd looked larger to him than any he remembered counting, gave up somewhere past four thousand, lost among the press of carts and pack-mules and children darting between the pans to steal a pinch of the white crust before their mothers caught them at it. He wrote the figure down anyway, rounding it plainly rather than pretend to a precision the crowd itself had never held still for, and it stood, in the fair's own rolls, as the largest day the town's salt trade ever saw. Nobody standing on the flats that morning had any way of knowing it was the height the town would reach; the chronicle that named it afterward the richest place in the north-east wrote no more, in truth, than what every salter and every carter on those flats had already believed of their own town without needing telling.

Further south, by {{year:III-0148}}, {{place:burg:23}}'s market drew more carts off more roads than the capital's own, until a trader newly come to the island could stand in either market and know within a morning which town he was in by the noise of it alone. The capital kept the crown, and kept the name of the greater town in every old roll that still gave it so, but the weight of custom, such as there was in barter and salt and silver neck-rings, went where the goods went, and the goods went, season on season, more and more to Cathair mhòr. No council sat to write a law about it, for a town does not need a law to outgrow another; it needs only more roads meeting inside its own walls, and Cathair mhòr had those roads, and kept gaining them.

A carter of the southern road, asked once by a clerk of the red hall why he always sold his barley at Cathair mhòr's market and not the capital's, said only that a man carried his load to whichever gate opened first and paid best, and that the clerk's own question was the sort a man asked from a hall, not from a cart. The clerk wrote the answer down as given, without comment, and it is the plainest word that survives of how the one town came to outgrow the other: a thousand carters, each on his own morning, choosing the gate that opened first, with no single act any leaf remembers behind it.

## II. Of the Stair under the Hill

Calum Dubh mac Iain had cut stone on the quarry face north of the capital since he was old enough to hold a hammer without dropping it on his own foot, and by {{year:III-0149}}, the year the face gave way under him, he was nine-and-twenty, broad through the shoulders from the work and quieter than most men of his age, the kind of quarryman a foreman set to a stubborn seam because he would go on chipping at it a full day without once losing his temper over it. He had a wife at home and a boy not yet walking, and nothing about the morning the face opened marked it out, to him, from any other morning he had climbed the ladder to the working ledge with his lamp and his hammers slung over his shoulder.

The rock had been sounding wrong for the better part of a week, a hollowness under the ring of the tools that the older men on the ledge muttered about between themselves and the younger ones mostly let pass. On the morning it finally let go it did not fall so much as open: a slab the size of a door swung inward on nothing at all and left a black mouth in the rock where a solid seam had been standing a breath before. The men nearest it stepped back, as any man will step back from a hole where stone had just stood, and for a long moment nobody said a word, because the air that came up out of the opening was cold as well-water and carried no smell whatever, not damp earth nor old stone nor the tar-and-tallow reek of a worked gallery, and a nothing where a man's own nose looks for something is a stranger thing to stand near than any bad smell would have been.

One of the older men held his lamp in at arm's length, leaning as far over the black edge as he dared, and the light found steps: a stair cut into the living rock, tread below tread, each one worn to a shallow bowl at its middle as though feet beyond any counting had climbed it long before a single quarryman now living had drawn breath, going down and down past the point where the lamp's own light gave out entirely. It was Calum Dubh, standing a little behind the rest, who said the name before any other man on the ledge could get it past his own teeth: Toll-dubh, the shaft of the old tellings that every quarryman's grandmother had used to frighten children quiet at the hearth, and that no living soul had ever set eyes on, since no living soul had ever known where the ground had hidden it. A boy who had come up that morning with the water-skins said afterward that the quiet on the ledge, once Calum Dubh had said the name, had a weight to it he had never once felt on a quarry face before, as though every man standing there had remembered, in the same breath, a thing they had agreed among themselves long ago, and never once out loud, not to look at too closely.

The foreman asked, in the flattest voice a man can manage when he is asking for something he does not want done, whether any of them would go down and look. Nobody answered him at once. Calum Dubh said afterward, to the one man who ever got more than a handful of words from him about that morning, that he could not rightly account for why it was his own hand and not another's that reached first for the second lamp; he had made no decision he could remember making, and found the lamp already lit and his own boot already on the top tread before the thought of volunteering had properly finished forming. Four men on the ledge drew straws for who should pay out the rope behind him, and every one of the four, by his own later telling, was glad past speaking of it to have lost.

His fellows watched the lamp go: a small light turning on the stair's own bends, dwindling fold by fold of the rock until it was no bigger than a star low on a winter sky, and then nothing at all, the dark of the shaft closing over it as complete as water closing over a dropped stone. They waited. Rope paid out and rope stood still; somebody counted his own heartbeats for a while and then gave it up, unable to say afterward how many he had reached. One of the men who kept that watch, an old quarryman near the end of his own working years, told his grandchildren once, and told no one else in his life besides, that they had waited long enough on that ledge to grow ashamed of how glad every one of them was, all at the same instant, when the light came back at last, small and rising, climbing the same bends it had gone down by.

Calum Dubh came up into the grey daylight of the ledge, sat himself down on the loose stone at the quarry's edge, and said nothing at all. He sat there long enough that the foreman sent the water-boy running for the vein-house's own custodians rather than wait on a report from a man who plainly had none in him yet to give. When Calum Dubh did speak at last, to the foreman alone and out of the other men's hearing, he said nothing of what he had seen below. He said only, in a voice the foreman described afterward as tired past the ordinary run of tired and entirely steady besides, that the quarry face should be closed before any other man went near it, whatever word came back from the mountain.

The custodians rode down within the week, four of them, and stood a long while at the mouth of the opening without going in themselves, looking at the black of it the way a man looks at a dog he is not sure will bite. Their order, when it came, held fewer words than the morning seemed to want: the mouth of it was to be walled, stone set on stone and mortared and left, and no quarryman was to work that face again as long as the vein-house had any say in the matter. It was done inside a month, a plain wall of dressed stone set flush into the face as though the stair had never once opened behind it, and the custodians took what little Calum Dubh gave them of his own account and entered it in the custody-book exactly as he gave it, adding not one word of their own to fill out its silences.

He went on cutting stone for six-and-thirty years more, on other faces of the same quarry at first and later, as his own knees grew less willing, on quieter work nearer his house; and in all that time he never once told a living soul what he had seen on the stair below the last bend his fellows' watching had lost to the dark. His wife asked him, that first year and for some years after. His son asked him once, grown and thinking, perhaps, that a father might tell a grown son what he had never told a wife. Even the vein-house's own scribe asked him, gently, of an evening when the custody-book's entry on the matter seemed a good deal thinner than the occasion deserved. He gave every one of them the same answer, in the same tired, steady voice the foreman had first heard on the ledge: that a man who had gone down and come back owed the rock, if he owed it nothing else, the leaving of what he had found there exactly where he had found it. He died in his own house in his sixty-fifth year, an old man well liked in the quarry town for a quiet, careful kindness that carried in it nothing of a man weighed down by a fright he could not set aside; and whatever he had seen on the stair under the hill went into the ground beside him, unspoken to the end, first and last.

The walled face itself outlived him by no time at all worth remarking, for the quarry moved on to easier stone within a few years and the mortared stones grew a skin of moss no different from any other unworked rock on that hillside. But the quarry town kept, for as long as the town itself stood, a way of speaking of a close-mouthed man that named no stair and no shaft at all: to say of someone that he had *gone down and come up like Calum Dubh* was to say, plainly enough for anyone raised in that town to understand it, that here was a man who had seen something and judged, on his own authority and nobody else's, that it belonged kept rather than told. It was said of more than one quarryman, in the years that followed, who had seen a bad fall and would not speak of it, and of at least one custodian who kept his own counsel on a matter of the vein-house's business that the coal-right households never did learn the whole of. None of them, so far as any leaf remembers, had gone anywhere near Toll-dubh at all; the saying had outgrown the man who first earned it, as sayings do, and stood on its own by then, needing no stair beneath it to be understood.

## III. Of the Ruins Ailean Measured

By {{year:III-0150}} the copper-workers of {{place:burg:23}} had begun casting cooking pots for every hearth on the island that could spare a season's barter for one, each pot stamped on its underside with its own maker's mark, a leaf, a fish, or a plain letter worked into the mould before the metal was ever poured. They went out of that town on every road at once, north and along the coast and south to the market-towns, and a housewife of Ros fhionn or Cuan shean setting one over her own fire for the first time was, more often than she ever knew, cooking over the work of a hand she would never meet in a town she would never see. Some of those pots are still turned up by a plough or a ditcher's spade with the maker's mark worn thin on the underside, and no leaf that survives can say now which hand cast which.

It was in {{year:III-0151}} that Ailean mac Dhùghaill, a scholar of the druid school at Dùn thais, walked north with a measuring cord and a set of wax tablets to the broken ring of standing stone the island called Làrach an Teampaill, the temple-ruin, which no living memory could say who had raised or to what purpose. He was nine-and-twenty that year, a careful, unhurried man his own teachers had marked out early as fitter for measuring stone than for arguing a bench, and he had asked the school's leave to spend a season doing nothing else at all but walk the old ruin's circuit with his cord and set down, plainly, whatever he found there. He laid his cord across the altar-stone at the ring's centre and found, when he had done, that it faced squarely toward the central mountains and turned its back on the sea; a plain fact, and one that no teller of the ruin's few surviving tales had ever thought worth the mentioning, since every teller before him had simply assumed, without once saying so aloud, that so old an altar must face the water as the schools' own shrines faced it now. Who had set the stone so, and to what end, Ailean would not guess. He said as much, in almost those words, to the two or three fellow scholars who pressed him for an opinion before his tables were even finished.

He went on, in the years after, to measure the other two ruins the island still argued over at its own hearths: Làrach an Dùin, the fortress-ruin whose fallen wall a farmer's own plough still turned up stone by stone at the edge of his nearest field, and Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, the watch-fort on its headland where a shepherd could still stand, on a clear day, and see three days' sail of coast running away in either direction. The farmer at Làrach an Dùin met Ailean at his own field boundary with a barrowload of the stones his plough had turned up that season and asked him, half in earnest, whether the school meant to pay a fair price for the ones his own field kept giving back every spring whether he wanted them or not. Ailean bought none of them, but he set every stone the farmer showed him into his tablets before it went back onto the boundary wall, the farmer standing over him the whole while with his arms crossed, plainly unconvinced that a stone worth measuring so carefully was not, by rights, a stone worth paying for. Each ruin he walked as he had walked the first, every standing wall and fallen stone measured with his own cord, every faced stone's bearing set into his tablets before he trusted himself to draw so much as a single conclusion from the whole of it. More than once a pupil pressed him to say what the numbers meant before he had checked them a second time against the first pass, and more than once he sent the pupil away unanswered until he had.

By {{year:III-0152}}, the year he judged the work finished, he had a set of tables giving the measure, the stone and the lie of all three ruins laid out side by side, set down more exactly than any single teller's account of any one of them had ever managed before him. He wrote, at the tables' close, that he could not himself say with any confidence whether one people had raised all three or three peoples each their own, for the stone of each ruin was cut in a manner none of the others quite matched, and the compass-bearings of their altars and gateways agreed with one another in no pattern he could find, however many ways he laid the figures side by side to look for one. He set the finished tables before the school at Dùn thais for its own judgment, and the school, after a winter's argument on its own benches, judged only that his measurements stood sound and his caution on the larger question stood sounder still.

No scholar who came after him, in all the years his tables were kept and copied out fresh for the school's own shelves, ever found in the numbers a pattern that Ailean himself had missed. His caution left the question no room to be answered more boldly than he had left it, and so the question the three old ruins had always asked of the island simply closed, from that year onward: measured as far as honest measuring could carry it, and no further, in the same plain hand as the rest of his own tables, which set down, alongside every figure he had taken, exactly how much of the matter must be left standing unmeasured.
