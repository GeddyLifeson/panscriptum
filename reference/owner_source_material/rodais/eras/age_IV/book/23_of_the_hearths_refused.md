# Of the Hearths Refused

*LE 558 – 560*

## I. Of the List in the Yard

Eòghann mac Dhùghaill had kept the store of {{place:burg:491}} for eleven years, and in all that time he had done one thing only: he had measured out the handful. A household came up the white road with its sealed stick, and he weighed the coal against the notches on the beam, and he wrote the weight in the book, and the carrier went down again with the basket on his back. That was the whole of his work, and his father's before him, and it had not changed since the law was old.

He was standing in the yard before first light, stamping to keep the blood in his feet, when Raghnall Ruadh came out of the hall with a board under his arm.

The carriers were already gathering at the gate, breath standing white above them, the baskets stacked and the measures laid out on the trestle as they were laid out every morning of the world. Beyond the gate the road went down grey and empty into the grey shires, and somewhere below the mist a cock was crowing at a fire that had not yet been lit.

Raghnall's clerk had drawn the board up by lamplight; Eòghann could see, even across the yard, that the man had barely slept, his hand shaking a little as he held it out to his lord for a last look. Raghnall took it, and read it through once in the thin light, his breath going out slow through his nose, and then he crossed the yard and put it into Eòghann's hands.

It was a list of shires. Eòghann knew most of the names on it: the granges above Doire mhin, the temple of Macha at Cathair mhòr, three market towns that had never done him any wrong, half the households along the Abhainn fhionn. Every hearth and every temple hearth in the shires that had held for Catrìona or for Dùghall, in one column, in the clerk's small cramped hand, with the amount each had drawn last winter written beside it, as though the winter before had been an ordinary winter, and could be totted up like any other.

"None of these," said Raghnall Ruadh.

Eòghann looked at the list, then at him. Nine sons of the store had held this yard before him, back past his father's father to a man whose name was only a mark in Leabhar a' Chùraim, and none of them, so far as the book showed, had ever measured a lie into the beam, and none of them had ever measured nothing. Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig was older than any king who claimed the crown of it. It did not know how to withhold.

"Lord," he said, and did not know how to go on.

"None of these," Raghnall said again, in the same voice, as if the first time had not been heard, and he turned and went in out of the wind, and the door of the hall shut behind him with the ordinary small sound a door makes, which seemed to Eòghann, standing there with the board cold in his hands, a very great deal too small a sound for what had just been done.

He stood a while with the list. Then he went to the trestle and told the first carrier in the line, a girl from one of the near granges he had weighed for since she was old enough to carry, that there was nothing for her house this week, nor for the temple at the crossroads, nor for her aunt's hearth at the mill. She did not understand him at first; she thought he was making some kind of joke that store-masters did not make, and asked him twice, and the second time he heard his own voice come out flatter than he meant it, reciting the names off the board the way he had been taught to recite the weights, because it was easier to say a list than to say a reason.

By noon the yard was three-quarters empty of baskets. The store still had coal in it, heaped black under the roof, more than enough; that was the thing Eòghann could not get past, all that day and for a long time after. Nothing had failed. Nothing had run short. A man in a hall had looked at a board and said a word, and the wealth of the mountain sat exactly where it had sat the day before, only now it belonged to no one who needed it.

He weighed the handful for the households still on the board until the light went, because that at least the law still let him do, and then he banked the store's own fire for the night, and stood a moment at the gate looking down the white road with its churned tracks going nowhere in particular, half of them turned back on themselves where a carrier had come up hopeful and gone down again with an empty basket. Somewhere down there, in shires he had never seen and mostly never would, some several hundred hearths that had burned every winter of his life, and his father's, and further back than the book troubled to say, were about to go without.

He did not sleep well that night, nor for many after, though the store went on exactly as before: the beam, the notches, the book, the weighing of what was allowed to be weighed. He kept the list. Why he kept it he could not have said, except that it seemed to him a wicked thing to throw away, as if the names on it deserved at least to be kept somewhere, since they could not be kept warm.

A month into the refusal he broke a habit of eleven years and left the yard himself, on the pretence of counting a grange's stock in person, though the store had never once in his time needed his hands to count what the notches already told him. He walked down the white road past the turn-back tracks to the mill at the edge of the nearest cold shire, and found the girl from the trestle there, the one he had told first, sitting with her mother by a hearth built of nothing but hedge-cuttings and a little dried dung, smoke going up thin and bitter and not much warmth in it that he could feel from the doorway. He had meant to say something to her, some word of the kind a man carries down a road for a month rehearsing, and found when he stood in the doorway that there was nothing in it that would not sound like an excuse.

"You'll tell your notches I came," the girl's mother said to him, not unkindly, only tired past the point of anger, "and they'll not warm us any the more for it."

He went back up the white road with nothing said that he had meant to say, and weighed the handful the rest of that day for the households still allowed it, and found that his hands, at least, still knew the beam even when the rest of him did not know what to do with a law that had stopped, for a season, being a law at all.

## II. Of the Cold Shires

Seònaid nic Coinnich was nine that winter, and she had already learned, at Cathair mhòr, that a fire has more than one kind of life in it.

There was the hearth-fire, which her mother tended in the byre-house where they had lodged since Doire mhin burned, banked low at night and built up at dawn with whatever the priests of Macha allowed a family that owned nothing and had brought nothing away but its names and its grief. There was the temple fire in Macha's low stone house by the market, which the priests kept for the rites of planting and the rites of the dead, and which every family of the burned granges had come to think of, without quite saying so, as the true fire of the town, the one that could not go out because it had never gone out, not in anyone's telling.

It went out that winter, in Dubhlas, on a morning so still that the smoke from the last coals stood up straight before it thinned and was gone.

She was in the market when word went round. It went the way such word does, from one mouth to the next faster than a runner could carry it, and by the time it reached her it had already grown a shape: Taigh na Fèithe had shut its gate on the coal, all of it, to every hearth in every shire that had not declared for Raghnall Ruadh, and Cathair mhòr had declared for Dùghall in the second year of the war and so Cathair mhòr's fires were among the first refused.

Her mother did not weep, which frightened Seònaid more than weeping would have. She only stood in the doorway of the byre-house with her arms folded into her shawl and looked toward the temple, where a thin crowd was already gathering around a cold hearth as if looking at it hard enough might light it, and said, "We had a fire once too, and lost it once too," meaning Doire mhin, and went in to see how long the wood they had gathered from the hedges would last them, since wood was a thing no one's law could measure out or take back.

It was a hard winter for that alone, without the coal; the moss-cutters said afterward that it was no colder than other winters, only that a house without the black stone felt every ordinary cold twice over, because it had grown used, in a generation, to not needing to feel it at all. Children coughed who had not coughed before. An old woman two doors from them, who had come out of Doire mhin on the same day as Seònaid's family and had never really warmed since, was found dead in her bed on a morning when the frost had drawn flowers on the inside of the shutter. No one said the cold had killed her; that is a thing left unsaid of an old woman in a hard winter. But everyone who carried her out knew it, and said nothing, which came to the same end.

The priests of Macha did what they could with wood alone, and it was not little; they sent boys out along the hedgerows with billhooks from first light, and kept a common pot going at the temple door for any household whose own hearth had failed, and one grey-headed priest whose name Seònaid never learned told her mother, more than once, that Macha's own rites did not need the black stone, only the fire itself, of whatever the fire was made. But a common pot feeds a family; it does not warm a house through a night, and Seònaid came to know the particular sound a door made when someone went out before dawn to walk the hedgerows for themselves rather than wait on the priests' boys, a sound she heard from her own family's door more mornings than she liked to count.

It was in that season that the verse went about the cold shires, sung low over the mended hearths and the unmended ones alike, by no one whose name was kept:

> *Cha do chaill sinn blàr, ach chaill sinn an teine.*
> We did not lose a battle, but we lost the fire.

Seònaid heard it first from a carter's boy in the market, who did not know where he had learned it either, only that everyone seemed to know it by the second telling, the way a tune is known before it is thought about. She never afterward heard anyone claim to have made it. It seemed, she thought later, to have come up out of the cold shires themselves, the way water comes up out of ground that has held enough of it.

Her own family's hearth went grey twice that winter and was built up again on borrowed wood both times, once by a granary man who had known her father, once by strangers who asked no thanks and gave none of their names. She watched her mother measure out the last of the good turf on the coldest night, dividing it by eye into portions no bigger than a fist, the way, she thought without yet having the word for it, the store at the mountain measured its own handful, except that her mother's measure was made out of love and the mountain's, that winter, was made out of something else.

Being nine, she did not yet understand that she would spend the rest of her life explaining that winter to people who had not lived it. What she understood, standing at the temple with the crowd that would not disperse from the cold hearth even after the priests had gone in, was only this: that a fire withheld does more work in a shire than a fire burning ever does. A burning fire is only warmth. A withheld one is a lesson, said over and over to everyone who stands and watches it not be lit, about who may give and who may not, and who has decided that they, and not the law, will be the ones to decide it.

## III. Of the White Road

Catrìona nic Aonghais heard the verse of the cold shires before she heard the full account of what had made it, and she understood at once, hearing only the first line sung by a woman selling barley at Cathair dhomhain's gate, that the war had changed its shape under her without her leave.

Six years she had fought for a claim she still, in her own mind on quiet nights, was not wholly sure was better than her cousins' claims. No one had been broken at the ford who had not first broken at her; Cathair dhomhain had been taken only because it would not open, and its garrison let go free because there seemed to her no honour in doing otherwise to men who had only held a gate they were told to hold. She had told herself, through all of it, that she fought for a right that was hers by blood and no worse than her cousins' rights, and no better either, and that the houses would decide it in the end the way the houses decided everything, slowly, and with much talk, and eventually.

Raghnall Ruadh had put an end to that manner of thinking for her, and, she came to believe, for every house on the island, on the morning he handed a list to his store-master.

The word reached her at Cathair dhomhain within the month: Taigh na Fèithe shut. It was shut to no enemy's host, only to an enemy's hearths, granaries she had never besieged going cold because their reeve had once declared the wrong way, temples that had held the same coal-rite since before there were kings, now cold on the same law's authority that had lit them. She had grown up on Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig the way she had grown up on the tide; it had simply always been there, a thing that measured and did not choose. To see it turned into a weapon in one man's hand frightened her more than any field had.

Her clerk read her the tally of shires that had gone cold, town by town, and she made him stop before he had finished. She did not doubt the count. It had only begun to feel, hearing it read out in a level clerk's voice as though it were any other tally, like another siege, laid on her behalf, in her name, by a man she had never once met on a field. She had sat forty days at Cathair dhomhain's wells and told herself the dead there were the war's ordinary cost; she found she could not tell herself the same of hearths going grey in towns she had never marched near, in shires whose only fault, so far as she could learn, was to have once sent word that they held for her, or for Dùghall, or simply had not troubled to send word that they held for Raghnall.

Dùghall Garbh came to her camp himself, in Faolach, with six men and no herald, which she took, rightly, as a kind of oath before he had said a word. He was near the age her father would have been, grey through the beard, and he had lost the enthusiasm for the crown that she remembered in him from the first year of the war; what was left in his face, she thought, looking at him across her own hearth-fire, was something plainer and harder to argue with.

"He has made it so that none of us can have it now," Dùghall said, without preamble, meaning Taigh na Fèithe, meaning perhaps the crown itself. "Whichever of us wins it after this, we win a right that can be shut like a door. I would sooner lose to you than let that stand."

She did not answer him at once. She thought of the wells at Cathair dhomhain, fouled with her own dead and his allies' dead together, and of forty days she did not enjoy remembering; she thought that a woman who had done that for a claim of blood had no great room to be shocked that another claimant had done worse for the same claim. But she thought too of the verse the barley-seller had sung, unbidden, to no one in particular, and understood that a claim which had to be enforced by cold hearths was no longer only a quarrel among three houses; it had become everyone's quarrel, in every shire that burned coal, which was every shire on the island.

They joined their hosts in the same season, without ceremony beyond the joining itself: two war-bands that had spent six years in rival camps now making one camp, uneasily, old wounds still being counted on both sides. A man of Dùghall's who had lost a brother at the ford stood a long while the first night looking across the fires at the men who had broken it, and it was Catrìona herself who went and stood beside him and said nothing, only stood, until he went in to his own fire without doing whatever it was he had been minded to do.

The march north was slower than either host would have made alone, hampered by its own mistrust as much as by the snow, and it went through shires that had gone cold themselves, where the marching men, whichever banner they had followed into the war, found themselves giving up rations at doors they passed rather than be seen by their own captains eating well before people with nothing in the pot. No order was given for it. It happened all along the line of march, in ones and twos, and by the time the host came in sight of the mountain it had fed, by Catrìona's own later reckoning, more mouths than it had killed men to get there, which she thought, when she had leisure to think of it, was as strange an arithmetic as she had ever kept.

They came on Raghnall below {{place:burg:354}} on {{date:III-0110}}, snow lying thick enough on the ground that the hosts could be tracked by it afterward for a mile in either direction, black lines through white where men and horses had gone down. Catrìona remembered little of the fighting with any order; she remembered the noise of it going strangely flat in the cold, as though the winter swallowed half of it before it could reach her; she remembered Dùghall's standard going down and coming up again in another man's hand within the same breath, and not knowing, in the moment, whether that meant he lived or had only been carried off to die somewhere she could not see. He had lived, that day; the wound that would carry him off within the year was taken on that field, though no one there judged it more than a hard cut at the time, one among many.

Raghnall Ruadh was killed before the light failed. She did not see it done, and was glad, afterward, that she had not; she heard only that it was quick, and that his own men, when they knew it, did not press on for him. By dark the field was theirs, and she stood on it a long while in the last of the light with the cold coming up through her boots, looking at the shape of a battle already going still and ordinary under the snow, the way any field looks the day after, whatever was fought over it.

The next morning, before she had sent any order of her own to Taigh na Fèithe, its gates opened from within. Raghnall's own custodians, the men who had measured and weighed and written in Leabhar a' Chùraim all through the winter of the refusal, came out and struck off the seal Raghnall had put on the store, without waiting to be told which claimant now held the mountain, because, as one of them said plainly to her clerk, the law had never asked them to wait for that; it had only ever asked them to measure the handful, and they had gone a season without doing the one thing the law had ever asked of them, and were glad, that morning, of an excuse to do it again.

Eòghann mac Dhùghaill was among them at the gate that morning, the board still kept in the store where he had set it that first day, face to the wall, unwilling to scrape the names from it and unwilling to hang it up. He did not show it to the custodians, nor to Catrìona when she came up to the yard some days after and thanked the store for its work as though the refusal had been a hardship visited on the store as much as on the shires, which in its way it had been. He kept the board a long time after, years past the point when anyone but himself remembered what had been written on it, and never scraped it clean; he found, to his own surprise, that he never forgot a single name on it either, whether the board was clean or not.

The carriers who had turned back at the gate two winters running went up the white road once more, and this time came down it loaded, baskets on their backs, breath white in the cold morning the same as every ordinary morning of the world, going down toward shires that had learned, that winter, exactly how much they depended on a road they had never before had cause to think about.

Word of the first hearth relit reached Catrìona at the mountain, brought by a rider who had ridden through the night to bring it, as though it were news of a battle rather than the small quiet thing it was: a temple fire at Cathair mhòr, built up again from a carrier's basket by hands that had built it up from borrowed turf and hedge-wood all that winter, catching at last on coal that was, once more, simply coal, and not a lesson.

She thought of the verse then, the one line of it she had by heart, and thought that whoever had made it unmade, without knowing they were doing it, had left out the other half of the telling: that a fire can be lost without a battle, but it can also, as this one was, be won back without one, by men who had only ever wanted to go on weighing the handful the way their fathers had, and by two claimants who had learned, at last, on the same field, what it cost to let the mountain be turned against the shires it was meant to warm.
