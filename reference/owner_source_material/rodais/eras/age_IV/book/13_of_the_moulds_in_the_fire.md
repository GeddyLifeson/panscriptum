# Of the Moulds in the Fire

*LE 68 – 109*

> *Beathaich an seabhag, ach na sàsaich e.*
> Feed the hawk, but do not glut him.
>
> — the first saying in Leabhar an t-Seabhaig

## I. Of the Fellowship After Alasdair

Coinneach mac Fhearghais had been the first apprentice Alasdair Saor ever swore, a boy of fourteen with a stammer and hands too big for the rest of him, and he was the last of that first swearing left alive when the old man's yard passed to him. He kept the stammer into old age, worse when he was angered and gone almost entirely when he was easy, and the wrights of the fellowship had long since stopped remarking on it, the way a household stops remarking on a low door once every member of it has learned to duck. He took the mastership of the fellowship of the strand as Alasdair had held it, by the plain fact that no one else living had built so many hulls under the old man's own eye, and he kept the fellowship's oath and its coal exactly as it had been kept before him, swearing no fewer apprentices than the yard could rightly feed and turning away, more than once, a father who came with a willing son and no craft in the boy's hands to show for it.

There was less for the fellowship to build by then than there had been in Alasdair's day, no one on the island wanting a hull that meant to lose sight of land, and the yard at Seann Skell that had once laid three keels at a time to the fleet's own hunger kept, in Coinneach's years, to one coaster at a season and sometimes none, the wrights filling the slack months with the mending of fish-boats and the cutting of oars for households that had never owned a boat worth the name. Coinneach never complained of it where any apprentice could hear him. Niall, who came down to the strand most autumns to enter the yard's launches in his boards, noticed that the old man had taken to standing longer than the work wanted over each new keel laid, running a flat hand along the wood the way another man might run a hand along a horse he did not expect to keep much longer.

Niall asked him once, the autumn he came down to enter a coaster no grander than any of the season's before it, whether a small boat built to carry a fish merchant's catch to market gave a wright the same satisfaction a deep-keeled hull once had, and Coinneach was a long while answering, turning the question over the way he turned over a plank before he trusted an adze to it. He said at last, the stammer catching only on the first word, that a boat was a boat whatever water it was built to cross, and that a man who could not find his satisfaction in a coaster well built had likely never had much satisfaction in a hull's building at all, only in the size of the thing he had built, which was a different pride and a smaller one. Niall wrote none of that down, judging it a wright's own business rather than the harbour-house's. He remembered it all the same, and in the years that followed set the same question to more than one man who complained to him of smaller work than he had once been given.

In his seven-and-seventieth year he told his eldest journeyman, a quiet man named Griogair who had been with him since before his own beard came in grey, that he did not think he would see another spring's launching from the strand himself, and he said it without any weight in his voice that Griogair could afterward point to as grief, only as a fact set down the way Coinneach set down every fact, plainly and once. He died the following winter, in the between-moon that comes some years to true out the count with the sun, in the same house on the strand where he had lived since his marriage, and it is told that his last words, got out whole and without the old stammer for the first time in years his family could remember, were that the fellowship's coal was to go to Griogair and to no one else, whatever any elder journeyman might claim by right of years alone. Niall entered the death the day word of it reached the harbour-house, and set beside it, from old habit rather than any instruction from the boy himself, the note that Coinneach mac Fhearghais had been the last man living who remembered kneeling on the shingle with Alasdair Saor's own hand over his.

## II. Of the Last Journeymen and the Moulds Burned

Griogair kept the fellowship sixteen years more, and it is told of those sixteen years only that they were quiet ones, the yard laying its one coaster a season as before and losing, each year, an apprentice or two to households that had work nearer home and no patience left for an oath that fed a man no faster than the sea did. In the eighty-fourth year of the count Griogair called what journeymen the fellowship still had to the strand at the season's turning and told them, without ceremony, that there was not craft enough left in the ordering of the island's own boats to keep an oath sworn for the building of a fleet, and that he meant to release every man there from it and close the fellowship's coal himself, being the last to hold it and having no wife's son to pass it to. The journeymen took the release as men take a thing they have half expected for years and half hoped would not come in their own time: some went to the rope-walks at Cuan shean, some to farm the strips their fathers had left them, and one, a young man called Tòmas who had sworn only two years before, walked the whole way to Doire fhionn to learn the woodward's craft among the stumps of the old grove, saying to anyone who asked that a trade with a future in it, however small, was worth more than the memory of one that had none. Griogair kept for himself only his own tools and the last coaster laid before the release, a boat he finished alone over the following winter with no journeyman left to hand him a plank, and sold it at spring to the same fish merchant of Inis àrsaidh whose father had bought Alasdair's own last hull, saying nothing of the fellowship's ending to the merchant beyond that the price would be a little higher, there being no apprentice's wage to spread the cost across any longer.

Niall entered the fellowship's closing in the boards the day Griogair brought him word of it, and found, setting down the entry, that he did not know what heading to give it under, Rolla a' Chalaidh having no place kept for the ending of an oath that no one had thought to enter as a beginning, Alasdair having sworn his first apprentices on the open shingle with no scribe there to write it down. He set it, in the end, under the same heading he had used for the launching of every hull the fellowship had ever built, judging that an ending belonged with its beginnings whatever the boards' own ruling said, and let the matter stand.

The following winter came in hard and early, the worst any living at Seann Skell could remember, and in the depth of Faolach, the storm-moon, with the town's own store of driftwood and cut peat running thinner than any household cared to own to its neighbours, someone went into the shed on the strand where the fellowship's old patterns and keel-moulds had stood since the fleet's own building, untouched by anyone in years, and carried the whole of it out board by board to be burned. No name was ever set to whose hand did the carrying, and none of the households that burned that wood in their own hearths that winter thought, so far as anyone afterward owned, that they were burning anything more than seasoned oak cut to no purpose any man living still had use for. Murchadh mac Raghnaill, the harbour-master, noted in his own reckoning that the fellowship's shed on the strand had come down that winter and its ground stood empty, and wrote nothing of what the shed had held, the moulds and patterns by which every hull of the fleet had been laid true having by then passed so far out of any use that their going seemed to him no more a loss than the pulling-down of an old fence. Niall, reading Murchadh's leaf at the harbour-house board, thought of asking after it, and did not, telling himself that a scribe who chased down every burned plank in a hard winter would have no ink left for anything else the town wanted written.

## III. Of a King's Death and a Line in the Custody-Book

Word came to Seann Skell some weeks late, as word from Cathair dhearg generally did, that Raghnall mac Uilleim, who had held An Talla Dearg since Niall was a young man, had died in his eighty-fifth year, and that the heads of the shires had crowned his son Raghnall mac Raghnaill in his place without dispute, the new king seven-and-forty years old and already grey at the temples when the crown was set on him. Niall entered the death and the crowning on the same leaf, as Leabhar a' Chùraim at Cathair dhearg was said to have done before him, one line for the reign that had just closed and one for the reign beginning, and found, turning the matter over afterward, that he could not himself recall a single thing the old king had done in the whole of his seven-and-thirty years on the hall that a harbour scribe at Seann Skell would have had cause to write down. It was not, he thought, that Raghnall mac Uilleim's reign had wanted for length; it was that it had wanted, so far as the west coast ever heard of it, for anything happening in it at all, the vein measured out as it had always been measured, the shires kept as they had always been kept, and Rolla a' Chalaidh of Seann Skell no fuller nor emptier for a change of crown than they would have been for a change of season.

The town kept no rite for the old king's death beyond what any household kept for its own dead, a light set out and a word said, and the taprooms along the strand talked more, in the weeks the news was fresh, of the hard winter just past and the fellowship's burned shed than of the man who had worn the crown for seven-and-thirty years and left it, by every account that reached the west coast, exactly as he had found it. Niall wrote the new king's name into the boards under the old one and did not, this once, add anything of his own beside it, judging that a scribe who had nothing to say of a reign owed that reign at least the honesty of his silence.

Dòmhnall, keeping the trade rolls at the same board, said once, reading the two lines over his son's shoulder, that a king who left nothing behind him for a harbour scribe to write was perhaps the kindest king a coast so far from An Talla Dearg could hope for, since every king the west coast's own tellings remembered with any weight had cost it a war, a levy, or a fleet, and Niall allowed that this might be so, though he added that he would not himself care to be remembered, when his own boards were closed, by two bare lines and nothing beside them. Dòmhnall answered that a harbour scribe's own boards were a different matter entirely from a king's reign, being read by fewer eyes and closer ones, and the two men let the question drop there, as they let most questions drop by then, having worked the same board together long enough to know which ones were worth pressing and which were not.

## IV. Of the Death of Niall mac Dhòmhnaill

Niall kept the harbour-house boards six-and-forty years in all, ten fewer than his grandfather Calum had kept them, and in his last years his own hand grew as unsteady over a leaf as Calum's had been, though it was his sight that held and his fingers that failed him, the opposite trouble from the one he had watched close in on the old man in the room above the harbour-house when he himself was a boy holding the lamp. He worked to the very end of his strength, entering a small dispute over a landed catch's weight in the same careful hand he had used for every dispute of forty years before it, and it is told that the entry stands unfinished in the boards to this day, the last stroke of the last letter trailing off where his hand stopped rather than where the sense of the sentence did.

He died in the season of Bealltach of the hundred-and-fourth year of the count, at Seann Skell, five-and-seventy years old, in the harbour-house rather than in any bed, having been carried there by his own wish when it grew plain to those about him that he would not see the next morning out. In the last hours anyone sat with him he asked for his grandfather's hazel stick to be brought and set in his hand, the same stick he had carried down as a boy of eleven to the west quay wall on the night of An Diosal, and he held it a long while without speaking, running his thumb along the row of notches cut too fast and too many to be read again for any true count. Those who sat with him afterward could not agree, comparing what they remembered of that night, whether he had said anything at all in the hours before the end, or whether the working of his lips they took for speech had been no more than the working of an old man's breath. What is agreed is that he never once, in six-and-forty years at the boards nor in the hour of his death, told any living soul the number he had come to, privately, in all the years since that night, for the hulls that went round the pool at the turn; and the stick passed, at his own asking, into the ground with him rather than to the shelf, so that whatever count it had held for him, cut too fast on a boy's knee in the dark, went with it.

His eldest daughter, grown by then to a woman with children of her own about the harbour-house, asked him once, in the last year of his life, whether he had ever settled the count for himself, having heard him spoken of all her childhood as the boy who counted the hulls. He told her, she said afterward, that a man who had spent forty years keeping other men's counts honest owed his own the same honesty, and that an honest count, once cut in haste on a dark quay by a boy of eleven with cold hands and a bad light, could not be mended by an old man's wishing at his own fireside forty years on, whatever number the wishing might land on. She did not ask him again, and the boards of Seann Skell passed, in the season after his death, to hands the harbour-house had already chosen against the day, the office running on as offices at Seann Skell had always run on, one keeper's leaf taken up where the last one's had stopped.

## V. Of the Coal-Breakers Below the Hill

At Muileann dhearg, below Taigh na Fèithe where the coal-road came down off the hill, a man named Eachann Briseadair had for some years kept a shed of his own, of a different kind from the fellowship's, where the raw coal brought down from the galleries was broken and sorted before it went out to the shires under Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig's own measure. He had learned the breaking from his father, who had learned it from a custodian of Taigh na Fèithe two generations before either of them, and he was, by the seventieth year of the count, the only man on the hill whose own household did the whole of the work rather than hiring it out season by season to whoever's hands were free.

It is told at Muileann dhearg that Eachann built his first house of stone below Taigh na Fèithe's own gate in the two-and-seventieth year, his sons and their wives building three more beside it within the decade, so that what had been a scatter of turf shielings at the foot of the hill became, before Eachann himself was an old man, a settled row of houses with its own well and its own smith, known at first only as the coal-breakers' row and in time, once it had grown past what any one word for a scatter of houses could properly hold, by the name Taigh na Fèithe had long given the whole of that ground: Muileann dhearg, the red mill, for the colour the dust of the breaking left on every stone and every hand that worked there. Eachann lived to see it a town in more than name, with its own reeve answering to Taigh na Fèithe rather than to any shire, and died in it at sixty, in the same shed where he had broken his first load of coal as a boy at his father's side, his own sons by then old enough to keep the work without him and his grandsons already learning it at their fathers' hands, the way the breaking had come down to Eachann himself.

He was buried, at his own asking, on the rise above the shed rather than within it, where a man standing at his grave could see the whole of the row he had raised and the coal-road climbing past it to Taigh na Fèithe gate, and it is told that his eldest son set no stone over him beyond a lump of the coal itself, unbroken, judging that his father would rather be marked by the thing his hands had spent a life breaking than by anything a mason could cut for him. The custodians of Taigh na Fèithe, hearing of it, sent down no objection, the coal being Eachann's by Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig's own measure to burn or to keep as any household chose, and the stone-that-was-not-a-stone stood on the rise above Muileann dhearg for longer than any household there could afterward say, weathering no differently than Taigh na Fèithe's own walls did.

Donnchadh mac Thormoid, who had kept Taigh na Fèithe's own boards since he was fifteen years old and had by the seventieth year of the count grown into the office as fully as any man grows into a coat cut for him young, entered Eachann's first house in his own leaves the season it was raised, and it was in that same season, or near enough to it that Donnchadh himself could never afterward be certain which had come first, that word reached Taigh na Fèithe from Cathair dhearg that the crown's own scribes at An Talla Dearg had begun to date their rolls by the count he himself had kept alone at Muileann dhearg for near thirty years, setting aside the reckoning by any king's reign for the turning he had first set his own hand to as a clerk of thirty with no one asking him to. He said nothing of it to anyone about him when the word reached him, only sat a while at his own board with the leaf before him unmarked, and it is told that he wrote, at the foot of that day's ordinary entry, a single line that named no pride and claimed no credit, only that An Talla Dearg now counted its years as Muileann dhearg had long counted its own, and let the matter stand at that.

## VI. Of the Lots Drawn Among the Seven Houses

Na Seachd Taighean that held the right to the coal under Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig had each, since the houses were made, kept a claim on this gallery or that within the vein without any settled order to which house's claim ran deepest, and disputes over a gallery's true bounds had grown, in Donnchadh's own years at the boards, frequent enough that the custodians of Taigh na Fèithe judged the matter could not be left to grow any older before it was settled once for all. In the hundred-and-ninth year of the count, a generation after the fellowship at Seann Skell had released its last journeymen, the heads of Na Seachd Taighean were called to Muileann dhearg and set, each in turn, to draw a stone from a covered bag before the custodians and the whole household of Taigh na Fèithe looking on, each stone marked in a hand none of the houses' own heads could read, so that no house might afterward claim its gallery had been given rather than drawn.

Donnchadh kept the boards that day as he kept every day, but he wrote afterward, in his own private hand rather than in Taigh na Fèithe's formal rolls, that he had never in all his years at Muileann dhearg seen seven grown heads of houses stand so still nor speak so little as they stood and spoke while the bag went from hand to hand, each drawing his stone and reading its mark aloud to the custodian before any other man in the hall was let see it. The galleries fell as the stones fell, some houses drawing deep and rich ground and others drawing thin, and it is told that not one head among the seven, in all the years that followed, was ever heard to say aloud that his own house's stone had wronged it, whatever might have been said of it in private at his own hearth. The stones themselves, once every gallery was claimed and entered, went back into the same bag they had been drawn from, and the bag was hung on a peg in Taigh na Fèithe's own hall rather than broken up or put away, so that any man who doubted the drawing might come and see the very stones that had made it.

## VII. Of the Stones Shown to a Visitor

That same year Donnchadh was an old man in his own right, the youngest apprentice at Taigh na Fèithe calling him, without any unkindness meant by it, the oldest thing on the hill after the coal, and it fell to him more often than to any custodian to show a visitor about the hall when one came, Taigh na Fèithe's affairs by then running largely on the memory of a scribe who had kept them longer than anyone else living. A grandson of Eachann Briseadair's, a boy of ten sent up from the coal-breakers' row on some errand of his father's, found himself waiting a while in Taigh na Fèithe's hall for the custodian he had been sent to, and Donnchadh, seeing him stand alone and plainly curious rather than put a task in front of him, took the bag down from its peg and let him hold it.

The boy asked what the stones were for, and Donnchadh told him, in fewer words than the telling had ever taken him before, of the day the seven heads had stood in that same hall and drawn them, and of how no house since had ever been heard to say the drawing had wronged it. He did not tell the boy which stone had fallen to which house, judging that the boy's own family, being coal-breakers and not among the seven, had no more claim on that knowledge than any other visitor might, and the boy, turning the stones over in the bag with the plain interest of a child handling a thing older than his own grandfather, asked only whether the stones were magic, and Donnchadh told him, honestly, that he did not know, having never asked a custodian the question and not meaning to begin asking it now, in his own last years, when the not-knowing had served him well enough for fifty. He hung the bag back on its peg before the custodian came, and the boy went off to his errand having learned, so far as Donnchadh could tell, only that Taigh na Fèithe kept old stones in a bag and an old man who would talk of them if a boy stood still long enough to be talked to.

## VIII. Of the Last Entry

Donnchadh wrote, in the same season the boy had come and gone, the last entry Taigh na Fèithe's own boards ever received from his hand: the season's burning of coal, household by household, in the measure Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig had held since Fionnlagh Dall, set down in the same careful order he had set it down every season since he first took up the boards at fifteen, beginning with Na Seachd Taighean in the order their galleries had fallen at the drawing and ending, as it had always ended, with the coal-breakers' row that answered to Taigh na Fèithe rather than to any house's claim. He died that same season, at Muileann dhearg, in the room above the hall where he had slept since his father first walked him up the coal-road as a boy, and it is told that a custodian, comparing the last leaf of his hand against the first he had entered at fifteen, could find no difference between them that any stranger to both would have thought worth remarking, the same careful order and the same plain hand at five-and-ninety that had been set down at fifteen.

No one at Muileann dhearg troubled to say, over his grave, anything grander than that the boards had lost the only hand that had ever kept them, since none living could remember another, and Taigh na Fèithe went on, in the season that followed, exactly as it had gone on through every season of his keeping, the coal broken and measured and carried down the road below the hill to the row of houses that bore his own count in their gate as surely as the crown itself now bore it in An Talla Dearg's rolls. The bag of Clachan a' Chrannchuir hung where it had hung since the drawing, on its peg in Taigh na Fèithe's hall, and no hand troubled to take it down again for some while after, the boy from the coal-breakers' row being the last, for a long season, to have held it and turned it over and set it, unread, back in its place.
