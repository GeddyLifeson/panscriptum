# Of the White Sorrow

*LE 50 – 55*

## I. Of What Was Said at Seann Skell

Nothing was known of the fleet after the tale that stopped at Calum's torn leaf, and a thing not known does not stay empty long in a harbour town. By the fiftieth year of the count men who had never crossed water further than the fishing grounds off Cuan ruadh spoke of the crossing as though they had stood at the rail of it themselves, and the harbour-house at Seann Skell, which kept the only leaf anyone could point to and call a record, was asked so often what it knew that Niall mac Dhòmhnaill's father took, in the end, to answering every asker the same way, tired of hearing his own silence mistaken for a secret: that the boards held what the boards held, and a man who wanted more than that should go and ask the sea.

The tale that took hold hardest, and outlasted every other, was told first, so far as anyone could afterward say, by a carrier of dubhan who had never in her life set foot on a hull, and it ran that the children born to the departed on the far shore came into the world with less in their hands than their parents had carried away, and the children born to those children less again, until by the third generation over the water there would be nothing left in a family that Dia-thìr would know for its own. No one could say how the carrier had come by it, and she herself, pressed on it at the fair the next spring, only shrugged and said that a thing does not have to be seen to be felt, and that a mother knows when her milk is thinner than her own mother's was, whether or not a physician can explain to her why. It was not a tale that could be tried against anything Seann Skell held. There was no letter from the far shore to set beside it, no child of the crossing to measure, only the shape of the tale passed mouth to mouth along the strand and up the lanes, gathering, as such tales do, a little more certainty with each telling than the last mouth had given it.

A custodian of the coal, down from the vein-house on the shire's business that same year, heard the carrier's tale told over a jug in the harbour-house taproom and gave it an answer of his own that the taproom liked better, or at least argued over longer. The coal, he said, plainly enough that a man three tables off could hear him, was not a thing carried in the blood the way a chin or a temper was carried; it answered to the hand that had learned the galleries and kept the Law, and a hand once away from the vein for a lifetime, let alone two, would forget the galleries the way any hand forgets a trade it does not practise, whatever blood ran in it. The strength was not thinning in the departed's children, on his reckoning. It had simply been left behind at Muileann dhearg, the way a man leaves a tool in a shed he no longer visits, and a tool left long enough in a shed is a tool a man's own son will not know how to use: no one has put it in his hand and shown him.

The two tales did not agree, and nobody at Seann Skell troubled overmuch to make them agree, for both said, in their different ways, the one thing the harbour town most wanted said: that something in the departed, or their children, was growing less than it had been. A third telling, heard less often and never in the taproom but sometimes from an old woman at a hearth, held that nothing was lessening at all, that the far shore had simply never had the coal to answer to in the first place, so that the children born there were exactly what any child would be, no thinner and no lesser, only unremarked because there was nothing on that shore to remark them against; and this telling, though it troubled fewer households to argue over, was, for reasons no one who told it could have named, the one that most often silenced a room rather than filled it, and was therefore told least. Of the three, or the dozen half-tales that stood between them, nothing was proved and nothing disproved, for there was no one aboard any hull who could be asked, and the vein-house, which alone might have spoken with authority on what the coal answered to and what it did not, said for some years nothing at all in public, keeping its own reckoning, as it kept everything, inside its own walls.

## II. Of the Second Count

It was the vein-house's own reckoning, made twice ten years apart and set down without a word of what the harbour towns were saying, that in the end carried more weight than any tale told over a jug, though it travelled less far and to fewer households, for a custody-book is not a thing that passes mouth to mouth. Eòghann mac Mhuirich, who had counted the households of the coal-right once before the fleet sailed and once again the autumn the roll of the willing closed, was dead these many years by the fiftieth season of the count, and the counting fell, as most things at the vein-house now fell, to younger hands under an older name; but the custom of the count had not changed, and in the tenth year past the turn the custodians sat down once more with the roll and the custody-book open side by side, as they had sat down twice before, to see what stood and what did not.

Donnchadh mac Thormoid was thirty-six that year, no longer the boy who had written his first fair copy by a winter fire, and it was his hand, steadier now than any other at the table, that carried the greater share of the tallying, column against column, hearth against hearth, the roll of the willing checked off one name at a time against the households that remained on the coal-right. It was slow work and dull work, the kind that asks nothing of a man's judgement and everything of his patience, and it took the better part of a season before the figure stood plain before them: one hearth in five of all the hearths that had held the right to the coal at the turn was gone from the coal-right now, neither more nor fewer than the fifth that had gone aboard the fleet, so that the going, whatever else it had done, had at least done nothing further to the number left behind. It was the figure the older custodians round the table had half expected and had none of them, Donnchadh noticed, troubled to say aloud before the count was finished, as though naming it early might somehow alter what the counting found.

He wrote the figure into the custody-book in his own plain hand, one hearth in five, and beneath it, where a lesser clerk might have let the matter rest, he set down a second line that no one at the table had asked him for and that he had thought a long while, by his own account afterward, before he let himself write. He had gone back, in the slow weeks of the counting, over every measuring the vein-house had made since the turn, and had found in them nothing that answered to the tale carried about the taproom at Seann Skell or to the custodian's tale of a tool left in a shed. The vein, he wrote, answered no differently now, to the hands of those who had stayed, than it had answered before the turn; the handful measured true against every hearth that held the right, exactly as it had always measured, and if some thinning was at work upon the children of the departed on whatever shore they had reached, it was at work there, on that shore, and not here, upon the coal or the hands that remained to serve it. It was a small line in a large book, four sentences among ten thousand others, and it settled nothing that the taproom at Seann Skell had been arguing over for a decade, since it spoke only of what stood on this side of the water and said nothing at all of the other. But it was carried up to Cathair dhearg with the rest of that season's reckoning, as every reckoning was, and it came, in the ordinary course of the red hall's business, before the queen.

Beathag read the figure once, standing at the window of the small room off the hall where she kept the season's papers before they went to the council, one hand in five, a figure she had carried in her own reckoning, unwritten, since the roll had closed ten years before and had never once, in all that time, let herself say aloud to anyone. She read Donnchadh's second line beneath it, the note that the vein answered no differently for those who had stayed, and stood a while with the leaf in her hand before she read it again, more slowly the second time than the first, as though the sentence might give up something on a second reading that it had withheld on the first. It gave up nothing more. It was, she saw when she had read it twice, exactly what it had seemed at the first reading, a clerk's plain observation, offered without comfort and without cruelty, that whatever was becoming of her sister's children on their far shore, it was becoming of them there and not here, and that the coal itself, the thing every custodian at the vein-house had spent his life learning to serve, held no opinion at all on who had gone and who had stayed. She set the leaf down on the pile with the rest of that season's business, in its place, neither above the grain figures nor below them, and told her scribe, when he came in, that the council might have the whole of it at its next sitting, and said no more of it to him or to anyone else that day.

## III. Of the White Sorrow

It was in the same year, or the one after, that the queen first wore the undyed wool, and the court, which had grown used by then to reading its mood in Beathag's dress the way a farmer reads weather in the sky, marked the change before any word was given for it.

She had worn colour at Cathair dhearg all her reigning life, the deep blue the house of Dòmhnall had kept since before her mother's time, and on the morning she first came into the hall in wool taken plain from the fleece, undyed and uncarded fine, the whole board fell silent before she had crossed half the floor, for a queen does not change the colour she has worn for thirty years without a household somewhere reading it for grief. She gave the court no announcement, taking up the day's first petition as she always took it, a boundary dispute between two farms on the river no different from a hundred such disputes the hall had settled before, and saying nothing at all about the wool, so that for the length of that first morning the hall was left to guess, in the low voices men use when the person they are guessing about sits ten feet from them, whether the queen mourned a death not yet announced, or a death long past that had only now found its proper dress.

It was her own steward, in the end, who gave the court what word there was to give, and he gave it plainly, having asked the queen directly, as few others at that board would have dared to. She mourned, she told him, her sister, who was ten years and more beyond any word reaching Cathair dhearg and would, by every reckoning a sensible woman could make, remain beyond it now; and she had chosen, since there was no grave to stand at and no body to wash, to mourn as she could, in the only cloth that seemed to her to hold no lie in it, since undyed wool asks no dye-master's skill and tells no story about the house that wears it beyond the bare fact of the wearing.

She set, besides the wool, one place at her own table that no one sat at: a cup, a trencher and a straight knife, laid at the board's foot every evening the queen dined in hall, whether she dined alone or with the whole council about her, and left untouched through the meal and cleared away after with the rest, as though the person for whom it was laid might yet, on any given evening, come late to the hall and take the seat. No one at court asked who the place was laid for. There was no need to ask. It was laid, plainly enough for every eye in the hall to read, for Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, who had gone from the same hall thirty years before with a promise between the sisters that neither had spoken of since, and who would not, by any road the hall could imagine, ever come late to a meal at Cathair dhearg again.

The court did not at first know what face to keep before it. A death it could have mourned in the old way, with black and a wake and an ending that let grief run its course and be done; but this was a grief with no ending fixed to it, a sister neither known to be dead nor known to be living, mourned in a rite that asked the whole hall to sit each evening beside an emptiness that gave back no word either way, and more than one at that board found, in the weeks after the queen first sat down to it, that they did not know whether to speak of the empty place at all, or to pass it in silence as they might pass any other piece of the hall's furniture, and settled, most of them, on the second course, since it asked less of a man than the first.

It did not stay only at Cathair dhearg. The court called it, before the year was out, an Tùrsa Geal, the white sorrow, the name settling on it the way names settle on a thing that answers to no better one, and within a generation, so it came to be told afterward at more hearths than the red hall's own, a household anywhere on the island that had given someone to the fleet might keep the same rite in its own small way, undyed wool for the season of the mourning and a place laid empty at the table, cup and trencher and a knife laid straight, for whoever of theirs had gone and not been heard from since. It asked no priest to keep it and no vein-house to license it. A household that had never sat within a hundred miles of Cathair dhearg and had never heard the queen's own name spoken twice in a year kept it all the same, in whatever manner its own hearth could manage, because the emptiness it answered to was the same emptiness in every house that kept it, high or low, and a rite that answers a true emptiness spreads on its own account, needing no crown behind it and no law.

## IV. Of the Seven Houses

The fifteenth year past the turn brought the vein-house a piece of business that had been coming toward it, by every custodian's own reckoning, since the year the roll first closed, though no one had troubled to name it plainly until that year was upon them.

A fifth of the coal-right's households were gone, and the coal-right, whatever else it might be, was above all a thing that had to be served: galleries walked, the handful measured, the custody-book kept true against every hearth still standing on it, and a fifth fewer hands to serve it than had served it at the turn. The custodians who remained had spent the years since the turn stretching the old apprenticeship thinner than it had ever been stretched, a master taking two pupils where he had once taken one, a gallery walked by a single custodian where two had walked it before, and it had held, after its fashion, for fifteen years; but it was Donnchadh mac Thormoid, now the vein-house's senior clerk and no longer its youngest hand at any table, who said first, at a sitting called for another matter entirely, that a Law built for the numbers of the old apprenticeship had gone as far as the older custodians' own stretching could carry it, and that the vein-house owed itself, if it owed nothing else, an honest look at what remained rather than a continued pretence that nothing had changed.

What the vein-house found, when it looked honestly, was seven kindreds among the remaining custodian households large enough, and old enough in the coal's service, to answer for a share of the galleries between them without waiting on a new apprentice's seven years to grow into the work: the households that had stood, at the turn, under the names of Muirich, Fearchar, Ìomhar, Raghnall, Tormod, Coinneach and Lachlann, each with enough hands left to it, counted across its whole kindred and not household by household, to keep a gallery's measure true on its own account. It was not, the older custodians were careful to say among themselves before the matter ever came to a full sitting, a thing done lightly or done because seven was a number the island's tellings favoured; it was done because seven kindreds, and no more nor fewer, were found, on the plain evidence of the roll, to hold between them hands enough for the work that fifteen custodians the year before the turn had needed to hold.

The sitting that set it in order ran the better part of two days, the longest the vein-house had held in Donnchadh's own memory, and it was not without its arguing, for a custodian whose household stood outside all seven kindreds, of an old name that had kept a single gallery honourably for three generations but stood too small, after the turn, to answer for a share on its own account, spoke long and with some bitterness against a Law that would fold his household's right into a neighbour's kindred rather than let it stand, however narrowly, on its own feet as it always had. He was heard out to the end, as the custom of the sitting required, and answered by an old woman of Clann Thormoid who said only that she had buried a brother and two nephews to the same roll that had thinned his own household, and that she would sooner see her own right folded into a larger house that could still walk every gallery it answered for, than see it stand alone and proud over galleries it could no longer properly serve; and it was her answer, more than any argument the master of the vein-house himself made, that the sitting was afterward said to have turned on.

The right passed, from that year, by house and no longer by the apprenticeship alone: a custodian born into one of the seven, Muirich, Fearchar, Ìomhar, Raghnall, Tormod, Coinneach or Lachlann, held a claim upon the coal's service that an outsider still had to earn by the seven years' apprenticeship as before, but held it now as a birthright of the kindred rather than as a thing each hand had to prove afresh from nothing. The seven heads, chosen by their own kindreds and sworn at the vein-house before Crom's stone in the same week the sitting closed, took an oath none of them had taken before, since none of the island's older Laws had ever asked a custodian to answer for a whole kindred's hands rather than his own two, and Donnchadh, who wrote the oath into the custody-book in the same plain hand he had used for every entry of his working life, found himself, when the seven had sworn and the book lay open to dry, thinking less of the Law than of the fifth of hands the sitting had never once, in two days of arguing, troubled to name aloud: the hands that were not there to be stretched or folded or sworn to anything, because they were thirty years and a whole sea away, on a shore the vein-house's own book had no line to enter them against any longer.

## V. Of the Crowning at Cathair dhearg

Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill was seventy and six years old in the summer the vein-house swore its seven heads, older by a decade and more than any ruler of her line had lived to be at Cathair dhearg within living memory, and had sat the red hall for one-and-sixty years, since a girl of fifteen had been crowned in a hall too large for her, before the fleet was so much as spoken of on the western quays. She had outlived the consort who had stood beside her at that first crowning and the sister who had sailed from beneath her own promise, and she had governed, through all of it, in the plain manner the court had come, over six decades, to call simply the queen's way: neither sending nor keeping back, neither swearing to a grief nor forbidding the swearing of it, a hand that measured a boundary ditch on the same morning it measured a fleet's departure and gave neither more of itself than the other seemed to want.

She died at Cathair dhearg that same summer, in the season when the barley stood green and short in the strips above the river, much as it had stood the year she came down to Seann Skell to walk the new quay; and the court, which had watched her grow slow and then slower over the two years before it, was told afterward that she had held the boundary petition of that very morning to its usual end, hearing both farmers out and giving her ruling in the plain voice she had always used for such matters, before she went to her own rooms and did not rise from them again. Seventy and six years old, she was the oldest of her line to sit the red hall since the years when a ruler's age had meant something other than what any other man's or woman's age meant, and no one at that court thought to remark upon the number, for by then a long life was simply a long life, and a queen who died at seventy-six after one-and-sixty years upon the hall's stone floor had lived and reigned exactly as long, and no longer, than any woman of good health and hard use might reasonably hope to.

Her son was crowned in the same hall within the month, Raghnall mac Uilleim, son of Beathag and of Uilleam mac Iain who had died young at the hall's own gate five-and-forty years before, a man of seven-and-forty himself by the year of his crowning, grey already at the temple and no stranger to the red hall's business, having sat at his mother's board through the greater part of two decades and heard more of the coal-right's disputes and the shires' grain reckonings than most men twice crowned elsewhere on the island ever heard in a lifetime. He was crowned, as his mother had been and her mother before her, in the plain rite of the house of Dòmhnall, no more and no less than the setting of the old circlet upon his head by the eldest custodian present and the reading of his name into the roll of the house; and it was noted, by more than one at that crowning, that he wore, beneath his own crowning colours, a strip of undyed wool at the wrist, worn quietly and without remark, as a man might wear a thing he had grown up watching his mother wear and had never once, in seven-and-forty years, thought to ask her leave to wear himself.

## VI. Of the Empty Place Laid Once More

They kept the wake for Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill three nights at Cathair dhearg, as the house of Dòmhnall had always kept its wakes, the hall lit end to end and the court coming and going through the small hours as custom asked of it, and it was on the second of those nights that the queen's own steward, grown old himself in her service and near the last of those who had served her from her own youth, did the thing that afterward the whole court spoke of longest, though he did it quietly and without any word of ceremony to mark it.

He had the cup, the trencher and the straight knife brought out once more and laid at the board's foot, exactly as they had been laid every evening the queen dined in that hall for the better part of six-and-twenty years, and left there through the whole of the wake's second night, untouched, as the place had always been left, while the court passed it going to and from the queen's own bier at the hall's head, and few among them, that night, could have said with any certainty which of the two sisters the place was laid for.

It might have been laid, as it had always been laid before, for Mòrag, gone these six-and-thirty years and further from any word reaching Cathair dhearg than she had ever been in the twenty years before that; the steward himself, asked afterward, would say only that he had laid it as he always laid it, in the manner the queen herself had kept, and that he had not thought, doing it, to lay it for any different reason than the one that had first set it there. But it stood that second night at the foot of a board where the queen herself now lay cold at the hall's other end, and more than one who passed it going to the bier found their eye caught by the small emptiness at the board's foot before it was caught by the greater emptiness at the board's head, and could not, standing between the two, have said honestly which absence weighed the heavier on them, the sister thirty and six years across an unanswering sea, or the queen not yet a full day gone from the hall she had sat for one-and-sixty years, both alike unreachable now by any road the living court could travel, and both, that one night, kept in the same plain wool and the same untouched place, as though the hall, having mourned one so long in this fashion, had found in the end that it knew no other way to mourn the second.
