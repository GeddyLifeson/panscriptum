# Of the Keel-Song

*LE 1,320 – 1,439*

> *Druim fon aisne, aisne fon chlàr,*
> *clàr ri clàr is an cuan fodhpa.*
> Keel under rib, rib under plank,
> plank to plank and the sea beneath them.
>
> — the rope-makers' song of Cuan shean, as Iseabail nic Lachlainn set it down

## I. Of the Law That Lapsed

King Iain Ciar died at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0185}}, eighty-six years old and the longest-reigning of Raghnall's line since the peace at the road-house had made him king over his brother. He had kept his own grain law by will more than by love; the shire seats had grumbled at the cost of a full year's store set aside against a war that did not come again in his lifetime, and more than one reeve had let the measure slip a bushel or two in a lean season and been made to answer for it before the old king's own steward. Within a few years of his death the law went the way he had foretold in the note his clerk found among his leaves. No one troubled to repeal it; it simply fell away, store by store, seat by seat, until the granaries held what each town's own harvest gave them and no more. Pàdraig mac Ruairidh, who had written the account of the Brothers' War in the old king's praise, had died some years before him, and no one came after to keep the granaries' tale as he had kept the war's.

The road-houses he had chartered stood, though, for a charter is a harder thing to let slip than a habit of storing grain, and the keepers went on meeting once a year at An Sligeanach Fad' às to settle their accounts and complain, in the words the charter-roll had grown used to, of the price of everything and the honesty of no one. It was at two of those houses, in the years just after the old king's death, that the circuit's record set down the small things that were all it had left to tell.

## II. Of the Houses on the Circuit

An Taigh-seinnse Òir, at the eastern end of the mining road, was written into the charter-roll in {{year:III-0186}} for a dish of beef cooked slow in its own fat and a syrup the house kept secret, served to the custodians' reeves on their winter rounds when nothing else on the road stayed warm past the second hour of riding. The entry that follows it in the roll is a complaint, in a reeve's own hand, that the house had raised the price of the dish three years running without raising anything else about it, and that a man who had eaten it twice a winter for a decade had come, in his own words, to resent it a little more each time even as he asked for a second helping. The keeper's answer, set down beneath the complaint by whichever clerk thought it worth the ink, was that a secret cost what it cost, and that the reeve was welcome to eat elsewhere on the bare northern road if he found one.

He was not, as it happened, for the bare northern road offered him only {{place:marker:8}}, and it was there, a few winters later, that the charter-roll gained its longer entry. A party of reeves bound for the last shire seat before the pass was caught by a fall of snow that closed the road behind them and did not open it again for the better part of a season. They lived, by their own account written afterward at length and in a hand that grew steadily worse as the winter wore on, on pickled spinach and the keeper's own fire milk, a spirit distilled from whatever grain the house could spare and named, not kindly, for the way it burned going down. The keeper of {{place:marker:8}} kept them fed and kept her own accounts through it besides, and when the road opened she sent the reeves on their way and sent after them, by the first cart through, a bill for every jar of spinach and every cup of the fire milk, itemised. It was paid in full. The reeves' complaint about the season stands in the charter-roll beside her bill, the two together, and neither party comes off the better in the telling.

## III. Of the Hull That Filled

At Seann Skell, in {{year:III-0188}}, a boat-builder named Seumas mac Iain set himself to raise a deep-keeled hull from what the strand still remembered of the Sundering fellowship's craft: the shape of a rib, the run of a plank, a keel laid true along its whole length rather than built up in the short sections the island's boats had used for generations since the last shipwrights of that lore died without apprentices. He had no drawing to work from, only what the old men of the harbour could still tell him and what he could reason out for himself from a wreck's ribs that a storm had once turned up on the strand at Ceann mhòr and that no one had thought, until Seumas came asking, to measure properly before the tide took it back. He worked at her through two winters, in a shed by the harbour that the town let him have rent-free on the strength of his father's name, and the harbour watched him do it the way a harbour watches anything that costs it nothing and might yet come to something.

She went into the water on a morning bright enough that half the town came down to see her float, and floated she did, for the length of time it takes to walk from the shed to the end of the quay and back. Then she took water at a seam along her new keel that no one, not even Seumas, could afterward say they had watched open, and settled by the stern, and went down in water shallow enough that her mast still showed above it an hour after. She went down slowly, by degrees, and the crowd that had come to cheer her stood the whole while and watched the mast go over, which some said afterward was the cruellest part of the whole business.

Manannan's priests at Taigh Mhanannain preached on her for a year after, from the same text each time, that the god had unmade the fleet's craft once with reason and did not mean it remade by a boat-builder's guesswork. Seumas mac Iain did not build again. He kept the shed a few years longer, mending ordinary boats for ordinary fares, and when he died the harbour-court noted only that he had once tried something no one else had tried in living memory and that the town was neither richer nor poorer for it, which was as near as the record ever came to kindness about him.

## IV. Of the Fishers Who Went East, and the Lane to the South

The harbour at Caol ìseal had been silting for longer than any one season could be blamed for it, and by {{year:III-0189}} the fishing families who had worked it for generations gave the last of it up and moved east along the coast to Caol bheag, where the water still ran clean enough to float a boat at any tide. They did not go emptyhanded. They carried their nets, their smoking-racks, and the small shrine at the harbour's edge that had stood as long as the harbour had, stone by stone, and set it up again at Caol bheag exactly as it had stood, so that a stranger coming to the new place might have thought, from the shrine alone, that nothing at all had changed. Caol ìseal itself did not empty. The houses nearest the silted water turned to farming the ground behind them, and within two generations the town that had once smelled of fish and tar smelled, on most days, of turned earth instead.

Further south, the fishers of {{place:burg:49}} had for as long as anyone could remember kept their boats inside the wild strait that gave the town its name, never venturing past its own two capes into open water. In {{year:III-0189a}} a handful of the younger men, restless with a strait that gave up less each season than it had the season before, took their boats round the southern capes instead, into water none of their fathers had fished, and found it ran the whole length of the south coast without a harbour worth the name until the mouth of the Abhainn uaine, where they came at last to the landing of {{place:marker:39}}, below the busy river-town of {{place:burg:44}}. The dried fish they had carried down that coast for want of anywhere nearer to sell it fetched grain at a price better than their own market had offered in years, and they came home with full holds and a lane behind them that the charter-roll named, plainly enough, for the men who had first sailed it: Slighe-mhara Caol fhiadhaich. The older harbours further along that coast, which had never in living memory put a boat past their own headlands, took a season or two to believe the story and then, one after another, began to follow the same lane south.

## V. Of Iseabail nic Lachlainn

Iseabail nic Lachlainn was born into the royal household at Cathair dhearg the winter the reeves lay snowed in on the bare northern road, the daughter of a woman who kept the king's linen and a father no one at court troubled to name. She grew up under the stairs and along the kitchen passages of a house that had more songs in it than most, for every servant who came to the king's hall from every shire brought a handful of her own along with her box and her letter of service, and Iseabail, who was quicker with her ears than with her hands, learned to sit still in a corner of the kitchen and take them all in without being sent off to some more useful work.

By the time she was grown she had a name in the household for knowing more songs than the harpers who were paid to know them, and it was the king's own steward, more from wanting the girl out from under his feet at the linen-presses than from any great faith in the work, who first sent her out on the queen's road with a mule and a letter asking any shire seat's reeve to give her lodging, to see what she could bring back from the towns that no one at court had thought to ask before. She was thirty-four years old the first time she set out, and she did not come back to Cathair dhearg for good until she was past sixty, having in the years between walked or ridden the length of every road on the island that a mule could manage, and a fair number that one could not, taking down in her own hand whatever the island's kitchens and rope-walks and byres still sang while they worked.

## VI. Of the Rope-Walk at Cuan shean

She came to {{place:burg:282}} in her middle years, in {{year:III-0190}}, for no better reason than that she had heard, from a carter on the road above it, that the rope-makers there sang while they walked their strands, and that she had not yet taken down a rope-maker's song from that stretch of coast. The rope-walk ran the length of a field behind the harbour, half of it given over that year to barley for want of enough hemp to fill it, and the men worked backward along it in a line, twisting the strands as they went, each keeping pace with the man ahead of him by the beat of a song rather than by any word passed between them.

Tormod Ròpaire led them, a big, slow-spoken man who had walked that same field since he was a boy carrying spinning-tops for his father's crew, and it was his voice she heard first from the harbour road, keeping the others in step with the ordinary stuff of a working song: a line about a girl in the next town, a line about the price of hemp, the kind of thing every rope-walk on the island sang and no two walks sang quite alike. It was a cold, bright morning, the tide well out and the mud of the harbour showing its whole grey breadth below the field, and half the walk's length that year stood in barley rather than hemp, so that the men worked backward through green stalks for the first thirty paces and only came onto bare turned earth past the low wall where the true crop began. She stood at the field's edge with her tablets out, taking down what she could of it as she had taken down a hundred like it before, and might have gone on to the next town with nothing more than that, had Tormod not, without any word of warning to the men behind him, struck into a different tune altogether.

The others fell in behind him at once, as a crew that has sung together for years falls into a tune it knows without needing to be told it is coming. Iseabail caught two words of it clear across the field, *druim* and *aisne*, keel and rib, and put her tablet down and went nearer, and stood at the very edge of the walk with the strand-dust settling on her skirts, writing as fast as her hand would go while the men kept time with their feet and never once looked round at her.

When the song ended and the men stopped for their meal, she asked Tormod what it was about. He said it was about rope, the way every song on the walk was about rope, whatever else it seemed to be about. She asked him what the words further on meant, the ones after *druim* and *aisne*, and he looked at the men beside him, and they looked back at him, and none of them could tell her. It was, he said at last, the song his own father had taught him, who had it from his father before him, back further than any of them could put a name to, and none of them had ever thought to ask what the words meant, only to keep the tune and the count right, for a rope walked out of step is a rope with a bad lay in it, and a bad lay is a rope that parts when a hull most needs it not to.

They were, all of that crew, sworn to Manannan's order, as most of the rope-walks on that coast had been for longer than anyone could say, wearing the god's grey knot at the collar the way every sworn rope-maker did; and when she asked whether the god's own priests might know the words, since the song plainly spoke of a hull's own bones, Tormod said only that the priests knew what Manannan wished them to know, and that a rope-maker's song was not, in his experience, high on that list. He sang it because his father had sung it and his father's father before him, and he saw no reason a man needed to understand a thing in order to keep it. One of the younger men, a stripling not yet trusted with the lead, said he had always supposed the odd words were simply older speech, worn down past hearing by however many hundred years of mouths, the way a stone step wears smooth under feet that never mean to wear it at all; and Tormod told him that might well be so, and that it changed nothing about how the strand was to be walked.

Iseabail stayed at Cuan shean the better part of a month, taking the song down whole across a dozen hearings, matching one man's memory of a line against another's where they differed, until she had as much of it as the rope-walk itself could give her. Two lines she could set beside island words she knew and be certain of: keel under rib, rib under plank, plank to plank and the sea beneath them. The rest ran on in sounds she could shape with her pen well enough but could not turn into sense in any tongue she had ever gathered a song in, on that coast or any other.

## VII. Of the Reading at Court

Two years after, when the whole of that circuit's gathering was written fair, she brought it back to Cathair dhearg and set it before the king's court, in the hall where such things were read when a gatherer came home from a long round of the shires: the harpers first, to hear whether anything she had taken down was worth the setting to their own strings, and after them whoever else at court cared to listen, which on the day she read the keel-song was near enough the whole hall, for word of it had gone round in advance and a song no one could explain draws more of a crowd than one everybody already half knows.

She read the ordinary verses first, the girl in the next town and the price of hemp, and got the laugh from the hall that such verses are meant to get. Then she read the rest of it, keel under rib and the words that followed it, in the plain voice she had trained herself to read in, without dressing it up as more than it was.

The hall went quiet in a way it had not gone quiet for the rest of her reading. Even the king's own hounds, that had been shifting and scratching under the benches through the ordinary verses, lay still while she read the rest of it through. An old harper, who had been at court longer than most of the household could remember and had heard every song brought back from every shire in that time, asked her to read the strange lines again, and she did, twice more, and he shook his head each time and said he knew no tongue on the island that fit them, not the plain speech, not the priests' own forms, not the oldest words the harpers kept for the oldest songs. A clerk of the vein-house, who was there for some other business and had stayed to listen, said that the shape of the words put him in mind of the custody-book's oldest entries, the ones copied so many times over that no one now living could say for certain they had ever been read straight through rather than copied blind, letter by letter, by clerks who trusted the hand before theirs to have got it right. He did not say the two things were the same. He said only that neither of them made sense to him, and that this was, in his long experience of old writings, not nothing.

The king himself, who had sat through the reading with the same patience he gave any other business of the hall, asked at the end of it whether the song might be sung as well as read, since a tune sometimes carried a meaning that plain speech let drop. Iseabail said she had no voice for singing it as Tormod Ròpaire and his crew had sung it, walking the strand in step, but she gave the court what she could of the tune all the same, and the hall listened to that too, and was no wiser after it than before.

No one in the hall that day could tell Iseabail what the words of the keel-song meant, any more than Tormod Ròpaire and his crew had been able to at their own rope-walk, and she wrote as much into her own account of the reading, plainly, without trying to make more of the not-knowing than the not-knowing was.

## VIII. Of the Schism, the Storm, and the New Harbour

While she was still on the road gathering the last of what would go into that reading, the temple of the Stone at {{place:burg:166}} split over a question that had lain under its floor for longer than either side liked to admit: whether the dead, who belonged to Donn, might rightly be prayed for by name at Clach, or whether such prayer trespassed on ground that was not the Stone's to offer. Those who said no kept the old temple. Those who said the dead might be named there built a second house at {{place:burg:232}}, finished within ten years of the first quarrel, and the two congregations went their own ways from that day, neither troubling the other much and neither, in all the years that followed, giving ground.

A storm the coast called Gailleann Ghlas came in out of the east within a few years and tore the harbour apart at {{place:burg:249}}, leaving the village standing but its quay in pieces past any mending worth the cost. The boats that had worked out of it went to {{place:burg:119}} instead, which built a second harbour arm within the decade to take them, and throve on the fishing of both towns' waters for the rest of the age, while Seann Tarr kept its houses and its shrine and let its harbour go, and became, in the charter-roll's own dry word for such places, a village again.

Tormod Ròpaire died at Cuan shean within a few years of it, seventy years old, still walking the same field he had walked as a boy, though by his last years he let a younger man keep the count and the lead while he sang along behind. His crew kept the keel-song after him as they had kept it before him, word for word as he had given it to them, understanding no more of it than he had, and no less either.

## IX. Of the Queen Small and the Grey Glaze

Catrìona Bheag of Clann Raghnaill was crowned at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0195}}, twenty-nine years old, the first queen the island had raised in more than three centuries. The record, in the plain way it keeps to for such things, notes that she was small, and that no one at her own court said so twice.

At Muileann gheal, in the last years the record has to tell of this stretch, the potters of one household found a grey-green glaze that no other kiln on the island could match for its colour or its wear, and the custodian houses bought their table-ware from that one family and no other. The secret of it stayed within Clann Mhic Phàil, mother to daughter, and was not written down, for a thing kept in the hand and the eye, they said, needs no writing to be kept; which was true of it, as it was true of the keel-song, until the day it was not.

## X. Of the Song Unexplained

It is remembered of Iseabail nic Lachlainn, in the household she served the rest of her life, that she was asked more than once, in her last years at court, whether she had ever learned what the keel-song's strange words meant, and that she answered every time in nearly the same way: that she had asked every scholar the household could bring before her, and every priest of Manannan who would speak with a woman on such a matter at all, and had been told, by all of them together, less than the rope-makers of Cuan shean had told her standing in their own field with the strand-dust on her hem.

She kept the tablets herself until she died, and left them to the vein-house with the rest of her collection, where the clerks who took them in wrote, in the plain hand of an inventory and not of a song, that the gathering held one item they could not describe by its meaning, only by its sound and its keeping: a rope-maker's work-song out of Cuan shean, sung by Manannan's own people, who did not know what they sang and sang it anyway, that spoke, as far as anyone could tell, of a keel, and a rib, and a plank, and the sea beneath them, and of nothing that any hall at Cathair dhearg, that day or any day after, could put into plainer words than its own.
