# Of the Empty Harbour

*LE 56 – 67*

## I. Of the Boards Given Over

Calum mac Nèill kept the boards of Taigh a' Chalaidh for fifty-six years, and in the last two of them his eyes wanted so much light to read a leaf by that his son Dòmhnall took to holding the lamp close over his shoulder whenever an entry was made, so close that the old man complained, more than once, that his own son meant to set his hair alight before his years did the rest of the work themselves. He died in the between-moon of the fifty-eighth year of the count, at Seann Skell, in the room above the harbour-house where he had slept every night of his keeping, and it is told that the last thing his hand reached for, before it stopped reaching for anything, was the corner of the board nearest his bed, the one on which the earliest names of the roll still stood, cut through where they had been struck and plain where they had not.

He had kept a promise that no one had asked him to keep twice: the boards would not be closed while the queen had not closed them, and they were not closed at his death either, though there was no roll left open on them by then for any household to enter or strike its name from, the fleet eighteen years gone and no new leaving to be counted. What Calum's boards had become, in the years since An Diosal, was less a roll than a wall that the town looked to and did not read closely, the names on it grown as familiar and as little regarded as the harbour-house's own stones, until a stranger asked after one of them and the whole town remembered, for the length of the asking, what the boards were for.

The harbour-house did not go to Dòmhnall, who had held the lamp for his father and had expected, without ever quite saying so aloud, that the office was his by the same right it had been his father's, passed from a scribe before Calum in its turn. It went instead to Niall mac Dhòmhnaill, Dòmhnall's own son, a man of nine-and-twenty who had been eleven years old on the night of An Diosal and had stood on the west quay wall with a hazel stick and a knife, cutting a notch for every hull that went round the pool. Calum had never told the town, in so many words, which of the two men he meant to follow him, but he had begun, in the year before his death, to send for Niall rather than for Dòmhnall whenever a difficult entry wanted a second hand, and the town of Seann Skell, which watched such things closely because it had little else that season to watch, had drawn its own conclusions some while before Calum's will was read aloud at the harbour-house door.

Dòmhnall took it as well as a man takes such a thing when he has had a year to see it coming and no year at all to make his peace with it. He stood at his father's shoulder through the reading of the will, said nothing while it was read, and afterward, alone with Niall on the harbour strand, said only that the boards were the queerest inheritance a man could be passed over for, since no one living had ever grown rich or great from keeping them, and that his father, whatever else he had been, had never once in fifty-six years asked whether the work he gave his whole life to was worth the giving. Niall answered that he did not know either, and that he meant to keep them all the same, and father and son, who had lived in the same house under the same lamp, worked the harbour-house together civilly enough for the rest of that year and every year after, Dòmhnall keeping the trade rolls and the harbour dues, Niall keeping the boards that no household entered any longer and the knife that no hull now needed counting.

He took the stick down from the shelf where it had stood since the night of the turn, the hazel stick with its row of notches cut too fast and too many to be read again for any true count, and he did not put it back. It stood from that day at his own elbow when he sat to write, in the same place his grandfather's ink-horn had always stood, though he used it for nothing and could have told no one, asked, exactly why he kept it there rather than on the shelf with the rest of what Calum had left behind him.

His grandmother Seonag, the goose-keeper Calum had married before Niall's own father was born, outlived her husband by less than a year, and in the months between the two deaths she came down most mornings to the harbour-house to sit by the fire while Niall worked, saying little, watching him form the letters at the board her husband had sat at for fifty-six years. She told him once, in one of those quiet mornings, that Calum had never once in all his keeping told her whether he thought the boards worth the life he had given them, and that she had stopped asking him after the first few years of their marriage, having come to believe that a man who kept such a question to himself that long meant to keep it there for good. Niall did not ask himself, aloud or otherwise, whether his own answer would differ from the one his grandfather had never given, but he noticed, in the years that followed, that he had stopped expecting anyone to ask him.

## II. Of the Lamps Grown about the Berth

No one had ordered a rite kept at the quay the ships had sailed from, and no one, so far as Niall's own boards or his grandfather's before them ever showed, could name the year it began, only that by the time Niall took up the pen it had already the settled shape of a thing long kept rather than newly made. On the eve nearest the day of An Diosal, as near as the harbour-house's own dating let anyone reckon it after so many years without a fixed count, the households of Seann Skell that had given someone to the roll, and a good number that had not, brought down to the west quay whatever small light they could carry, a rushlight, a fish-oil lamp, a coal from the hearth wrapped close in a clay pot against the wind off the water, and set it along the stones where the fleet had lain, and stood a while, and went home again without a word said over it that anyone thought worth remembering.

Niall had gone down himself, the first autumn of his keeping, more from a scribe's habit of wanting to see for himself what he might one day be asked to write of than from any devotion of his own, and had stood at the edge of it with his grandfather's stick, which he had brought without quite meaning to, held loose in one hand. The rite was not large by then, a few dozen lamps along stones that had once held the whole west quay's worth of light on a single night, and half those who kept it were old enough to remember the sailing themselves, or old enough at least to have been told of it young by someone who did. He watched an old woman he did not know set her lamp down at the very edge of the stone where the ships had lain, closest of any that were set that night, and stand over it a long while with her lips moving and no sound coming from them that he could make out, and when she had gone he asked a boy nearby, one of the harbour-house's own runners, whose lamp it had been, and the boy said only that it was old Mairead's, that she came every year, and that no one now living knew for certain which of her kin the lamp was kept for, since she herself, asked, gave a different name most years.

He wrote it into his own leaf that night, not because any board required an entry of a rite the crown had never ordered and Taigh na Fèithe had never asked after, but because it seemed to him, turning the matter over by his own late lamp, that a harbour scribe who wrote down every dispute over a cracked hull and every barrel landed at the quay and said nothing of the one night in the year when half the town came down to the water with a light in its hand, was keeping a stranger kind of silence than any his grandfather had ever kept before him. The entry he made was short, and it named no one, and it has outlasted every longer thing he wrote that year.

## III. Of the Harbour at Half Trade

Murchadh mac Raghnaill, harbour-master of Seann Skell, was of an age with Niall and had held his own office a year longer, having been given the berths and the dues of the harbour by a predecessor who retired to his daughter's house up the Abhainn naomh rather than die at his post as Calum had. It was Murchadh's business, not Niall's, to count what trade the harbour carried, and in the sixth year of Niall's keeping he brought his own count to the harbour-house door and set it beside the boards for anyone to read, a plain reckoning of the berths that stood empty against the berths that stood full on an ordinary market morning, and it showed, without any softening he cared to give it, that the harbour of Seann Skell carried, that year, not much more than half the trade it had carried before An Diosal.

It was not a mystery to either man why this should be so, and Murchadh did not write it as though it were one. The fishing households that had crewed the greater part of the fleet's boats before the turn, and had gone in no small number aboard the fleet itself, had left both fewer hands to work the harbour's boats and fewer mouths at Seann Skell to buy what those boats brought in, and the rope-walks along Cuan shean's stretch of the waterfront, which had once kept every hand they could hire twisting hemp against the fleet's endless want of cordage, found themselves that same year with more rope made than the harbour's remaining trade could use, and let hands go for the first time in the memory of anyone still walking the walks, old men who had twisted rope there since before the sailing and had never once, in all those years, seen the walk's own master turn a willing hand away.

Murchadh's reckoning was matter-of-fact where a lesser clerk's might have reached for grief, and Niall, reading it over at the harbour-house board, found himself wondering, not for the first time, whether the harbour-master's plainness came from a harder heart than his own or only from a kind of mercy that wore the shape of bookkeeping. He copied the figures into his own boards without comment, as his grandfather would have done, and set Murchadh's leaf beside Rolla nan Deònach on the same wall, the names of those who had gone and the count of what their going had cost the harbour they left behind, side by side on stones that had room enough, by then, for both.

## IV. Of the Word that Came and Was Not Written

In the seventh year of his keeping a boat put in at Seann Skell from up the coast with a hand aboard, a man Niall did not know and never learned the name of, who told it in the taproom of a house near the strand, over the second cup rather than the first, that he had heard from a cousin who had heard it from a trader out of Ceann mhòr that a second fleet, smaller than the first and gone by a different season, had put out from somewhere on the island's own shore in the years just after An Diosal, and had likewise never been heard from since.

It reached Niall the following morning, brought to him by the same runner who had told him whose lamp burned closest to the quay-stone, the boy repeating it with the particular relish of someone passing on a thing he has been told twice already and half believes for the telling's own sake, and Niall listened to the whole of it standing at his board with his pen already in his hand, as he had a hundred other pieces of harbour gossip in his years of keeping. He asked the boy who the man was and where his boat had gone, and got no clear answer to either, and asked whether anyone else in the taproom had heard the same tale from any other mouth before that night, and got, again, nothing he could set his hand to.

He did not write it. In seven years at the boards he had written a great many things a stranger might have judged smaller and stranger than this: a dispute over a landed catch's true weight, a fight between two carters over a berth neither had paid for, the exact hour a lost child was found asleep under an upturned coble. A tale with no name behind it and no second mouth to confirm it, he judged, was not a harbour matter at all, whatever else it might be, and the boards, his grandfather had taught him before he could properly hold the pen, were not the place for a thing a scribe could not stand behind. He thought of it again, once or twice, in the weeks after, turning it over the way a man turns over a ring he is not sure is good silver, and each time set it aside unwritten, until the thinking of it, too, grew less frequent, and at last stopped.

Dòmhnall, told of it over the trade rolls one evening, asked his son outright why he would not set down even so much as the hearing of it, a rumour being a fact of a kind in its own right, whatever the truth or falsehood of what it rumoured, and Niall answered that a boat's dues were a fact of that kind and a rumour was not, and that he did not mean to spend the rest of his life discovering where the difference between the two ran thin. Dòmhnall let the matter drop, as he had let a good many matters drop between them since the will was read, but it is told that he repeated the tale once or twice after, in the ordinary way of a man passing on harbour gossip, careful each time to say that the harbour-house's own scribe had heard it too and set it aside, as though Niall's silence carried a kind of authority the tale could be hung on.

## V. Of the Watch at Ceann mhòr

Twenty miles up the coast at Ceann mhòr, in the same year the rope-walks let their first hands go, a fisher's daughter named Seonag nic Dhùghaill climbed the headland above the town at first light on a morning no different, so far as anyone could afterward say, from any other morning, and stood there through the whole of the day's turning, looking out over open water, and came down again at dusk having seen nothing but gulls and a change of weather coming in from the west.

She had lost no one to the fleet herself, her own family being newer to Ceann mhòr than the sailing was old, but she had grown up on tellings of it from older fishing folk of the town who had, and she said afterward, when asked why she had gone up, only that it seemed to her a thing someone ought to be doing, and that she did not see why it should not be her. Other households of Ceann mhòr, hearing what she had done, began within the season to take a turn of their own on the headland, a day at a time, household by household, so that by the year's end there was scarcely a week the headland stood unwatched from first light to dusk, and the custom, which had no name at its beginning beyond "Seonag's watch", came in time to be called simply Faire a' Chinn, by fisher-folk up and down that stretch of coast who had never met Seonag herself and knew her only as the woman who had started it.

No sail was ever seen from that headland in Seonag's own lifetime, nor, so far as the harbour-house at Seann Skell ever had report of, in the lifetime of anyone who kept the watch after her, and it is told that she herself, asked late in her years whether she still believed a sail might come, said only that a watch kept for a sure thing was no watch at all, and that she had climbed the headland the first morning expecting nothing, and had found, in the not-finding, reason enough to climb it again.

## VI. Of the New Name and the Old

By the eighth year of Niall's keeping the old name of the west quay, the plain harbour name it had carried since before the roll was ever opened, had begun to fall out of common mouths at Seann Skell in favour of a newer one that no one could point to a single day of coining, Cidhe an Diosail, spoken now by carters and fish-wives and children too young to have known anyone who sailed from it, as though the stones had never been called anything else. The harbour-house's own boards had kept the old name a while longer than the town's own mouths had, out of a scribe's habitual caution about changing what stood written, but in the ninth year, with the old name gone so thoroughly from ordinary speech that a stranger asking after it by its old form was as likely as not to be misunderstood, Niall entered the new name into Rolla a' Chalaidh himself, in his own hand, Cidhe an Diosail, and let the old name stand beside it once, crossed through but not scraped, in the manner his grandfather had taught every apprentice of that board: a struck name stays visible.

The name that passed out of use at the west quay found a use elsewhere. Cidhe an Iar, the west quay's own old name, was taken up that same year by the fishers of the new wharf built at the strand's north end to handle the catch that no longer had the old quay's room to land at, and within a season or two the fishers who worked it used the name for their own wharf without, so far as Niall could tell, any sense that they had inherited it from anywhere, or any thought at all for where it had come from.

It was in the same years, and in no entry Niall could point to as the first, that he began to notice Rolla a' Chalaidh using a word he had grown up hearing spoken only with an object, roghnaich, to choose this or that, now standing alone in a clerk's hand, a household roghnaich, chose, with nothing following it that any reader needed told, since every reader by then knew well enough what a household that had chosen, unqualified, had chosen to do. He used the word so himself, once or twice, before he noticed he had done it, and when he did notice, he let the usage stand in his own leaf rather than correct it to the fuller, older form, judging, as his grandfather might have judged before him, that a scribe's business was to write the tongue his town actually spoke rather than the tongue it had once spoken and no longer troubled to.

## VII. Of the Children Who Never Saw It

Niall had, by the ninth year of his keeping, three children of his own grown near enough to grown that they helped about the harbour-house on a market day, and none of the three, nor any child born at Seann Skell in a dozen years and more before them, had ever seen the west quay with a fleet of nineteen hulls, or one-and-twenty, lying at it, waiting on a tide. To them the sailing was a thing told rather than a thing remembered, a story their own father kept the boards of and could point to the stones of, no different in its bones from any other tale an old woman might tell a child at a winter hearth, save that this one had a harbour-house full of leaves to back it and a wall of names a child could run a finger along and ask, of any one of them, who that had been.

His eldest daughter, a girl of ten that year, asked him once, watching him enter an ordinary day's dues, whether he himself had seen the ships go, and he told her that he had, that he had stood on the very wall she was leaning against with a stick in his hand cutting notches for the counting of them, and she looked at the wall a long while after he had told her, as though trying to see upon it something her own eyes could not find there, and asked no more that day. He did not correct whatever she had imagined in the looking, and did not know, himself, what it was she had hoped to see.

## VIII. Of the Last Coaster and the Last Entry

Alasdair mac Iain, Alasdair Saor, who had laid the first deep keel any hand on the island had ever laid, and had redrawn it in sand by a driftwood fire after the first hull lay down and drowned eleven souls off the headland, and had bound the wrights of the Seann Skell strand into one fellowship and seen a boy of that fellowship sworn before a custodian in the manner of the vein, was by the sixty-seventh year of the count an old man bent nearly double over the same strand he had first walked to Doire fhionn from as a young one, and still, to the last season of his life, unwilling to let a hull leave his yard that he had not laid a hand to himself.

His last work was a small thing beside the deep-keeled hulls of the fleet, a sewn-plank coaster of the old kind built for a fish merchant of Inis àrsaidh who wanted nothing grander than a boat that would carry his catch to market and bring him home again dry, and Alasdair built it that spring as he had built every boat before it, going down himself to check the set of every plank though his hands by then shook too much to hold an adze steady for more than a few strokes at a time, leaving the finer work to the journeymen he had trained and trusting, as he had trusted the fellowship's oath for sixty years, that a hand he had taught would not betray the eye that had taught it.

The coaster was launched at Inis àrsaidh in the last days of the moon of Teasach, and Alasdair died at Seann Skell before the week was out, at the end of that same summer, in the yard where he had laid the first frames of the fleet's first hull like the ribs of a beast on the open strand. Niall entered his death the same day the word of it reached the harbour-house, in the ordinary hand he kept for every death the town brought to his boards, and set beside it, on the same leaf, in the same sitting, the entry he had been putting off making for the better part of a year: that the west quay, called of old by a plainer name, stood now and would stand hereafter in Rolla a' Chalaidh as Cidhe an Diosail, and that the name it had given up passed, by the town's own usage rather than any order of his, to the wharf at the strand's north end.

He sat with the two entries a while after the ink of both had dried, the old shipwright's death and the quay's new name, set down one above the other on a leaf no different in its ruling from any other leaf in the harbour-house's long keeping, and it is told that he said to no one in particular, closing the book, a line the harbour folk of Seann Skell were already saying of their own accord by then, of one thing and another, though he may have been the first to set it down: is fhada an cuan, is goirid a' chuimhne. Long is the sea, and short is memory. He banked the harbour-house's night fire himself, the boy whose office it was having gone home early, and set his grandfather's stick back in its place at his elbow for the next day's writing, exactly where it had stood every night since the boards were given over to him.
