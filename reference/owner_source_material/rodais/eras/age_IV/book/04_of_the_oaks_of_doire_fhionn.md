# Of the Oaks of Doire fhionn

*LE 3 – 5*

## I. Of a Wright Who Sewed Planks

Alasdair mac Iain was a boat-wright of the strand below Seann Skell, and in the fourth year of the age he had never built anything that could not be carried up a beach by six men.

That was what a boat was, on Dia-thìr. The wrights of the strand built coasters and fishing boats as their grandmothers had: they split their planks from oak and ash with wedges, and shaped them with the adze, and laid them edge to edge on a frame of bent ribs, and sewed them together with split root and twisted gut through holes bored along the edges, and stopped the seams with moss and tallow. A good sewn boat was light and supple; she gave in a sea, every seam working, like a basket in a stream. She wanted to be pulled up every night and let dry, and her stitching looked to, and her moss renewed. She would take a crew of six along the coast from headland to headland in a day and bring them home loaded with fish. Three nights on the open sea would have killed her.

Alasdair had built perhaps forty such boats. He was a lean dark man, not tall, with a long jaw and a way of looking at a piece of timber that made the other wrights of the strand fall silent, as though he were listening to it. He was known on the strand as the best of them at a hull's lines. The strand knew him too as a man who asked too many questions, who would stand for half a morning looking at a sea running up the beach and not say what he was thinking.

What he was thinking, from the day he read the roll in the harbour-house, was a keel.

## II. Of the Keel in the Tellings

There was no keel on the island. There were keels in the tellings.

The old tellers of the west said that the boats of the Stone Kings, in the Age of Ailean, had been built on a spine of oak: a single long timber down the middle of the bottom, and the ribs rising out of it on either side like the ribs of a beast from its backbone, and the planks fastened to the ribs with wooden pegs, not sewn. Such a boat, the tellers said, stood stiff in a sea and cut the water where a sewn boat rode it, and she could stay out for days. The Stone Kings had sailed round the whole island in such boats, the tellers said, and to the islands off the coast; and then, as the tellers always said of everything in the Age of Ailean, the making of them had been forgotten.

Alasdair did not know whether to believe it. But he had noticed, as a boy, that the old coasters on the strand, the ones built by the grandfathers of the men who built them now, had a thicker plank down the middle of the bottom than the others, a plank that was more than a plank, shaped out of a single long timber with a ridge along its underside; and that the wrights who built them now left it out, because it was heavy and hard to make and nobody could say what it was for. He had thought, then, that it was what was left of something.

So through the winter after the queen's word, and all the next year, he built small hulls on the strand, no longer than his arm, on spines of oak. He built them with pegs, and with nails of hardwood, and with his own sewing, one way and another. He floated them in the river pool and pushed them over with a stick to see how they came up. Then he took them down to the mouth of the river where the sea came in and let them go on the ebb and walked along the shore to see where they came ashore, and how. The other wrights laughed at him, and then stopped laughing, and then began to come and stand beside him on the shore with their hands under their arms and watch.

By the end of the fourth year he knew four things. A keel would make a hull stiff. A stiff hull could be bigger. A bigger hull could carry water and stores for many days. And the keel would have to be made of a single timber longer than any oak he had ever seen.

## III. Of the Lady at Inis àrsaidh

He went to the queen's sister, because there was no one else to go to.

The queen had said she would neither help nor hinder. The custodians had said nothing, and were said to be quarrelling among themselves. The harbour-house kept the roll, but it had no money and no timber. The households on the roll were fishers and farmers and wrights, and none of them had anything to give but their own hands. But the lady Mòrag of Inis àrsaidh had been on the roll since the autumn of the queen's word, and she had asked the Cuan shean skipper about oak, and she had silver of her own.

She received him in the house at Inis àrsaidh, by the fire, with her husband. She had a child now, a boy of a few moons, who lay in a basket by the hearth and watched the flames. Alasdair stood awkwardly with his cap in his hand and told them about the keel.

He told it badly, he thought. He was not a talker. But Mòrag listened without interrupting, and when he had finished she asked him three questions: how long the keel must be, how many such timbers the fleet would want, and whether he knew where to find them. He answered the first two. To the third he said that he did not.

"Doire fhionn," said Aonghas, from the other side of the fire.

Alasdair looked at him.

"Up the north road," said the big farmer. "Forty-odd miles. There's a grove there, on the hill above the village, older than any of the Doires. My grandfather took me to see it when I was a boy. He said the oaks had been standing since before the Stone Kings. You couldn't see the tops from the ground." He was quiet a moment. "It's the grove the village is named for. They won't like it."

"They'll like it less if we don't ask," said Mòrag. She looked at Alasdair. "Go and look at them. If they're what you need, come back and tell me, and I'll pay for the felling and the hauling, and the households of the roll will give their labour. If they're not, come back and tell me that, and we'll look somewhere else."

"And if the village won't give them, lady?"

"Then we'll ask the village what it wants," said Mòrag, "and see whether we can give it."

## IV. Of the Road North

He set out at the turn of the fifth year, in the moon of Gearras, after Là Fhèill Brìde, when the hearths had been swept and relit and the days were beginning to lengthen. On foot and alone he went, with a pack and a staff and a coil of line with knots in it every arm's length, for measuring.

The north road went up out of the Seann Skell country along the river, and then over the long low hills toward the north coast, through a country of small farms and peat-moss and scrub birch, with the wind always in his face. He made fifteen miles the first day and slept in a byre. The second day it rained, and he made less. On the third day, in the afternoon, he came over a rise and saw below him a village of grey houses in a fold of the hills, with a stream running through it and smoke going up; and on the hill above the village, on its eastern side, a darkness against the sky that he took at first for a cloud.

It was the grove.

He stood on the rise and looked at it for a long time. He had seen oaks all his life. The strand below Seann Skell was built of oak, and every boat he had ever made was oak. These were something else. They stood on the crown of the hill in a ring, perhaps sixty of them, with a few lesser trees about their feet, and they were so high and so broad that the hill itself looked small beneath them, and their bare winter crowns made a black net against the grey sky that shifted as the wind went through it. He could hear the wind in them from where he stood, a mile off: a low roar, like surf.

He went down into the village as the light was going, and asked at the first door for the keeper of the grove.

## V. Of Eilidh nic Ruairidh

The keeper of the grove was an old woman called Eilidh nic Ruairidh, who lived in the last house of the village at the foot of the grove hill, with a cow and a great many cats. She was small and bent and brown as a nut, and her eyes were very clear. She had kept the grove for fifty years, she told him, as her mother had before her. It was not a hard office. The grove kept itself. The keeper walked round it at the four feasts, and saw that no one cut the living wood, and gathered the fallen boughs for the village hearths, and told the children not to climb.

She listened to him with her hands folded on her stick. When he had told her what he wanted, and why, she did not say anything for a long while.

"Come up in the morning," she said at last. "I'll show you."

In the morning they went up the hill together, slowly, because she was slow. The grove was bigger close to than it had looked from the rise. The trunks were so thick that three men could not have joined hands round the smallest of them, and they went up straight and grey and furrowed for sixty feet before they branched. The ground under them was deep in old leaves and mast, and very quiet. Even the wind, which roared in the crowns, did not come down to the floor of the grove. A few birds moved in the high branches. Nothing else.

Alasdair walked from tree to tree with his knotted line, measuring. He put his hands on the bark. He looked up each trunk, along its length, the way he looked along a plank for a warp. Eilidh walked beside him and said nothing.

When he had been round the whole ring he came back to her and said, "These are what I need."

"I know," said Eilidh.

"I need a score of them, or near it, for the keels. And the frames from the others, and the planks. I'd need most of the grove."

"I know that too." She stood leaning on her stick, looking up into the crowns. "Well. You'd better tell me which."

He did not understand her. She turned and looked at him with her clear eyes.

"You'll not take them as a crowd," she said. "Not in my grove. If they're going, you'll choose them, one by one, and you'll tell me why that one and not the next, and I'll tell you its name. They've all got names. My mother gave me the names and her mother gave them to her. And when you've chosen, you'll know what you've taken. That's my price."

## VI. Of the Naming of the Trees

It took them two days. He had thought it would take an hour.

He went from tree to tree again, with the old woman beside him, and at each one he stopped and said whether he wanted it and why. This one, because it was straight for seventy feet, and would make a keel with no scarf in it. This one, because it had a great branch coming off low down at the right angle to make the stem-post of a hull, the timber that rises from the fore end of the keel. Not that one; it was hollow at the heart, he could hear it when he knocked. This one, for frames, because it was crooked, and its crooks were the shapes of ribs.

And at each one Eilidh told him its name. The Grey Mare. The Old Man's Knee. The Two Sisters, which were two trunks grown out of one root, and which he took both of. Crom's Finger. The Bent Back. The Hawk's Tree, because a hawk had nested in it every spring for longer than anyone remembered. The Drowned Woman, for a reason Eilidh would not tell him. The Queen, which was the biggest in the grove, and which he stood under for a long time, and at last said he would take for the first keel.

He had not meant to feel anything about it. He was a wright; he felled oaks every year. But by the end of the second day he had a list of names in his head that he could not get out of it, and when he came to the last tree of the ring, a young one, no more than two hundred years old, standing a little apart, and said that he did not need it, he found that his hands were shaking.

"That's the Child," said Eilidh. "You'll leave it?"

"I'll leave it."

"And the others?"

He looked round the ring. Of sixty-odd trees he had chosen fifty-four.

"The others go," he said.

Eilidh nodded. She did not seem angry. She looked, if anything, relieved, as though a thing she had been waiting a long time to hear had been said at last, and it was worse than she had feared and not as bad as she had dreamed.

"It won't grow again," she said. "You know that. You can cut an ash and it comes up from the stool. An oak like these, you cut it, it's gone. There'll be nothing on this hill but the Child and the stumps, and the Child will never be what these are, not in my time nor yours nor anyone's we'll know." She was quiet. "And the village will still be Doire fhionn. The fair grove. It'll keep the name. It won't have any reason to."

He had no answer. He stood in the silent grove with the knotted line in his hands and could not think of one.

"Well," said Eilidh at last. "Go and tell your lady. And tell her to send men who know how to fell a tree so that it doesn't smash itself. I'll not have the Queen broken for want of care."

## VII. Of the Felling

They came in the moon of Sìolach, the seed-time: eighty men from the households of the roll, fishers and farmers and wrights, with axes and saws and wedges and ropes, and a dozen yoke of oxen from the farms up the Abhainn naomh, and Aonghas mac Fhionnlaigh at their head, because he knew oxen. Mòrag's silver paid for their bread and the village's barley and the fodder for the oxen. The village of Doire fhionn stood in its doorways and watched them come up the road, and did not speak to them.

The felling took most of the summer. Each tree was felled toward the downhill side, onto a bed of cut brush to break its fall, with ropes on its crown and men on the ropes to guide it. Alasdair was there for every one. The Queen went last. It took two days to bring down, and when it fell, in the evening, with a long tearing groan and then a crash that shook the ground under the village and brought the cows lowing out of the byres, the men on the ropes stood still and did not cheer, and some of them took off their caps.

Eilidh nic Ruairidh did not come up the hill while the felling went on. But at the end of each day, when the men came down to the village to eat, she was sitting in her doorway with her cats round her, and Alasdair would stop and tell her which tree had gone that day, by its name. She would nod, and say nothing, and he would go on.

After the Queen fell, he stopped at her door as usual. She was not in the doorway. He looked in, and saw her sitting by her cold hearth in the dark with her hands folded on her stick, and did not go in.

## VIII. Of the Ox-Sleds on the North Road

A keel-timber seventy feet long cannot be carried. It must be dragged.

They built sleds of lesser oak, low and broad, with runners shod in ash, and rolled the great trunks onto them with levers and ropes, and yoked the oxen six pair to a sled, and dragged them south down the north road toward the sea. It was forty-two miles. With a keel-timber on the sled, the oxen made three miles in a good day and one in a bad one. They broke two sleds on the first hill. They had to widen the road at every bend, cutting back the banks and the birch scrub, and at the stream below the village they had to build a causeway of stone before the sleds would cross it. The farmers along the road came out and stood at their gates to watch the trunks go by, and some of them cursed, because the road was ruined for their carts, and some of them brought out ale.

It went on all that summer and into the autumn. The sleds went down one after another, strung out along the road for twenty miles, with the drovers walking beside the oxen and the wrights walking beside the trunks, and the sound of it could be heard, the farmers said, a mile off on a still day: the groan of the runners on the stones, and the drovers' cries, and the oxen blowing.

Nearer the sea, on the shore of Loch chrom below the capital, there was a wood of wild oak, rough and crooked, that had never been anyone's grove; and in the same summer a party of woodcutters from the roll went there and built themselves huts at the wood's edge, to fell crooked oak for frames and knees that the fleet would need in hundreds. The huts did not outlast the felling; the woodcutters went home to the roll when the last knee was hauled out, and the bracken came up through the floors. The wood kept its name, Doire fhiadhaich, the wild grove, and the tellers of that shore say still that timber for the fleet was felled there.

## IX. Of the First Keel

The first keel came down to the strand below Seann Skell in the moon of Teasach, the hot moon, in high summer. It was the Queen.

Alasdair had cleared a length of the strand above the high-water line, and dug a saw-pit beside it, and laid a bed of heavy oak blocks in a long straight line on the shingle, level to a hair, which he had spent a week getting right with a line and a trough of water. The oxen dragged the sled down onto the strand and along beside the blocks, and fifty men with levers rolled the great trunk off the sled and onto the blocks, and it lay there, grey and furrowed and enormous, seventy feet from end to end.

Then they began to shape it.

It took them the rest of that moon and half the next. They split the trunk down its length with wedges, and hewed away the sapwood with axes and adzes, and squared it, and shaped it: flat on top where the frames would sit, and deeper at the middle than at the ends, and with a ridge along its underside, like the ridge on the thick bottom plank of the old coasters that Alasdair had wondered at as a boy. He shaped the ridge himself. He did it by eye and by the feel of the adze in his hands, walking up and down the length of the timber a hundred times a day, sighting along it, stooping, laying his palm on it, and cutting a little more. No one on the island had ever done this before, and there was no one he could ask.

On {{date:III-0008}} it was done, and he stood at the fore end of it and looked along it to the after end, and saw that it was straight and true; and the men of the strand stood round it in a ring and looked at him, and at it.

"Well," said one of them. "What's it want now?"

"Ribs," said Alasdair. "Then planks. Then as many more of it as there are oaks on the sleds."

## X. Of the Fellowship of the Strand

He could not do it alone, and he could not do it with a crowd. He needed wrights who would learn it as he had learned it, and keep it, and not wander off to their fishing when the herring came.

So in the moon of Lùghnas, at the feast of Lugh, he called the wrights of the Seann Skell strand together on the shingle beside the first keel, all of them, masters and journeymen, some forty men, and asked them to bind themselves to the fleet: to work at it and nothing else until it was built, and to take apprentices, and to share what each of them learned with all the others. He asked it badly. He was no talker. But they had all stood on the shore with their hands under their arms and watched his little hulls come up out of the water, and most of them said yes.

They made it a fellowship. The custody-book gives its name as Comann nan Saor-luinge, the fellowship of the ship-wrights. They took the custodians of the vein for their pattern, because the custodians were the only fellowship on the island that anyone respected: every apprentice was to be bound for seven years, as a custodian's apprentice was bound to the galleries, and was to learn the whole craft and swear to keep it and teach it. A custodian came down from the vein-house to swear the apprentices, a grave young man in undyed wool who did not know one end of a boat from the other and said so, and heard their oaths all the same over a coal from the vein in a clay dish, as the vein-house heard its own.

The first apprentice sworn was a boy of the strand called Coinneach mac Fhearghais, fourteen years old, the son of a coaster-wright, with big hands and a stammer, who had been hanging about Alasdair's little hulls for a year. He knelt on the shingle with his hands on the coal and swore to the fellowship in a voice that cracked on every other word. When he stood up he looked at the keel, and then at Alasdair, and grinned, and Alasdair found that he was grinning too.

## XI. Of the Frames Standing on the Strand

By the end of the moon of Pailteach they had the first frames up.

They rose out of the keel on either side in pairs, one pair for each arm's length of its seventy feet, curved crooks of oak from the Bent Back and the Old Man's Knee and the wild wood by Loch chrom, pegged into the keel with treenails of heart-oak and joined across at the top by beams. From the harbour-house at the head of the west quay, the town could see them over the roofs of the strand: two long rows of curved grey timbers standing against the sky, bare and pale, with the light coming through between them.

The town came down to look. People stood on the shingle in the evening and stared at the thing on the blocks. It was bigger than any boat they had ever seen, and it did not look like a boat. It looked, the harbour scribe Calum mac Nèill wrote on a board that night, like the ribs of a great beast lying dead on the shore, picked clean by the birds. He meant it kindly. He did not know how else to say it.

Alasdair heard none of it, walking up and down the length of the keel inside the frames, as he walked it every evening, with his palm running along the top of each rib as he passed, feeling for the fair curve.

He thought of the old woman at Doire fhionn, sitting in the dark by her cold hearth with her hands on her stick. And he thought of the Queen, the biggest oak in the grove, lying under his feet now, shaped and squared and pegged, with the ribs rising out of her. He thought that he had never made anything in his life that had cost so much, and that he did not yet know whether it would float.

The Child was still standing on the hill above Doire fhionn, alone among the stumps. Word of that came down to the strand later in the year with a drover, who said the village had built a low wall of stones round it, and that the old keeper went up every evening and sat beside it until dark.
