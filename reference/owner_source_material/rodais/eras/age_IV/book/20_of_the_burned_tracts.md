# Of the Burned Tracts

*LE 450 – 509*

> *Cha do chaill sinn blàr, ach chaill sinn an teine.*
> We did not lose a battle, but we lost the fire.
>
> — said afterward at Doire chiar, of the burning of the tracts

## I. Of the Woman Who Taught at Doire chiar

Eilidh nic Iain was born at {{place:burg:333}}, a village of turf and grey stone set among birch and low hazel where the vein-house's own writ ran thin and the coal had never been found worth the digging, and she was called Eilidh Dhubh from girlhood for hair black as a crow's wing rather than for anything darker in her, though those who did not love what she taught said it, before she was old, with more than one meaning. Her father kept bees and a scatter of goats on the hill above the village and had no more learning than his letters, but her mother's mother had been one of the last to sit under a teacher of the rival school, the school of the Holy Age that held the vein answered labour and not birth, and the old woman had kept the teaching in her own head the way a house keeps a coal banked under ash, needing only breath to wake it, and had given it whole to her granddaughter before she died.

Eilidh was thirty years old, unmarried and settled to nothing anyone in the village could name, when she began to say the teaching aloud at the well and afterward under the gable of her own house, to whoever of the village came to stand and listen: that the vein under the hill belonged to no house by right of blood, that a custodian's son was no more owed a gallery than a collier's, and that the seven houses who now wore the neck-rings of Muileann chiar had kept, over four generations, a measure meant for all of them alone, calling it custom and calling custom law. She did not shout it and did not dress it in threat. She said it the way a woman says a thing she has turned over long enough to trust, plainly, at whatever hour the listening came to her, and within a year she had written down, in a hand her mother had taught her, the whole of what she said, so that those who could not come every evening might have it read to them by whoever among them had letters.

The tracts, as they came to be called, were no more than a double handful of sheets by the second year, copied and copied again by whoever in the village or beyond it wanted a set of their own, on whatever paper or scraped hide could be found, so that no two copies in the whole country agreed exactly in their wording though none, by every account, disagreed in their sense. Eilidh herself never wrote a fair copy twice the same, saying that a teaching kept too carefully to one form of words was already halfway to a law, and a law was the last thing she meant it to become.

## II. Of the Reading at Harvest

It was at the harvest gatherings that the tracts travelled furthest, carried by pupils who went from Doire chiar to whatever gathering their own kin kept that season, in the north-east where the grain came thin off stony ground and every household's harvest was a shared labour before it was ever a shared feast. A gathering at {{place:burg:355}}, {{year:III-0094}}, heard the tracts read for the first time by one of Eilidh's own pupils, a young man named Coinneach Odhar who had learned his letters from her expressly so that he might read where she herself would not travel, and it is told that the head of the household hosting the gathering, a woman of no love for the vein-house's seven, let him stand on her own threshing floor to do it, the grain still unwinnowed at his feet, and gave him besides a bowl of the harvest's own new ale when he had finished, which he took as thanks enough for the walking.

Not every household took to it so easily. At a gathering nearer Muileann dhearg a farmer's wife, hearing the tracts read out over her own supper, said aloud that a teaching which told a collier he was owed a custodian's gallery was a teaching that would get colliers killed for believing it, and that Eilidh Dhubh, whatever the truth of her words, might have kept them for wiser company than a harvest table with children at it. Coinneach Odhar, by his own later telling, had no answer ready for her and gave none, and walked the two days home turning the woman's words over as carefully as he had ever turned Eilidh's own.

Eilidh heard of the exchange within the season, harvest news travelling as fast as harvest grain along the same roads, and said to Coinneach, when he told her of it, only that the woman was not wrong to fear for her sons, and that a teaching a body could not afford to believe out loud was not therefore a teaching that had stopped being true; the fear and the truth were two different debts, and a teacher's business was with the second, though she never claimed the first was small. She went on reading it herself wherever she was asked, and let her pupils carry it further than her own legs would go, and in this way, by {{year:III-0094}}, the tracts of Doire chiar were known, by name if not by the whole text, from the eastern hills to Cnoc bheag itself, though not one copy had yet been shown to anyone at the vein-house.

## III. Of the Order from Muileann dhearg

It could not stay unshown forever. A journeyman of the seam who had heard the tracts read at a gathering carried word of them, meaning no particular harm by it, to a cousin who kept the vein-house's own store at {{place:burg:491}}, and the store-keeper, being a cautious man in a cautious office, passed the word up the gallery to those whose business it was to be troubled by such things. The order that came down within the month was plain and, by the vein-house's own lights, moderate: every copy of the tracts of Doire chiar was to be brought to Muileann dhearg and given up, and no penalty would fall on any household that gave one up freely; only a household found still holding one after the season's end would answer for it.

The carriers who went from house to house collecting them, two custodians' clerks and a reeve's man riding with them for the look of the crown's own authority, found the giving up easier than any of the three had expected, and afterward not one of them could say for certain whether that ease came of fear or of indifference, since a copied sheet, however dear the teaching on it, cost little to write again once it had been read aloud in a hundred households and carried in as many memories. Some hundred sheets were brought to Muileann dhearg that season, more than the store-keeper's own reckoning had guessed the whole country held, and they were burned in the vein-house's own yard on a still morning with the smoke going up straight before the gathered custodians and, at the edge of the crowd, Eilidh Dhubh herself, who had walked the day and a half from Doire chiar to see it done rather than have it done and told to her after.

She said nothing while the sheets burned, and nothing was asked of her, and when the fire had burned down to a black smear on the yard's own stone she turned and walked the day and a half home again, and within the fortnight was teaching at her own gable as she had every evening for eleven years, a fresh sheet of the tracts already begun in her own hand before the ash at Muileann dhearg had properly cooled. No custodian of the seven ever gave an order against her person, then or after; whether that mercy came of the vein-house judging her too old, too poor, or too plainly harmless to trouble with a heavier hand, or of some custodian among the seven who had heard her once and could not afterward bring himself to move against her, is not told, and Eilidh herself, asked once by a pupil why she thought she went unpunished when her own words had gone to the fire, said only that a teaching burned once and taught again the next morning had already answered the question better than she could.

## IV. Of the Wood and the Road

The country beyond Doire chiar's own gable was, in those same years, quietly settling a handful of older claims that had nothing to do with a woman's teaching, and a teller who follows only the tracts would miss how much else, that generation, was being handed from one keeping to another without a blow struck over any of it. At {{place:burg:20}} the sacred forest, held in common since the Holy Age by more claimants than any one custody-book had ever troubled to name in full, passed by royal grant to the priests of the Hawk alone, {{year:III-0091}}, and the other claimants withdrew from the wood within the season, without protest that any scribe of the red hall thought worth setting down; why they went so quietly, whether from a weariness of sharing a single grove among too many hands or from some understanding struck away from any hall's hearing, is not told, and no teller since has been able to say more of it than that.

The druid schools, who had held no ground of their own since the Holy Age's own quarrels scattered the last of the great teaching-houses, took up in those same years the keeping of {{place:burg:355}}'s own holy wood, Flidais's wood, walking its bounds once in every year in a procession that began as no more than a handful of the schools' own masters and grew, within a decade, into the one rite the schools still kept open to any who cared to walk with them, {{year:III-0092}}. It was a smaller thing by far than the schools had once been, in the tellings that still reached Doire chiar from older men who remembered older tellings still, but it held, year on year, without any house of custody troubling to forbid it or any priest of the Hawk or the Stone troubling to claim the wood for a rival rite, and a schoolman who walked it in his old age was heard to say that a single rite kept faithfully a hundred years running was worth more, in the end, than a hundred rites let lapse in the first hard season.

On the drovers' road east of Muileann dhearg, where cattle had been walked over the hills to the mining towns for longer than any single family could claim to have begun the practice, the drovers of {{place:burg:256}} took, in the same span of years, to marking their own road with cairns, a stone set at every day's stage so that a beast lost in mist or a driver new to the trade might find the way again without asking, {{year:III-0093}}, the first roads on the whole island so marked, and within a generation every drover from the eastern hills to the mining towns knew the cairns of Àth chiar by name and by number, though not one of them, asked, could have said which drover had set the first stone or in what year.

None of these three things touched the vein-house's own quarrel with Doire chiar, and none of the three, set down together, made so much as a single line in the custody-book; but a country that grants a wood, opens a rite and marks a road, all in the span of a single generation and none of it fought over, is a country quietly rearranging what it keeps and how, and it was the same generation, and very often the same few years, that watched the tracts burned at Muileann dhearg and a fourth copy already begun before the ash had cooled.

## V. Of the Copy Kept at Doire chiar

One copy the carriers never found, kept whole from that year on in a hollow beam of Eilidh's own house, in her mother's own hand rather than her own, the single sheet the old woman had set down before her own death and given to no household but her granddaughter's, {{year:III-0095}}. Eilidh showed it to no one outside her own kin for the whole of her life afterward, saying that a teaching needed a living voice more than it needed a written one, and that the sheet in the beam was kept less for the country's sake than for her mother's, a thing to be shown, if it was ever shown at all, only when the living voice at last fell silent.

The years after the burning brought no second order and no second search, the vein-house's own attention turning, as a household's attention will, to nearer and louder troubles: a bad season on the eastern seam that same year, and beyond it the crown's own house shaking under three deaths inside a single generation, of which more is told below. Eilidh taught on at Doire chiar for twenty years more, growing old in the same house she had grown up in, her pupils growing old alongside her and their own children coming in turn to sit under the gable in the evenings, so that by the last years of her teaching she had buried more than one of the first who had ever stood to hear her as young men and women, and had grown, without ever asking for it, into the kind of teacher whose pupils no longer troubled to write down every word she said, trusting, as a village trusts a well it has drawn from all its life, that the water would go on rising however little thought anyone gave to where it came from.

She died at Doire chiar in her seventy-fifth year, {{year:III-0095}}, in the house where she had been born, with three pupils at her bed and a fourth sheet, unfinished, on the board beside her; she had been teaching, by every account of that last evening, within the hour of her death, a young woman of the village a point of the old argument she had made a thousand times before and never once, her pupils said afterward, in quite the same words twice. She was buried in the ground behind her own house rather than in any consecrated plot of the Hawk or the Stone or Macha's own priests, none of the three orders having ever claimed her nor she them, and her pupils raised over her no stone at all, judging, as she would herself have judged, that a teaching kept alive in the mouths of the living needed no monument the weather might in time wear blank. Coinneach Odhar, by then an old man himself, read the tracts at her own graveside from the copy he had carried these thirty years, and it is told that more of the village came to hear it read that one time than had ever come to hear Eilidh read it living, a thing he remarked on afterward with no bitterness that anyone could find in him, only a kind of tired wonder that a country waited so often until a well ran dry to learn how deep it had been.

## VI. Of Three Kings in a Single Generation

While Doire chiar kept its own quiet quarrel with the vein-house, the red hall at {{place:burg:19}} was losing, in the same span of years, three kings where an ordinary generation loses one. King Aonghas mac Uisdein, who had taken up the wood-watchers' rite of Macha in his own old age and set it, before he died, as the crown's own custom, died in his seventy-eighth year, {{year:III-0096}}, and the custody-book gave him, as it gave every crowned head before him, a single line: the name, the year, and no more, though the harbour scribes of the west coast were heard to say among themselves that a reign which had joined the Hawk to the crown's own household without a blow struck over it deserved a longer line than the book allowed it.

His son Aonghas, called Aonghas Òg though he came to the hall no young man, was crowned the same season at fifty years old, already grey and already, by his own admission to the men who crowned him, no longer certain his own knees would carry him through a rite so long as his father's had been. He kept the crown thirty-four years, a reign remembered afterward for little beyond its own length and its own quiet, the Hawk and the Stone having settled, in his father's day, into a peace neither order troubled to disturb again, and the vein-house's own quarrel with a woman of Doire chiar being, in the red hall's own reckoning, too small a thing to trouble a king's attention at all. He died in the winter of his eighty-fourth year, {{year:III-0096}}, the first king of the line to be carried to his grave by the rite of Macha rather than by any older custom of the hall, his own household standing at the yoke-poles of two horses he himself had ridden out to see brought up together every spring of his reign, and it is told that the wood-watchers who led that first royal burying under their own rite were themselves uncertain, until the day was nearly done, whether a king's body wanted the same ordering as any wood-watcher's own dead, and settled it, in the end, by giving him exactly that ordering and no more, judging that a rite which bent itself new for every man it buried was no rite worth keeping.

His son Iain mac Aonghais came to the hall at forty-nine years old, already a man with three sons of his own by two different wives, the eldest a young man grown, and reigned twelve years only, a reign the custody-book called short even while it was being lived, dying in his sixty-first year, {{year:III-0097}}, of no sickness any teller afterward could put a name to beyond the ordinary wearing-out of a man who had come late to a crown and found little joy in the wearing of it. The houses swore, within the same season, to his eldest son Tormod mac Iain, called Tormod Bàn for hair gone white before he was forty, crowned at thirty-six years old without a single house of the seven raising a question over the succession, a plainness the red hall's own scribes noted with something close to relief after a generation that had buried three kings in the time an ordinary house buried one.

## VII. Of the Second Son

The second of Iain mac Aonghais's three sons, a boy named Aonghas after his grandfather, was seven years old when his father was crowned and nineteen when his father died, old enough by then to stand at the yoke-poles himself at his brother Tormod's own crowning, though he stood, as a second son stands, a pace back from the hall's own centre and said nothing that any scribe thought worth the setting down. He had grown up, by his own later account given to his own daughter, in the half-attention a second son learns to expect and to make peace with: taught his letters alongside his elder brother but never quite so carefully, given a horse of his own but never the pick of the stable, loved well enough by a father grown old and tired in the office but never once mistaken, by that father or by anyone else in the hall, for the son the crown would fall to.

It was in the sixteenth year of his own life, {{year:III-0097}}, that his father took a second wife, his first having died some years before, and gave the household a third son, a boy called Dùghall for no uncle or grandfather that any of the household could afterward agree on, born at the red hall rather than at any lesser house, the first of the three brothers to be born under a crown his father already wore. Aonghas, by his own telling, held the child once in the first week of his life and thought little of him beyond the ordinary wonder any young man feels holding an infant too small yet to hold anything back, and could not have said, holding him, that the boy would grow to trouble his own brother Tormod's own reign more than either of the elder two sons ever did; of that trouble nothing is told here, since it belongs to years this telling has not yet reached.

Aonghas mac Iain took no office at his brother's crowning beyond the ordinary place of a king's own kin at the red hall, and within a few years had settled to the life of a younger son of the crown's own house: a modest hold some way from the capital, a wife of a lesser house of custody, and, in time, a daughter of his own, born at {{place:burg:166}} in the last years of this telling, whose own part in the country's history lay so far ahead of anything Aonghas himself could have guessed at, holding her as an infant, that no teller writing of these years is free to say a word of it here.

## VIII. Of the Byre at the Halfway Stage

In those same years the central mining road, which carried the ore and the coal of the eastern hills down toward the capital and carried, in the other direction, the grain and salt the mines themselves could not grow or draw, had long depended for its winter traffic on a single road-house at its halfway stage, a place the ox-drivers had for two generations simply called the great house, kept since before living memory by whichever family held the ground it stood on. In {{year:III-0098}} its keeper, a careful man named Iain mac Mhurchaidh who had held the house from his own father and meant, by every sign he gave, to hold it a good deal longer yet, judged that a single byre no longer answered the traffic the road now carried, the ox-teams of the mountain having grown, over his own father's lifetime and his, from a handful of wagons in a hard season to a train that some winters filled the yard to its gate before the first snow had properly settled.

He raised a second byre that same year against the far wall of the yard, of the same undressed stone as the first, and began, the winter it was finished, a book of his own: which team lodged which night, whose ox went lame and was nursed back on the house's own fodder, whose driver owed a debt of lodging still unpaid come spring, kept in a hand that grew steadily surer through that first winter as he found the shape the keeping wanted. It was no grand thing, the book, no larger a matter to the country than any careful man's private ledger, and no scribe of the red hall ever asked to see it nor any custodian of the seven houses troubled to learn it existed; but the ox-drivers who passed that way, winter on winter, came to trust the book's own memory better than their own, a driver more than once settling an argument over whose ox had lodged where the previous season by asking the keeper to look it up rather than trusting either man's own account, and in this small way the great house at the halfway stage became, without any of the crown's own notice, the one place on the whole central road where a plain fact, once written down, was believed over a plain man's word.

The worst of that winter's snow closed the road for the better part of three weeks, and the yard held, at the height of it, eleven teams and their drivers crowded into the two byres and whatever floor space the house could spare, fed on stores Iain mac Mhurchaidh had laid in against exactly such a season and had, by his own admission afterward, never quite believed he would need in full. He kept them fed, and kept his book through every night of it besides, by the same fire the drivers themselves sat around telling whatever news the road still carried before the snow had closed it: of a woman at Doire chiar not two years dead whose sayings a driver's own cousin still kept by heart; of a young king crowned that same season without dispute; of a second son of the crown's own house said to be settling, quietly and without complaint, into a life no history would trouble to write down. The fire burned low before the last of them slept, and Iain mac Mhurchaidh, closing his book for the night, judged, as he judged every winter after, that a country's true weight was carried less by what the red hall's own scribes thought fit to set in the custody-book than by the small, careful, unwitnessed keeping of a hundred houses like his own, each holding its own little ledger against the loss the years would otherwise make of it.
