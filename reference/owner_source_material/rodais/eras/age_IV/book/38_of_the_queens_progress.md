# Of the Queen's Progress

*LE 1,750 – 1,799*

## I. Of the Sea-Wall of Cuan dhearg

At {{place:burg:190}} the sea had come twice now over the same low street, and the second time, {{date:III-0229}}, it had put the water to the thresholds of An Gàire Dubh and left a rime of salt on the door-boards that no scrubbing got out before the next tide freshened it again. The first flood the town had answered by moving the street back a length of two houses and calling the matter closed; the second it answered otherwise. The harbour was a small one, fifty households at the most generous count, drying nets and curing fish and sending a little salt fish inland by cart, and a town that size does not build in stone for the pleasure of it. But the elders who had watched two floods in one lifetime, when their own fathers had watched none, judged that the sea was not moving the street back a second time, and set the whole harbour, from the oldest hand to the youngest able to carry a basket, to hauling stone up from the point for a wall.

Gormshuil nic Thormoid, then nine-and-thirty years old and no more than her father's daughter in the eyes of most who did not sit at his board, rode down to see it in its second summer of building, for her father the king no longer rode so far and had asked her to carry his eye where his own legs would not take him. The wall rose in courses no higher than a man's shoulder that summer, dressed stone laid dry at the base and mortared above, and the whole harbour worked it in shifts between the tides, so that the masons and the net-menders and the children set to carrying water for the mortar all learned, before the season was out, exactly how many hours the ebb gave them and no more. She stood on the half-built wall with the harbour-master, a woman with salt worked so deep into the creases of her hands that no washing showed them clean, and asked her plainly whether the wall would hold the third flood if a third flood came. The harbour-master said that no one living could promise her that, since the sea had never troubled to explain either of the first two floods to anyone. A wall a man could stand behind was worth more than a street he could only run from, she said, and the harbour meant to stand behind this one. She added, when Gormshuil pressed her further, that the town had buried the old boundary stones of the moved street rather than break them up, on the chance that some later hand might want to know how far the water had once come; Gormshuil thought this a harbour-master's own kind of custody-book, kept in stone instead of hide, and said so. The harbour-master allowed that she had never thought of it in those words, but did not deny them either. Gormshuil found she had no better answer to bring her father than the wall's own building, and rode home and gave him the whole of it, word for word, and the king, who by then answered most things that reached him more slowly than the shires liked, laughed once, short and dry, and said that it was the soundest counsel to come up from the coast in a year.

## II. Of the House Reopened at Dùn thais

Taigh na Deasbaid at {{place:burg:77}} had stood shut behind its own wall for the better part of a generation, since the last argument to be heard in it had turned, as such arguments in that house always turned in the end, toward the question Clàr nam Bliadhnachan had first set and no one since had been let ask again in the open: why the heads of Na Seachd Taighean lived out their years a little longer than the tenants who worked their coal-right for them. It was reopened, {{date:III-0230}}, on the one condition that had closed it, restated and accepted afresh: the question of longer lives would not be raised inside its walls, this time or any time after. The druids of the schools who had petitioned longest for the doors to be unbarred agreed to the condition without a murmur that reached the crown's own ears, and once inside took up instead the argument of the vein, whether Leabhar a' Chùraim's measure of the handful answered rightly to what the coal gave out, a question with edges sharp enough to fill a house of disputation for as long as anyone cared to keep arguing it and answer nothing that touched the older, forbidden one.

Gormshuil, riding to Dùn thais for the reopening at her father's side, asked the eldest of the schools' druids, an old man leaning on a stick cut from the school's own holy wood, whether he thought the coal question would satisfy the house as long as the other one had. He said that a house built to argue will always find something to argue, and that the schools had never once, in all the generations the record kept of them, run short of a subject; it was answers, not subjects, that a house of disputation was built to go without. Gormshuil thought this a strange thing to hear said with such evident contentment, and said so. The old druid told her that a woman who had not yet sat a lifetime in an argument that could not be closed had no cause yet to find it strange, and that she might ask him again when she had.

## III. Of the Death of the King

Tormod mac Ruairidh had held An Talla Dearg at {{place:burg:19}} for more than fifty years by the time his own legs failed him past riding even the short road to Taigh na Fèithe, and in the last of those years he governed from a chair set by the hearth, hearing what the shires sent up and answering it in a voice that had grown no less certain for growing quieter. He died {{date:III-0231}}, six-and-eighty years old, the longest-lived king Leabhar a' Chùraim had entered since Clàr nam Bliadhnachan first began to measure such things and find them wanting less than they once had. Gormshuil sat with him the last night, in a room kept warmer than the rest of the hall by a fire built up past what the season strictly wanted, and he spoke to her in the broken way of a man saving his strength between sentences rather than spending it on any one of them whole. He told her, near the end and with no great weight laid on the words, that he had built bridges over every ford he had ever had reason to be afraid of and had never once, in fifty years, found the courage to ask Taigh na Fèithe's own custodians a question Taigh na Deasbaid was forbidden to ask on his behalf. She asked him what the question was. He said that she would find it herself soon enough, being his daughter, and that finding it was the easier half of the trouble in any case; the harder half, he said, closing his eyes on it, was living long enough afterward to be satisfied with whatever answer she was given, and that he did not know, of the whole fifty years behind him, a single custodian who had managed that.

She was crowned in An Talla Dearg a fortnight after, one-and-fifty years old herself. The custodians of Na Seachd Taighean stood for her in the order Clachan a' Chrannchuir had long since fixed for such standings, and the priest of Macha who made the crowning said the same words over her that had been said over her father and his mother before him, word for word as Leabhar a' Chùraim kept them, so that no one in the hall that day could have named the year the words were first spoken without going to Leabhar a' Chùraim to be sure of it.

## IV. Of the Progress and the Book of Grievances

Where her father had governed from a chair by his own hearth, Gormshuil began, the year after her crowning, {{date:III-0232}}, to do what no ruler of the island had done since Sìleas Òg's own round two centuries and more before her: she rode out from Cathair dhearg to every shire seat in turn, and kept riding it, season on season, until the shires came to expect her the way they expected the winter burning, a thing that returned. At each seat her clerks opened Leabhar nan Gearan and wrote down, in the shire's own words as near as a clerk's hand could catch them, whatever grievance the seat's own reeve or a plain householder chose to bring before the queen in person: a boundary stone moved in the night, a firlot short-measured, a bridge toll raised past its charter, a coal-right disputed between two houses of the same blood. Gormshuil heard every one of them out, asked her own questions where a question seemed wanted, and answered none of them in writing before she rode on to the next seat.

A householder of {{place:burg:305}}, at one of the earliest seats of that first round, brought before the queen a boundary stone she said her neighbour had shifted a full arm's length into her own barley in the dark of the same Samhnas night three years running, always shifted back before any reeve could be fetched to see it moved. The neighbour, standing his own ground not ten feet off, said the stone had never once moved under his own hand and that a woman who watched her boundary by moonlight three Samhnas nights running had more reason to doubt her own eyes than his honesty. Gormshuil's clerk set both accounts down as each had given them, word for word, and Gormshuil herself measured the disputed ground with her own stride before she rode on, and told the two of them plainly that she came to write the ground down, not to judge it, so that the next time either side troubled to look, the writing would at least know exactly how far an arm's length of barley actually ran.

It became, over the years of her riding, the one thing every shire agreed to say of her, whether they judged it praise or plain complaint: that the queen wrote everything down and settled nothing on the page. A reeve of one of the smaller seats, more plain-spoken than most, asked her outright, the third or fourth year of her round, what the writing was for if no answer ever followed it. Gormshuil told him that a grievance written down in the shire's own words was at least a true grievance, and that an answer written down too quickly, before the queen had ridden the whole round and heard how the same trouble sounded in six other shires' mouths, was more often a false ease than a true one. The reeve said that this might satisfy a queen who meant to ride the round again next year, and asked, not quite as a question, whether she did. Gormshuil said that she did, and rode on, and kept riding it for as long as her own legs would carry her, which turned out to be longer than most at that first seat had wagered.

Fifteen years into the round, in a summer when her company had grown large enough that a single road-house strained to feed it, she came to {{place:marker:6}} on the eastern road, and its keeper, a woman named Peigi nic Dhonnchaidh, {{date:III-0234}} set pan-fried spinach and black wine before the queen's whole train and fed every rider and every horse-boy besides, though her own charter allowed her stores for nothing near so great a company. Gormshuil, hearing after the meal what it had cost the house to feed her round unwarned, forgave Peigi her road dues for the year that followed, and told her, when Peigi tried to protest that she had asked for no such thing, that a house which fed the crown's whole progress without being asked to had earned more than a year's dues forgiven and would have to be content with what the crown could actually give her. Peigi's black wine was spoken of at every road-house between there and Cathair dhearg for longer than the dues she was spared, though Leabhar a' Chùraim, being a custody-book, kept the dues and let the wine go untold beyond that.

## V. Of the Silver Petition Refused

Clann Choinnich had asked once before, in the reign of a queen long before her, for leave to burn the coal beyond the handful's own measure to feed the silver smelt at Muileann chiar, and had been refused by custodians who judged the vein's own long custom worth more than one house's silver. They asked a second time in Gormshuil's reign, {{date:III-0233}}, and this time three of Na Seachd Taighean stood behind them at Taigh na Fèithe board, more than had ever stood behind the asking before, so that the matter came, for the first time in Leabhar a' Chùraim's memory of it, within a single voice of passing.

The voice that held it back was Aonghas mac Lachlainn's, of Clann Lachlainn, who had ridden to Taigh na Fèithe that season, by every account that reached Gormshuil afterward, meaning to vote with the three houses and not against them. He changed his vote at the board itself, between the asking read aloud and the custodians' hands raised to answer it, and gave no reason for the changing beyond the bare word that he had changed his mind. Leabhar a' Chùraim's clerk wrote his name down as the voice that refused the petition, plainly, as the book required; he did not write, because Aonghas would not tell him, what had turned the vote in the short space between the reading and the raising of the hands.

Gormshuil, who sat the board that day as she sat every board Taigh na Fèithe held, asked Aonghas afterward, in a quieter corner of the hall than the board itself, whether he meant to say why he had turned. He told her, with a courtesy that gave nothing away under it, that a man's reason for a single hand raised at a single board was his own to keep, the same as any other man's, and that a queen who pressed every custodian for the private reason behind every private vote would find herself, before long, with a board full of men who voted as she wished rather than as they judged, which he did not think she wanted of him any more than he wanted it of himself. Gormshuil found she could not fault the answer, though it left her no wiser than she had been before the asking, and she let the matter lie, as Leabhar a' Chùraim let it lie, with his name written and his reason not.

## VI. Of the Last Count of Hands

Taigh na Fèithe counted, {{date:III-0235}}, the households across the whole island that still held the right to the coal, as it had counted them at long intervals since the first count made before the fleet sailed, and it was the last such count the age would keep. The tally came in fewer than any count before it, households having failed into other households or into no households at all across the generations since the last reckoning, and yet larger than any count before it too, for the households that remained held, between them, a greater weight of the coal-right than households of that number had ever held; Leabhar a' Chùraim's own clerk noted, in the dry hand the book had kept since Donnchadh's day, that three of Na Seachd Taighean now held more than half the whole right among themselves.

One house of the four smaller ones, hearing its own share of the right read out lower than it had stood at the count before, asked whether a house that held too little coal-right to keep its own hearth measure through a hard winter might be granted its neighbour's unused share rather than buy it, as Leabhar a' Chùraim allowed in years Taigh na Fèithe judged hard enough to warrant it. The custodians debated the asking a full afternoon and granted it in the end, though more than one voice at the board noted, in words the clerk set down as spoken and not softened, that a house asking such a grant twice running was a house whose trouble Taigh na Fèithe could ease once but not mend for good.

Gormshuil read the count at Taigh na Fèithe board with the custodians ranged along it as they had been ranged for Athchuingean an Airgid, and asked the clerk who had cast it up whether the coal had grown scarcer to account for so much of it falling into so few hands. The clerk said that the vein gave out what it had always given out, no more scarce and no less, and that it was the households, not the coal, that had grown fewer; three houses holding half the right meant only that the other four had, between them, less than half a right to hold, which was a different kind of scarcity than the coal's own, and one Taigh na Fèithe's count could show her plainly enough without being able to say what ought to be done about it. Gormshuil thanked him for the plainness and let the count stand in the book as it was cast, unaltered and unanswered, the way her own progress left every shire's grievance.

## VII. Of the Timber Out of the Mist

In the last years of her reign the fishers of {{place:burg:143}}, working the empty water off their own headland where Faire a' Chinn had lapsed seventeen centuries before, found a worked timber cast up on the strand, {{date:III-0236}}, longer by half again than any tree that had ever grown on the island and fastened along its length with nails of a make no smith on Dia-thìr had ever forged or seen forged. The fishers who found it stood over it a long while before any of them would put a hand to it, and it was the eldest among them, a woman who had fished that water sixty years, who said at last that a thing so plainly worked by hands could not have grown in the sea any more than a boat could, and that hands which forged nails unlike any island nail had not, in all likelihood, forged them on the island.

They carried it, by cart and then by boat the last stretch of the way, to {{place:burg:489}} and laid it before Manannan's priests on the same quay where Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh was kept each year, for if any order on the island could say what a worked thing out of the eastern mist might be, the priests who prayed the sea shut at every tide were judged the likeliest to know. The priests looked at it a long while too, turned it over, ran their hands along the nails, and said among themselves, in words the fishers standing by did not catch all of, that a timber which came from beyond the mist was better answered by fire than by argument. They burned it on the quay before the fishers' own eyes, the flames running strange colours along the metal of the nails before the nails themselves melted and were lost in the ash, and gave the fishers no clearer word than that of what it had been or where, exactly, beyond the mist, it had come from.

## VIII. Of the Winter Burning She Did Not Come Down From

Gormshuil went up to Taigh na Fèithe at {{place:burg:491}} for the winter burning, as she had gone up for every winter burning of her reign since her crowning thirty years before, riding the road herself rather than sending a custodian in her place, for she had told more than one custodian over those thirty years that a queen who never climbed the coal-road herself had no business asking Taigh na Fèithe to answer to her. She was eighty years old that winter, the oldest ruler Leabhar a' Chùraim had entered crowned and still reigning since long before Clàr nam Bliadhnachan, and the riders who went up with her noted nothing in her that winter that they had not noted in a dozen winters before it: a queen a little slower on the stair than she had been, taking the custodians' hands more readily than she once had to steady her climb, but asking the same plain questions of the same counted galleries she had always asked. The road up was iced that year past what the coal-carts liked, and the riders walked their horses the last steep mile rather than trust them at a trot, Gormshuil walking her own the same as the rest of them though more than one rider offered to lead it for her; she told them, as she had told them most winters, that a queen who let another hand lead her horse up the one road she still rode every year might as well let another hand rule from the chair at the bottom of it too.

She did not come down to Cathair dhearg again. Leabhar a' Chùraim records the winter burning kept, the coal measured, the handful cut as the law required; of the queen who had gone up with the riders and did not ride back down the same road she had climbed, nothing more is told. The custodians of Na Seachd Taighean, meeting at An Talla Dearg the following year in the place the crown's own chair had always stood empty for her progress and never before for any other cause, sent their word up the coal-road to Taigh na Fèithe by the same riders who had carried the crown's business all Gormshuil's reign, and Taigh na Fèithe sent back no word that unsettled the custodians' sitting, and the custodians sat on.

They wrote their letters that year and the year after in the crown's own hand, sealed with Taigh na Fèithe's own trellis and hollow stone as every crown letter had been sealed since Dòmhnall Ruadh joined the two, and above the seal, where a queen's name had stood on every such letter since Leabhar a' Chùraim's oldest leaves, they set no name at all, neither Gormshuil's own nor any other's, and no clerk of Taigh na Fèithe or An Talla Dearg was ever asked, in writing or otherwise, to explain the blank space where a name should have stood.

## IX. Of the Last Lamp at Seann Skell

Oidhche nan Lòchran at {{place:burg:489}} had been kept, in Gormshuil's own girlhood, by every house along the waterfront that could spare a rush-light and a window to set it in; by the last years of her reign it was kept by fewer houses each year that the harbour scribe troubled to count, until at last, {{date:III-0237}}, none of the waterfront's own households set a lamp at all. It was an old woman of the town, Ealasaid nic Iain, who had kept the custom in her own family's window since before her husband died and saw no reason the town's own forgetting should end her keeping it, who set the one lamp that burned on the waterfront that year, in the harbour-house window where Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh was kept each Là an Diosail on the stones below. The harbour scribe who noted it in Rolla a' Chalaidh, the same office that had once counted every lamp along the whole quay, wrote plainly that the lamp burning that year was Ealasaid's own and not the town's, and let the sentence stand without further word, the way the crown's own letters had learned, across these same years, to let a blank space stand where a fuller one no longer had anything true to fill it with.
