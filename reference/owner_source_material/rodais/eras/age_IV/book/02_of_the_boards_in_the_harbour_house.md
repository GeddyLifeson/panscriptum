# Of the Boards in the Harbour-House

*LE 2*

## I. Of Taigh a' Chalaidh

The harbour-house of Seann Skell stood at the head of the west quay, where the Abhainn naomh widens into its river pool before it goes down to the sea. It was a long low house of grey stone with a turf roof, older than anyone could say, and it had a single door facing the water and a single window facing the town, so that whoever sat inside could see both the boats and the people who owned them. The town called it Taigh a' Chalaidh, the harbour-house, and nothing else, as though there were no other harbour in the world.

In it sat the harbour scribe, and in the second year of the age the harbour scribe was Calum mac Nèill.

He was a spare, stooped man of thirty-odd with ink in the cracks of his fingers and a habit of humming through his nose while he worked, which his wife said would drive her into the river. His father had been harbour scribe before him, and his father's mother before that. The office was not a great one. He kept the rolls of the harbour: which boat lay at which berth, who owed dues and who had paid them, what came in and what went out, fish and salt and seal-oil, hides from up the river, barley down it. He wrote on boards. The custody-book at the vein-house was kept on good skin, and the queen's scribe at Cathair dhearg wrote on skin; but the harbour of Seann Skell had never had so much money that it could write its daily business on anything better than planed boards of alder, which could be scraped and written again, and hung on pegs along the walls in rows.

Calum liked the boards. He liked the sound of the knife cutting the letters, a small dry sound, and the curl of the shaving, and the way a board held what was cut into it. A skin could be scraped clean and no one the wiser. A cut in a board showed.

He was not a man who thought much about the sea. The sea was where the boats went, and he wrote down when they came back.

## II. Of the Opening of the Roll

The talk of the far coast had been going on in Seann Skell for a year and a half, as it had been going on everywhere in the west, when the first household came into the harbour-house and asked to be written down.

It was in the moon of Pailteach, the moon of plenty, at the end of harvest. The barley was in and the herring had not yet come, and the town was idle and restless. A fisher of Inis àrsaidh came in off the quay with his wife behind him, and stood before Calum's board with his cap in his hand, and said that he wished his household entered.

"Entered for what?" said Calum. He had a board of harbour dues on his knee and was adding it up.

"For going," said the fisher. "When the ships go."

Calum put the dues board down. "There are no ships," he said.

"There will be," said the fisher's wife. She was a sturdy woman with forearms like a baker's, and she looked at Calum as though he were being slow on purpose. "Everybody says. And when they go, there'll be a hundred wanting to go on them, and they'll want to know who asked first. So we want it written. Now. Who asked first. You write the harbour, don't you? This is harbour."

Calum looked at them for a long moment. He thought of telling them to go home. He thought of the queen at Cathair dhearg, who had said nothing all this while, and of the custodians at the vein-house, who had said nothing either; and it occurred to him that if he sent these two away, they would go to some other scribe, or to no scribe, and the thing would be written badly or not at all. And a harbour scribe of Seann Skell did not like a thing to be written badly.

So he got up and took down a new board of alder from the rack, and planed it smooth, and cut at the head of it, in his best letters:

*Those who mean to go.*

Then he sat down again and asked the fisher his name.

He wrote it on {{date:III-0004}}. That board was the first of the roll, which the custody-book afterward called Rolla nan Deònach, the roll of the willing, and it hung on the wall of the harbour-house at Seann Skell for longer than anyone then living would live.

## III. Of the Rule of the Whole Household

He wrote more than the fisher's name. That was the first thing he decided, and he decided it before he had thought about it, with his knife already in the wood.

"Who is your household?" he said.

The fisher looked puzzled. "Me. Her. The two boys. My mother, that lives with us."

"Does your mother mean to go?"

The fisher and his wife looked at each other. "We've not asked her," said the wife.

"Then go and ask her," said Calum, "and come back, and I'll write you all, or none of you."

They stared at him. The fisher said, a little hotly, that he did not see what his mother had to do with it. Calum put down his knife and explained it, slowly, as he would have explained a harbour due, and in the explaining he found out what he thought.

The coal was the reason. Every hearth on the island that held the right to the coal was entered by the vein-house in the custody-book, and every season the handful was measured out to the hearth, the whole house that sat round one fire, and never to a single man or woman. A hearth was one thing in the custody-book's eyes. If half of a household went and half stayed, what then? Two hearths where there had been one, each with half a right? Or one cold half-hearth and the handful measured to nobody? The custodians would not stand for it. And a household, Calum said, was a hearth to more than the custodians. A boy did not go over the sea and leave his grandmother by the fire. Or if he did, he should have to stand in the harbour-house and say so, before the whole town, with his grandmother beside him.

"So you'll write us whole," said the fisher's wife slowly, "or not at all."

"I'll write you whole," said Calum, "or not at all."

She thought about that. Then she nodded, once, as if she had been haggling over fish and had got a fair price. "That's right," she said. "That's how it should be." And she took her husband by the arm and went to ask his mother.

They came back the next morning, all five of them, the old woman on a stick at the rear, and stood in a row before the board while Calum cut their names, one under another, joined by a line down the left side that he cut last to show they were one household. The old woman watched the knife the whole time. When he had finished she said, "Is that us, then?" and he said it was, and she touched the board with one finger, very lightly, where her name was, and went out without another word.

That was how the roll came to be written by household and never by single name. The tellers have made a great deal of it since, and said that going was a thing a family did together or not at all. So it was. But it began with a scribe who did not wish to argue with the vein-house over half a hearth.

## IV. Of Those Who Came

After the first household, the harbour-house was never empty.

They came in off the quay all through the autumn: fishers with tar on their hands and salt in the creases of their faces, who had been out past the headlands in weather and thought they knew what the sea was; boat-wrights from the strand below the town; net-makers, rope-makers, a cooper. They came down the Abhainn naomh from the farms up the river in carts, the farm wives with a child on the hip and another at the skirt, and stood in the doorway blinking at the dark after the bright river. A shepherd came from the hills behind the town with his dog, and the dog lay down under the board and would not be moved. A young woman came alone, with no household but herself, and Calum had to decide whether one was a household; he decided that it was.

They asked him questions he could not answer. Where would they go? When? In what? Who would lead them? How many would there be? Would the queen send them, or forbid them? Would the coal go with them? He said, over and over, that he did not know, that he was only the harbour scribe, that he wrote down the harbour. They looked at him as if he were keeping something back. Some of them brought him gifts, a string of fish, a pot of honey, as if a gift would get them a better place on the board. He gave the gifts back, which offended them more than keeping them would have done.

By the moon of Damhrach there were four boards on the wall.

And they went away again. That was the part nobody had expected, and it was the part that stayed with Calum longest.

## V. Of the First Striking-Out

The first to strike out was a net-maker of the town, a man of forty called Iain, whom Calum had known all his life. He had entered his household in the first week, himself and his wife and three children, in a loud cheerful voice, and stood talking in the doorway afterward about the fish they would take off the far coast, as big as calves.

He came back before the moon was out.

Late in the day, when the harbour-house was empty, he came in and stood before the board with his face grey. He did not say anything for a while. Then he said, "Strike us out."

Calum looked up at him. "What's happened?"

"Nothing's happened." Iain rubbed his face with both hands. "Nothing. I woke up in the night and lay there, and I thought: the sea. That's all. I thought of the sea at night, with no land. And I couldn't breathe, Calum. I couldn't get my breath. My wife held me like a child. Strike us out."

Calum took down the board, and found the name, and took his knife and cut it through: one long stroke through the middle of each letter, from the first letter of Iain's name to the last of his youngest child's, and then the line down the left side that joined them, cut across at the top and the bottom.

Iain watched him do it. When it was done he said, low: "Can't you scrape it off? So nobody knows?"

Calum turned the board so that the net-maker could see it. The names were still there, every letter, with the stroke through them.

"No," he said. "I can't."

"You scrape the dues boards. I've seen you."

"When a due is paid, I scrape it, because it's done and no one needs it. This isn't done." Calum hung the board back on its peg. He did not know, until he said it, that he believed it. "You meant to go, Iain. That was true. And now you don't, and that's true too. The board will say both. If I scrape it off, it'll only say the second, and that's half a lie."

Iain stood there a moment longer. Then he laughed, not happily, and said, "You're a hard man for a scribe," and went out.

He came back the next spring and entered his household again. By midsummer he had struck it out again. Calum cut the second stroke through the second entry without a word, and the net-maker did not ask him to scrape it.

## VI. Of the Widow of the Strand

There were others. There was the young couple from up the river who entered, and struck out when the wife found she was with child, and entered again when the child was born, and struck out again when it sickened. A pair of brothers, rope-makers, who entered together and then quarrelled and came in separately on the same day, one to strike and one to stay, and had to be told that they were one household and must agree or be struck together; they went out shouting at each other, and came back together at dusk, and struck. There was the shepherd, whose dog died that winter, who came in and struck out and said it would not be right to go without the dog.

And there was the widow of the strand, whose coming and going ran through the whole of that first year and beyond it.

Her name is not on any board that survives, so she will not be named here. She was a boat-wright's widow, a thin woman with white hair who lived in a hut on the strand below the town among the sawpits and the half-built coasters, and who came up to the harbour-house in the moon of Samhnas, in the first rain, to be entered, alone. Calum wrote her. She came back in the moon of Dubhlas, in the dark of the year, and asked to be struck. He struck her. She came back in the moon of Faolach, in the wolf-storms, and asked to be entered again. He looked at her a long while, and then wrote her again, on a new line, below the first entry with its stroke through it.

In the moon of Gearras, after Là Fhèill Brìde, she came again.

"Strike me," she said.

He took the knife. Before he cut, he said, "May I ask why?"

She looked at the board, at her own name written twice and struck once. "My husband's in the ground behind the strand," she said. "Every time I think of going, I think of him there and nobody to cut the grass on him. And every time I think of staying, I think of him there and me on the strand looking at the water all the rest of my days, and I think, he'd have gone. He'd have gone the first day. He'd have been in here before the fisher." She was quiet. "So I go back and forth. Strike me."

He struck her. And she did not come back, that spring or ever; and when the harbour scribe of a later year took down the boards to count them, he found the widow's name written three times and struck three times, one under another, like a ladder.

## VII. Of the Harbour-House at Night

The roll changed the harbour-house, and it changed the town's use of it.

It had been a place where people came to pay their dues and quarrel about berths. Now it was a place where people came to say the most important thing they had ever said, in front of the harbour scribe and whoever else happened to be standing in the doorway. It became something between a court and a shrine. People came in who had no business with the roll at all, only to look at the boards, to read the names, to see who was going and who had changed his mind. Old men sat on the bench by the door all day and watched. Children dared each other to touch the boards. Once a woman came in and read the whole roll through, board by board, very slowly, and then went and sat on the quay and wept for an hour, and no one ever found out whose name she had been looking for.

People came at night, too. The ones who could not say it by day came after dark, when the quay was empty. So Calum began to keep a lamp burning in the harbour-house late, long after the harbour's own business was done, and to sit up by it with a board on his knee, in case anyone came. His wife complained of the cost of the oil. He paid for it out of his own pocket, and went on doing it.

And they came. A man who had fought his father for a month over it, and won, and could not bear to see his father's face while he said it. A girl who was not supposed to be out of her mother's house. A custodian's servant, sent secretly by his master to read what the roll held and count it for the vein-house, who confessed as much at once, and whom Calum let read, because the roll was not a secret.

## VIII. Of the Waiting for a Word

All that autumn the town waited for the queen to speak, and she did not.

It was the first thing anyone asked a rider off the river road: had the queen said anything? And the rider always said no, and the asker always looked as though he had been cheated of something he was owed. A great many people in Seann Skell, it turned out, had been sure that the crown would decide it for them one way or the other. Some hoped she would forbid it, so that they could be angry with her and stay. Some hoped she would command it, so that they could go and blame her. Most, Calum thought, hoped she would do something, anything, so that the thing would be taken out of their own hands and they could sleep.

The talk on the bench by the door ran every way. The queen was against it and waiting for it to die down. The queen was for it and waiting for the custodians. The queen's sister had been seen at Cuan shean asking about boats, so the queen must mean to send her. The queen had quarrelled with her sister over it and they no longer spoke. An old man who had once carried a message to the red hall and had never since let anyone forget it said that the queen was a deep one, like her mother, and would say nothing until the last moment and then say exactly the right thing; and everyone on the bench agreed with him, and none of them could say what the right thing would be.

The custodian's servant who had come to read the roll by night came again in the moon of Damhrach, and this time he brought a question from his master. How many households on the boards held the right to the coal? Calum did not know. He had not asked. The harbour roll was not the custody-book, and a fisher did not tell the harbour scribe whether his hearth took the handful. The servant went away with his question unanswered, and Calum sat a long while afterward looking at the boards, and understood for the first time that somewhere up in the mountain country, sixty miles off, men who had never set foot on the quay were counting his roll against their own, and that what they found in it would matter to them far more than it mattered to him.

It frightened him a little. He had cut the first board because a fisher's wife had told him it was harbour business, and she had been right. Now it was the vein-house's business too, and the red hall's; and he had the uneasy sense of a man who has laid a plank across a ditch for his own use and turns round to find a cart coming over it.

He did not close the boards. But he began to write more carefully, and to ask of each household, before he cut, whether its hearth took the handful; and he put a small mark beside those that did, a notch like a flame, so that when the question came again he could answer it.

## IX. Of Dòmhnall mac Chaluim and the Closing of the Boards

Calum's son Dòmhnall was his clerk that year, a quiet, serious boy of fourteen who was learning the knife. He watched the roll grow and shrink and grow again through that first autumn, and one night in the moon of Damhrach, by the late lamp he said what he had been thinking.

"You should close it."

Calum looked up.

"The roll," said Dòmhnall. "Close the boards. Nobody's asked you to keep them. The queen hasn't. The vein-house hasn't. You started it yourself, because a fisher came in, and now it's eating the harbour-house. You can't get the dues done for people in the doorway. Half the town's in here every day crying. It isn't harbour business. Close it and tell them to go to the queen."

Calum thought about it honestly. His son was right on every count but one.

"The queen hasn't said close it," he said. "Nor has she said open it. She's said nothing at all. And while she says nothing, who is to say whether a man may go or not? Not me. I'm the harbour scribe. I write the harbour." He laid his hand flat on the board on his knee. "These people have a right to have it written, what they mean. If I shut the boards, I've said no for them. And I'm not the one to say no."

"Then who is?"

"Nobody, maybe." Calum was quiet for a while. "Maybe that's the answer. Maybe there's no one who can say no."

Dòmhnall did not argue. But when he went to bed he looked back from the door, and saw his father still sitting by the lamp, not writing anything, looking at the boards on the wall.

## X. Of the Question His Wife Asked

Calum's wife was a practical woman named Seonag, who kept geese and sold their eggs on the quay, and she had watched the roll all winter with a face that told him nothing. She did not ask him about it and he did not tell her. They talked about the geese and the price of oil and Dòmhnall's knife-hand, which was coming along.

Then, the same night, lying in the dark in the box-bed with the wind going over the roof, she said: "Would you go?"

He lay still. He could hear the river running past the end of the quay, and a loose board somewhere on the harbour-house knocking.

"The boards need a keeper," he said.

She did not answer that. After a while she turned over, away from him, toward the wall. He thought she had gone to sleep. Then she said, into the dark, quite quietly: "That's not what I asked. And that's the answer anyway, isn't it. You want to."

He did not say anything. There was nothing he could say that would not be a lie, and he had spent the whole winter refusing to scrape a lie onto a board.

In the morning he went down to the harbour-house as usual, and lit the lamp, though it was daylight, and sat down by it. And the first to come in that day was a boat-wright of the strand, a lean dark man called Alasdair mac Iain, who did not come to be entered. He came to read the roll. He read every board on the wall, slowly, moving his lips, and then he stood in the middle of the floor and said to no one in particular, "There's not a boat on this island that could carry the half of them."

"No," said Calum.

"Then somebody had better build one," said Alasdair, and went out.
