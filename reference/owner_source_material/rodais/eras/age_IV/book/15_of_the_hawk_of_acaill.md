# Of the Hawk of Acaill

*LE 215 – 251*

## I. Of the Glove at Cnoc bheag

At {{place:burg:20}}, up the river from the coast where the dawn-hawk rite of the Holy Age had been kept without a break since before the fleet, the mews stood on the shoulder of the hill above the shrine, and the keeping of it had passed, as far back as anyone at Cnoc bheag could say, from father to child and never once to a stranger. Artair had kept it since before his own daughter was born, and by the time this telling opens he was five-and-sixty years old and had kept it the better part of his life, his hands grown too stiff in the cold mornings to knot a jess without help and his knees no longer equal to the climb from the shrine to the mews block before first light. His daughter Gormshuil climbed it for him now, most mornings, and had done since she was a girl old enough to carry a hawk on her own wrist without her arm shaking, though it was still Artair, sitting on the bench below with a blanket over his knees, who called up which bird was to fly and in what order, as his own father had called it up to him.

The shrine below the mews was a plain thing, a ring of standing stones no higher than a man's shoulder about a bare patch of turf worn down to the earth by feet, with a single stone taller than the rest set at the eastern edge for the sun to clear before the flying began. It drew, in the ordinary way of things, the households of the hill and the river-bend below it, coal-cutters and their wives come up before the day's work to stand in the cold and watch a bird cross the grey sky and come back to a glove, and go down again to their fires no wiser for it than they had come, save that a morning begun at the shrine was reckoned, by long custom, a morning less likely to end badly. Gormshuil had known most of those faces her whole life, and could have named, without troubling to think, which households sent their eldest daughter to watch and which sent a servant in their place, and what each family's coal-right at the vein-house came to that season, for the talk on the shrine's ledge before the flying ran as often to the size of the next handful as to the bird.

It was a young bird, not yet a full year in the mews, that gave them the lesson Gormshuil would carry the rest of her life, though neither of them thought it more than a bad morning at the time. A novice keeper new to the mews that season, eager to see the bird fly well before the rite's watchers, had fed it heavy the evening before, thinking a full crop would make for a calm bird and a steady flight. It made instead a bird that sat on the block at first light with its feathers loose and its eye half shut, and when Gormshuil raised her glove and called it, it looked at her, and looked away, and would not come. She called it twice more. The third time Artair, without being asked, said from the bench below that there was no use calling a bird that had already eaten its fill of the day before it began, and that she might as well save her wrist and let it sit.

"It will not fly hungry either," said the novice, red in the face, thinking he was being corrected for a fault rather than told a fact. "I have seen a starved one snap at the glove."

"You have seen right," Artair said, "and wrong in the same breath, which is the whole trouble with hawks and most other things besides. Feed it too little and it turns on the hand that starves it. Feed it too much and it will not leave the hand at all. There is one measure between the two, and it is not a wide one, and it is not the same for every bird or every morning, which is why the keeping of it is a craft and not a rule a man could write once and be done with." He looked up at his daughter, who still stood with her arm out and the sullen bird ignoring her. "Bring it down and don't fly it today. Let the novice sit with it and learn its crop by his own eye instead of by his fear of being wrong in front of the shrine."

Gormshuil brought the bird down, and said nothing at the time of what her father had said, turning it over instead in the weeks that followed the way a person turns over any true thing said carelessly, testing it against other true things until it either breaks or holds. It held. She had grown up hearing the shrine's telling that the hawk of the dawn rite was no common bird of the mews but stood for one far older, one the keepers before her father's father's father had called Mòd, and said to be as old as Fionntan mac Bòchra, eldest of men, who remembered all things with him from before there was a shrine to keep or a mews to keep it in; she had heard that telling so often it had worn as smooth in her mind as a step worn hollow by feet, a thing repeated rather than thought. It was her father's plain word about a young bird's crop, not the old telling, that first made her think hard about the measure rather than the bird that carried it.

Three years on, Artair's knees had failed him for the climb entirely, and it was Gormshuil, not the novice nor any other keeper of the mews, who went up alone in the grey before dawn and called Mòd down from the block for the first time with no one but herself to answer for the flight. She was three-and-thirty years old that morning, by her own later count, and she would say afterward, to the few who asked her directly, that she had gone up frightened of the bird and come down frightened of something else, though she was slow for a long while to say what. Her father, watching from the bench with his blanket over his knees, said only, when she came down to him, that Mòd had flown as well for her as for any keeper he had watched in a long life, and that a bird cared nothing at all for whose wrist it left so long as the wrist knew the measure, which was the last thing of any weight he told her before his own strength failed him further and his talk turned, as an old man's talk will, to smaller things: the weather, the state of the mews roof, whether the novice keeper had learned yet to judge a crop by his own eye instead of by fright.

## II. Of the Empty Perch

Artair died at {{place:burg:20}} five years after the morning of the overfed bird, in his own bed above the shrine with his daughter beside him, three-score and ten years old, and the mews he had kept the better part of his life passed to Gormshuil without dispute, as it had passed to him and to his father before that. They buried him on the hillside above the mews, in the plain way the hill's own households buried their dead, wrapped in undyed cloth and laid with his face to the shrine rather than to any house of the departed the coast might have kept for him, since a keeper of the mews was reckoned, on that hill, to belong to the birds and the standing stones before he belonged to any other rite. The coal-cutters and their wives who had stood so many mornings on the shrine's ledge came up in numbers greater than the ledge had ever held for a living rite, and it was noted afterward, by those who noted such things, that not one household of the hill sent a servant in its own stead that day.

The shrine kept its rite the morning after his burying as it kept it every morning, because a rite that stops for a death is a rite that will stop again for the next one, and Gormshuil went up to the block in the grey light with her father not two days in the ground and called Mòd down herself, dry-eyed at the flying and not dry-eyed after it, alone on the hillside where no one from the shrine below could see her face.

It was in the weeks of her mourning, when the watchers who came to the shrine asked her, as was the custom, to say a few words over her father's empty place on the bench, that she first put into open speech what she had turned over quietly since the overfed bird. She told them her father's plain word about the measure, and then, because the watchers waited for more and she found she had more to give them, she told them that the hawk of the rite was Mòd himself, old as Fionntan mac Bòchra and remembering with him all that the island had forgotten of its own beginning, and that the vein under Muileann dhearg, which fed every hearth on the island its handful and no more, was a hawk of that same kind: a hungry hawk, to be fed the measured handful and starved of nothing it truly needed, but never once glutted past what the giving hand could spare. A hawk overfed will not hunt, she told them, and a hawk starved will turn on the hand that starves it, and the keeping of the measure between the two was not a rule written once at the vein-house and left to stand, any more than a bird's crop could be judged once and trusted for every morning after. It wanted watching, the way her father had watched it, all his life and to the day his knees gave out under him.

The watchers who heard her that first time were mostly her father's own people, keepers and their households who had come to the shrine for years and would have listened to Gormshuil nic Artair out of long habit whatever she had said over the empty bench. But she said the same thing again the next season, and the one after, shaping it a little further each time without changing what stood at the centre of it, and it began, in the slow way such things begin, to be spoken of beyond the shrine's own households, still nameless as a teaching, only a thing the hawk-keeper's daughter at Cnoc bheag said over the bird, worth a morning's walk to hear for oneself.

## III. Of Those Who Came to Hear

Word of it reached the north coast before it reached the capital, carried, as such things were carried on the island, by the ordinary business of trade rather than by any messenger sent for the purpose: a wool-buyer of the Hawk bay who had business at Cnoc bheag over a debt of hides heard Gormshuil speak one dawn while he waited for the shrine's steward to finish the shrine's own accounts, and carried the telling home with the hides, embroidering it a little in the carrying as such tellings are embroidered, so that by the time it reached the hawk-keepers of the coast it had already begun to sound less like a woman's plain word over her father's empty seat and more like a teaching worth sending someone to hear at first hand. Three keepers of the coastal shrines came inland that same year to judge it for themselves, and stayed a week, sleeping in the mews steward's own house and rising each dawn to stand at the back of the shrine's ledge among the coal-cutters' wives, saying little until the week was nearly out. The eldest of the three, a woman near Gormshuil's own age who had kept a shrine on the Hawk bay since before Gormshuil was a hawk-keeper's daughter at all, asked her on the last morning whether she had ever herself gone hungry enough to know the second half of what she taught, or only ever watched a bird do it for her. Gormshuil answered that she had gone hungry twice in her life that she counted, once as a child in a hard winter and once the week her father died, and that she had disliked the lesson both times and kept it both times besides, which the elder judged, after turning it over a moment, answer enough. The three went home saying the teaching had lost nothing in the carrying after all, which was unusual enough to be remembered on its own account.

Within a few years more, and without the vein-house's own scribes troubling themselves over what was said at a hillside shrine two days' walk from the capital, the watchers who climbed to Cnoc bheag for the dawn rite had grown from the handful of her father's own households to a congregation the shrine's ledge could no longer easily hold, and Gormshuil, who had never asked to be more than her father's daughter keeping her father's mews, found herself asked, more mornings than not, to say the teaching whole rather than in the pieces it had first come out in. She gathered it, in the end, into the shape it would keep for the rest of her life and after it: that Mòd, the Hawk of Acaill, was old as Fionntan mac Bòchra and remembered with him what the island no longer remembered of its own first years; that every hearth's coal was a feeding of the Hungry Hawk beneath the mountain, owed the measured handful and no more; and that the craft of keeping the measure, in a mews or at a vein-house both, was a watching that wanted doing fresh each time, never a law fixed once for every morning after, just as her father had watched a young bird's crop rather than trust a rule written the year before.

Those who heard the teaching whole, gathered under one roof rather than caught in pieces on the shrine's ledge, called themselves before long a house rather than a congregation, and in {{year:III-0062}} they set about raising one: a low hall of timber and dry stone below the mews block, roofed before the following winter, that took the name Taigh an t-Seabhaig, the house of the Hawk, and gave the old dawn-hawk rite of Cnoc bheag, for the first time in its long keeping, a hall of its own rather than an open hillside and a shrine ledge worn hollow by generations of standing feet. Gormshuil kept the mews as she always had, and kept the house besides, though she said, more than once to those close enough to hear her say it, that she would rather have gone on calling Mòd down to an empty hillside than to a hall full of watchers waiting for her to speak, and was believed by those who knew her, and not quite believed by those who did not.

## IV. Of the House at Ros gheal and the Flood at Seann Vell

The teaching did not stay at Cnoc bheag. Among the watchers who had climbed the hill in the years the house was building were two of Gormshuil's own pupils, a brother and sister of a coastal household at {{place:burg:24}}, who asked her leave to carry the dawn rite down to the shore rather than make every fisher's family of that coast walk two days inland to hear it. She gave them the leave, and something more besides: she went down to Ros gheal herself in {{year:III-0063}}, the season the second house was raised, to see the first rite kept there and to judge, as her father had once judged a young bird's crop by his own eye, whether the teaching had travelled whole or had thinned on the road the way a telling carried by a wool-buyer will thin. The rite at Ros gheal was kept on a spit of rock above the tide-line rather than a hillside turf, its ring of stones half salt-eaten already, and a wind off the sea made the hawk's flight a shorter, harder thing than the still air of Cnoc bheag's morning; the fishers who stood at the ledge there smelled of net-tar and brine rather than of coal-smoke, and asked the pupils, when the flying was done, after the size of the season's catch rather than the size of any hearth's handful. Gormshuil judged the teaching had travelled whole enough for all that, though the sea air and the salt-caked stone of the coast gave it a harder edge than the hillside's had, and she came home content to let the house at Ros gheal keep its own measure of it rather than ask it back to Cnoc bheag word for word. Between the two houses, her pupils took on the country that lay between the coast and the hill, so that a shire that had once sent every dying hearth's household two days inland for the rite could now walk half a day to the shore instead.

It was a pupil of the Ros gheal house who brought her, some years after, the report she remembered longest of any that reached her at Cnoc bheag, though she had no part in the flood and never travelled far enough south-east to see it with her own eyes. In {{year:III-0064}}, a wet spring, the river at {{place:burg:94}}, far off on the south-east coast, rose in a night past every bank the mill-wrights had trusted, and carried the lower mills of that town away whole, wheel and stone and all, so that the millers of Seann Vell had to build again on the higher ground the following season, cursing, as millers everywhere curse, a river that had kept its banks faithfully for longer than any living miller could remember and chose that one wet spring to break them. The pupil who brought the report to Cnoc bheag had gone to Seann Vell on other business and stood on the higher bank himself, watching the new mills go up, and he told Gormshuil, half in earnest and half hoping to please her, that the flood was a kind of overfed hawk: a river fed too full too fast, turning on the hand that had trusted it. She heard him out, and told him plainly, in front of the others who had gathered to hear the report, that a river was a river and not a hawk, and that a teaching stretched to cover every misfortune under the sky stopped, before long, covering anything at all; a keeper who saw the measure in a hawk's crop and nowhere else had understood it better than one who saw it everywhere and had therefore, in the end, seen it nowhere. It was remembered afterward, among her own pupils, as one of the harder things she ever said to a young man trying to flatter her, and one of the truer. She sent him back to Seann Vell before the season turned, not with any correction to carry to the millers, who wanted none, but with a plain instruction to stand a while longer on the higher bank and watch how the new mills were set before he judged a whole river by one wet spring; he went, and came back the following year saying only that the wheels turned as well on the higher ground as they ever had on the lower, which she thought, though she did not say so to him, the better report of the two.

## V. Of the Marked Sayings

Gormshuil kept the mews and the house both into her own old age, calling Mòd down from the block most mornings until her hands, like her father's before her, grew too stiff for the climb, and after that sitting on the bench below where her father had once sat, watching a younger keeper fly the bird and saying, when she was asked, no more and no less than the measure wanted. She grew, in those last years, sparer of speech than she had been as a younger woman gathering the teaching into its shape, as though she had come to trust that the shape once given would hold its own weight without her standing over it every dawn to prop it up, and those who sat with her on the bench in the cold remembered her as often silent as speaking, watching the flight with the same close, unhurried eye her father had once turned on a young bird's crop.

She died at {{place:burg:20}}, two-and-sixty years old, in the same season the house at Ros gheal marked its own eighth year, and was buried, at her own asking made long before, on the open hillside above it, beside her father, rather than within the new hall she had raised, where the dawn rite had been kept before there was any roof over it at all.

Among those who had sat longest at her teaching was a young man of the house named Ùisdean mac Pàdraig, who had come to Cnoc bheag as a boy to be apprenticed to the mews and stayed to be apprenticed, in the end, more to the teaching than to the birds. It fell to him, in the years after her death, to gather what she had said into a single book, so that a teaching that had travelled by wool-buyers and pupils and half-remembered reports for thirty years might be kept in one hand rather than scattered across every household that had ever heard her speak. He set himself, before he wrote a word of it down, one rule he never afterward broke: that every saying he set on the leaf without having heard it from her own mouth would be marked as such, plainly, so that no reader coming after him would mistake another keeper's memory of her for his own. To fill the book at all he had to travel further than he had ever travelled in her lifetime, down to the salt-eaten shrine at Ros gheal and along the country between it and the hill, sitting with pupils grown old themselves now and asking them, patiently, to give him back word for word what they remembered her saying, and marking, more often than not, that two households a day's walk apart remembered the same saying in two different shapes, so that he was left to choose which shape to set down and mark the choice as his own rather than hers. He kept the rule to the letter for all that, and when the book was finished the marked sayings, gathered from every house and shire the teaching had reached in her lifetime, far outnumbered the few he had heard for himself, standing at the bench below the mews block on mornings when he was too young to know he was hearing anything worth marking at all.

He finished the book in {{year:III-0065}} and called it Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, the Book of the Hawk, and set at its head, before any of the sayings he had gathered from other hands, the one he had heard himself, standing below the block on an ordinary morning with the mews still smelling of the night's straw and Gormshuil's voice carrying down to him no louder than she needed it to carry, the whole of the teaching folded, as she had once folded her father's plain word about a young bird's crop, into a single line short enough for a child to carry home unbroken:

> *Beathaich an seabhag, ach na sàsaich e.*
> Feed the hawk, but do not glut him.
