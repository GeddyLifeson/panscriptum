# Of the Star-Steersmen of Cuan ruadh

*LE 11 – 18*

## I. Of the Six Who Went East

Catrìona nic Iain was eleven years old the first night she went out with her father's boat past the Ros àrsaidh bar and did not know, coming home, which way the harbour lay until the smell of the smoke told her.

She never forgot it. She was a pilot of Ros bheag by the time she was sixteen, one of a handful of young ones who could bring a boat home along the coast in fog by the fall of the swell alone, and she thought herself good at her trade. Then the roll opened at Seann Skell, and the wrights began to lay down hulls no coaster's pilot had ever been asked to steer, and it came out, in the talk on the quays, that none of them, not the oldest, not the best, had ever held a course out of sight of land after dark. A coaster hugged the shore and put in before dusk. A hull bound for a coast three weeks off the mist could do neither.

The custodian Fearchar mac Ruairidh, who by then went about the west coast on the households' business more than he sat at the vein-house, put the question to the harbour-masters of four ports in one season: who, on this coast, has ever steered by the stars alone? The answer came back the same from all four. No one now living. But there were tellings, thin and half-believed, of steersmen away on the eastern shore, at a town called Cuan ruadh, who still did.

It was a hundred miles off, by the map that hung in the harbour-house at Seann Skell, drawn crooked from an older one and marked in Alasdair Saor's own hand where the roads ran; and no road ran straight to it. A man would go up the coal-roads into the mountain country, and down again on the far side, and a week on foot was thought a fast crossing.

Six were chosen to go: two from Ros bheag, two from Ceann mhòr, one from Cuan shean, and Catrìona, because she had asked to be sent before anyone had thought to ask her. She was the youngest of the six and the only woman, and the master of the Ros bheag boats, an old man who had taught her the coast himself, told her plainly to her face that a season away from her own harbour would lose her half her custom.

"Then I'll get it back," she said, "and something with it."

The other four she came to know on the road better than she had known them at their own quays, the way a fortnight's walking will do. The two Ceann mhòr men were brothers, broad and slow-spoken, who had never in their lives been more than a day's sail from the headland watch; the Cuan shean man was a rope-maker's son who had gone to sea only twice and admitted it, on the third day, without shame; and of the second Ros bheag pilot, a lean, silent woman named for a grandmother nobody living remembered, Catrìona learned only that she had lost a brother at sea and had asked to go east for reasons she never once spoke of the whole journey.

They went out in the moon of Bealltach, when the roads were driest, with staffs and packs and a little silver among them from Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill's own purse, sent by a rider with word that the lady of Inis àrsaidh wished them well and asked to be told, when they came home, what they had learned. They went up the coal-road past Muileann dhearg, where the vein-house stood black on its hill and the custodians looked out at them from the gate and said nothing; and over the shoulder of the mountain country the road gave out into a track between screes, where the wind never stopped and the going was a mile in an hour over broken stone. On the fourth night the weather turned on them above the last of the lochs, a cold rain driving level off the moor with no shelter in it anywhere, and they spent the dark hours crowded under an overhang of rock with their backs to each other for warmth, and in the morning the Cuan shean man's feet were so cut about with the stones that the Ceann mhòr brothers had to carry his pack the rest of that day between them.

They went down again by a country none of them knew, of heather and small lochs and cairns whose names they did not have, and on the seventh evening they came over a last rise and saw the sea again, grey and enormous, with a town at the water's edge and boats drawn up along a strand that faced, for the first time in any of their lives, east, out over open water with no far shore in the telling of it at all.

Cuan ruadh smelled different from any harbour on the west coast. Catrìona noticed it before she noticed anything else: no weed-smell of low tide flats, but a clean cold reek of open water, as though the town stood at the very edge of something with nothing beyond it but distance. The boats drawn up were narrow and deep for their length, built, she saw at once, on a different rule than any coaster she knew, sewn like the rest but with a thick shaped plank down the middle of the bottom, of the kind the old men of the west said their grandfathers' boats had carried.

"They go out of sight of land here," said one of the Ceann mhòr brothers, low, as they stood looking down at the strand. "They always did."

Nobody at Cuan ruadh made much of them. A boy took them to a byre where they might sleep and a woman brought them fish and barley bread without asking who had sent them, as though six strangers off the mountain road were a thing that happened often enough not to need remarking on; and it was only the next morning, when Catrìona asked at the well where the steersman lived, that the woman drawing water looked at her a long moment before she answered, in the way of someone deciding how much a stranger deserved to be told.

## II. Of the Steersman of Cuan ruadh

The man they were sent to was called Seumas Reultach. It was a by-name the town had settled on him so long ago that no one now living had heard the name his mother gave him. He was old, older than any of them had reckoned an old man could be and still climb a cliff path without a staff, and he received them at his own door with no surprise at all, as though six strangers from the far side of the island turned up asking to be taught the stars once in every generation, which, it came out over the following days, was very nearly true: his own grandmother had taught two men from the north coast, long before any of them were born, who had gone home and, so far as Seumas knew, taught no one.

"You'll have to come at night," he told them, "for the stars aren't out by day, whatever else you may have heard," and it was the only joke Catrìona ever heard him make in all the months that followed.

They came at night, every clear one, up a path above the town to a headland where the wind stripped every sound but its own, and Seumas Reultach, wrapped in a sealskin that had been his father's, taught them to stand still and look up until the sky stopped being a scatter of light and became a shape.

The first night he taught them nothing at all. He walked them up the path in the last of the dusk, sat them down in a half-ring on the cold grass with their backs to the lights of the town, and told them to be silent and to look up, and for the better part of two hours nobody spoke and nothing was named. The rope-maker's son grew restless and was told, once, without heat, to be still; the lean woman from Ros bheag did not move at all. When at last Seumas rose and said that was enough for one night and led them back down, the Cuan shean man asked him, aggrieved, what they had learned.

"You have learned that it takes a while," said Seumas, "before a man stops seeing points of light and starts seeing a sky. Come back tomorrow if it's clear."

He began, on the second night, with what he called the wheel: how the whole sky turned, slow as a millstone, about a fixed point low over the northern sea that never moved though everything else did, and how a man who found that point and kept it always at his shoulder, whichever way the boat's head lay, need never be lost in the dark. The star that stood nearest it, low and steady above the water on a clear night, he named to them: Reul na Stiùire, the steering star. From three others set in a line he showed them how to find it, so that on a cloudy night, if even one showed through a gap, a man who knew the shapes could still find his star by where the others would have stood.

Catrìona had thought, before that first night, that she already knew the sky a little, from six years of putting to sea before dawn. She learned, on the headland above Cuan ruadh, that she had known nothing: not one true name, not one bearing, not the difference between a star that held its place the whole night through and one that only seemed to, low near the water, and set within the hour.

Seumas taught them the whole turning year of the sky as a farmer taught a son the turning year of the fields: which stars stood over the stern at Samhnas and which at Teasach, how the wheel itself shifted its whole aspect between the black months and the bright, so that a man steering the same course by the same star in winter and in summer must know to look for it in a different quarter of the sky or lose his heading entirely. He taught them to read the smell of the dark, the particular cold that came before a change of wind, the look of the water under starlight when a swell was building that the eye alone, in daylight, would have missed. Most of it he gave them only as it was given him, mouth to ear, night after night, with nothing written, because there was nothing to write it on that would survive a sea crossing and because, he said once, a thing carried in the hand is more easily lost than a thing carried in the head.

The lessons did not always go easily. There were nights of cloud when nothing could be taught at all and the six sat idle in the byre while the rope-maker's son grumbled that they had come a hundred miles to learn nothing; there was a week in the first winter when the lean woman from Ros bheag fell sick of a fever off the crossing and Seumas himself came down from his house each evening to sit by her, not to teach, only to sit, until she was well; and there was a night, deep in that same winter, when one of the Ceann mhòr brothers, cold and homesick and tired of being corrected, said aloud on the headland that a man could learn to fish by starlight for twenty years on the west coast and never need a word of any of this, and that they had been sent on a fool's errand by a custodian with too much silver and not enough sense. Seumas let him finish. Then he asked him, quietly, how he meant to find his way home across open water once the last headland of Dia-thìr had sunk below the sea behind him, and the man had no answer, and came back to the headland every clear night after that without being asked again.

Catrìona asked more questions than the rest of them together, and it was to her, more than to any of the others, that Seumas began after the first winter to speak of things beyond what he set out each night to teach: the names his own teacher had given the stars, some of them older, he thought, than the town; a telling of steersmen from an age before the Stone Kings who were said to have gone west of the sunset and not come back, which he would neither say he believed nor quite say he did not; and once, on a night of no cloud when the wheel stood very clear, the plain admission that no one now at Cuan ruadh, himself least of all, any longer sailed by night as their grandfathers had, because there was nowhere left on that coast that the daylight coasting trade could not reach before dark, and the knowledge had gone on being taught, generation after generation, to fewer and fewer, for the sake of the teaching and not because anyone still needed it. "Until now," he said, and looked at her, and said nothing more that night.

In the second winter Fearchar's rider came again, with word that the ships were being laid down in earnest now and asking whether any part of the teaching might be set down, that it should not all ride on six heads and the chance of the sea. Seumas thought about it three days. Then he sent for a scraped hide and an awl, and set his own granddaughter, whose hand was steadier than his own had ever been, to cutting the marks as he named them to her by the fire: the wheel, the steering star and the three that found it, and the four turning marks of the year. Catrìona watched the girl's awl go into the hide, stroke by careful stroke, and thought it the strangest thing she had yet seen at Cuan ruadh, stranger than the deep-keeled boats or the wheel of the sky, that a thing which had lived only in old men's mouths for as long as anyone could tell should be made, in one winter evening, to hold still.

When it was done, and no more, the Cuan shean man asked why not the rest of it, the swell-reading and the wind-smell and the hundred small signs that had taken two winters to teach. Seumas Reultach looked at him a long while and said that some things a hide could hold and some things only a life could, and that he had given them the first kind and meant to go on giving them the second kind as long as he had breath, and after that it would go the way such things went.

He had less breath than any of them knew. In the third spring, in the moon of Faolach, in the wolf-storms, he took to his bed with a cough that would not leave him, and for a fortnight the six of them took it in turn to sit with him, for there was little else by then to teach and much, Catrìona thought, that they still owed him. He rallied enough, on the first clear night after the fortnight's rain broke, to insist on the cliff path once more, leaning on two of them going up and refusing, at the top, to be helped to sit. He named the wheel to them one last time, as though they might have forgotten it, and fell silent, and they thought he had only gone quiet the way he sometimes did. When the others thought at last that he had gone in out of the cold as he always did, he had not. They found him at the top of the cliff path in the morning, sitting with his back against the stone he always leaned on, his sealskin drawn close, his face turned up to where the wheel had gone round while he sat there and left the steering star exactly where it always was.

They buried him at Cuan ruadh, among his own people, with the whole town come down to the graveside though none of the six had ever seen more than a handful of its people at once before; and his granddaughter gave the hide with its marks into Catrìona's keeping, saying only that her grandfather had meant it to go west, and that she herself could read it as well as any of them and had no need to keep it. Catrìona said afterward that of all the things she had been taught that winter the truest was the one nobody had set out to teach her: that a man could hold a whole sky in his head and pass almost none of it on, and that whatever they carried west out of Cuan ruadh, hide and memory both, it would always be less than what he had known.

## III. Of the First Trial and the Star Named on the Quay

They went home in the moon of Faolach still, still six, for Seumas had never been one of them to lose; over the mountain country by the same track, with the same wind, carrying nothing on the outward road that they had not carried in, and everything that mattered on the road back in their own heads, and the hide, wrapped against the wet in a fold of the lean woman's cloak because she alone among them thought to ask for the driest place to carry it.

At Ros àrsaidh, where the coast lay open enough for a night's sailing without much risk of the rocks, they held the first trial that summer, and half the town came down to the strand to see it fail. Two boats went out after dark: one steered the old way, close along the shore with a man forward calling the water at every length, lanterns swinging at bow and stern so the watchers on the cliff could follow her the whole night; and one by Catrìona alone, with two of the Ros àrsaidh men to work the sail and say nothing, out past the headland into open water and every lantern doused, with nothing to go by but Reul na Stiùire held at her shoulder as Seumas had taught her, and the smell of the wind, and the set of the swell under the keel. For the first hour, she said afterward, she was as frightened as either of the men with her, listening for the sound of a wave breaking on rock that she could not see and trusting, against everything her years on that coast had taught her, that the star at her shoulder and not the black loom of the land was the thing to steer by. Then the fear went, the way it had gone on the headland above Cuan ruadh once she had stopped listening to what she thought she knew and started listening to what she had been taught, and the rest of the night she held her course as easily as she had ever held one along a shore she could see.

She brought her boat back before the other one, and put it exactly where she had said she would the evening before, at first light, on the tide she had named; and the two men who had gone with her, sick with fear the whole night through by their own later admission, would not say a word against it afterward, though one of them would not go out with her by night again for a year.

Word of the trial went along the coast faster than Catrìona could follow it herself, and by the next year she had more asking to learn from her than she and the other five together could teach, and no time to teach them, for the harbour-house was calling in the households of the roll that spring, and every one of the six pilots had entered long since, and was wanted.

She reached Seann Skell in the moon of Sìolach, late, having stopped a fortnight at Ros bheag to see her mother, who was old and would not be going, and to leave with her what little silver she had, and to say the kind of goodbye that neither of them named for what it was. The harbour road into Seann Skell was busier than she had ever known it, carts and barrows and whole families afoot with what they could carry, and the town itself, when she came down into it at last, smelled of pitch and sawdust and woodsmoke in a way it never had in her girlhood, so that for a moment, tired from the road, she hardly knew it. When she came down at last to the harbour front she found the boards already crowded and the town talking of nothing else: the households of the roll had met, it was said, and chosen who should lead them, and it was done, though nobody she asked could say quite how, only that it was Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, the queen's own sister, and Fearchar mac Ruairidh of the vein-house, and that the two of them were even now down at the new stones of the west quay looking over the first hulls.

She found them there in the evening light, the queen's sister in a plain grey cloak with the wind pulling at it, and the custodian beside her with his hands behind his back, the two of them standing where the stonework was still raw and pale and the tide had not yet worn it, looking at a hull that stood half-ribbed on its frame like the bones of something not yet grown its skin. Along the strand behind them the town went on with its evening as though nothing had changed in it at all, boats coming in, nets spread to dry, a woman calling a child in to supper; and Catrìona thought, standing a little apart with her pack still on her shoulder and the hide from Cuan ruadh wrapped inside it, how strange it was that a choosing which the whole harbour was talking of should have left the harbour looking exactly as it always had.

"You're the one who went east," said Mòrag, before Catrìona had said who she was.

"I am."

"And you came back knowing the stars."

"I came back knowing some of them. There was more to know than any six of us could carry."

Mòrag looked at her a moment, the way, Catrìona thought afterward, a woman looks at a thing she has been waiting to be given without knowing its shape beforehand. "I have a hull," she said. "An Ròn Glas. She'll carry my own household and I don't yet know how many more, and she'll need someone at the steering oar who can hold a course the night the coast goes out of sight for good, because after that there'll be nothing else to hold it by. Will you be her pilot?"

Fearchar said nothing, but he was watching Catrìona's face as if the answer mattered to him nearly as much as it did to Mòrag, and afterward, when it was too late to ask him, she wondered whether it was because he already knew, better than anyone else on that quay, how few of them there would ever be who could do what she could do. He had, she remembered later, counted households on a copied roll and found them fewer than he wished; he was a man used to finding that a thing the venture needed came in short measure, and used to taking what there was of it without complaint.

"I will," said Catrìona, and found, saying it, that she had known her own answer since the headland above Cuan ruadh and had only been waiting, without knowing it, for someone to ask her the question in words.

Mòrag nodded, once, as though a thing long uncertain had at last been set down in its right place, and turned back to the hull. But before she did, Catrìona, not quite knowing why she said it, lifted a hand toward the darkening sky over the river mouth, where the first stars were beginning to show through the last of the light, and found the one she wanted without having to search for it, as she no longer had to search for anything in that part of the sky.

"That one," she said. "Low, over the water. That's the one I'll be steering by, my lady, the night the coast goes."

Mòrag looked where she pointed, and said nothing for a while, and the light went on failing over the river, and the star Catrìona had named stood where it always stood, the one point in the whole turning sky that never moved.
