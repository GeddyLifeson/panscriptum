# Of the Brothers' Peace

*LE 1,266 – 1,319*

## I. Of the Two Courts

The council that had failed to fill the blank space on the burial roll did not try again the following year, nor the year after that, and in the space where an heir's name should have stood a kind of silence settled over Cathair dhearg that everyone at An Talla Dearg understood and no one troubled to name aloud. Iain Ciar, the elder by two years, kept his own following at An Talla Dearg, in the wing his mother's kin had held since before his birth; Alasdair Bàn kept his in the south country, where his mother's people had given him land and men enough that he need ask leave of no one to sit at his own table. For four years the two households traded no worse than cold courtesy, gifts sent at Na Ceithir Fèilltean, messengers who ate at one hall and rode to the other without any word carried between them that either brother could afterward be blamed for.

The keeping of a house that was not quite a court and not quite a private hall had fallen, some years before the war broke, to a woman of the mining road with no part in either brother's quarrel: Mairead nic Mhurchaidh, who took up An Taigh-seinnse Mòr from her own father, the last of a long line of keepers descended, the road said, from Iain mac Mhurchaidh, who had built the house its second byre and begun, in a careful hand, the book of the ox-teams that lodged there through the mountain snows; she kept both after her father without changing so much as the ruling of the ledger's columns, and travellers on the central road who had known her father's keeping said, more than once, that they could not have told the two hands apart on the page had the years not told them which was which. Nothing in those years marked the road-house as a place either brother's fortunes would ever turn on. It was simply the halfway stage, the byre, the book, the fires kept through the worst of the snow for whichever ox-team the weather had caught between one shire and the next.

It could not hold, and everyone at both tables, if asked plainly, would have said so from the first winter. Iain Ciar was thirty years old when it broke and Alasdair Bàn twenty-eight, both of an age to have stopped waiting on their fathers' word and begun to trust their own, and each had spent four years hearing, from men who wanted a lord's favour more than they wanted peace between brothers, that the other meant to move first if he was given time enough to gather the strength for it. A messenger of Alasdair Bàn's, riding to An Talla Dearg with the ordinary gift at the feast of Bealltach, was turned back at the gate that fourth year on some pretext of the season's business too full to receive him, the first courtesy either household had refused the other in four years of careful giving, and it is told that Alasdair took the refusal, small as the matter was on its own account, as the answer he had been waiting four years to hear. Alasdair moved first. In {{year:III-0177}} his men took Cnoc naomh in a night, a small holding worth little on its own account but sitting square on the road between the two households' lands, and held it eleven days before Iain Ciar's own riders came up and took it back with less blood than either side afterward claimed. Na Seachd Taighean, who had watched the four years of cold courtesy without committing themselves to either table, could no longer sit apart once blood had been drawn on ground both brothers claimed. Four declared for Iain Ciar and three for Alasdair Bàn, and it was from that first taking and retaking of a holding no one had thought worth a war that the fighting which followed took its name: Cogadh nam Bràithrean.

What the two brothers said to one another in those first weeks, if they said anything at all beyond what their messengers carried, no leaf that survives records. It is told only that neither sent to the other any word of grief for what four years of cold courtesy had come to, and that both, within the same season, called up every man their own three or four houses owed them, and set every road between the two halves of the island under one following's watch or the other, so that a carter who had crossed freely from west to east his whole life found, that first winter of the war, that he must now say plainly at every second crossing which brother's peace he travelled under.

## II. Of the Burning of Ceann mhin and the Fall Below Muileann ghlas

Alasdair Bàn's ships came down the coast to Ceann mhin in {{year:III-0178}}, which had declared for his brother, and burned its waterfront before the town's own bell had finished ringing the alarm. The fire took the rope stores first, where the tarred cordage went up faster than any hand could carry water to it, and then the granary beside them, so that by the second morning Ceann mhin had lost in one night both what it needed to put its boats back to sea and what it needed to feed itself until the next harvest came in. Men who had spent the day hauling water from the harbour to a blaze no bucket-line could master were found by dawn simply sitting on the strand among the salvaged nets, too spent even to grieve aloud over the char that had been the granary. The town sent to Cathair mhòr, which had taken no side in the quarrel and could afford to be asked, and Cathair mhòr sent grain enough that no household at Ceann mhin went hungry that winter, though the debt of it, one way and another, was still being spoken of at the two towns' markets a generation later, a favour neither given nor asked for lightly and neither forgotten easily either.

Iain Ciar heard of the burning three days after it happened, at An Talla Dearg, from a rider who had changed horses twice to bring the word faster than any single beast could carry it the whole way. He is said to have asked the rider only two questions, how many had died and whether the granary had burned before or after the rope stores, and to have given no answer of his own to either the rider or the hall until he had heard both answers in full. What he did afterward needed no further asking. He called up Na Ceithir Taighean sworn to him and marched west through a season of hard rain that turned the mining road to a mire under every cart and every marching foot, and in {{year:III-0179}}, below Muileann ghlas where the road narrows between the river and the hill, he brought Alasdair Bàn's own men to a stand at last and broke them.

Little of the fighting is remembered in any particular that survives; what is told is mostly the outcome. Alasdair Bàn was taken alive on the field, and his captains were let go home unransomed and unpunished on Iain Ciar's own order, given only his word that the war their lord had made was ended so far as they themselves were concerned. It was a mercy some at Iain Ciar's own table thought unwise, arguing that a captain sent home free was a captain free to be called up again if Alasdair's cause ever found new strength; Iain Ciar is said to have answered that a war fought to its bitter last man on both sides left nothing behind it but sons who would fight the next one for their own dead fathers, and that he had rather end this one clean than begin the next one already owed. Alasdair was carried east under guard to a house of his brother's own choosing, kept comfortably and watched closely, while the two households that had traded cold courtesy for four years and then blood for four seasons more waited to learn what their two lords would make of what was left between them. Word of the victory reached An Taigh-seinnse Mòr within the week, carried by an ox-driver who had turned back from the mountain road rather than push his team through country still unsettled by the fighting, and Mairead nic Mhurchaidh, hearing it at her own hearth, is said to have asked the driver only where the two brothers now stood to one another, and to have got, in answer, a shrug and the plain word that no one yet knew.

## III. Of the Keeper's Day

The peace, when it came on {{date:III-0180}}, was made on ground that belonged to neither brother: An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, the great road-house at the halfway stage of the central mining road, where the ox-teams of the mountain waited out the snow every winter of the year and had done since before either brother's grandfather was born. Mairead nic Mhurchaidh was given, by whichever messenger carried the choosing of the ground to both households at once, a single day's warning that the two brothers and their two followings were riding for her hall, there to settle, one way or another, what four years of cold courtesy and four seasons of war had left unsettled.

She spent the day scrubbing. It is the one particular of that day that every telling of it agrees on, whatever else each teller adds or leaves out: that Mairead nic Mhurchaidh, keeper of a house that answered to no lord but the road, took a single day's warning of two kings' followings arriving at her door and used the better part of it on her knees with a bucket and a brush, scouring a floor that had taken the boots of ox-drivers and reeves and pilgrims for longer than her own father could remember, because she had heard it said, by travellers who had seen greater houses than hers, that great men will quarrel over the length of a bench sooner than they will quarrel over the matter that brought them to the table, and she meant to give neither brother the smallest excuse. She set the long board in the common room with a bench to either side, and measured the two benches against one another with her own hands until neither ran so much as a finger's breadth longer than its match, and she set at each end of the board the same loaf, baked from the same batch of the same flour, and the same jug of the same ale, so that no clerk of either household, however he wished to find a slight in it afterward, could find one in the setting of the table.

Both followings drew up in the yard outside a little before noon, the two bodies of armed men keeping a careful distance from one another across the frozen mud, and it was into that silence, thick enough already that Mairead nic Mhurchaidh heard every hoof-fall on the yard's stones from inside her own kitchen, that Iain Ciar came in first, on foot from the yard where his own men had halted a bowshot short of the door, and sat at the end nearest the fire without waiting to be shown to it. Alasdair Bàn came after him under guard that fell back at the threshold and did not follow him in, walking stiffly in the way of a man not long out of the custody of another man's house, and sat down across the board from his brother without a word of greeting passing between them that the keeper, standing by the fire and trying to look as though she were not listening, could afterward report to anyone who asked her. For a long while neither brother spoke at all. What she remembered longest of that silence, she told those who came after her at the house, was the sound of the oxen shifting in the byre behind the common room, a sound so ordinary on any other day that she had never once, in all the years of her keeping, troubled to notice it before that afternoon, and that grew, the longer the two men sat without a word, to fill the whole of the room in her own hearing until she could have named which beast had shifted its weight by the particular creak of the timber under it.

What passed between the two brothers in the time that followed, she never told, whether because she never fully heard it over the fire's own noise or because she judged, once the telling of it had been asked of her a hundred times by a hundred different travellers over the years that followed, that a thing two brothers had settled between themselves belonged to the two of them and not to a keeper's fireside story. Asked by an old reeve of Cathair mhòr, years afterward, whether the two men had raised their voices at any point in the long silence, she answered only that she had heard nothing through the door she had not already heard from her own oxen, and would say no more of it however the reeve pressed her. The end of it she would tell, and told the same way every time she was asked: that Alasdair Bàn gave up whatever claim he had carried into the room and was granted, for the whole of his own life, the lordship of Seann Chwen and nothing more; that Iain Ciar, thirty-six years old that year, was acknowledged crowned by the brother who might otherwise have contested it to both their deaths; and that when the two men rose at last from the board, each loaf at each end had been broken by the hand nearest it and neither had been eaten, as though the breaking of the bread had stood in that room for something neither brother had needed nor wished to put into plainer words. Mairead nic Mhurchaidh swept the crumbs from the board herself that evening, after both followings had ridden their separate ways, and is said to have kept, for the rest of her own keeping of the house, a horror of seeing any loaf served whole and any loaf served broken set down together on the same table, a small superstition her own daughters after her never quite understood and never quite gave up either.

## IV. Of the Years the Road Kept Its Peace

Alasdair Bàn kept the peace he had made for the whole of his own life, and it was said of him at Seann Chwen, where he held his lordship without complaint to the end of his days, that a man who has once sat across a board from his own brother with nothing between them but two whole loaves learns something about the keeping of a promise that no amount of being told can teach a man who has never sat there. He is remembered to have gone up to An Talla Dearg himself twice in those years, once at the birth of a nephew he would never rule after and once at the burying of an old captain of his own who had asked, dying, only that Alasdair carry the news of it to the king with his own mouth rather than send it by a rider; Iain Ciar received him both times without ceremony and sent him home again with a following no larger than the one he had come with, a small courtesy that satisfied neither brother's clerks, who would have preferred either a grander welcome or none at all, but seems to have satisfied the two brothers themselves well enough. He died at Seann Chwen in {{year:III-0181}}, a man of forty-nine, in the house his brother had let him keep, and no word of any quarrel renewed in his name is told of the years between the peace and his death. His lordship, on his dying, returned to the crown, as the peace had settled it should, and Iain Ciar is said to have kept, for the rest of his own reign, the lordship vacant rather than grant it to any other man, a small stubbornness An Talla Dearg's own clerks never once asked him to explain.

It was the burning of Ceann mhin, more than the battle that avenged it, that Iain Ciar is said to have carried longest in his own reckoning of the war. Having seen one shire seat beg its bread of another for the whole of a hard winter, he commanded, once his throne stood settled enough for such commands to be kept, that every shire seat of the island hold in store against the next war a full year's grain, measured and counted by the crown's own reeves at each autumn's close. The law, made {{year:III-0182}} and called after him Lagh an Stòir, was kept faithfully while he himself sat An Talla Dearg to see that it was kept, and every granary on the island filled and emptied and filled again each year exactly as he had ordered.

The road below the road-house, in the years between, carried a new trade the two brothers' war had never touched. Masons of Ceann leathan, building a new front for their own temple, sent north in {{year:III-0181a}} for a stone none of their own quarries could match, a veined white marble found in the hills above Baile Mòr dhomhain, and from the year the first cart of it came south the road below An Taigh-seinnse Mòr carried a traffic that had never passed that way before: carriers who smelled of stone dust rather than coal or grain, and who paid, more often than not in cut stone itself rather than in silver or salt, for whatever fodder and shelter the road-house could offer their teams. Mairead nic Mhurchaidh kept a slab of it, unworked and no bigger than a hand, on the mantel above her own hearth for the rest of her keeping, a small vanity she never fully explained even to those who asked her outright why a road-house keeper should want a piece of stone bound for someone else's temple.

Pàdraig mac Ruairidh, a clerk of the king's own household, came to An Taigh-seinnse Mòr himself in those years, sent by no one but his own wish to set the war down properly before those who remembered it firsthand had all died out of the telling of it. He arrived on a wet evening with a satchel of wax tablets and a servant to carry them, and stayed three nights under the road-house's roof at his own expense rather than the crown's, working each evening by the same fire the two brothers had sat beside on the day of the peace, and asking, of every ox-driver who came in from the road, whether they had heard the tale told any way but the one the keeper herself told it. He sat with Mairead nic Mhurchaidh at the same board, by then scarred with the ordinary use of years rather than scrubbed for a single afternoon, and asked her plainly what had passed between the two brothers on the day of the peace. She told him, as she had told every asker before him, only the setting of the table and the silence and the two loaves broken and left; whatever words the two brothers had exchanged across that board she either had not heard or would not repeat, and Pàdraig, for all his asking, came away from her hearth with no more of it than anyone else who had asked her the same question over the years. He is said to have thanked her for her patience all the same, and to have left, before he rode on, a small silver ring of no great value pressed into her hand for the fire and the bed, which she kept afterward on the same mantel as the mason's slab of white stone, two keepsakes of two very different visitors set side by side without any comment from her that either was worth more than the other.

## V. Of the Book, and the Meeting at the Far-Off House

The account Pàdraig mac Ruairidh finished at last, {{year:III-0183}}, praised the king at every turn it could find a turn to praise him on, and it is the only account of Cogadh nam Bràithrean that survives whole to be read by anyone who asks after it, a fact that has vexed more than one later reader who would have liked a fuller word from the losing side of the table. Of the keeper's day it says only that the peace was made at a road-house between the brothers' lands and that both loaves set upon the board were left uneaten, which is not quite the whole of what Mairead nic Mhurchaidh herself had told him and is, all the same, the only part of her telling that Pàdraig's book chose to keep.

Mairead nic Mhurchaidh died at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr in her own bed, in the sixty-ninth year of her life, on a night an ox-team lodged in the byre below her window as ox-teams had lodged there every winter of her own keeping and her father's before her, so that the last sound the household afterward remembered her waking to was the same shifting of hooves on straw that she had once told a hundred askers she remembered above every other sound of the brothers' peace. The keeping of the house passed from her to a sister's daughter who had grown up under the same roof and learned the same careful measure of a bench and a loaf that her aunt had never once, in all the years since the brothers' peace, allowed to slip; the ledger her forebear had begun and her aunt had kept without altering so much as its ruling passed with the house, and the niece continued it in a hand the ox-drivers who had known both women said they could not tell from either.

It was this niece who stood at the door of An Sligeanach Fad' às on the eastern road, in {{year:III-0184}}, when the crown at last chartered the seven great road-houses of the mining circuit and made their keepers answerable, each for the stretch of road nearest their own hearth, to one another rather than to any single lord. Six other keepers stood there with her that same day, from six other houses of the circuit she had never before had cause to set eyes on, each carrying the same charter in the same crown hand, and it was a strange thing to her, she said afterward, to find that six strangers already knew the shape of her own work as well as she did, down to the particular trouble of a byre in a hard winter and the particular trick of stretching one season's stores through an unlooked-for extra month of snowbound travellers. The seven keepers met there for the first time that same winter to settle their accounts, a meeting the charter itself commanded be held once every year from that one onward, and it was at that first gathering, among keepers who had never before had cause to sit at one table together, that the new keeper of An Taigh-seinnse Mòr was asked, more than once, whether the road-house tale of the two brothers and the two loaves broken and left uneaten was truly as her aunt had always told it.

She answered, each time she was asked, exactly as her aunt had answered every asker before her: the table set, the benches measured to a finger's breadth, the silence, the sound of the oxen in the byre, and the two loaves broken and left, and nothing more, for a thing two brothers had settled between themselves at her aunt's own hearth was not hers to finish telling, whatever the charter now asked of the keepers who had inherited the roads their fathers and mothers had kept before them. One of the six other keepers, an old woman of a road-house on the bare northern stretch who had kept her own house through worse winters than any of the others at that table could match, said only, when the answer was given, that a peace kept quiet by the one who made the table for it was worth more than a peace any keeper cared to dress up for a stranger's asking, and the seven of them, meeting for the first time under a charter none of them had sought, drank to that and to nothing grander before the account-books were opened and the year's long business of the roads began.
