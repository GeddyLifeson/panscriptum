# Of the Ferry on the Abhainn fhionn

*LE 510 – 552*

## I. Of the Bell at Muileann naomh

Tormod mac Iain had been eighteen years crowned when the bell-founders of {{place:burg:326}} asked him to stand at the casting of the first bronze bell ever made on Dia-thìr for a hawk-house, and he went south to see it, partly because a king who is asked ought to go, and partly because he had heard the founders' own account of the mould and wanted, before he grew too old to care about such things, to watch metal take the shape of sound.

He was four-and-fifty that year, and had been called Tormod Bàn since before he was crowned, for hair gone the colour of good wool while he was still a young man; his father's line ran to early grey, and he had made his peace with it the way a man makes peace with weather. He stood at the founders' pit at {{date:III-0099}} with the vein's own custodians beside him, the households of Clann Fhearchair, Clann Raghnaill and the rest come down to see a thing none of them had seen before, and watched the bronze poured and cooled and the bell hoisted and hung and rung for the first time under a clear sky, a sound none of the hawk-keepers present had ever heard from anything but a human throat or a struck stave. It cracked in its first hard frost that same winter, a fault low in the lip that no one had thought to test for, and the founders cast a second the following spring, thicker at the waist, and that one held, and rang for the burning that year and every year after.

Tormod said little at either casting. He was a king who had learned, watching his own father and grandfather's short reigns pass one after another through a house that had buried three kings in a generation before he was born, that a crown which spoke seldom and watched closely outlasted one that spoke often; and Muileann naomh, in the years that followed, became known the length of the south coast as the house where the Hawk had first put its call into bronze, and rang its bell at every measured burning long after the manner of casting it had passed out of anyone's living memory but the founders' own sons.

It became something more than that within a dozen years. Hawk-houses further north, on the coasts where the Stone or the sting-oath of Manannan pressed close against the Hawk's own ground, had begun by then to let their teaching thin from one generation of keepers to the next, a rite kept out of habit more than conviction in towns where half the young no longer troubled to learn the old questions; but at {{place:burg:326}} the teaching held as firmly as the bell held its note, {{date:III-0101}}, so that the house came to be counted, by common report up and down the coast, the southernmost place where the Hawk's own word was still said in full and believed in full, and pilgrims who wanted the old teaching plainly given, without a northern town's newer softenings, began to come to Muileann naomh from towns a week's walk further south than any hawk-house had drawn from before.

His household by then was a small one for a king's: a queen, Oighrig nic Dhùghaill, of a western house that had given the crown no trouble in three generations, and two sons born in the early years of his reign, both alive and grown by then into the ordinary business of being a king's sons, which was mostly waiting. He had, besides them, a brother nearer his own age than either boy, Aonghas mac Iain, settled at {{place:burg:166}} on the south-west coast with a household of his own, and a half-brother younger than both of them, Dùghall, whom their father had got on a second wife of Baile Mòr dhomhain and who kept, by his own choice more than anyone's decree, to that house and its country rather than to the red hall. Tormod had never quarrelled with either of them. He had watched enough of his own line's short reigns to know that a house which quarrelled among its own brothers before it needed to was a house asking to be quarrelled with later, and he meant, so far as any king could mean such a thing, not to be that kind of fool.

## II. Of An Gàire Dubh

Five years on, in the moon the wood-watchers call Sìolach, a storm the south-coast rolls name An Gàire Dubh, the Black Laugh, came up out of the west in a single night and drove the sea clean over the waterfront of {{place:burg:190}}, the other Cuan dhearg, on the red cliffs of the south, and spoiled the whole season's salt fish where it hung drying on the racks below the town.

The tidings reached Cathair dhearg nine days later by a rider who had ridden two horses to death getting there, and Tormod heard them in the hall at {{date:III-0100}} standing rather than seated, which his own scribes remarked on afterward as a thing he did only when a report troubled him past sitting still for it. He sent grain south before the roads closed for the winter, not because Cuan dhearg's reeve had asked for it — no rider had come with any such asking, only the bare report of the wave — but because he judged, as he told his household plainly enough that a scribe wrote it down, that a town which had just lost a season's food had better things to spend its breath on than begging for what any king with eyes could see it needed.

Cuan dhearg rebuilt that winter and the spring after, a full street back from the old waterfront this time, on ground the sea had never yet reached in any telling the town's oldest households could call to mind, and it held. Tormod rode down himself the following summer to see the new street laid, the only time in eighteen years of kingship, his scribes noted, that he had gone anywhere on the strength of a storm alone rather than a war, a crowning, or a rite the crown was bound to keep.

He walked the new street with the town's reeve, an old salter named Pàdraig Odhar who had lost his own drying racks to the wave and rebuilt them with the rest, and asked him, as they came to the end of the street where the ground began to fall back toward the old waterfront, whether the town had thought of building further back still, out of the wave's reach entirely rather than merely out of this one wave's reach. Pàdraig Odhar said that a fisher who builds too far from the water spends his life walking to it and back, and that a street a street further back was as far as any salter of Cuan dhearg meant to walk for the rest of his days, wave or no wave. Tormod said nothing more of it, judging, as he judged most things by then, that a king who had never salted a fish in his life had less business telling a fisher how far from the sea to live than a fisher had telling a king how far from the vein to dig.

## III. Of the Naming at Tobar dhìreach

It was the custom of Tormod's house to have every child born into the king's own household named at {{place:burg:276}}, among the wells that claim the mason's crack, rather than in the red hall at Cathair dhearg, a custom his mother had brought into the family and that none of her sons had troubled to break; and in the moon of Bealltach, nine-and-twenty years crowned, he rode south again, this time for the naming of a steward's son, the child of Ailean mac Lachlainn, who had kept the king's own horses and hounds since before Tormod was crowned and asked, as was his right, for his newest boy to be named under the same slab as the king's own children had been.

A reciter of Clann na Ceiste came down from the keepers' house to say the boy's line over him, as their order had done for every child named there since before Tormod's grandfather was crowned, and the reciters of that kindred kept, among the lines they knew, more than one thread a listener might be given: most often, for a child of the king's own household, the long line of Somhairle, the maker whose name every naming at Tobar dhìreach had called on for generations past counting. This reciter, an old man named Muireach mac Ailein who had said the naming over Tormod's own children and thought himself long past being asked to choose anything new, stood over the boy a long moment with his hand not yet raised to begin, and then said, to the mild surprise of the household gathered and the plain unsurprise of no one at all, the line of the Mason instead: Neachdan and those who came after him, back to the first fire taken from the burnt vein.

Nobody asked him why. It was not the custom to ask a reciter why one line came to his mouth before another; the lines were held, Clann na Ceiste's own teaching ran, not chosen, and a reciter who felt one line rise in him over another said what rose. Tormod, standing at the back of the household with his own two sons beside him grown men now and both a head taller than their father, thought little of it beyond the ordinary pleasure a king takes in a well-said naming for a servant who had earned one. Muireach mac Ailein himself, asked once by a younger keeper of the house why the Mason's line and not Somhairle's had come to him that day, said only that a reciter does not choose his own mouth's business any more than a river chooses which bank it wears away, and that the boy's own line, whatever it carried, was his to grow into and no one else's to read ahead of him.

## IV. Of the Child at Inis thais

The next year, in Faolach, word came from {{place:burg:166}} that Aonghas mac Iain's wife, Mairead nic Ailein, had borne a daughter, the first child of that marriage to live past her own naming, and Tormod, who loved his brother more plainly than he had ever managed to say to him outright, rode the sixteen miles down to the coast himself rather than send a rider with the crown's ordinary good wishes.

He found his brother's household in the small stir any first living child brings to a house that has waited for one, Aonghas himself more pleased than Tormod had seen him in years and no less awkward for it, holding the girl as a man holds something he is half afraid of dropping and wholly unwilling to hand to anyone else. They named her Catrìona, for no reason either brother could afterward remember clearly enough to write down, and Tormod stood at the naming with his own two grown sons beside him, come south with their father as they had come to Tobar dhìreach the year before, both a little restless at a rite that was, to their eyes, no more than a cousin's ordinary naming and no business of theirs to stand still for.

Aonghas asked his brother, that evening, over the kind of talk two brothers have when the day's business is done and neither is yet ready to sleep, whether a girl born to a king's brother carried any weight in the world beyond her own father's house. Tormod said he did not know, and did not think it mattered much either way, since the crown sat well enough on his own two sons and would sit on their sons after them, God and the ford willing; and Aonghas, hearing that, said nothing more of it, and the two of them sat a while longer by the fire talking of lesser things, a good catch that season and a dispute over a boundary ditch that Tormod would have to settle before the moon was out.

## V. Of Aonghas mac Iain's Death

Aonghas mac Iain died four years later, at {{place:burg:166}}, in his six-and-fiftieth year, of a wasting that took him slowly enough that his household had time to send word to Cathair dhearg and Tormod time to ride down and sit with him three days before the end, though not long enough for either brother to say all that two brothers who have never quarrelled might still wish they had said.

Tormod buried him at Inis thais rather than bringing the body north, judging, as he told Mairead nic Ailein when she asked whether the crown would want its own brother laid at the red hall, that a man who had chosen to live his whole life on that coast had earned the right to lie in its ground rather than in soil his own feet had rarely troubled to stand on. He rode home slower than he had ridden down, by his own scribes' account, and was heard to say once on the road, to no one in particular, that a king buries his father and his brothers and learns nothing from either burying that a farmer does not learn from his own, save that a king's grief has a great many more people standing about to watch him carry it.

Catrìona, four years old that spring, was too young to be told plainly what had happened to her father and was told instead, in the way households tell such things to the very young, that he had gone on ahead of them to wait, and she asked, for some years after, when the household might be going to join him, until she grew old enough to stop asking and to understand the answer she had already half worked out for herself.

Her mother kept the household at Inis thais rather than take her daughter north to the red hall, though Tormod offered more than once to have them both come and live under his own roof, judging, as she told him plainly enough on the one occasion he pressed it, that a fatherless girl raised in a king's own household would grow up thinking herself owed something no one had yet agreed she was owed, and that she would rather Catrìona grew up a shipwright's neighbour and a fisher's neighbour than a king's ward with a claim nobody had named aloud. Tormod did not press it again, though he rode down to see them twice every year for as long as his own legs and the roads allowed it, and it was noticed, by those of his household who rode with him, that he never once, on any of those visits, spoke to the girl about her father in any way she could later have called an answer.

## VI. Of the Ferry on the Abhainn fhionn

Four years after that, in the wet end of Damhrach when the {{place:river:28}} ran high with the season's rains, Tormod's two sons, well into their thirties by then and neither yet married to a house that suited every custodian's liking, rode north together on the crown's business, a dispute between two shires over a fishing weir that neither elder brother nor younger had much taste for settling but that their father had sent them to settle together, judging it past time the two of them learned to carry the crown's small business between them before either was asked to carry anything larger.

The ferry that crossed the Abhainn fhionn at the ford-keepers' crossing had carried carts and cattle and kings' messengers across that water for longer than any ferryman then living could say, a broad flat hull worked by a rope strung bank to bank, and the ferryman that day, a man named Iain Odhar who had worked the rope himself for eleven years and lost neither man nor beast off it in all that time, judged the water high but not past crossing, as he had judged it a hundred times before in a hundred wet ends of Damhrach, and brought the hull out into the current with the two king's sons, their four horses, and a farmer's cart of wool besides.

The elder of the two, by every account gathered after, had stood near the rope himself for most of the crossing, asking Iain Odhar the ordinary questions a man asks a ferryman he does not often ride with, how deep the channel ran at this height of water and how the rope held against a current that strong, more from the plain habit of a man who liked to understand a thing than from any fear of the water; the younger stood further back with the horses, keeping them quiet, for the elder was always the one who talked to strangers and the younger the one who minded the beasts, a division of the work between them that had held since they were boys and that neither had ever troubled to question.

What came down the river that morning, from higher ground than any of them could see, was a spate the water above had not shown any sign of an hour before, a wall of brown water carrying broken timber and the wrack of some upstream bank, and it took the ferry broadside before the rope could be run in, tipped the flat hull on its beam, and put every soul and beast aboard into water running too fast and too cold for swimming to matter much either way. Iain Odhar came ashore a quarter mile below the crossing, half drowned and clinging to a length of his own rope, and lived another thirty years telling, when anyone asked and a good many times when no one had, that he had reached for the elder of the two boys and had the young man's sleeve in his own hand for the length of three strokes before the current took it from him.

The bodies were found two days apart, the younger downstream on a gravel bar within the day, the elder not until the second morning, caught in driftwood a mile further down where the river widened before Loch chrom. Tormod was at Cathair dhearg when the first rider came, and it is told there that he heard the man out standing, as he had stood to hear the tidings of Cuan dhearg's storm years before, and said nothing at all until the rider had finished, and then asked only which son, and was told it was not yet known which, since only one body had yet come ashore, and stood a while longer with that not-knowing before he asked anything else. {{date:III-0102}} is the day the custody-book sets against it, though the finding of the second body came two days later, and the custody-book, for once, notes both dates rather than choosing between them.

He had the ferry rope cut and burned that same week, and ordered a second ferry built a mile upstream where the ford-keepers judged the crossing safer in spate, though he never crossed the water again by either rope, in all the years he had left to him, without a rider going ahead first to test how the current ran.

Iain Odhar came to Cathair dhearg himself that winter, on foot, to say before the king what he had already said before the ford-keepers and the shire's own reeve: that he had judged the water as he had judged it a hundred times, that no spate of that height had ever come down without warning in his eleven years on the rope, and that he asked no pardon, since he did not hold himself guilty of anything a hundred crossings before it had not already proven safe to do. Tormod heard him out standing, in the same way he had heard the first rider out, and told him at the end of it that a king does not pardon a man for a river's business, since the river had asked no leave of either of them; and he gave the ferryman a small holding of land above the new crossing, not as payment for the two lives the old crossing had taken, which no holding could pay for, but as a keeper's fee for a man who would watch the new rope the rest of his days more closely than any king's order could make him. Iain Odhar took the land and the office both, and watched the new rope, by every account, more closely than any king's order could have made him for the thirty years he had left to keep it.

## VII. Of the Thirteen Years

He had no other child, and no brother living now but Dùghall, whom he had never distrusted and never quite loved either, the two of them being too far apart in their mothers and their years to have grown close as boys, and too settled in their separate lives as men to grow close afterward. The houses did not press him to name an heir in the first year, out of what plain kindness a court can manage toward a man burying two sons within a week of each other, but by the second year the custodians of the vein-house had begun, quietly and among themselves at first, to reckon who stood nearest the crown should the king die without naming one, and by the fourth year the reckoning was no longer quiet.

Three names came to stand nearer than any others. Dùghall, the king's own half-brother, had a following at Baile Mòr dhomhain that owed him rather than the crown its first loyalty, built up over years of holding that country's own small disputes without troubling the red hall to settle them. Catrìona, Aonghas's daughter, grew up at Inis thais under her mother's roof, the last of the old king's own blood by his brother's line, and though no one had yet said the word claimant to her face, she had begun, by the time she was fifteen, to notice how the households of that coast spoke to her with a weight they did not spend on other girls of her age, and to wonder, without yet asking anyone plainly, what that weight was for. And Raghnall Ruadh, head of Clann Raghnaill, was made master of the vein-house at {{place:burg:491}} in the year Catrìona turned fourteen, the custodians choosing him over two older men of longer service because, as one of them put it to a colleague afterward, a vein-house that might soon answer to no clear crown at all had better answer, in the meantime, to the steadiest hand available; and Raghnall took the office and the custody-book both, and said nothing at all, in the years that followed, about any claim beyond the one he already held.

A councillor who had served him since before the ferry asked him plainly, in the seventh year, whether he meant to name one of the three before age chose for him, and Tormod answered that he had buried two sons who had done nothing to deserve the water that took them, and that he was not minded to hand a crown to a niece, a half-brother or a vein-master while there was still breath in him to hold it himself a while longer, whatever the custodians whispered among their own counting. The councillor did not raise the question again, and neither, in the years that followed, did any other man of the red hall, though the counting went on regardless of what was or was not said to the king's own face.

Tormod watched the three of them grow into whatever they were growing into, from a red hall grown quieter each year, and did what a childless old king does who cannot bring himself to choose among three claims he has not yet been asked to choose among: he ruled the small business in front of him, a boundary here, a grain dispute there, the yearly burning kept as it had always been kept, and let the larger question stand unanswered, telling himself, in the years he still had strength to tell himself anything, that there was time yet, and that a crown decided in haste was worse than a crown left, for one more season, undecided.

## VIII. Of the King's Death and the Houses Divided

Tormod Bàn died at Cathair dhearg in his seven-and-eightieth year, {{date:III-0103}}, in his own bed and by no hand but age's, having named no heir in all the years he had left after the ferry, and it is told at the red hall that his last clear words, said to the scribe sitting with him, were an order that the boundary dispute at Àth chiar, still unsettled after three seasons, should be given to the custodians to finish rather than left for whoever came after him to inherit along with the crown.

He was one-and-fifty years crowned, among the longest reigns his house had seen, and the scribe who wrote his death into the custody-book noted, in a line no one had asked him to write and that he might as easily have left out, that the king had outlived his brother and both his sons, and had ruled the small business of a kingdom faithfully to the very end of a life that had given him, by any household's ordinary reckoning, more to grieve than to rule.

The seven houses met within the month, as the custom of a vacant hall required, and found that no house among them could bring itself to name one of the three claimants over the other two, Dùghall having the loyalty of the western country, Catrìona the old blood and a growing following of her own along the south-west coast, and Raghnall the vein-house and the custody-book both in his keeping. So the houses held the hall in common: a council of custodians sitting in the red hall in the crown's stead, keeping the small business of the kingdom moving, the grain rulings made and the yearly burning kept, while the larger question sat over all of it unanswered, the way a debt sits over a household that has agreed among itself only that it must, eventually, be paid.

It could not hold. In the fifth year of the common council, within the same season, messengers came from Baile Mòr dhomhain, from Inis thais, and from Muileann dhearg, each naming the same crown for a different head, and the council of custodians, which had held the hall in common as long as holding it in common was possible, found that it no longer was. {{date:III-0104}}: three claims stood laid before the houses at once where one crown had stood a generation before, and the seven houses, which had kept a common council for five careful years rather than choose among the three, chose at last, each house for itself, and found themselves divided.
