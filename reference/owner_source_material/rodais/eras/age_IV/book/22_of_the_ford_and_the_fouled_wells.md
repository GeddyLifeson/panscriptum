# Of the Ford and the Fouled Wells

*LE 552 – 557*

## I. Of the Gates Shut Against All Three

When the three claims were laid at once before the houses, in the year the custody-book calls the year of the three crowns, there was one town on the capital shore that did neither. Seann Warr stood on the western water with its salt-pans steaming in the cold and its citadel above the harbour, and when the heralds of Catrìona nic Aonghais, of Dùghall Garbh and of Raghnall Ruadh came one after another to its western gate, each was met by the same woman, and given the same answer.

Mairead nic Ruairidh had held the reeve's staff of Seann Warr for eleven years, mostly for settling disputes over the flax fields and the price of salt, and had never expected to hold it for anything harder. The town she governed was a small one as the capital shore counted towns, its wall built in the Holy Age against raiders who had not come in generations, its citadel more often used for storing salt-fish than for standing a watch; but it sat on the one road that ran the whole length of the western shore, and every cart that went from the salt-pans to the capital's own market passed under its gate, so that a claimant who wished to move grain or men along that shore had first to reckon with whoever held the bar across Seann Warr's road.

She heard the first herald out in the gate-house, standing, with her arms folded in her shawl against the wind off the loch, and when he had finished naming his claimant's right she said only that Seann Warr answered to no house until the houses could agree among themselves which one it answered to, and that the gate would open to trade and shut to hosts. The herald, a young man of Cathair dhearg with a good horse and a better opinion of his own errand, told her that this was no answer a reeve of a town so small had any right to give three claimants to a crown. She told him that a town so small had exactly the right its wall could hold, and that his own claimant was welcome to come and find out how much that was.

She said the same to the second herald that afternoon and the same to the third the day after, word for word, so that afterwards men said she had learned it by rote before the first ever came, though she had not; she had simply found, each time, that it was still true, and had seen no reason to trouble herself with new words for an old fact.

{{date:III-0105}} is the day the custody-book sets down her answer, though it came, in truth, three times over three days, and was the same answer each time. The bar went up across the western gate that evening, and the men of the town's own watch, farmers and salters who had never carried more than a fowling spear, stood it in turn through that winter and every winter after. Few of them loved one claimant over another; they stood the gate because Mairead nic Ruairidh had told them plainly what would happen to Seann Warr if it chose wrong, and none of them could tell her she was mistaken.

## II. Of the First Siege

Dùghall Garbh came first, in the moon of Faolach the following year, with a host of some four hundred, and sat down before the walls a fortnight, cutting the road east and turning back the carts of salt that were the town's whole trade. He did not want the town badly enough to burn it; what he wanted was for it to open for him rather than starve, and he waited to see which it would do.

It did neither. The salt-pans went on steaming inside the walls, for a town with the sea at its gate does not go hungry as a landward one does, and the brine springs behind the reed marsh gave up more salt than four hundred idle men could eat fish to trade for. Mairead nic Ruairidh sent word out on the ninth night, by a boy who swam the loch shallows below the wall, that the town would give Dùghall's host bread for silver at the market rate and nothing else, and that his men were welcome to buy it standing in the road like any other traders. Some of them did. By the fortnight's end his own carters were grumbling louder than her townsfolk, and he took his host east again, having spent more silver feeding it before Seann Warr's wall than the town's whole autumn trade was worth.

"You have made a fool of him," said her steward, watching the last of Dùghall's spears go up the road.

"I have made nothing of him," Mairead nic Ruairidh said. "He made a fool of himself, coming to sit before a town that keeps its own salt. I only told him the price of bread."

## III. Of the Ford at Àth shean

It is told at Cathair dhearg, from the custody-book and from the men who carried the tale home, how Dùghall Garbh and Raghnall Ruadh met at the northern ford of Àth shean in the spring of the third year of the war, each having heard that the other meant to cross there, and neither willing to let the other hold the crossing first.

Raghnall Ruadh came to the ford by the straight road, in good order, and made his camp on the near bank in the last light, meaning to force the crossing at first light before Dùghall's host, coming by the longer eastern way, could reach the far side in strength. What he did not know, and what the ford-keepers of Àth shean, who owed him no love after two winters of the vein-house's silence toward their town, did not trouble to tell him, was that the river ran a mile of shallows above the town where a man could wade it dry-shod at low water, and that Dùghall's guides, drawn from among the drovers who had used that shallows for forty years to move cattle without paying the ford toll, had told him of it three days before.

Dùghall crossed there in the last watch of the night, in silence, in a column two men wide, the water at the shallows no higher than a man's waist and running cold enough that the drovers who led the crossing had told his men beforehand to bind their weapon-belts high and their breath low, for a man who gasped at the cold could be heard across still water a long bowshot off. It took the better part of two hours to bring four hundred men over dry enough to fight, and Dùghall stood on the far bank the whole while, counting them across in the dark by touch as much as sight, because he had given his word to no man that the shallows would hold and meant to be the last one over if they did not.

They came down on Raghnall's camp from the landward side an hour before dawn, while Raghnall's own watch stood facing the ford and the empty road beyond it, looking for a crossing that was not going to be made where they were looking for it. What followed at the ford was not a battle so much as a breaking. Raghnall's host, caught between the river and a host it had not seen come, went for the water in the dark, some to swim, most simply to be away from the spears, and the ford took more of them than Dùghall's men did. Men who had crossed that ford on market days their whole lives, who knew its stones by their bare feet, went into it in the dark not knowing which bank they were swimming for, and the custody-book's own account, taken from survivors on both sides in the weeks after, says that more than one man drowned within a spear's throw of solid ground because he had turned himself about in the water and could no longer tell which way the current ran.

The custody-book counts three hundred dead the next morning when the bodies had come down onto the gravel bars below the town, and says plainly that it could not count how many more the river carried on to the sea uncounted. Raghnall Ruadh himself got away with perhaps a third of his host, riding, they say, without a cloak, having left it and much else on the near bank in the dark, and did not stop riding, by his own men's account, until he had put two days between himself and the ford.

Word of it reached Catrìona nic Aonghais at Cathair dhomhain nine days later, where she lay before the walls with her own host, and she heard it standing in the door of her tent with the morning's report of the siege lines still in her other hand, and said nothing for the length of time it takes a man to walk twice round a small fire, and then asked only whether Dùghall meant to press east after Raghnall or turn south. The messenger did not know. She sent him back with an answer for Dùghall that named no ally and asked for none, only that if he came south of the ford he should come knowing that Cathair dhomhain held for him, not for her, and that she would not thank him for taking it out from under her while her lines were dug.

## IV. Of Doire mhin, and of the Child Seònaid

Raghnall Ruadh's men, going south from the ford in the days after, three hundred short of the host they had been and with no silver, no grain and no standing anywhere on the road home, came in the evening to the granary village of Doire mhin, which had given carts and coal both to the vein-house without complaint for as long as anyone there could remember, and had done nothing at all to deserve what it got that night, save stand on the road they had to pass.

They took what grain the granary held, which was less than they needed, and fired the granary and a dozen houses about it before they went on. The custody-book gives them small credit for hatred; they burned the place, it says, so that whoever followed them south, whether Dùghall's men or Catrìona's, should find nothing there to feed on. {{date:III-0107}} is set against it, the second burning the custody-book records in that war and, as it turned out, the last.

Doire mhin had never been a large village, forty-one households at the fire-count the vein-house kept of it, set in a fold of good barley land with a granary at its centre built of stone against fire and flood both, and had given its grain and its coal-carts to the crown's stores for longer than any household there could trace in its own memory. Coinneach mac Ailein farmed eleven acres of it, barley and a strip of flax by the stream, and had two things he was known for on the village green: a temper slow enough that men said you could walk to the granary and back before it rose, and a daughter who asked more questions in a day than the priest asked in a season.

Seònaid nic Coinnich was six years old and asleep when the first of it came through the wall of the house. She remembered afterward, when she was old enough to write it down herself, very little of the fire and a great deal of her father's hands: how Coinneach mac Ailein had her under one arm and a sack of the year's seed-corn under the other before she was properly awake, and ran with her out through the smoke into the yard, where her mother already stood with the two younger children and nothing else, the byre door standing open behind her and the cow already loose and gone into the dark, because a beast, her father said afterward, has better sense in a fire than most men.

They stood at the edge of the near field and watched the granary go, and Seònaid, who did not yet understand what a granary going meant to a household that lived by it, asked her father why the men with the spears let it burn. Coinneach mac Ailein gave her no answer that night. He answered her years later, when she asked him again as a grown woman with a clerk's stylus in her hand, and by then he could say it plainly: those men had come to burn, and a man who has come to burn does not stop for a bucket.

They walked south for six days, with what neighbours had lost the same and no more, forty households or near it, carrying children and seed-corn and very little else, sleeping the first two nights in a byre a farmer three miles off let them share with his own cattle, and the rest under whatever hedge or wall the road offered, because word of the burning had run ahead of them and few doors opened easily to forty households with nothing to trade for a night's floor. Seònaid's mother carried the youngest the whole way and would not be carried herself, though she had been on her feet since before the fire and her own feet, by the fourth day, had begun to bleed through her shoes; Coinneach mac Ailein carried the seed-corn on his back the whole distance and would not set it down even to sleep, because it was, he said, the only thing that stood between the household and a second winter as bad as this one, whatever a first winter without a granary was going to be.

They came at last to Cathair mhòr, where the temple of Macha stood open on the market square and its priests, who had fed the burnt-out and the plague-struck of that shore for longer than the temple's own records ran, took them in without asking whose claimant they had voted for, because the temple had never asked that of anyone and was not going to begin for a war it had not made. The temple's own roll, set apart from the custody-book's numbered days, records forty-one households sheltered at Cathair mhòr that winter on the temple's grain, Coinneach mac Ailein's among them, and gives their names in a hand that runs on for the better part of a page.

Seònaid remembered the temple floor as warmer than the house had been, which puzzled her all her life, since the house had had its own hearth-coal and the temple floor had none to speak of; she came to think, later, that it was only that so many bodies lay close together on it, and that this was itself a kind of answer to the question her father would not answer, though it took her another twenty years to see it as one.

## V. Of the Second Siege

Dùghall Garbh, having broken Raghnall at the ford, spent the two years after in the slow, grinding work of holding what the ford had won him, and came to Seann Warr a second time, better provisioned and in no mood to buy his own bread at the market rate, and sat before it a full month.

Mairead nic Ruairidh did what she had done before, save that this time the town had less salt trade to spare and the winter came on hard behind the siege, so that by the month's end there were nights when the watch on the western wall stood without dry boots and the town's own children went without the second meal. What broke the siege, in the end, was a rider from the east, three days from Cathair dhearg at speed, who brought Dùghall word that Catrìona had sat down at last before Cathair dhomhain's own walls in earnest, closing its water gate as well as its land gate, and that if he meant to have any voice in the fate of a city that had held for him five years, he could not spend the winter sitting before a town that had never once opened a gate to him and plainly never would.

He took his host east a second time, and Seann Warr did not open its gates to any of the three claimants in the whole ten years of the war that followed, though it buried, by the war's end, more of its own townsfolk to cold and short rations than it ever lost to a spear. The salt-pans never stopped steaming through either siege, winter or summer, because Mairead nic Ruairidh held that a town which let its own trade go idle for another man's war had already lost something no gate could keep out; and it is told that on the worst nights of the second siege, with the watch standing the wall in wet boots and half the town's children crying with hunger, she walked the wall herself before dawn each morning with a lantern. There was little to see by it in the dark; the watch stood easier only for watching her carry it.

Mairead nic Ruairidh held the reeve's staff another twenty years after the war's end, through the crowning and the quiet that followed it, and when she died the town buried her, by her own asking, standing in the gate-house doorway, facing the road, so that she should go on watching it, her steward said, whether or not there was anything left on it worth the watching.

## VI. Of Cathair dhomhain, and the Wells

Cathair dhomhain had declared for Dùghall Garbh in the second year of the war, chiefly because its harbour-master had once carried grain for him as a young man and thought well of him, and Catrìona nic Aonghais came against it in the autumn of the sixth year with a host she had held together, through two lean winters, on little more than her own word that she would not disband it while a rival crown stood claimed against her own.

The city stood well for a siege: walled in the Holy Age, its citadel above the river mouth, its granaries deep enough to hold two winters' grain against one summer's siege, and its harbour open behind it to the loch, so that at first Catrìona's lines along the landward wall did nothing at all but let the city go on feeding itself by water, while her own men grew thin on requisitioned oats and the households of her own following began, quietly at first and then less quietly, to ask her captains how much longer a siege that fed the besieged better than the besiegers was worth keeping.

She called her captains to her tent on the seventieth night of it and asked them, one by one, what they would do in her place, and had from most of them the same counsel: raise the siege, or storm the wall while the host still had the strength to storm it, for a siege that starved no one was no siege at all. Only her harbour-master, a fisher's son of Inis thais who had come to her following for no better reason than that his own boat had been burned in the war's first year, said that the wall could be starved as easily as the granaries, for a wall's real weakness lay less in its stone than in its water; and it was from him, a man bred to boats and never to sieges, that she took the notion that broke it. She broke it in the eleventh week by sending her own boats, sewn coasters borrowed and begged from every fishing household on the near shore, out onto the loch by night to close the water gate as her lines closed the land gate, and after that the siege began to be a siege in earnest.

It ran forty days from the water being shut. The custody-book gives the figure plainly and gives no other: forty days, and no battle in them worth a name of its own, only the slow grinding-down that a siege is, worse for those inside a wall than for those camped before it, because those camped before it can at least walk away from their own dead. Cathair dhomhain buried its dead of the siege, at first, in the ground beyond its own walls where custom set the burying-ground; but a city under close siege cannot always spare the men, the daylight, or the ground beyond the wall for burying, and toward the end the dead of the siege lines, its own soldiers fallen at the wall and those Catrìona's engines had reached within it, were laid nearer the well-heads than any custom would have allowed in a season of peace, because there was nowhere else near enough to lay them.

The wells fouled in the fifth week of the closer siege. It began, as such things do, quietly: a taste in the water that the brewers noticed before anyone else, and then a sickness of the gut that ran first through the youngest and the oldest in the crowded inner streets, and then through the garrison itself, so that men who had held the wall a month against Catrìona's engines began to fall to something no engine had thrown. The garrison's captain, Niall Mòr mac Leòid, held the wall another nine days after the sickness took hold among his own men, because he had sworn to hold it and did not, in his own account of it afterward, know how a man unswears an oath once it is given; but by the thirty-ninth day he had buried more of his garrison to the water than to Catrìona's spears, and on the fortieth he sent to her lines under a branch, asking terms.

## VII. Of the Oath, and the Garrison Walking Out

Catrìona nic Aonghais received his herald standing in the door of her own tent, as she had stood to hear the news of the ford three years before, and this time she did not send the man away with a short answer. She asked him, first, how many of the garrison still stood, and he told her, and she was quiet over the number longer than the length of time it takes to walk round a small fire.

"Tell your captain," she said at last, "that I will take the city, because it has held five years for a claim I do not mean to let stand. But I will not take it over the drowned wells of forty days, and add his men's names to the stone the priests will raise for the well-sickness. Let him march his garrison out under arms, with their own wounded carried and their own dead named, and swear before my whole line that they will not take the field against me again while this war runs, and I will let them go to their own homes, wherever those stand."

The herald asked what surety she would take for the oath, since Niall Mòr mac Leòid had, after all, held the city five years for her rival.

"His word, given before my whole host with his own wounded watching him give it," Catrìona said. "A man does not swear falsely with his own dying men at his back to hear him. If I am wrong in that, I will have learned something worth the learning, and it will not be the first city I have to take twice."

Niall Mòr mac Leòid marched his garrison out of Cathair dhomhain on the forty-first day, some three hundred left of the six hundred that had held it, between two lines of Catrìona's host drawn up silent on either side of the road, and swore the oath standing with his own carried wounded set down behind him where the whole line could see them, in a voice, those who heard it said, that did not shake though the man himself, by then, could barely stand from nine days without proper food or sleep. Catrìona rode the length of both lines afterward, alone, and said nothing to any man in them until she came to where Niall Mòr stood, and there she got down from her horse and took his hand in both of hers before the whole host, garrison and besiegers together, and told him he had held the city better than most kings hold a kingdom, and that she hoped, when this war was done, to have the use of a man who kept his oaths that well.

He did not take service with her then; he went home first, to whatever was left of a household five years' siege had not seen, and it was two years more before he came to her at Cathair dhearg and offered his sword for what remained of the war. But it is told at Cathair dhomhain still, and was told to Seònaid nic Coinnich herself when she gathered the old men's memories of that war for her book, that the garrison walked out between the two silent lines with the wells still fouled behind the wall they left, and that not one spear was raised against them the whole length of the road, though there were men in Catrìona's own lines who had buried brothers to arrows shot from that same wall not a fortnight before.
