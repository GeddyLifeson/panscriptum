# Of the Crown Joined to the Vein

*LE 841 – 939*

> *Is buan a' bheinn, is goirid an rìgh.*
> Lasting is the mountain, short is the king.
>
> — said at {{place:burg:491}} of every ruler since

## I. Of the Empty Hall

Queen Eilidh Bhàn died at {{place:burg:19}} at ninety years old, on {{date:III-0136}}, in the same red hall she had been crowned in at eighteen, and with her the house of Catrìona, six rulers and near three hundred years, came to its end. She left no child living and had never named one to come after her; the clerks of the red hall, who had served her seventy-two years and thought they had prepared for every ordinary grief a crown could bring, found they had prepared for nothing at all for this one, and stood about the hall for the better part of a day not knowing whose office it now was to give an order. One of them, an old man who had cut her own coronation roll as a boy of twelve, was heard to say that he had buried five clerks under her and had never once thought to ask who would bury the queen herself, since he had somehow supposed, without ever putting the thought into words, that she would simply go on.

Riders went out that same evening to the seven houses, as riders had gone out at every death of a ruler for two centuries, each carrying the same plain summons; and it is told that the seven houses did not meet at Cathair dhearg to settle what should follow, as the custom of two hundred years might have led a stranger to expect, but rode instead to {{place:burg:491}}, up to Taigh na Fèithe above the mountain, and sat there among the galleries and the lot-stones and the old smell of tallow and coal-dust rather than under the red hall's own roof. No one wrote why, and the custody-book, when it came at last to enter the matter, entered only the fact and not the reason. A scribe of the vein-house who was young that year and very old when she told it afterward said that the head of one house had proposed the red hall first, as custom asked, and that another had answered him, not unkindly, that a house with no ruler in it was no fit place to make one, and that the mountain, at least, had never once in all its years stood empty of an office worth the sitting in. Nobody argued the point a second time. It seemed to every head who rode up the hill, she said, a plainer thing to choose a king among the lot-stones than among the tapestries the house of Catrìona had hung, and nobody thought to question it further because it felt, from the first hour, less like a choosing than like a homecoming.

They sat first nine days in the long room under the vein-house's north window, the same table at which Eòghann mac Pheadair and the crown's own scribe had once laid two counts of years side by side and found neither able to prove the other wrong. The capital sent no word and asked for none; a runner rode down twice a week with grain prices and boundary pleas for the clerks to hold against whatever hand should next take them up, and rode back again each time having settled nothing, for there was nobody yet at Cathair dhearg with the standing to settle it, and the clerks learned to keep the pleas in a growing stack rather than pretend an office existed to answer them. The houses rode home from that first sitting with nothing settled, and rode up again at each season's turning for five years, while the red hall stood empty and the stack of pleas grew. The sittings are not told entire in any leaf that survives, save that they were quarrelsome for the first years and quiet for the last, that two heads left the table more than once vowing they would not return and returned each time before the evening meal, and that in the fifth year a name was spoken that had not been spoken among the seven houses in living memory, and was not spoken down again once it had been raised.

## II. Of the Yard

Dòmhnall Ruadh was a man of {{place:burg:491}} in one sense and of the eastern hills in another, for his own household held its galleries and its house both at Muileann chrom, a morning's ride below the mountain, where snow still lay thick some winters on ground his own great-grandfather's father had once held before a battle changed whose it was. He was fifty-six that year, a quiet, careful man, heavier in the shoulders than in the face, who had spent most of his life managing three galleries of the coal-right and disputing boundary stones with neighbours who had long since stopped expecting to win an argument with him, for he kept his tallies as honestly as any custodian on the mountain and would give up a claim as readily as press one, if the measuring told against him. He had ridden up to the vein-house at the second summons not because he thought the matter concerned him — he said as much, more than once, to the men who rode beside him — but because every head of every house that held so much as a gallery's worth of the right had been asked to come and be counted among the voices, and Dòmhnall Ruadh did not think it his place to be the one man absent from a table his own house had a lawful seat at.

The last morning of the last sitting found him standing in the yard among perhaps forty men and women who had ridden up for the same counting and had nothing else to do with the waiting but stand in the cold and talk of anything but the choosing itself, when word came down from the hall that the houses had settled on a name; some little while passed before he understood, from the way the men nearest him had gone quiet and turned to look at him rather than past him, that the name was his own. He said afterward, to the one clerk who thought to ask him plainly what he had felt in that moment, that he had felt mostly a kind of embarrassment, standing in a yard he knew as well as his own threshing floor, in his working clothes with mud still dried grey on his boots from the ride, being looked at as though he had done something remarkable, when he had done nothing that whole morning but come when he was asked and wait his turn like any other man of the coal-right.

It was an old man among the custodians, one of the last few alive who could remember his own father speaking of it, who took Dòmhnall Ruadh a little apart from the others and told him, plainly and without much ceremony, whose yard he was standing in: that it was the same stones, worn no smoother in nearly three hundred years, on which another man of his own house, Raghnall Ruadh, had once stood before a season of refused hearths and a war that killed him, and had taken a board of claimants' names from a shaking clerk's hands and said, of every name on it, *none of these*, and turned and gone in and shut the door with a sound the old man's own father had called, all his life, a very great deal too small a sound for what had just been done. Dòmhnall Ruadh had known the story his whole life, as every child of Clann Raghnaill knew it, half as a shame kept low at table and half as a thing nobody in the house had ever quite managed to be ashamed enough of to stop telling; he stood a while with his hand on the same stone gatepost the old man pointed to, and said only that he hoped he would prove a different sort of answer to the same question the mountain had once been asked, and the old man told him that hoping so, standing where he stood, was a better beginning than most men who had ever been offered a crown were given.

He sent that same afternoon for his wife and his two grown sons to come up from Muileann chrom, and it was his wife, by every account, who wept at the news and Dòmhnall Ruadh who did not, sitting up late that first night going over, aloud and to nobody in particular, which of his neighbours he could trust to keep his own galleries measured honestly in his absence, as though the crown of Dia-thìr were one more piece of land he had been handed to manage and nothing grander. He was crowned nine days later at Cathair dhearg, in the hall his own house had never once, in nearly three hundred years, been asked to enter as anything but petitioners with a board of names and a plea; and the crown sat, those who were there said, a little awkwardly on a man who had spent the whole ride down from the mountain more troubled about a boundary quarrel he had left half-settled at Muileann chrom than about who would keep the kingdom he was riding to receive.

## III. Of the Joining

Through the first years of his reign he kept two households running side by side and badly, the crown's business at Cathair dhearg and the vein-house's business at the mountain, riding the thirty-nine miles between the two so often that the road's own keepers came to know his horse before they knew his face, and complained, in the way of men whose work has grown easier for a king's habits, that they had never in their lives shod one animal so many times in one man's service. The first winter he tried to hold court at Cathair dhearg for half the season and sit with the custodians at the mountain for the other half; by the second winter he had found that a boundary quarrel at the mountain waited no better on a king's absence than a grain plea at the capital, and that a man could not, in truth, be two rulers wearing one crown between two houses forty miles apart.

It was his reeves, not he, who first wrote the plain remedy into the custody-book, on {{date:III-0138}}: that the ruler of Dia-thìr was, from that day, also master of the vein-house, and that the two offices, which had grown up under different roofs since before any ruler now living remembered a time they were not separate, should from then on be kept under one seal and answered for by one hand. Dòmhnall Ruadh read the entry, when it was set before him for his own mark, a long while before he set it, and said to the reeve who had drafted it that it read less like a new law than like a plain account of a thing that had already, in practice, been true since the morning he stood in the vein-house yard; the reeve answered that most of the best laws he had ever copied were exactly that, and Dòmhnall Ruadh laughed, which those who knew him said he did rarely enough that the reeve remembered it the rest of his own life.

The heads of the seven houses, who had chosen him at the mountain and not at the hall, took their old office and carried it with them into the new arrangement without much debate, for it cost them, in the end, little that they had not already been doing in one form or another since the roll of the coal-right was first drawn. They sat thereafter as the king's own council at whichever of the two houses the business of the day required, and the custody-book and the crown's rolls, kept apart for near three hundred years on separate shelves under separate roofs, were from that season copied by the same scribes onto the same shelves, a labour that took the vein-house's clerks the better part of two years to finish and left, by the end of it, not one leaf of either record that a stranger could tell at a glance from the other.

The seal was cut that same year at {{place:burg:491}}, by a metalworker of the mountain town who had never before been asked to cut anything grander than a gallery-marker or a boundary stake, and who took, by his own telling afterward, near a season over the die where a marker would have cost him a morning. He complained, more than once, in the hearing of the custodians who came to watch him at it, that the gold would not sit as he wished it against the blue, and set the wax to the fire and cooled it and set it again more times than any of them cared to count; but what he cut, on {{date:III-0138a}}, was, when it was finished, judged by every custodian who saw it to be the vein-house's own old device exactly, unaltered in its bones: the trellis of gold that the custodians read as the galleries of the mountain, laid on a field of blue, and over it the hollow stone, the mascle, for the slab that had covered the first break long before any ruler's name was written anywhere on the island. Across the whole of it, at the crown's own asking and at nothing more, he set a single band of white, thin as a finger's width, running the field from side to side. Dòmhnall Ruadh, looking at the finished die before it was ever set to wax, turned it over once in his hands and said that he could find nothing in it that was properly his to add, and the master of the vein-house, an old woman who had buried three custodians and outlasted two kings before him, told him that a ruler who wished to add nothing to another man's seal was, in her own long memory of rulers, the rarest kind there was.

From that seal forward every letter the crown sent bore the mountain on it as plainly as it bore the crown's own arms, and no clerk at Cathair dhearg, writing out a grant or a summons in the years that followed, needed telling twice why a trellis and a hollow stone stood over the king's own name where once only a plainer mark of the house of Catrìona had stood.

## IV. Of the Mountain's Own King

He reigned five-and-twenty years, most of them, by his own account late in life, spent doing at the red hall exactly what he had once done at three galleries in Muileann chrom: walking boundaries, hearing disputes between neighbours who each believed the other had moved a stone, keeping a measure honest that others were forever tempted to lean on when they thought nobody was troubling to check. He never moved the seat of the crown to the mountain, though more than one of the seven heads pressed him to in the early years, saying plainly that a king who was also master of the vein-house belonged among the galleries and not among the tapestries the house of Catrìona had left behind; he answered, each time he was asked, that a crown kept too near its own coal soon forgot it had ever been asked to answer for anything else besides the coal, and that he meant, so long as he held it, for it to go on being asked to answer for a great deal more.

A quarrel came before him in his ninth year that those who served him remembered longer than most: two households of Muileann chrom, his own old neighbours, disputing a boundary stone that had stood, by both sides' account, exactly where it now stood for four generations, until a spring flood shifted the stream beside it and left each side certain the water, and not the stone, had moved. Dòmhnall Ruadh rode down from the capital himself rather than send a reeve, walked the ground with both households at his heels arguing the whole way, and settled it at last by asking each household's oldest living member, separately and out of the other's hearing, where they remembered playing as children; both answers agreed, within a few paces, and Dòmhnall Ruadh set the boundary there and told the assembled households, a little dryly, that he had settled more quarrels in his life with an old woman's memory of where she had once chased a goat than with every measuring-rod the vein-house owned.

He died at Cathair dhearg in his eighty-second year, on {{date:III-0139}}, the first man of his house in three centuries to be given, by the same seven heads who had once refused his ancestor to his face, every honour a dying king could be given: the lamps of the red hall kept lit three nights running, the roads from every shire seat sent a rider to carry the word, and the custody-book, when it came to enter his death, gave him a longer leaf than it had given any ruler before him, though the leaf said little more, in the end, than that he had joined the crown to the vein and kept both honestly for five-and-twenty years. He was carried, at his own asking made long before he was ill, past the house of Catrìona's tombs at the capital and up to {{place:burg:491}}, to be buried under the mountain his family's name had once been driven from by a war and a refused board of claimants. The custodians dug the grave themselves rather than send for labourers, working through a night and half a day in a gallery no longer worked for coal, its walls still black with the old dust, and laid him with a single unlit lamp at his head, which the vein-house's own rite asked for no other ruler before or since, the lamp's being unlit taken, by those who kept the rite, to mean that a man who had spent his whole reign making sure the measured handful reached every hearth honestly had no further need, in the ground, of a flame kept for him alone.

His son Raghnall Òg was crowned at Cathair dhearg a fortnight after, six-and-forty years old and already, from his father's long teaching, a good deal more at ease with a boundary dispute than with a court's ceremony, so that the clerks who had arranged his coronation found him asking, twice during the rehearsal of it, whether the whole business could not be shortened by an hour so that he might see a shire plea before dark. The crown and the vein-house went on being one office under him without a word needing to be said about it, as though the joining his father had written into the custody-book had already, in a single reign, stopped being a thing anyone thought to remark on at all.

It was in the years just after, while the mountain's own harbour of {{place:burg:36}} finished its stone mole and the first barges of the coal-roads' carters began tying up where before they had only beached on shingle at low water, that a rider bringing the news south remarked, without much thinking about it, to a clerk at Cathair dhearg that he had ridden the whole way from the mole to the red hall under one seal the entire road, the mountain's trellis pressed into the wax of every letter he carried, and had not troubled, for the first time in his years of carrying such news between the two houses, to wonder for a moment which office he rode for.

## V. Of the Hawk that Split

It is told that the Hawk of Acaill, whose priests had for six hundred years taught the vein as a bird fed a measured handful and never more, met in council at {{place:burg:20}} on {{date:III-0140}} and broke apart over a question its own founders had never thought to settle in all their teaching: whether the measure Mòd was fed could ever rightly be raised as the island's own numbers grew, or whether the handful fixed at the very first teaching, in a smaller age, must stand unchanged for as long as the order stood, whatever else changed around it. The council sat three days at Cnoc bheag, the elders on one side of the fire and the younger priests, most of them raised under teachers who had never questioned the measure at all, gathered on the other; and it is said that the argument turned at last on a single young priest rising to ask the elders, plainly, whether Mòd, could he be asked, would rather starve a flock that had outgrown his old handful than see the handful grown to meet it.

Those who held the older reading kept the name of An Seabhag Acrach, the Hungry Hawk, and kept the house at Cnoc bheag as it had always stood; those who held that a greater flock, grown these many generations past the numbers the first teaching had ever reckoned on, might rightly be fed a greater handful, were called from that council on An Seabhag Biadhte, the Fed Hawk, and were asked, before the winter was out and not unkindly, to keep their teaching somewhere it would not be heard daily at Cnoc bheag's own hearth. They went north, most of them, to {{place:burg:132}}, the Seann Skell of the north coast and not the river port of the same name where the harbour rolls had first been opened, and it was there, under a priestess named Beathag nic Sheumais, herself no more than a girl of nineteen at the schism and a woman near forty by the time she had built the house up from four families' worth of following to a proper school, that the Fed Hawk raised their own house and kept their own teaching within sound of a sea that had never once, in six hundred years, given the island back so much as a word of what lay beyond it.

She rode south to Cathair dhearg herself, in her fiftieth year, to ask the king's council in person rather than trust the asking to a letter, and stood before the seven heads in the same red hall Dòmhnall Ruadh's own house had once entered only as petitioners, and asked, on {{date:III-0143}}, that the hearths of the north coast, which she said went colder in a hard winter than the Law had ever meant any hearth to go, be given a quarter more than the measured handful allowed them. She spoke, those who heard her said, without raising her voice once, laying out the north coast's winters as plainly as a scribe laying out a tally, and sat afterward while the council debated her plea across the length of a single short winter's afternoon; and the seven heads, with Raghnall Òg among them, heard her out in full and refused her, the custody-book noting, at some length and for the first time in the whole record's history, every reason it gave for a refusal rather than the bare fact of one.

Beathag nic Sheumais rode back north with the refusal written out in her own satchel, as a leaf of another kind had once ridden south in another woman's satchel generations before her, and told her own house, when she reached it, only that the council had answered as councils answer, and that a hearth denied a quarter more was still a hearth, and would go on being kept whether Cathair dhearg agreed with the keeping of it or not. The Fed Hawk's house at Seann Skell stood after that exactly as it had stood before, neither larger nor smaller for the asking, and its priests went on teaching, to any hawk-keeper of the north coast who cared to climb the hill and hear them, that Mòd's true hunger and the mountain's own measured hunger were not, whatever Cnoc bheag still held to the contrary, one and the same question at all.

## VI. Of the Fourteen Names and the Posts on the Road

A second tremor shook the hills above {{place:burg:123}} on {{date:III-0144}}, seventy years after the joining, and brought down a gallery that fourteen of the custodians' own labourers were working when the roof let go without warning in the small hours of the morning shift. The vein-house closed the gallery for good rather than dig the dead free of it, judging, as the custodians of Cill ghlas had judged once before after an older fall, that the mountain had already taken what it meant to take and would give nothing more back for the digging; and it set, at the gallery's mouth that same season, a plain stone with the fourteen names cut into it in the order they had gone down, the youngest a boy of thirteen new to the coal-face and the oldest a woman who had worked that same gallery forty years without once being late to a shift. The custodians called the stone afterward Clach nan Ceithir Ainmean Deug and kept it swept of snow every winter that followed, whoever held the crown at Cathair dhearg, and it became the custom, among the mountain's own households, for a family that had lost someone to any gallery, in any year, to leave a small stone of their own at its foot on the day of the fall, so that within a generation the fourteen names lay half buried under stones that were not theirs, left by people the fourteen had never known.

It was in the ten years that followed, with the memory of the fourteen still fresh at every gallery-mouth on the mountain, that the king's council took a matter into its own keeping that had never before needed anyone's keeping at all: the roads that carried the measured coal down from the mountain to the coast towns and their temples, which any house with a grudge or a boundary quarrel might, until then, have closed for a season as easily as Raghnall Ruadh had once closed a granary to every hearth that had not declared for him. One of the heads of the seven houses, laying the matter before the council, said plainly that fourteen names on a stone at a gallery's mouth were proof enough that the mountain gave up its coal at a real cost, and that a coal given up at such cost had no business being stopped on the road by any quarrel that was not the mountain's own; and none of the seven spoke against him.

On {{date:III-0145}} the council ordered every such road marked with the crown's own sign, that no house, whatever quarrel it carried, should ever again be free to stop the measured handful on its way to a hearth that had done that house no wrong: the carriers' road down to {{place:burg:19}}, the road over the pass by Muileann fhada that the mountain folk called the northern way, the road south by Muileann chrom where Dòmhnall Ruadh's own people held their galleries, and the road down to Baile òg in the south. The posts went up through that whole autumn, a trellis and a hollow stone cut into each one at the joints where one shire's road gave over to the next, and the men who set them, coal-carters most of them, hired for the season and paid out of the tolls the new roads now raised, worked without much ceremony and without, so far as any leaf remembers, a single word said over the driving of the last one.

A carter named in no roll but his own household's, who had helped set posts on three of the four roads that autumn, was asked, the following spring, what the work had been like, and said only that a man could walk the whole length of any of them now, from the mountain to the sea, and never once lose the road for want of knowing whose it was; and it was that plain answer, more than any leaf the custody-book kept of the council's own debate, that the mountain's households were still repeating to their children when the posts themselves had long since gone grey and lichened in the weather, the trellis and the hollow stone worn faint but never, in all the years that followed, unreadable.
