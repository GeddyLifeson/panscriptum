# Of the Table of Years

*LE 1,165 – 1,266*

> *Druim fon aisne, aisne fon chlàr,*
> *clàr ri clàr is an cuan fodhpa.*
> Keel under rib, rib under plank,
> plank to plank and the sea beneath them.

## I. Of the Narrows and the New Harbour

The salt of Ros fhionn had gone out by cart for as long as anyone at the market-court could remember, over Drochaid Ros fhionn and down the coast road to whatever harbour would take it, and for as long as anyone could remember the carters had grumbled at the bridge's toll and paid it anyway, because there was no other way to the sea that did not cost a full day more on the road. The salt-masters had moved their loading down to {{place:burg:3}} ten years before, when the river silted below the flats and the barges could no longer be trusted to clear the bar; but Baile ghorm's harbour was shallow too, and open to every gale that came up from the south, and more than one master had said, standing on its strand with a ruined cargo at his feet, that a harbour chosen in haste answered a haste and nothing more.

It was the masters themselves, in {{year:III-0167}}, who put their money together and sent it north to Caol gharbh, a fishing village on a deep inlet that had never held more than a few dozen boats and a smokehouse. They walled the inlet's mouth against the sea and dug the mud from its bed until a laden barge could lie there at any tide, and within three years Caol gharbh was posting its own harbour dues and calling its own quay, without much argument from anyone, the salt port of the north-east. The village that had smoked herring for its own tables now weighed and shipped more salt in a season than Ros fhionn's flats had once sent out in three, and the men who had cut turf for the smokehouse fires found themselves instead loading barges and learning a trade none of their fathers had known.

Not every cargo that left the new harbour came safely to open water. A season after the walls were finished, a barge heavy with the first full loading struck the reef at the narrows below the inlet, in the channel the fishers had long called Coinneachadh na h-Amhaich, where the tide off the point meets the river's own current and neither gives way to the other. Those who had grown up on that shore knew the place by the standing wave it threw up at the ebb, a ridge of broken water that never lay flat even on the stillest day, and every fisher's child was taught early to keep well clear of it in anything smaller than a laden hull. The barge's own steersman had crossed it a dozen times before without harm and misjudged the tide by less than the turn of a glass; the hull struck, held a moment, and broke her back on the rock, going down with half her salt still in her before the two men aboard could do more than throw themselves clear. They swam ashore in water cold enough that neither worked a hand rightly for a fortnight after, and the elder of the two, asked afterward whether he meant to go back to the loading, said only that a man who had felt that water close over his head once did not need telling twice what it could do. The harbour-masters set a withy at the rock the following spring, the first mark Caol gharbh's channel had ever needed, and it stood there the rest of the age, renewed each year by whichever household the harbour-court named to the duty.

Drochaid Ros fhionn, meanwhile, fell quiet in {{year:III-0168}}. The salt no longer crossed it, and within a few years neither did much else, for the road it carried had been built for the salt trade and little besides. The office of bridge-keeper, which had passed from father to daughter and back again for longer than the custody-book troubled to record, was done away with when the last keeper died and no one came forward to ask for it in his place; the crown's reeve at Ros fhionn simply let the post lapse; and travellers who still crossed the old stone, a few carters, a few pilgrims bound for the coast shrines, found the toll-gate standing open and unmanned and did not trouble to ask why.

Yet the market-court did not move. It had sat at Ros fhionn since before the bridge was built, judging boundary pleas and grain disputes for the whole of that shire, and though more than one voice argued in those years that the court's business had followed the salt to Caol gharbh and ought to follow it in body too, the court's own clerks answered, when the question was put to them formally, that a court sat where the shire's disputes were brought to it and not where its trade happened to lie that season, and that moving a bench built of oak two centuries old to chase a harbour wall built in three years struck them as a poor exchange. The court stayed. Caol gharbh grew rich on salt and never once, in all the years that followed, gained so much as a single clerk of its own to hear a plea.

The channel that had swallowed the barge was marked properly at last a few years on, Seòlaid Ros fhionn laid out with withies from the old bar to the deep water beyond the flats, so that a pilot new to the trade might learn it in a season rather than in the ten years the old salt-masters had needed. It answered well enough while the withies were tended. In the years the harbour-court grew careless of the duty, the marks drifted and the channel with them, and more than one barge found the bar again the hard way before a fresh set of withies put it right.

## II. Of the Millers and the Watcher in the Sea

At Muileann shean, some way inland of the salt trade's noise, the millers cut a new gearing for their wheels in {{year:III-0169}}, oak toothed against oak so that one wheel now turned two stones where it had once turned one. The first winter it ran, half the town came to watch it work and went away arguing whether the flour it ground was finer or coarser than the old way's, a question no two households ever answered alike; within a generation the gearing had spread to every mill of consequence in the north, and the millers of Muileann shean, who had cut it first, kept a carved model of it hung over their own door as a kind of boast, though the boast grew quieter with each mill that copied it without asking leave.

Off the thinned coast of the north-west, in the same years, the fishers of the small boats still working those waters began to speak again of the thing they called Uilebheist na Mara, the Sea-Watcher, seen close enough in {{year:III-0170}} to foul a net and leave it in ribbons on the strand the following morning. Whether it was one beast or the name given to whatever the sea chose to show a frightened crew, no two tellings agreed, but the boats of that coast drew further in after that season and stayed nearer the headlands than their grandfathers had ever needed to, so that the last few miles of open water off the north-west cape went, within a lifetime, from a fishing ground to a stretch the boats no longer troubled to cross.

It was into a household of that same north-west coast, at a farm two days' walk from the thinned water, that Raghnall mac Chaluim had been born, six years after the salt-masters first walled Caol gharbh's inlet. His father kept sheep on ground too poor for much else, and his mother's people had once fished the same water the Sea-Watcher now kept for its own, a trade her own father had given up before Raghnall could remember him at it. The boy showed no gift for either sheep or nets. What he showed, from an age his mother could never afterward fix exactly, was a habit of counting: stones in a wall, sheep through a gate, the notches on a tally-stick left over from some dispute of his father's, and asking, of anyone patient enough to answer him, whether the count came out the same the second time. His mother said once, late in her own life, that she had known him for a strange child the evening he came in from the fold in tears, six years old and furious, because he had counted the flock at the gate twice and got two different numbers, and would not be comforted until his father had gone back out with a lamp and counted a third time to settle which of the boy's two counts had been right. It was the sheep that were wrong, as it happened, one having slipped a hurdle and gone back to graze; the boy remembered only that a thing could be counted carefully and still come out crooked, and it troubled him, his mother said, far past the age when it ought to have stopped troubling a child at all.

A priest of the schools of the stag, passing that way on his own circuit the year the millers' new gearing was still news even on that coast, heard the boy through a whole afternoon of it and told his father, not unkindly, that a mind so set on counting things twice belonged at a house of disputation and not on a hillside among the sheep. His father, who had four other sons for the sheep and could spare the one, sent him south the following spring, a boy of fourteen, to Dùn thais, where Taigh na Deasbaid had stood since before any hand now living could say who first raised it, and where scholars of every rite came to argue questions the rest of the island had no leisure to ask.

## III. Of the Table of Years

Raghnall spent the better part of twenty years at Dùn thais before he wrote the thing that would carry his name past his own death. He came to the house of disputation as a boy who counted sheep through gates and left it, in the eyes of the scholars who taught him, a man who counted people the same plain way, without flinching from what the count showed him.

The question that took hold of him was one he said, later in his life, had first occurred to him watching his own uncles: that his mother's brothers, who had fished the thinned water until the Sea-Watcher drove them off it, had died younger, every one of them, than the custodians' kin he came to know at Dùn thais, whose galleries fed them well in every season the vein answered at all. It was an unremarkable thing to notice and say aloud over a meal. It was another thing to test it.

He began, in his thirtieth year, going through every roll of births and deaths the house of disputation would let him copy: the custody-book's own entries where they touched the seven houses, the shire seats' tallies of their dead, the death-rolls the priests of every rite kept for their own dead alone. He set the heads of the custodian houses beside the tenants who worked their galleries and their land, house against house, generation against generation, and totted the years each had lived before the custody-book or a priest's roll marked them gone. It took him the better part of nine years, most of it done by lamplight after the house of disputation's own business was finished for the day, and more than one scholar who shared his table said afterward that they had thought him, for years together, a man chasing a private grief that any ordinary grief ought to have worn thin.

On {{date:III-0171}} he laid the finished table before the house of disputation entire, called together in its own hall on a morning of hard frost, the scholars sitting in their winter cloaks because the fires had not yet been lit that far into the season and no one had thought, until the doors were shut, to send for wood. Clàr nam Bliadhnachan was a single broad sheet ruled into columns, house against tenant, generation against generation, the years each had lived set down in a hand grown small and cramped from nine years of the same close work; Raghnall had had it copied fair the week before onto a hide large enough that two scholars were needed to hold it up before the hall could read it whole. The heads of the seven houses of custody lived, on the whole, some years longer than the tenants of their own lands lived, and the gap did not close, house by house, no matter how the sums were run again. He read the table aloud himself rather than let another scholar do it for him, his voice, those present said, steadier than his hands, and he asked, when he had finished, the one question the sums could not answer for him: whether the vein answered the hand that held it, and answered it more kindly than it answered the hand that only worked it.

No one at the house of disputation that day gave him an answer either. Some scholars called it a fair piece of counting and nothing more, the kind of pattern a mind will find in any sufficiently large pile of numbers if it looks for one hard enough. One rose before the hall had properly settled and said as much aloud, that a man might set any two rolls of the dead side by side and find some difference or other between them if he wished hard enough to find it, and that a wish dressed in columns was still a wish. Raghnall answered him only that he had run the sums four times over four winters and would gladly run them a fifth in the scholar's own presence, on rolls the scholar chose himself, and the scholar, after a silence the hall remembered longer than the exchange itself, sat down without taking up the offer.

Others left the hall without speaking to him at all, and one old scholar, a custodian's own son by birth, is said to have told him afterward, in private, that a table was a poor vessel for a question that could unmake every house on the mountain if it were ever answered wrongly, and that he would do better to burn the sheet and keep the question, if he must keep it, in his own head.

Raghnall did not burn it. He sent a fair copy to the vein-house, addressed to no one custodian in particular, and kept his own working copy at Dùn thais, and for a few years the table passed from hand to hand among scholars of every house, argued over, copied badly, copied well, until word of it reached the king's council at Cathair dhearg by a route no one afterward could trace to its first carrier.

On {{date:III-0172}} the council forbade the table's copying. Taigh na Deasbaid was shut for a generation by the same order, its scholars sent back to their own shires and its hall given over, for a time, to the storing of grain against a lean year that never came. Raghnall himself was neither punished nor praised; the council's order named the table, not the man who had made it, and Raghnall, forty-four years old that year and past the age when a scholar without a school might easily start again elsewhere, went home to the north-west coast and kept sheep, badly, for the rest of that season, before a custodian of the vein-house he had never met sent word asking whether his own working copy still existed and might be sent to the mountain for safekeeping.

He sent it. It was entered in the vein-house's own inventory that same year, one line among the galleries and the lot-stones and the old tallies of coal owed and coal burned, Clàr nam Bliadhnachan, a table of years, forbidden to be copied, kept. No custodian who read the entry after him is recorded to have asked that it be destroyed, and none is recorded to have asked that the forbidding be lifted either. It stayed on the shelf the vein-house had made for it, read by whoever came to the inventory with leave to read it and closed again by whoever came after, a question kept rather than answered, which is not, Raghnall is said to have told the custodian who took the copy from his own hands, quite the same thing as a question forgotten.

## IV. Of the Gale, the Boundary and the Pilgrims

Raghnall lived fourteen years past the forbidding, most of them quietly, at a small house near Dùn thais that the shut school let him keep for the asking, and it is told that he went on counting things to the end of his life, though what he counted in those last years no leaf that survives says plainly.

In {{year:III-0173}}, before the grain fleet had sailed for the season, a gale drove up the river at {{place:burg:17}} higher than any then living had seen. It came in the night out of the north-east with no warning any watcher on the strand could afterward point to, and the harbour's own people woke to a wind that laid the reed thatch flat on the landward side of every roof and a river running backward with the sea's own push behind it. Boats moored two deep along the strand were crushed against one another faster than a lantern could be got down to them, and by the grey of morning the strand was heaped with broken plank from stem to stern of the harbour, timbers that had been whole hulls the evening before now fit for nothing but a boat-builder's fire. The fleet that should have carried that year's grain south did not sail until the following spring. Inis mhòr rebuilt what the gale had taken through that whole winter, every hand in the town given some part of the work whether it had ever held an adze before or not, and when the spring fleet finally went out a season late, the harbour counted itself no worse off in the end than a hard winter might have left it regardless.

Raghnall mac Chaluim died at Dùn thais in his sixty-fifth year, in the small house the shut school had let him keep, and the scholars who had once argued his table over a shared table of their own carried him to the burying ground the school no longer had the standing to consecrate, and buried him there anyway, judging that a house shut by a king's council was not thereby unmade in every other sense that mattered to the dead. No stone was cut for him. A tally-stick was, notched once for every year the making of the table had cost him, and set at the head of the grave, where it stood until the weather took it, as weather takes most things left standing in the open on Dia-thìr.

In {{year:III-0174}}, in the far north-east, Baile ruadh and Achadh naomh went to law over the grazing on the hills between them, a quarrel old enough that neither shire seat's own clerks could say, when the case was first brought, how many times it had already been argued and settled and argued again by their grandparents. Each side came to Cathair dhearg with its own boundary stones to point to and its own old herders to swear where the line had always run, and each old herder swore a different line from every other, so that the council's clerks, hearing the third day of testimony, are said to have privately agreed among themselves that no one now living had ever known the true boundary and that both sides believed, with equal honesty, in a line neither could produce a single stone to prove. It came before the king's council three times before it was settled at last by dividing the disputed ground along a stream that ran, conveniently for the judgment though not, the council's own clerk noted dryly, for either party's satisfaction, almost exactly down its middle. Both shire seats grazed their flocks to the water's edge from that year on, and the herders on each bank, meeting at the ford in ordinary weather, were said within a generation to have forgotten there had ever been a quarrel at all, though the stones each side had carried to Cathair dhearg and carried home again unused stood afterward in both towns' own boundary walls, still pointing, for whoever troubled to walk out and look at them, to a line the water had long since made no matter of.

The keepers of the Old Spirits renewed, in {{year:III-0175}}, the pilgrimage to Allt an Àigh, where a stream ran clear as glass and was said, by every rite that had ever sent a pilgrim to it, never once to have failed those who drank of it at the right turning of the moon. A guest-house was built beside the water that same year for those who walked from every shire to drink there, timber and thatch enough for a dozen travellers at a time, with a hearth kept lit through every season by whichever keeper held the turn of duty. Its own book of names, kept from the first season, showed within a few years pilgrims of every rite on the island entered side by side on the same leaf, dawn-hawk beside wolf-sworn, Manannan's sworn beside the wood-watchers of Macha, a mixing the keepers of the stream had not expected and, once it had happened, did not trouble to prevent, for the water itself, they said, had never once asked a pilgrim which rite had sent him before it answered him.

## V. Of the Two Sons

King Ailean mac Raghnaill died at Cathair dhearg on {{date:III-0176}}, sixty-six years old, having reigned long enough that few at the red hall that year could remember another king's seal on a letter. He left two sons by different mothers, and it was known, before the year was out, that each of the two had been told plainly, by whichever parent had raised him, that he and not the other was the one meant to wear the crown after their father. Neither had been told this as a secret; both households had said it openly for years, at hearths and hall-tables where a saying travels whether it is meant to or not, and the red hall's own clerks, drawing up the king's burial roll, are said to have left a blank space where the name of the heir should have stood, unable to agree between themselves which of the two names to write and unwilling, in the king's own house and so soon after his death, to write neither. The council that had once forbidden a scholar's table of years met that winter to consider a harder sum than any Raghnall mac Chaluim had ever set before it, and found, by the time the snow came, that it had no answer ready either.

At the vein-house above Muileann dhearg the inventory was read out that same winter, as it was read every winter, gallery by gallery, tally by tally, down to the single line a custodian long dead had entered nearly fifty years before: Clàr nam Bliadhnachan, a table of years, forbidden to be copied, kept. The custodian reading it that year paused over the line a moment longer than the others, as the custodians before her had done and the custodians after her would go on doing, and then read on to the next line, for the inventory did not wait on any one entry, however long a shadow it had cast, and there was a great deal more of it still to be read before the reckoning of that year's coal could be closed.
