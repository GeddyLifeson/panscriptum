# Of the Loft over the Mill

*LE 1,630 – 1,749*

## I. Of the Barge Kept on the Strand

By the seventeenth century of the count the island had grown better roads than it had ever had reason to build ships, and the coasting trade that had once gone by water from Ros chiar to Caol mhin, the cold way along the north coast, went by cart instead, year on year, until the last barge that had worked that water was laid up for good at {{place:burg:17}} in {{year:III-0215}}. The men of Inis mhòr did not break her up for her timbers, as they would have done with any hull too old to caulk. They hauled her above the tide-line, chocked her level, and left her there, and told their children she was the last of the boats that had gone the cold way, though none of the children, and precious few of the fathers, could have said any longer what the cold way had been the cold way from, or to.

It was the temper of those years, on the whole island and not only at Inis mhòr: to keep the last of a thing on a strand or a shelf once there was no more need to use it, rather than let it go the way of the thing before it, which no one had troubled to keep at all. Fifteen years on an earthquake shook {{place:burg:315}} and cracked the wall of the spirit-house there, and the keepers took the crack for a warning and set a lamp before it, and could not agree among themselves, that year or any year after, of what the warning had been. A shudder of ground that had done no man any harm was made, by the very fact of nobody being harmed, into a thing that had to mean something; it is the way of an age that keeps more than it uses and asks more of what it keeps than the keeping can answer.

## II. Of the Widow of Muileann chaol

At {{place:burg:246}}, on the millrace above the ford, Beathag nic Thòmais had kept the mill alone since her husband Artair mac Dhòmhnaill died of a fever four years into their marriage, leaving her a widow at thirty with the wheel, the stones, and the ledger of who owed the mill grain and who the mill owed nothing. She kept it well, better by most accounts than Artair himself had kept it, for she had the kind of head for a ledger that does not let a debt run past the season it was struck in, and the millers up and down the ford came to her before they came to their own kin when an account needed settling without ill will on either side.

It was not the ledger that filled her loft, in the years after. She had grown, somewhere in the quiet of a house with no children in it, a taste for old writings that had nothing to do with the mill's business at all: a taste her neighbours found harmless enough, if a little strange in a woman with a trade to mind, and one that cost her, at the start, no more than the odd measure of meal for a board of accounts some dead reeve's grandson was glad to be rid of. She began, in {{year:III-0217}}, to ask about the ford and the market at Muileann chaol, and then further, by rider and by the carters who came through with grain, whether any house that no longer troubled to read them had an old copy of Leabhar a' Chùraim lying in a chest, or a page or two of Rolla a' Chalaidh of Seann Skell that some great-grandfather had kept for reasons nobody living now remembered. Houses that had such things, more often than not, were glad enough to sell them. A page nobody reads is worth less to the house that holds it than the meal a miller's widow will give for it, and Beathag paid a fair measure and never once haggled a seller down, which is why, within a few years, word of what she wanted travelled further than her own riders ever went.

She stacked what she bought in the loft over the mill, above the grain floor, where the heat of the stones kept the damp off better than any chest at ground level could. Her neighbours called it, at first, the widow's rubbish, and said so to her face more than once, in the way a small town says a true unkindness and calls it a joke so that no one need answer for it. Beathag answered nothing to it either way. She went on buying, and stacking, and in a year or two the loft held more pages of the island's own past than any single chest in any single hall from Cathair dhearg to Seann Skell, though not a soul beyond her own ford knew it yet.

A carter out of {{place:burg:23}} brought her, one wet Faolach, a board no bigger than a serving-tray, black with age at the edges, that a house on the Cathair mhòr road had been using to weight down a cheese-press for as long as any living man there could remember. He had noticed, only because the light happened to fall across it while he waited for his own cheese to be wrapped, that the underside of the board carried a hand of writing, and had thought of the widow at Muileann chaol who paid a fair measure of meal for such things and asked no questions past the price. Beathag scraped the mould from the board's grain with the flat of a bone comb before she would let herself read it, and found a tally, three generations old, of what one house's coal-right had paid out at four winter burnings running, and nothing in it that mattered to anyone alive. She paid the carter his meal all the same, set the board on the shelf with the others, and told him, when he asked why she troubled over so small a thing, that a board a house had forgotten it owned was worth exactly as much to her as one it still prized, since neither house could tell her, any longer, which of its old writings would matter and which would not, and she had long since stopped trying to guess in their place.

It became, in that way, less a taste than a trade of its own, though she never asked a price of anyone for what she kept and would not, when asked, put a name to what she was doing beyond "keeping what's going begging." A vein-house clerk riding through on the crown's business, hearing of the loft from an innkeeper who thought it a curiosity worth a traveller's amusement, climbed the mill stair one afternoon out of nothing better than idleness, and came down some three hours later a good deal less idle than he had gone up, having found among her boards a copy of a custodians' ruling his own office at Muileann dhearg had lost the only other copy of in a fire two generations back. He asked her, before he rode on, whether she meant the loft for the crown's use, or Taigh na Fèithe's, or her own. Beathag said she had not decided, and did not think it was hers to decide alone in any case, and the clerk, who had ridden in expecting a curiosity and was riding out with a ruling his own custodians badly wanted back, allowed that this was, on the whole, a wiser answer than most he had been given that year.

## III. Of the Rag Paper

A neighbour of hers at the same ford, a woman a full twenty years her junior named Anna nic Coinnich, had a trade Beathag's own custom had unwittingly fed: Anna's father had kept a fulling-mill on the same race, beating cloth clean of its grease in wooden troughs, and Anna, who had grown up among the rags that trade threw off and never burned, for a good rag burns badly and smells worse, took to soaking the worst of them down to a pulp and pressing the pulp thin and letting it dry, the way her father's cloth dried on the tenter-frames, until in {{year:III-0218}} she had a sheet that would take ink from a reed pen without tearing and without drinking the ink into a blot the way old skin sometimes did in a damp season. It was the first such sheet made on Dia-thìr, though Anna herself, standing over her troughs with her sleeves wet to the elbow, would have said only that it was a way of not wasting a rag, and thought no further of it than that.

Beathag was the first customer she had for it who wanted more than a single sheet to try. She had watched Anna work the pulp for a season before she ever asked to buy any of it, standing at the trough's edge with her sleeves pinned back the same as Anna's own, asking why this batch pressed thinner than that one and whether a rag of wool behaved as a rag of linen did, until Anna, more amused than put out by a widow twenty years her elder learning her trade at her elbow, told her plainly that she might as well take a hand at the frame herself if she meant to stand there asking questions a whole afternoon. Beathag did, and pressed a sheet with her own hands that came out uneven at one corner and perfectly good at the other three, and kept that first sheet of her own pressing, uneven corner and all, on a shelf of the loft apart from the rest for as long as she lived, though she never said, even to Anna, quite why.

Leabhar a' Chùraim of Taigh na Fèithe, which had been kept on hide since Fionnlagh Dall's own day and passed hand to hand at every winter burning until its oldest leaves were soft as cloth themselves, was copied that same generation onto Anna's paper for the first time, a leaf a week through one whole winter, by a clerk of Taigh na Fèithe who complained, at first, that the new stuff took the ink strangely and gave it up, by spring, that it took the ink better than hide ever had once a hand grew used to it. It was Beathag who carried the proposal to Taigh na Fèithe in the first place, riding the sixty miles herself rather than trusting it to a rider, for she judged, rightly as it turned out, that a custodian would sooner hear a strange notion from a widow with a mill's ledger behind her name than from anyone he could take for a huckster with a rag-heap to sell. The custodian who heard her out asked only whether the new stuff would hold the ink another sixteen centuries as the hide had; Beathag said she could not promise him that, since no one had yet had sixteen centuries to try it in, and the custodian, who had spent a long career answering harder questions with less certain answers than that, said it would do, and let the copying go ahead. Beathag bought reams of it herself for her own copying after, and it is from that winter that the loft's papers begin to run, in among the older hides and boards, on the paper of the same ford that made them.

## IV. Of the Three Copies

Among the things that came to her loft, in the ordinary way of a house up the coast selling off what a dead grandfather had kept, was a leaf that gave the tidings of the fleet as they had first reached Seann Skell, sixteen centuries before: two coasts found, not one, and the fleet parted between them on the far side of the sea. Beathag read it once and thought little of it, for every child on the island had heard as much at a hearth in one telling or another. It was only when a second copy came to her, some years later, from a different house entirely, that she noticed the two did not agree: one gave the northern coast first and the southern second, and the other gave it the other way about, and where one said nineteen hulls reached a shore the other said one-and-twenty.

She went looking, after that, the way she had never gone looking for anything else in the loft, and within two more years had a third copy, from a house at Cathair dhearg that had kept it folded inside the cover of an older book entirely, as though whoever put it there had wanted it kept and not read. The third agreed with neither of the first two, not in the order of the coasts nor in the count of the hulls, and in {{year:III-0219}} Beathag laid all three out on the boards of the loft floor, side by side in the low afternoon light that came through the one window, and read them through again from the beginning, each in turn, looking for the seam where one could be shown to be the copy and the others the truth it had been copied wrong from.

There was no such seam to find. Each of the three was written in a hand that meant to be believed, none troubled to say where its own figures had come from, and all three, read close, agreed on the one thing that mattered most, that two coasts had been found and the fleet parted upon them, and disagreed on everything a court might have wanted settled beyond it. Anna, who had come up the mill stair with a fresh ream under her arm and found Beathag still on her knees over the three leaves at dusk with a cold supper untouched at her elbow, asked her outright which of the three she believed. Beathag said she believed all three of them, in the sense that she thought none of the three writers had set down a single figure he did not himself hold to be true; and she believed none of the three, in the sense that three men equally certain and equally honest had between them given the island three different pasts, and a woman with no better claim on the truth than any of the three dead hands that had written it down had no business choosing one of their certainties over the other two merely for the comfort of having chosen.

Beathag wrote out a fair copy of each, in her own hand, on Anna's paper, laid the three fair copies together in the loft where the originals had lain, and chose none of them. It was not laziness in her, nor a want of nerve; she had settled harder questions than this one for lesser men over her own mill's ledger without blinking, and more than one debtor at the ford had learned that a mild manner in a widow was no sign at all of a soft one underneath it. She judged, and said as much to the one or two who ever asked her outright, that a woman who had not been on the quay that grey morning had no business making three uncertain tellings into one false certainty merely because a false certainty reads easier than three that will not agree. A niece of hers, hearing the tale told at the fireside years after, asked why she had not simply burned the two that were wrong and kept the one that was right, since three could not all be true together. Beathag, by then an old woman, said that this was precisely the question the whole loft existed to refuse an answer to, and that the girl would understand it better the day she found she could not tell, of two things equally well attested and wholly at odds, which one to burn.

## V. Of the North-West Counted

The crown, in the same years, sent a reeve down the empty roads of the north-west to list what households still stood between {{place:burg:305}} and {{place:burg:143}}, for the tallies that fed the winter burning had not been walked that coast in a long while and Taigh na Fèithe wanted a true count before another season's measure was cut. He rode it alone, with a boy to lead the pack-horse and carry the tally-sticks, and found, at more than one landing along that coast, a row of berths cut square into the old harbour stone with no boat standing in any of them and no house behind them with a fire lit, the roof gone in on some and the door simply left standing open on others, as though whoever had gone out last had not troubled to shut it behind them and no one since had troubled to notice it stood open. He counted the berths at each landing before he counted the households, from a habit of a careful man doing the easier reckoning first, and by the time he came to the households themselves he already half knew, from the berths alone, what shape his answer would take.

The reeve came back, in {{year:III-0220}}, with a figure smaller than the one the old rolls gave him to expect, and smaller by more than the ordinary slow drift of the years could account for: fewer households, by his own careful count, than there were berths cut into the old harbour stone at any one of the three towns he walked through. He wrote his figure into the crown's own book, in the plain hand a reeve is taught to keep for such things, and rode home, and no one at Cathair dhearg asked him to walk the coast a second time to be sure of it, for a second count, by then, was a thing the island had learned long since not to want too badly. The boy who had led his pack-horse, grown to a man himself, told it afterward that his master had said only one thing to him the whole ride back that was not business: that a berth cut in stone outlasts the hand that cut it and the household it was cut for both, and that a coast can go on looking, from the sea, exactly as it always looked, for a long while after there is no one left on it to look back.

## VI. Of the Loft Left to the Town

Beathag died at Muileann chaol in {{year:III-0221}}, seventy-four years old, in the same room above the mill she had slept in since her wedding night, with Anna nic Coinnich, by then a woman of middle years herself, sitting by her through the last of it. In her last days she asked twice to be carried up the mill stair to see the loft once more, and was twice refused by Anna, who told her plainly that a woman of seventy-four with a fever on her had no business on a ladder-steep stair for the sake of looking at boards she had looked at every week for forty years and knew, by then, better than she knew her own hands. Beathag did not ask a third time. She asked instead that the shelf holding her own first, uneven sheet of Anna's paper be brought down to her, and held it a while, and gave it back, and said nothing more about the loft until the day she died.

She left the mill to a nephew who had no interest in old paper and a great deal in grain, and the loft, with everything in it, to the town, on the one condition that nothing kept there should ever be burned, for whatever reason and by whatever hand. The nephew, reading the will aloud to the assembled kin the day after her burying, stumbled over that one clause twice, as a man will stumble over words he privately thinks foolish and would rather not be the one caught reading aloud, and more than one of the kin present said afterward that they had marked the stumble and thought the less of him for it, though none said so to his face while there was grain business still to be settled between them.

The town of Muileann chaol kept that condition, and did rather less well by the rest of her wishes, which had run, in the fuller will her nephew read out and then quietly set aside, to a hope that some clerk might be found each generation to keep the loft in order and add to it what the age still produced. No such clerk was ever paid to do it. The loft stood, its door latched but never locked, and townsfolk who had a paper they no longer wanted burned took to bringing it up the mill stair rather than to the hearth, more from habit than from any oath sworn over Beathag's grave, until the habit itself became the nearest thing the town had to a keeper. A reeve of a later generation, passing through on the crown's business and finding the loft kept by no one office and no one name, asked the miller who had it in charge by then what right the town had to hold papers that plainly belonged, by their matter, to Taigh na Fèithe or the crown or both. The miller, who was Beathag's own great-nephew and had never in his life climbed the stair for any purpose but to fetch down a paper some other traveller had come asking after, said only that the town had held them fifty years without Taigh na Fèithe or the crown ever once asking for them back, and that he took a fifty years' silence, in such a matter, for an answer of a kind.

## VII. Of the Roads, the Beasts, and the Empty Harbour Kept Thin

The crown that Beathag never once wrote of, though she must have heard its business talked over at every ford she rode, passed in these same years by a road of its own that the loft's papers do not trouble to follow closely. Tormod mac Ruairidh of Clann Raghnaill was crowned at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0222}}, nine-and-twenty years old, and held the throne longer than any king before him in Leabhar a' Chùraim's own count, though the record keeps of that long reign chiefly its roads and little else. Within a few years the priests of Manannan at Seann Skell, who had restated their rule of sight-of-land at their last synod, took to saying their own prayer for the shut sea on the very day of Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh, so that the two were kept together on {{place:burg:489}}'s old quay ever after; and neither the empty-harbour keepers nor Manannan's own priests were pleased to find the other's rite standing on the stones beside theirs, though neither would give an inch of the day to the other either.

Tormod spent the better part of two decades building stone bridges over the fords his own riders had once had to swim their horses through in spate, one of them at {{place:burg:91}}, and gave land in amends to the ferry-keepers whose living the bridges took from them, which the ferry-keepers, on the whole, judged fair dealing and said so. One ferryman of that ford, offered his choice of two parcels in amends, is said to have asked the king's own reeve which of the two had the better crossing to it, meaning the crossing of a road and not of water, and been told, with no more of a smile than the question deserved, that a man who had spent thirty years reading a river's moods might do worse than spend his last years reading a market's instead; he took the parcel nearer the market and did well enough by it that his sons never went back to the water at all.

A few years into his bridges the panthers of the southern roads crossed in numbers beyond any herder's memory, and for a whole season the days of the panthers' crossing ran into one another without a clear morning between them, so that flocks from {{place:burg:466}}'s hills to the coast were kept folded far longer than any shepherd liked and lambed thin that year for it. Old men of that country, telling it after, said they had never known the beasts run so many nor so long, and disagreed among themselves, as old men telling such a thing will, on whether it was the worst crossing within memory or only the worst any of them happened to have lived through, which is not, they allowed on being pressed, quite the same claim.

The salt pans of {{place:burg:443}} failed within the same reign, the sea having risen over them by degrees no one pan-keeper's lifetime was long enough to notice until it was done, and the trade that had once fed the north-east from that coast went south instead, to a town that grew fat on it while Caol gharbh itself grew, as the record's own dry word has it, poorer than the coast it had once undersold ever was. A south-westerly storm, not many years after, put the low town of {{place:burg:170}} under water to more than a man's height and drowned three-and-twenty of its people before the water turned, and the town that survived it raised its own sea-wall the height of a man higher than before, and has not since let the sea in a second time.

It is told, of Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh in those same years, that fewer families kept it each turn than had kept it the turn before, until at the last only the harbour priests of Cidhe an Diosail and a handful of the oldest houses of Seann Skell still stood on the stones for it at all. A scribe out of Cathair dhearg, sent down the coast on some other errand of the crown's and told by his own masters to note the rite while he was there for the crown's own book, stood among that handful one grey Là an Diosail near the reign's end and asked, first one old woman and then another, on what day, in the true telling, the fleet had gone down the river all those centuries before. Each gave him an answer, and no two of the answers he was given agreed, any more than the three copies that had once lain on Beathag nic Thòmais's loft floor had agreed with one another; and he wrote as much into the crown's own book, in a hand that meant to be believed as much as any of the three had, and could no more say which of his own answers was the true one than Beathag, fifty years dead by then, had ever been able to say of hers.
