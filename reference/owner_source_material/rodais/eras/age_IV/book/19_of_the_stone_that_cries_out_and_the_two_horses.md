# Of the Stone that Cries Out and the Two Horses

*LE 400 – 446*

## I. Of the Net-Maker of Inis thais

Dòmhnall mac Ailein mended nets for a living at {{place:burg:166}}, on the strand below the citadel where the boats came up at low water, and he had done so since he was a boy at his father's knee, and would have gone on doing so, by his own account given late in life, if he had not one morning found himself with more idle hours than herring to mend and a mind that would not let a plain thing lie plain.

The plain thing was the stone that stood a little back from the tide-line, grey and long as a man lying down, which the town had always called Clach and left alone, and around which a handful of old families still kept a watch by turns, as their fathers had kept it before them, road-guards once, in some telling now half forgotten, who had sworn themselves wolf-sworn on a carved stone at another town's gate and had brought the name south with them, or had it already, no one now living could say which. Dòmhnall was thirty-five years old, a spare man with a net-maker's hands, thickened and scarred across the palms from years of twine, and he had never in his life been taken for anyone the town would gather to hear. He began by talking to the men who mended nets beside him, because they were there and would not walk off while his hands were still moving.

He told them the stone was watching, and that it would cry out one day beneath the man or woman who by right should sit An Talla Dearg at {{place:burg:19}}, as a hound knows its own master's step among a crowd of strangers' steps; and that the families who watched it kept a household still under oath, wolf-sworn still, whatever the wolf they had first sworn against, no mere custom grown stale with age. Their proper name, the name their oath deserved, was an Fhianna, who keep the whole island as a wolf keeps its range, walking the bounds of it though no one has asked them to and none but themselves remembers why. Some of the net-menders laughed at him the first day, not unkindly. By the second day fewer laughed. By {{date:III-0086}} there were men and women come down from the upper town as well, standing at the edge of the net-menders' work to hear a man they did not know for a teacher say plainly what half of them, it turned out, had half believed since childhood and never once said aloud.

One of the old watching families, hearing a net-maker put words on a duty they had kept without words for three generations, sent their eldest down to the strand to ask him plainly what right he had to name a thing he had not sworn to. Dòmhnall answered, by the account still told of it at Inis thais, that he had sworn nothing and claimed no watch of his own, and that the naming was not his to give or withhold; he had only said aloud what the stone had already been doing under their own hands for longer than any of them could count, and if the name displeased them they were free to keep the older silence and let him talk to the empty air instead. The old man went back to his family and reported, so it is told, that the net-maker was either a fool or telling the truth, and that he could not himself tell which, though he came back down to the strand the following morning to hear him again, and brought his own sons with him the morning after that.

He never claimed to have made anything new. He said only that he had put a true name on an old thing, the way a man finds the right word for a smell he has known all his life and never named, and from that day the ones who stood to hear him, growing by ones and threes through that year and the next, called themselves the first gathering of Òrd na Cloiche at {{place:burg:166}}, though the order, as the older men among them were careful to say, was no younger than the hall built about the wolf-stone at another town's gate, generations before any of them were born. Dòmhnall did not correct them when they called him a founder, and did not claim the word for himself either. He went on mending nets. He only did it now with people standing near.

## II. Of the Wolf-House Roofed with Slate

What his hearers wanted, once they were past the first summer of standing on the strand in whatever weather came, was a roof, and Dòmhnall, who understood twine and rope and very little of timber or stone, let the ones among them who understood such things see to it. A shipmaster of the town, a woman named Beathag nic Raoghnaill who had carried wool and hides along the southern coast for twenty years and had never once thought herself religious, offered to fetch the roofing herself rather than trust it to a stranger's hold, and sailed north and came back a season later with a hull low in the water under the weight of northern slate, blue-grey and heavier by far than the thatch the town was used to laying.

They raised the hall about the stone through that year and the next, a plain building of undressed stone with the slate laid close above it, and it stood, when it was finished, {{date:III-0087}}, the first roof on the whole coast built to outlast the man who built it, or so the town said of it afterward, half in wonder and half in complaint at what the slate had cost to bring. Dòmhnall stood under it the day the last course was laid and put his hand flat against the wall beside the stone, and said to Beathag, who had brought the slate and stayed to see it laid, that a roof built to last past its builder was a kind of oath, though a duller one than a man swore with his own mouth, and she said back to him, being a plain woman and no teacher, that a roof built to last was mostly a roof that did not leak, and that she would leave the oaths to him.

The families who had watched the stone before Dòmhnall ever spoke of it took their old watch under the new roof and called themselves, from that year on and without argument among themselves, an Fhianna, and kept a horn there that was blown at the turn of every tide, morning and evening, the way the boats themselves were called and released. Children of the lower town, who had grown up half afraid of the grey stone and its silent watchers, began instead to set their morning's business by the horn without a thought for what it sounded for, the way any horn, once it has sounded long enough, is heard before it is remembered.

The horn fell, by lot among an Fhianna's own households, to whoever's turn it was that season to keep the tide-watch, and the blowing of it became, within a few years, a small honour that passed from house to house the way the old road-watch had once passed, so that a child of Inis thais might grow from a boy who feared the stone to a young man who blew its horn and back to an old man again without the town ever once needing to write down whose turn came next. Dòmhnall's own hearers, once they had a roof to gather under, began to travel: a fisherman of the town who had heard him twice went inland one winter to sell hides and came back having told the naming to three villages he passed through, none of which he ever visited again, so that the stone's true name went out from Inis thais the way water goes out from a spring, in threads too small and too many for anyone to trace back afterward to their single source.

## III. Of the Harbour Shrine at Caol leathan

Not every town took the naming so easily. At {{place:burg:1}}, where the wolf-stone's own hall had stood since before any of Dòmhnall's hearers were born, there was a shrine down by the harbour that the priests of Òrd an t-Seabhaig had also long claimed, a low stone house above the tide where offerings of both faiths had been laid, uneasily, side by side, for longer than either order cared to reckon.

It came to a head, as such things do, over a feast day that fell the same year for both: the hawk's own reckoning marking it a day for flying the young birds down to the water for the first time, Òrd na Cloiche's marking it a night an Fhianna kept vigil against no enemy either could name. Both orders arrived at the shrine door on the same morning to find it wanted for the same hours, and neither would give way, Òrd an t-Seabhaig's priests saying their reckoning was the older, an Fhianna saying the stone did not reckon by the hawk's moons and never had.

The king's reeve at Caol leathan, a level-headed man named Murchadh mac Ailpein who had settled land disputes along that coast for a dozen years and had no patience left over for the disputes of gods, walked down to the shrine himself and stood between the two crowds until they consented, more from weariness than agreement, to hear him. He proposed nothing new: only that the shrine's days be halved between the two orders as the sacred woods of an older age had once been halved between rival claimants, each order keeping what hours were its own and neither trespassing on the other's, {{date:III-0088}}. Both sides took it sourly at first, Òrd an t-Seabhaig's priests muttering that half a shrine was no shrine at all, an Fhianna saying much the same in different words, but the division held, through that year and every year after. Neither order ever came to love it; they only found, in all the years that followed, no better quarrel to replace it with.

Murchadh himself, asked by his own clerk why he had troubled to walk down to a shrine dispute at all when the crown's business at Caol leathan ran to harbour tolls and fish-weirs and rarely to gods, answered that a harbour town with two faiths quarrelling over one roof was a harbour town that would soon enough be quarrelling over its weirs and tolls as well, whatever the first quarrel had been about, and that a reeve who let the small fire burn unwatched had no business complaining of the larger one later. He wrote the division down on a single strip of hide, one side for Òrd an t-Seabhaig's hours and one for an Fhianna's, and nailed it to the shrine door himself, and it hung there, replaced twice as the hide wore through, for as long as the division held.

## IV. Of Seann Skell Walled and the Market at the New Gate

In those same years the northern town of {{place:burg:132}}, on the bay the hawk-keepers called their own, put up a wall around its own streets for the first time, the old open harbour town having grown past what an unwalled shore could keep safe of its own goods, and the crown granted a market inside the new landward gate where the amber of that coast was sold alongside whale-oil, honey, and the wool of the northern moors, {{date:III-0088a}}.

The amber-workers of the bay, who had for two generations sold whatever rough stone washed up along their own shingle to whichever trader offered first, whether inside a wall or out, swore among themselves, as the price of the gate's protection, to sell no rough amber outside the walls from that year forward, so that the crown's own toll-takers might see every piece pass and take their due of it. One of them, a young man named Ailig an Òir who had never in his life paid a toll on anything and saw no reason to begin, was caught within the year selling a season's gathering to a passing trader on the open shingle a mile short of the gate, and was made, by his own guild before the crown's reeve ever heard of it, to forfeit the whole of that season's take to the market fund inside the walls; and he paid it, by every account that reached the town after, without argument, judging that a guild's own anger was a costlier thing to cross than any single season's amber.

The market, once it was a season or two old, kept its own small order of business without much help from the crown's toll-takers at all: honey-sellers set up nearest the gate, where the smell of it might draw a buyer in off the road before he had thought to look for anything else, and the whale-oil men kept to the far corner by the wall, downwind, where the lamps that burned it through the winter dark could be filled without the whole square smelling of the trade. Sheep from the northern moors came down twice a year, at the two turning feasts, driven in by drovers who slept the nights before and after in the lee of the new wall and grumbled, as drovers everywhere grumbled, that a walled town charged more for a night's shelter than an open shore ever had, and paid it anyway, because the wall kept the wind off as well as it kept the amber counted.

## V. Of Dòmhnall's Death

Dòmhnall mac Ailein taught on the strand at {{place:burg:166}} for five-and-thirty years past the day he first opened his mouth to the men beside him, and never once, in all that time, took up any office the order might have given him, saying that a net-maker who called himself a priest was worth less to the stone than a net-maker who went on mending nets. In his last years his hands grew too stiff for the finer twine-work, and he sat instead on an upturned creel at the edge of the strand while younger hands mended what he could no longer mend himself, and talked, when anyone stopped to listen, of nothing more than the tide and the weather and, now and again, of the stone, in much the same tone he used for the tide and the weather, as though the three had never seemed to him three separate subjects at all.

He died in his seventieth year, not long before the fire that would take the roofs of a village he had never seen, quietly, in the small house he had kept his whole life within sound of the tide-horn he had never asked anyone to blow, and an Fhianna carried him to the shrine themselves and set him down before it, not within it, judging, as he would himself have judged, that the stone's own watch belonged to those still living to keep it. It is told at Inis thais that Beathag nic Raoghnaill, by then an old woman herself, stood longest at the grave of anyone in the town, and said to no one in particular that she had brought a roof to shelter a man who wanted no roof of his own kept over him, and did not know, to her own dying day, whether that had been a kindness or a joke the tide alone understood.

The old man who had once sent his eldest son down to question Dòmhnall's right to name the stone came to the shrine that day too, bent now with age past what his own sons had been on the morning of that first asking, and stood a long while before the grey stone with his hand on it, saying nothing that anyone thought worth carrying home afterward. He had blown the tide-horn himself, in his own turn, more mornings than he could any longer count, and it was noticed, though not remarked on at the time, that he was the last to leave the shrine that evening, after even the dead man's own household had gone home to their supper.

## VI. Of the Two Horses

At {{place:burg:19}} King Aonghas mac Uisdein, seven-and-forty years crowned and come nine-and-sixty years old himself by then, had watched the quarrels of Òrd an t-Seabhaig and Òrd na Cloiche from An Talla Dearg with the wariness of a man who owed his own crown to neither order and meant to keep it that way. He had grown, in his father's house and his own, alongside a third rite altogether, older on the northern moors than either the hawk's flight or the stone's watch: Òrd Mhacha' rite of Macha, kept by families who tended two horses foaled of one mare on one night, Liath Macha the grey and Dubh Sainglenn the black, yoked from their first spring to a single chariot pole so that neither ran without the other.

It was said among Òrd Mhacha, and Aonghas had heard it said at his own father's table before he had any crown to weigh it by, that the two horses were two powers kept always in balance, one light and one dark, neither swallowing the other, and that the vein under the hill at {{place:burg:491}} belonged to both alike, fed by dark labor and bringing up bright fire, and that a house or a crown that forgot to keep the two yoked together would find, sooner or later, one horse running loose and the chariot broken behind it. He remembered, from a boy's own memory and no clearer than a boy's memories usually are, being lifted onto his father's shoulder once at such a rite to see over the heads of the grown worshippers of Òrd Mhacha, and asking why there were two horses and not one, and his father telling him that one horse pulling alone always pulls a chariot in a circle, and that it takes two, pulling together and never quite agreeing, to pull it straight.

Aonghas had no love of quarrels; he had watched, these forty years and more, the priests of Òrd an t-Seabhaig and an Fhianna divide a single shrine's hours between them and count it a victory, and he had begun to think, as an old man thinks slowly toward a decision he cannot quite date the start of, that a crown ought to keep something that did not need dividing. When he first told his own household he meant to ride out to Òrd Mhacha' rite himself, one of his older councillors, a man who had served his father before him and had buried two wives and outlived most of his own objections to anything, said only that a crown was free to keep whatever custom pleased it, but that a crown seen kneeling to a moor-custom might find Na Seachd Taighean asking, before the year was out, why a crown could not be seen kneeling to their own custom instead. Aonghas answered that he did not mean to kneel to anything, only to put his hand where the rite asked every hand to go, and that a house wanting the same honour need only send its own hands to stand at a yoke-pole, since Òrd Mhacha, so far as he had ever heard, turned no one away who came to pull.

He rode out to Òrd Mhacha' own rite the spring he turned nine-and-sixty, {{date:III-0089}}, a man come to stand at a yoke-pole among the families who had kept the rite since before his own house held the hall, rather than a king come merely to be shown a wonder, and he took the near pole in his own two hands when the horses were brought up together at dawn, one hand to the grey side and one to the black, the way the rite asked of anyone who meant to stand in it and do more than watch. The court that had ridden out with him, seeing their king at a yoke-pole in the wet grass like any wood-watcher's son, did as courts do, and within the year half An Talla Dearg's own household had taken up the rite alongside him. So Òrd Mhacha, which had kept to its own moors and its own quiet families for longer than any crowned house had troubled to notice it, became from that year the order of {{place:burg:19}} and of the crown, without a single quarrel fought over it, the way a stream that has run quietly along a field's edge for years becomes the field's only water once a dry summer comes.

## VII. Of the Fire at Tobar dhearg

That same year, in the season Òrd Mhacha call the between-moon, a fire ran through the thatch of {{place:burg:398}}, a small place among the southern quarries where the roofs had always been thatch because the quarries near it cut building stone and had never troubled to cut slate, and it took most of the village before the last of it was beaten out with wet sacking and river water carried up in whatever would hold it, {{date:III-0090}}. No one died in it, though three families lost the whole of what stood over their heads, and the village's own headman, walking the black ground the next morning with his boots already through to the ash beneath, said only that a roof was easier lost than a life and that the village would build again before the winter found them without one.

They built again in slate, this time, the quarries near Tobar dhearg holding, as it turned out, stone fit for roofing as well as for building, once a man troubled to look for it there, and word of it went as far as {{place:burg:132}} within the year: that a village burned to the ground in the spring had roofed its houses again in stone before the first frost, and had never once gone back to thatch. Tobar dhearg was known, from that year on, along the whole of the southern road, for its slate roofs, the way another town was known for its wool or its market, a plain fact that outlived, as plain facts do, the fire that had made it true.

One of the three burned-out families, a quarryman's household that had lost a byre and half a season's stored grain along with its roof, took the new slate first of any house in the village, judging, as the quarryman himself put it to whoever would listen, that a man who cut the stone for a living had less excuse than any neighbour to go on sleeping under straw. His own children slept that first slate-roofed winter, so the family told it afterward, more soundly than they had slept since before the fire: less for any particular warmth the stone gave that thatch had not, than for the plain unfrightening weight of it overhead in a bad wind, a weight thatch had never had and had never been missed for until the night it was gone.

Aonghas heard the tidings of the fire at An Talla Dearg and sent what the crown's own granaries could spare south before the winter closed the roads, and then, the following spring, rode out again to Òrd Mhacha' rite as he had the year before. A thing done once by a king and never repeated is no rite, only a single day's fancy, and he meant this one to hold. He came to the yoke at dawn as the horses were brought up, Liath Macha pale as the mist off the northern sea and Dubh Sainglenn dark as the coal Taigh na Fèithe sent up by the basket-load, and he set his hand to the pole again, one side and then the other, an old man now by any village's reckoning though not yet by his own house's long custom, and stood there a while in the wet grass with a hand on each yoke-pole, the grey horse and the black quiet under his two hands together.
