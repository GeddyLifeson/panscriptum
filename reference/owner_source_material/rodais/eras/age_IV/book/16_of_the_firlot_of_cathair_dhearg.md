# Of the Firlot of Cathair dhearg

*LE 254 – 289*

## I. Of the Girl Who Counted the Baskets

Sìleas nic Fhearchair was eleven years old when she first understood that a king's word was only as good as the thing it was measured against. She stood that day at her father's elbow in the market below the red hall at {{place:burg:19}}, where two women had come near to blows over a basket of barley. One swore it held its full weight; the other swore it did not, and that she had been cheated by the same basket twice before at the same stall. King Fearchar Donn, grown heavy and slow with age by then and disliking to be drawn from his hall for such things, sent a steward to weigh the grain against the palace's own measure and settle it. The steward came back and said the baskets did not agree: the market's was smaller than the hall's by the width of two fingers across the rim, and had been for longer than anyone selling there could remember.

"Then whose is the true one?" Sìleas asked, before anyone had thought to ask it of her.

Her father laughed, not unkindly, and said that was a question for men older than either of them, and led her away by the hand. She did not forget it. That night at supper she asked him again, in front of the steward, whether the market's basket or the hall's was the true one, and the steward answered that the hall's was, being the elder measure, and her father said only that it hardly mattered which was elder so long as folk agreed on one, and went back to his meat. She turned the two answers over in her head for days, the steward's and her father's, and could not make them into a single thought, and kept trying anyway, for she was a child who did not like leaving a thing half understood.

She was a quiet child, given to watching rather than speaking, and the court took her silences for shyness until it learned better. Her mother had died birthing her; the one brother she had been born after had died a child, and she had no sister, only her father's slow decline and the hall full of men who spoke over her head as though she were a stool set in the wrong place. She learned early to sit very still at the edge of a council and be forgotten, and to carry away afterward more of what had been said than any of the men who had said it would have credited a girl of her years with keeping.

Fearchar Donn had reigned since he was a young man, son of the first king the custodian houses ever chose, and he wore his last years like a coat too heavy for the season. His last ride north to Muileann dhearg, to see the vein-house's winter burning as every king before him had gone at least once, fell in his fifty-ninth year, and he never made the ride again. He sat in the red hall and let the shires send their disputes to him on skins and boards, and answered them slower each year, until the answers came so slow that the shires had generally settled the matter themselves before the king's word reached them.

In the spring of LE 256 he took to his bed with a cough that would not leave his chest, and did not rise from it again. Sìleas sat with him through the worst of it, thirteen years old, dry-eyed because she had already grieved him in pieces over the preceding years and had none left to spend on the last of him. He died in the month of Bealltach, sixty-eight years old, and was carried up to be laid among the kings at the burying-ground behind the hall.

She was not crowned that year, nor the next. The custom the houses kept, and had kept since the choosing that made her grandfather's line, held that no one too young to be answered for their own oath should be crowned to make one, and thirteen was reckoned too young. The heads of the seven custodian houses, who had sworn to keep the vein and the right to it in the year of the white sorrow, took it in turn to sit the hall in her name, a month at a time, and quarrelled among themselves over which of their number should have her ear. She learned in those two years to tell a man's true business from the business he gave as his reason for coming, and she learned it well, for she had nothing else to do but listen.

## II. Of the Crowning at Fifteen

She was crowned in the moon of Damhrach, LE 258, fifteen years old, in the same hall where her father had died and his father before him. The cup that was passed hand to hand at a crowning was old beyond any teller's naming of it, worn thin at the lip where generations of royal mouths had touched it, and the oath said over it was the same oath, word for word, that had been said over Fearchar mac Lachlainn when the houses chose him. Sìleas said it in a clear voice that carried to the back of the hall, and an old woman near the door, who had heard four crownings in her life, said afterward that the girl's voice had not shaken at all, which the old woman took for a good sign and was not wrong to.

She did not weep, and she did not smile. She took the cup, drank from it, and handed it on, and the heads of the seven houses came forward one by one and put their hands between hers and swore to hold the vein and its right as it had always been held. Their faces she watched as they did it, the way she had learned to watch faces in the two years of the regency, and marked which of them meant it and which of them were only saying words that custom required, though she said nothing of what she marked, then or for years after.

That night, alone in the chamber that had been her father's, she found the old market steward's tally of the two baskets, still among his leaves where no one had thought to clear it. She kept it. It was a small thing to keep, a scrap that answered nothing by itself, but she was a girl who kept small things until they came useful, and this one came useful sooner than she expected.

## III. Of Am Fuath Mòr and the Hooked Wall

In the autumn of her second year crowned, a storm out of the north broke over the harbour of {{place:burg:37}}, on the island's north coast, that the fishers there came to call Am Fuath Mòr, the great hate. It came in the black of night with no warning the watch could give, and it took the old harbour wall down in one working of the sea, stone from stone, and with the wall went forty boats at their moorings and eighteen men and women who had gone down in the dark to try to save them. The town buried its dead on the headland above the ruined wall, in a row so long that the reeve who kept the count lost his place twice and had to begin it again.

The reeve was a young man named Raghnall mac Nèill, thirty-four years old that winter, who had held the office only four years and had never buried so many at once. He had gone down to the strand himself in the worst of the storm, roused by a boy pounding on his door, and had stood in water to his knees hauling on a rope with three other men to bring in a boat with two children still lashed inside it, and had not been quick enough with two more ropes that broke before they were ever thrown. He carried that failure with him the rest of his life and never once, in any letter or telling that survives of him, laid the blame anywhere but on the wall.

That spring he wrote to the capital, not asking for grain or silver, which the crown could ill spare after a hard season, but asking leave to send to the masons who had built the queen's own bridge and ask them what a wall might be built to, that the sea could not take down twice.

Sìleas granted it without a council, which some of the older heads thought unqueenly of her, a girl of seventeen deciding a matter of stone and mortar on her own word. She had read Raghnall's letter twice and noted that it asked for nothing but knowledge, and thought that a request worth granting on its own account. The masons went north and stood a long while on the ruined headland with Raghnall, turning stones over with their boots and arguing among themselves in a tongue of angles and courses that the fishers standing about could not follow. What they gave him in the end was a plan for a wall built higher than the old one and turned at its outer end in a hook, so that the worst of the sea's push broke against the curve of it and spent itself before it could reach the harbour behind.

It took three years to raise, stone hauled from a quarry two days inland by cart and sled, and every able hand in the town given over to it in the slack months between one catch and the next. There were those in the town who grumbled that a hook at the wall's end was a strange shape for honest stone, more suited, they said, to a fish-trap than a harbour, and would not believe it until they saw the first winter gale come on and watched the sea's whole strength break against the curve of it and slide away spent, no higher at the harbour's throat than on a calm evening. Raghnall stood on the finished wall the day the last course was set and put his palm flat against the stone, the way a man tests a beast's flank before he trusts it, and it stood through every storm that came after, in his lifetime and long past it.

## IV. Of the Walls of Dùn dhearg and a Supper Kept Cold

The same year the mason's plan went north to Seann Toll, Sìleas took a consort of her own choosing: Dùghall mac Ìomhair, a young man of the eastern hill-house Clann Ìomhair, whom she had known since the two years of her regency, when his father had sat the hall in her name for one of the twelve months and brought his son along to the capital because there was no one else to leave him with. Dùghall was quiet in a way she found she liked, slow to speak and slower to promise, and where the other heads' sons had courted her with gifts and verses she had never asked for, he had once, in her sixteenth year, simply told her which of the men speaking to her in the hall that day meant what they said and which were only speaking to be seen speaking, and been right about all six. They were married at Cathair dhearg in the month of Faolach, with the seven houses' heads in attendance and Raghnall mac Nèill's letter about the wall arriving, by chance, the same week, so that the wedding feast and the granting of the masons were spoken of together for a season after, as though one had somehow caused the other. At the feast Dùghall's father rose and made the kind of speech a father makes at such a thing, full of the honour done his house and the long friendship between it and the crown, and Sìleas, watching him say it, thought she heard under the honour a note of a man reckoning what the marriage had bought him, though she could not then have said what she thought he meant to spend it on.

It was not a full year later that word came from the east that Clann Ìomhair had begun to wall their own seat at {{place:burg:422}} in stone, without asking the crown's leave to do it. No custodian house had raised walls of its own before; the crown had always been asked, if only as a form, and the form had always been granted, but this time no one had asked. Sìleas heard it first from a rider and said nothing for the length of a held breath, then asked only how far along the work stood, which told the rider she meant to let it stand rather than to send men to pull it down, though she gave no word either way that day.

She summoned Dùghall's father to the red hall that season, on some other business that both of them understood to be a pretext, and had a supper laid for him in the hall's smaller chamber, the one kept for guests who were neither strangers nor quite family. It was a good supper, by every account that survives of it: fowl and barley bread and the last of the summer's fruit, laid out with proper care, and good candles burned rather than the tallow the chamber usually made do with. No one who was in the room that evening ever called it a warm one. Dùghall sat between his wife and his father and said almost nothing through the whole of it, turning his cup on the board without drinking from it, and his father, for his part, ate well and spoke of the roads and the harvest and never once mentioned the stone going up around his own hall, and neither did the queen. Twice the old man asked after her health with a warmth that fooled no one at the table, and twice she answered him with the same warmth, in the same measure, and both of them understood exactly what was being said by everything that was left unsaid.

When the meat was cleared the old man made as if to speak of the roads a third time, and Sìleas set down her own cup with enough plainness that he heard it, and asked him instead how the quarrying went at Dùn dhearg, since she had heard the stone there cut cleaner than the stone at Cathair dhearg's own quarries. He allowed that it did. She said she was glad to hear a custodian house so well provided in its own hills that it need trouble the crown for nothing, not even leave, and let the silence that followed sit as long as it wished to. When the old man rose to go, she walked him to the door herself, which was more courtesy than the evening had otherwise shown him, and told him only that a house wise enough to wall itself was wise enough to remember whose land it stood on. He bowed and said nothing to that either, and the walls of Dùn dhearg went up all the same, and stood, and no crown's men were ever sent against them.

## V. Of the Quarrel over a Basket

It was in that same year, riding to the market below the hall on some ordinary business she no longer troubled to remember afterward, that Sìleas came on a quarrel over a basket that put her in mind of the one she had watched as a child of eleven. She did not get down from the cart. She sat above the crowd and watched a woman selling oats and a farmer buying them come near to open blows over whether the basket between them held what it was sworn to hold, and heard, under the shouting, the same complaint she had heard as a girl: that the baskets of the market were not the baskets of the shire seat, nor the shire seat's the same as the next shire's, and a man might be cheated or might cheat without either party quite meaning it, simply because no two measures on the island agreed.

That same evening she sent for the old steward's tally, the scrap she had kept since her crowning night, and set it beside her on the table while she wrote out, in her own hand, what she meant to do. There would be one measure, and one only, cut to a fixed size and bound in iron so that no season's swelling or shrinking of the wood could cheat it, and copies of it, exact to the width of a finger, sent to every shire seat on the island, so that a basket weighed true at Cathair dhearg would weigh true anywhere a Dia-thìrich stood to sell or buy. She had the first of them made by the capital's own coopers, oak staves bound with three iron hoops, and had her own mark burned into the base of it before it left her hands, so that no one could ever claim a later copy was truer than the standard it was copied from. It went out from Cathair dhearg by cart, one to each shire seat, through the summer and autumn of that year, and the shires came to call it, simply, the firlot of Cathair dhearg, for they had no other name to give it and needed none.

It did not end every quarrel over a basket; nothing so simple ever does. But within a handful of years a man buying grain a hundred miles from the capital could set his own basket against the shire seat's firlot and know, without argument, whether he had been cheated or had cheated, and where before the answer to that question had depended on which of the two disputants shouted longer, it now depended on nothing but the iron-bound oak, which shouted at no one.

The first quarrel it settled outright, and the first that was ever written down as settled by it and nothing else, was at {{place:burg:67}}, the granary town, some nine years after the firlots went out. A carter and a miller had fallen out over a load of oats, each swearing the other had shorted him, and had gone on swearing it in the open street until the reeve, tired of the noise, sent for the town's firlot and measured the load out in front of both of them and the crowd their shouting had drawn. It came out exact to the mark. The carter, who had been the louder of the two, said nothing further and went home; the miller, more graciously, offered to stand the reeve a cup for his trouble, which the reeve accepted, and the reeve's own account of the day, which survives among the granary's older boards, ends by noting that the whole matter had taken less time to settle than the quarrel itself had taken to shout.

## VI. Of the Tally-Houses and the Long City's Market

Her son Uisdean was born in the twelfth year of her reign, a quiet, watchful child who watched the court the way she had watched it herself as a girl, and she found she could not decide whether that pleased her or unsettled her, and did not try very hard to decide.

The firlot solved the size of a basket, but it did not solve what a man was owed for what the basket held, and by the twenty-sixth year of her reign she had turned her mind to that harder question. A weaver of the capital might bring cloth to the granary and take grain for it, and swear afterward that the grain given was less than the cloth was worth, and there was nothing but memory and temper to prove either side of it once the goods had changed hands and both parties had gone their ways. She set a tally-house in each of the crown's own granaries, a plain room with a table and a box of hazel sticks, where a clerk sat through the market days and cut a notch for every measure of grain given over for cloth, or salt, or a day's labour on the roads, splitting the stick lengthwise so that each party to the trade kept half and neither half could be altered without it showing against its match. It was the first reckoning of trade that ran the length of the island in one hand, and no coin passed through any of it, for the island had none and did not yet know it wanted any. A trader from the long city in the south, watching the first sticks cut at the granary of Cathair dhearg, remarked to the clerk that he had never in his life seen a bargain that could not be argued about afterward, and the clerk answered, without looking up from his cutting, that this was rather the point of it. The trader asked what was to stop a man simply cutting a false notch on his own half after the fact; the clerk held up the two halves of a finished stick, showing how the grain of the wood ran unbroken from one to the other only when they were laid together true, and said that a man was welcome to try, and welcome to explain the false cut to whoever held the other half.

The long city was Cathair fhada, on its ridge in the south, and it was there, three years on, that Sìleas granted the charter the merchants of that country had been asking for since before her father's day: a market on the ridge at every full moon, with its own tally-house for the trade of the south, so that the honey and hemp and wax and pottery that had gone north by ones and twos, whenever a single trader found the road worth the walking, might now go up together, a company at a time, timed to the moon so that no one need travel the road alone. The first full-moon market was held in the month of Ògach, and drew traders from three days' walk in every direction; the reeve of Cathair fhada, an old woman who had petitioned for the charter longer than anyone else living, stood at the ridge's edge that whole first day and would not be persuaded to sit down, though her family begged her to, saying she had waited twenty years to see it and meant to see all of it standing up.

## VII. Of the Bridge

In the one-and-thirtieth year of her reign the old stone bridge over the Abhainn dhomhain at {{place:burg:19}}, which had stood since before her grandfather's grandfather and had never in living memory been wide enough for two loaded carts to pass each other without one backing to the bank, was widened at last, the masons cutting new stone in against the old so that the join could hardly be told from the road above it. It was, by then, an ordinary kind of work for her to order; she had ordered walls and wharves and granary floors in every shire on the island by that year, and thought little more of a bridge than she thought of any of them.

She rode down to see it finished on a grey morning in Bearnach, with Uisdean, nineteen now and already carrying himself like a man who expected one day to be asked for his own judgment on such things, riding beside her. The masons had cut her name into the downstream parapet, low down where a woman leaning over the rail to watch the water might find it without looking for it, in letters plain enough that a child learning them could read them without help. She had not asked for this, and stood a long moment looking at it, the water going by beneath, brown and quick with the spring rain, before she got down from her horse and put her hand flat on the stone and ran her thumb along the cut of her own name, feeling the edges of the letters the way a blind woman might read a thing by touch rather than by sight.

She said nothing to Uisdean about what she was thinking, and he, watching her, asked nothing, having learned that much from her already. Behind them the carts that had waited on the near bank began to cross, two abreast for the first time in the bridge's long life, and neither of them turned to watch them go.
