# Of the Handful for the New Hearth

*LE 19 – 31*

> *Dòrlach airson an teallaich ùir,*
> *is an seann teallach fhathast blàth.*
> A handful for the new hearth,
> and the old hearth still warm.
>
> — the saying kept afterward at the vein-house, of the ruling of LE 22

## I. Of the Boy Given to the Vein-House

Donnchadh mac Thormoid was nine years old when his father walked him up the coal-road to Taigh na Fèithe and left him there, the way a man leaves a lamb with a shepherd he trusts and does not look back at, for looking back is thought to unsettle the lamb. It was the custom, by then, to swear a boy or a girl to the vein's service for seven years if the household could spare the hands and the vein-house would have them, the same manner in which the wrights of the strand at Seann Skell had begun to swear their own apprentices; and Thormod, who cut turf for three households on the moor above Muileann dhearg and had five children and not much land, thought his youngest son's hands better spent at a desk than on a spade. He said as much to the boy on the road up, not unkindly. Donnchadh remembered the words for the rest of his life, though he came in time to doubt his father had meant them as a kindness at all, only as the truth, plainly given, the way a poor man gives a true thing to a child because he has nothing softer to give.

The vein-house stood black on its hill above the diggings, built of the same dark stone the galleries were cut from, so that from a distance it seemed less built than grown, a knuckle of the mountain that had learned to keep a roof. Within, it smelled of tallow and cold stone and, faintly, always, of the coal underneath, a smell Donnchadh never afterward could smell anywhere else without seeing the low black door of the custody-room and the shelf where the book was kept. He was set to sweeping at first, and to carrying water for the elder clerks, and only after the better part of a year to holding a stylus at all, and then only to copy, letter after letter, the names already written by better hands, until his own hand no longer shook doing it.

The master of the vein-house in those years was Eòghann mac Mhuirich, an old man even then, stooped from forty years bent over the custody-book, who had written the figure of the counted households in the winter of LE 3 and, as the tale went among the clerks, had not looked up from the page for the length of a hundred breaths after he wrote it. He kept Donnchadh at his own elbow more than the other boys, not, Donnchadh came to think, because he showed any particular gift, but because Eòghann liked a quiet hand nearby and Donnchadh had learned early not to talk while a man was thinking.

It was in his first winter at the vein-house, before he had held the stylus at all, that Donnchadh saw Fearchar mac Ruairidh come up the hill on a grey horse with mud to its hocks, and heard, crouched by the door with an armful of kindling he had no reason to be slow about carrying in, the beginning of the trouble that would take three years to settle and that he would, in the end, be given the pen to close.

## II. Of the Petition

Fearchar mac Ruairidh had gone, by then, from a custodian who sat at the vein-house board twice a season to a man who was hardly ever off the road: down to Seann Skell to see the boards, out to the shire seats to hear how the levy of sail-cloth was going, up again to the vein-house with word of what the harbour needed and what the households of the roll had asked of him, though none of them, strictly, had any right to ask him anything at all, the roll being a thing of household and hearth and not of office. He had taken the asking on himself, the clerks said, the way a man takes on a debt nobody has billed him for, because somebody had to carry it and he found himself standing nearest when it fell.

What he wanted, that first day and for three years after, was simple to say and hard, it turned out, to grant. The departing would go with what they could carry on their backs and in their hulls: tools, seed, cloth, the small worth of a household uprooted. They would not go with fire already in their hands, unless the island gave it to them, and there was only one fire on the island that was not any man's own to give: the coal of the vein, which the custody-book measured out hearth by hearth under the Small-Burning Law and had never, in all the years since Fionnlagh Dall filled the cup, measured out to anything that was not a hearth standing still on Dia-thìr.

"Let each hull that is fit to sail carry a measure," Fearchar said, the first time Donnchadh heard him say it, standing in the yard with his hat still in his hand and rain running off his shoulders, to Eòghann and two of the elder custodians who had come out to hear him because it was raining too hard to make him wait outside. "One measure. Sealed, counted, entered in the book like any other hearth's. I ask for no more than the Law already gives a household that stays."

"The Law measures a hearth," said one of the custodians, a heavy man named Cailean whom Donnchadh came later to know as a hard voice at every board and a soft one at every sickbed, "not a keel. A hearth stands where it stood. It does not go anywhere. What you are asking, Fearchar, though you dress it kindly, is that the vein answer for a fire it will never see burn and never see go cold, on the far side of I know not what water, for I know not how long. This is a new law, and none of us here has the right to make one."

"Then I ask you to make one," said Fearchar, "or else to tell the households of the roll plainly, before they go, that the coal they have held the right to since their grandmothers' grandmothers will end, for them, the day they step aboard. I would rather they heard it now, from you, than found it out cold, three weeks past the mist."

Nobody answered him that day. Eòghann sent him down the hill with a cup of broth against the rain and a promise that the matter would be put to the board at the next burning, which was the vein-house's way, Donnchadh learned, of saying that a thing was too large to be settled standing up in a wet yard.

## III. Of the Winter Burning

It was not settled at the next burning, nor the one after. Donnchadh grew a hand's width and lost two milk teeth and buried his mother's brother, who died of a fever that went round the coal-breakers' cots below the hill, in the years the vein-house argued Fearchar's petition back and forth across its own board without coming any nearer an answer than it had been the day he stood dripping in the yard. He came to know the shape of the argument the way he knew the shape of the hill from his window: Cailean and two or three others holding that the coal had never crossed water and never should, that a measure sealed and sent out of sight of the vein-house was a measure the custody-book could no longer answer for, gone from the Law's keeping into nobody's; and Fearchar, with a smaller knot of custodians who had come round to him one by one, holding that the Law measured hearths because hearths were what the island had ever had need of measuring, and that a hearth crossing open water was still a hearth, only a stranger one, and deserved no less than any other.

The winter burning of LE 22 was the one the vein-house remembered afterward, because it was the one at which the argument, having worn itself thin over three years, finally wore through. Donnchadh was twelve that winter, old enough to be let sit at the back of the hall with the other clerks' boys while the custodians spoke, though not old enough to be asked his own mind on anything, and he remembered the hall by its cold more than by anything said in it for the first hour: a long low room cut into the hill, lit by rush-lights that guttered whenever the door opened, with the custodians ranged along both sides of the board and the custody-book lying closed at its head, as it always lay when a matter touched what was written in it.

Cailean spoke longest, and hardest. "We are not asked to give a handful of coal," he said, near the end of it, when his voice had gone rough with two hours of being answered. "We are asked to say that the Law follows a household wherever a household chooses to go, though it go past the edge of everything the Law was ever made to answer for. If we say it once, for a ship, we may be asked to say it again, for I do not know what, and I would have us think three times before we hand away the one thing on this island that has never once been given past its own bounds."

"And if we do not say it," Fearchar answered him, quiet now himself, all the road-weary force gone out of his voice and something flatter and more tired left in its place, "then I will stand on the west quay the day the fleet goes out, and I will watch fires kindled on those decks with wood alone, and I will know, better than any man in this hall, which households among them held the right to the coal their whole lives and go without it now for the first time, because we would not answer a small thing simply because it frightened us to answer it. I have counted those households, Cailean, with my own hand. Better to answer for a measure of coal gone where none of us can follow it than answer to them, one day, for why we let them go cold out of pride in a Law that was never made to be proud of anything."

There was a silence after that which Donnchadh remembered as longer than it can truly have been, the kind of silence in which a room decides something without any one voice being heard to decide it. Eòghann, who had said almost nothing the whole evening, put both hands flat on the board at last and said that he would have the custody-book opened, and the question put to writing, and that they would vote it as they voted any other matter that had been talked enough. They voted before the rush-lights burned out. Donnchadh did not, at twelve, understand every word of the ruling as it was first spoken aloud, but he understood, from the way the hall emptied afterward, quiet and a little spent, like a house after a birth that has gone on too long and come out well at the last, that something had been decided that would outlast the argument that made it.

## IV. Of the Fair Copy

The ruling was simple enough, once it was written plain, though it had taken three winters and one long night to arrive at: each hull that should be found fit to sail might carry one sealed measure of the coal, no more than a single household might burn in a season under the Small-Burning Law, entered in the custody-book as any hearth's measure was entered, and sealed by the vein-house's own mark so that no man could say, after, that it had been taken rather than given. It answered for no hearth once it was gone past the mist, and promised no more fire after that one measure than the household would have found in its own grate at home in a single ordinary winter: exactly the size of thing the custody-book had always measured, Donnchadh thought later, and no bigger. Fearchar had asked for a hearth's worth and been given, in the end, precisely that, dressed up in three years of argument to make it look larger than it was.

He was fifteen, and had been three years at the desk by then, when Eòghann called him to make the fair copy. His hand was not the finest in the vein-house; the ruling had been argued and voted and set down in rough marks by an old scribe's cramped fingers, and somebody young enough to sit a whole day without his back giving out was wanted to render it clean for the book that would stand, unaltered, for as long as the vein-house kept records at all. It was his first entry in the custody-book proper, and he remembered every line of it afterward the way another man remembers his first field ploughed alone.

He copied it letter for letter, the sealed measure, the single household's season, the vein-house's mark; and when he had finished, and sanded the page, and sat back to let the ink set, he noticed a thing that nobody in the hall, arguing three winters over whether the coal might go at all, had thought to argue over: that the ruling, in giving the coal, gave nothing else with it. Every other entry he had copied that year, weddings recorded for the dowry-right, deaths recorded for the inheritance of a hearth, opened or closed with some small word of the vein-house's own, a blessing on the hearth, a wish for its long burning, the kind of thing a scribe wrote as much from habit as from instruction. This entry had none of it. It measured the coal and said nothing over it. Donnchadh sat a long while with his sanded page before him, deciding whether that was an oversight to be mended or a thing the custodians had meant, without saying so aloud, and in the end he copied it exactly as it had been given him, with no blessing added, because it was not his place to add one, and because, he thought, turning it over afterward on the walk back to his own cot, perhaps a hall that could not agree for three winters whether to give the coal at all had simply had no blessing left in it to spare.

## V. Of the Measures Sealed

The measures themselves were made the year after: not counted out against any tally of hulls, for nobody at the vein-house yet knew, or claimed to know, how many hulls the strand at Seann Skell would in the end put in the water, but sealed as they were asked for, each one against the words *for each hull that shall be ready*, a phrase Donnchadh had copied into the ruling himself and had not, until he watched the first measures made, understood the weight of. It meant that the vein-house had bound itself to a number it could not know in advance, open-ended as a debt with no term set on it, and that every measure sealed that year and the years after would go down the coal-road to Seann Skell without any man at the vein-house ever adding up how many had gone, because the ruling had never asked for a count, only for readiness.

He watched the first of them sealed on a grey morning in the moon of Dubhlas, in the low room off the custody-hall where the vein-house kept its scales: a store-clerk weighing out the coal against the same stone weights used for any household's handful, no more careful and no less, and Eòghann himself pressing the mark into the wax while Fearchar stood by, hat again in hand, saying nothing at all, as though he had used up every word he had on the matter three years before and had only silence left to spend on seeing it done.

## VI. Of the Masons Sent West

The queen sent no household of her own to the roll, then or ever, but in the same season that the first measures were sealed she lent the harbour's trade something the vein-house could not give it: hands. A gang of masons out of the capital, under a man named Ailean mac Nèill, a broad, sun-browned mason not yet thirty who had cut his teeth dressing stone for the Red Hill's own walls, came down the river road to Seann Skell that spring with wagons of squared stone and the queen's word that they were sent for the harbour's trade and for no other reason the rider would name. It was said afterward, at the vein-house as everywhere else, that Beathag had sent them for the fleet and called it trade because trade was a thing a queen might send masons for without anyone asking whom she had sent them to help; but it was also said, by those who had watched her rule since her mother's day, that she had never once, in fifteen years on the red hall's high seat, called a thing by any name but the truest one she could give it without breaking her own word, and that if she called it trade, then trade, in some fashion known best to herself, was what she believed it to be.

Ailean's gang lengthened the west quay through that summer and the next, dressed stone laid course on course where the old timber staging had done duty since before anyone living could remember, so that the deep hulls building on the strand above might one day lie alongside at low water without grounding their new keels on the mud the old quay had never needed to clear. It was hard, wet work, the tide taking back at every ebb what the masons had set at every flood, and Ailean, the harbour-house's own scribe Calum wrote that year in a note that came up with the coal-tallies and that Donnchadh, still a boy at his desk, read twice for the pleasure of a line written plain, "curses the sea in three tongues and lays stone against it anyway, which is more than most men do against anything."

## VII. Of the Queen on the New Stones

It is told that Beathag herself rode down to Seann Skell in the second summer of the quay's building, the only time in all the years of the fleet's making that she is known to have gone to see it with her own eyes, though her sister's household had been three years on the roll by then and her sister's hulls three years abuilding on the same strand. She rode the twenty-four miles in a day, with a small following and no herald sent ahead to have the town made ready for her, and stood a while on the new-laid stone in the evening light, watching the tide come in against courses that had not yet worn smooth, while Ailean's men, who had not been told a queen was coming and would not have known her by sight if a rider had not named her quietly among them, went on laying stone within a few yards of her as though she were any woman come down to watch the work.

Mòrag came to her there, on the stones, the two sisters standing together where the masonry was still raw and pale and the tide had not yet worn it, and it is told that they spoke a while of nothing that mattered: of a horse Mòrag had lately sold, of a leak in a roof at Inis àrsaidh that had taken three tries to mend, of the queen's boy growing too fast for his boots. Neither sister spoke, that evening, of the coal, or the roll, or the last of the hulls the stones under their feet would one day carry off the quay; it was, the harbour-house's own note of that day says, as though each had decided, without a word passing between them to decide it, that an evening might be spent as an evening and not as a leave-taking, though it is also told that when the queen rode home the next morning she looked back once, from the top of the harbour road, at the stones and the half-built hulls and her sister standing among them, and did not look back a second time.

Donnchadh never saw that evening; the vein-house's business kept him on the hill that whole summer, sealing measures against tallies that came up from Seann Skell in a hand he did not know. But the note of it came to him with the coal-figures, as most news of the coast did, folded into the same rider's pouch as a tally of barrels and a line about the weather, and he copied the line about the queen's visit into the custody-book's own margin, where such things were sometimes kept though they answered to no ruling and no Law, because it seemed to him, turning fifteen that year and beginning to understand that not every true thing needed a ruling to be worth the keeping, a fact the vein-house ought not to let go unwritten.

## VIII. Of the Death of Uilleam mac Iain

In the last days of that same year, in the moon of Damhrach, word came up the coal-road that the queen's consort, Uilleam mac Iain, was dead: a man who had never sat at the vein-house's board nor asked it for anything in his life, kind, it was said, and fond of hounds, who had given Beathag a son in the cradle before ever the roll opened and had stood, by every account Donnchadh ever heard, quietly behind her through fifteen years of a crown that was never his own to wear. He had been ill through the summer, the rider said, and had not mended.

The queen kept the funeral small: no great muster of the shires, no feast that would have cost the granaries a season's worth of grain in a year the harvest had been thin, only the red hall's own household and the few custodian heads whose presence courtesy required, gathered a single day at Cathair dhearg and gone home again before the week was out. It was remarked on, at the vein-house as everywhere the tidings travelled, that a queen who had refused to send her own sister anywhere and refused, with the same breath, to keep her back, should bury her own husband as quietly as a crofter buries a wife: no more show made of her grief than the Law made of a hearth's handful. Eòghann, who was by then very old and had himself less than a year left to him, said only, when the tidings reached the vein-house's board, that a queen who measured everything else in her kingdom by what it truly was and not by what it might be dressed to look, would hardly begin dressing up her own sorrow at the one moment in her life least suited to the dressing.

## IX. Of the Last Stone and the Sealing by Lamplight

Ailean mac Nèill's gang set the last stone of the lengthened quay in the following spring, a plain block no larger than any other in the whole course, laid at the seaward end where the new masonry met the old timber staging and the two were bound together with an iron cramp that Ailean himself drove home, the harbour-house's note says, with three blows and no more, as though he grudged the stone a fourth. There was no ceremony made of it that the tellings remember: the tide came in over it that evening exactly as it had come in over every stone before it, and Calum's own boy Dòmhnall, running the new-laid course end to end because a boy will always run the length of a new thing before an adult thinks to stop him, was the only soul on the whole quay who marked, aloud, that it was finished.

On the same evening, though neither of them ever knew the other's hour, Donnchadh sealed a measure of coal by lamplight in the low room off the custody-hall at the vein-house, alone but for the store-clerk who weighed the coal against the stone weights, pressing the vein-house's mark into the wax with a hand that no longer shook at all, sixteen years old and four years come from a nine-year-old led up a hill by a father who had not looked back. He had by then sealed a good many measures and copied a good many entries, and this one, so far as he knew, differed from the last only in the number written against it in the book; but he found himself, that evening, holding the lamp a moment longer than the sealing needed, over a page that gave, as every page like it had given since his own fair copy first set the words down plain, a handful of coal and no blessing, to a hearth not yet built, on a shore no one at the vein-house had ever seen and no one there, by then, much expected to.
