# Of the Grain Strife and the Forty-One Barges

*LE 37 – 39*

## I. Of the Lean Harvest

Tormod mac Coinnich had kept the granaries at Àth shean for eleven years before the harvest failed under him, and in all that time he had let no petitioner leave the store-house door without a plain answer, yes or no, said to his face. It was the only pride he owned to. He was not a clever man, by his own reckoning, and he had never once been asked to sit at the red hall's board; but he could tell to the bushel what a granary held, and he had buried, as a boy, two brothers and a sister in a year when the barley failed and the reeve of that day had answered every petitioner with a smile and no grain, and he had never in his life forgotten the smell of the empty store-house in that spring, which was of nothing at all, of swept stone and mouse-droppings and cold.

He kept the memory of that spring the way another man might keep a scar: not spoken of, not forgotten, touched now and again in the dark to be sure it was still there. He had been seven years old that year. The store-house at Àth shean, he remembered, stood open and swept and empty, while the reeve of that day, a smiling man named Uisdean whom the shire had trusted for twenty years and trusted no more after, told petitioner after petitioner that there would be grain enough by the new moon, and the new moon came and went with no grain in it. He remembered his mother boiling the last of the seed-barley rather than let it go to the following year's planting, because a planting a family does not live to see does a family no good; and he remembered, most of all, the particular nothing-smell of an empty granary in a hungry season, swept stone and old dust and a cold that seemed to come up out of the bare floor, which he had gone back to smell, deliberately, more than once in the years since, on ordinary days when the stores stood full, as a man touches an old wound to be certain it has healed and to remember, at the same time, exactly how it had felt when it had not.

In the autumn of LE 37 the barley came in thin across the shires south of the mountain, the fourth-poorest harvest of Tormod's eleven years by his own tally-sticks. The granaries at Àth shean, well kept, would carry the country through to the green without much pinching, so long as nothing else was asked of them. Something else was asked of them.

The word came down from Cathair dhearg in the moon of Damhrach, carried by a rider who had ridden two days and slept badly: the fleet at Seann Skell must be fed for its crossing, and the grain for it would come, in whatever measure the council settled on, out of the south shires' stores, because the south shires' stores were the fullest on the island and the nearest to the rivers that ran to the coast. Tormod heard it read out at his own door and said nothing to the rider beyond thanking him for his trouble. That night he sat a long while at his own table with the tally-sticks laid out in their rows, running his thumb along the notches as though he might read something in them beyond what was cut there, and his wife watched him and did not ask what he was thinking, because she had been married to him twenty years and knew.

## II. Of the Barred Doors

The council at the red hall divided on the question through that whole dark season, and divided, by every account that ever reached Àth shean, along no line a plain man could follow. Some of the queen's own councillors held that a household could not be sent out onto open water without bread enough to see it through the crossing, whatever that crossing cost the island behind it; others held that the Small-Burning Law measured a hearth and had never measured a granary, and that grain given to the departed was grain the island's own children would not eat the following spring. The queen herself, it was said, sat through the sessions with her hands folded and gave no opinion that any rider carried south, which was, by then, well known to be her custom in matters she had not yet decided to settle by her own word alone.

What was decided, and by whom, and on what day, Tormod mac Coinnich never learned, then or after. He only knew that no ruling came, week on week, while the rivers ran high with the autumn rain and the barges that would have carried grain to the coast sat idle at their moorings, and the petitioners for the fleet's stores began to come to his own door at Àth shean asking for grain on the strength of a council that had, so far as he could tell, not yet spoken.

He turned the first of them away with a plain no, as was his custom, and thought that would be the end of it. It was not. Within the week a second petitioner came, with a sealed leaf from a captain of one of the hulls that Tormod could not read and did not trust, and after him a third, and by the turn of the moon there were men at his gate most mornings, some civil and some not, asking for measures of barley on no authority he could name. He remembered the reeve who had smiled at his mother with an empty store-house behind him, and on a morning in early Samhnas, with no ruling yet come from the capital and the granaries at Àth shean the last full ones south of the mountain, Tormod mac Coinnich barred the store-house doors himself, set a watch of four men on them day and night, and told the next petitioner, and every one after, that the grain would move when the council's own seal came down and not a day before.

It was not a popular thing to have done. A captain's steward, a broad man with a fleet's own urgency in him and none of a shire's patience, climbed the granary steps on the third day of the bar and asked Tormod straight out whether he meant to let households starve at sea for the sake of his own tallies.

"I mean to let no one starve, here or at sea," Tormod told him, "which is why the grain stays counted and under a roof until I am told, by someone whose word I can write down, how much of it is to go and where. I have watched a granary opened on a smiling man's word before. I will not watch it again."

"You'll answer for this at the red hall."

"I have answered for worse to worse men than sit there," Tormod said, which was not, strictly, true, but the steward did not know that, and went away down the steps red in the face, to the plain satisfaction of the watch, who told the tale in the town that evening with more relish than the moment had held.

Worse than the steward, by far, were the quiet ones. A woman of the south shires came one grey morning with a basket over her arm as though she meant only an ordinary errand, and asked Tormod, in a voice that never once rose, whether her own son's household, entered on the roll and waiting at Seann Skell with two small children, would go hungry on the crossing for Tormod mac Coinnich's stubbornness. He gave her the same answer he gave every petitioner, that the grain would move when the council's seal came and not before, and she heard him out, and thanked him with more courtesy than the steward had shown, and went away down the road with her empty basket still on her arm; and it was she, and not the steward, whom Tormod thought of on the cold watches of that winter, turning her quiet voice over in his mind at an hour when there was no one to answer it and nothing to be done but stand by a barred door and wait for a word that did not come.

The watch stood through Samhnas and Dubhlas and on through the black months after, four men at the granary doors in every weather, relieved every third day, eating from the same stores they guarded because Tormod would not have it said that his own watch went hungry while it kept a full store shut. He walked the round with them himself most nights, more from restlessness than from any doubt of the men he had set there, and came to know the sound the wind made off the river against the granary's north gable better than he knew, by then, his own wife's step on the stair.

Riders came from the capital twice that he counted, once with a leaf that named no figure and satisfied no one, once with nothing written at all, only a spoken word that the matter was still before the council. He read the leaf by lamplight at his own table and set it aside and did not sleep well that night. His own doing gave him no trouble; what kept him awake was a slower doubt, that no one above him at all knew what should be done, and a granary reeve who has begun to think that has nothing left to stand on but his own tally-sticks and his own memory of an empty store-house, which is a narrow thing to build a winter's stand on.

He was not a man given to speech, and he made none to the watch beyond the ordinary business of the round; but on one bitter night near the turn of the year, with the four of them huddled under the granary eave out of a sleet that had not let up since dusk, the youngest of them, a lad not yet twenty who had taken the watch mostly for the extra measure of barley it earned his own mother's house, asked him outright whether he thought the grain would ever move at all, or whether they would stand there the rest of their lives guarding a door nobody meant to open.

"It will move," Tormod said, "when someone above me stops being afraid of his own decision and puts his name to one." And then, because the boy looked so cold and so young standing there in the sleet, he added, more gently than he had said anything to anyone that whole hard season, "It is not your fear to carry, lad. Stand your watch and go home to your mother when it's done, and leave the rest to older backs than yours."

The boy said nothing more that night, but he told it afterward, many years on, as the truest thing he had ever heard said of that whole strange winter, and the only comfort anyone had offered him in the whole of it.

## III. Of the Doors Reopened

One morning in early Sìolach, with the frost still on the ground and the year's first green not yet showing along the ditches, a single rider came down to Àth shean with no leaf at all, only a word for Tormod's own ear: that the grain might move, at such a measure as the council had fixed, and that the reeve of the granaries was thanked for his care of them. Nothing was said of how the matter had been settled, nor by whom, nor why that morning and not another; the rider, when Tormod asked him plainly, said only that he had been sent to say what he had said, and no more, and Tormod, who had asked harder men harder questions all that winter and got no better answer from any of them, let it go.

He took the bar down from the store-house doors with his own hands, though it was work for two men, and stood in the open doorway a while in the cold with the granary dark and full behind him, before he called the watch off and told the waiting carters they might load. The rider who had brought the word lingered long enough to take a cup of small ale against the cold of the road ahead of him, and Tormod, pouring it himself, asked once more, plainly, whether the rider truly knew no more of the matter than he had said.

"I know less than you think, reeve," the rider told him, not unkindly. "I was given a word to carry and a road to carry it on. What was said at the red hall to make that word, I was not in the room to hear, and if I had been, I doubt I'd have understood the half of it. They argue there the way rivers argue with their own banks, all winter, and then one morning the ice goes out and no one can say which thaw broke it." He finished the ale, thanked the reeve for it, and rode on north before the light failed, and Tormod never afterward learned so much as his name.

He felt, standing in the doorway once the rider had gone, less like a man vindicated than like a man who has held his breath a long time and is only now allowed to let it out, with no one troubling to say whether he had held it for anything at all.

No one asked him back to his office that spring. A new reeve was named to the granaries at Àth shean before the barley was in the ground again, a younger man with better friends at the capital, and Tormod mac Coinnich, who had kept the stores of the south shires for eleven years and shut them for the better part of one hard winter on his own reckoning and no one else's, packed what was his own out of the reeve's house and walked home to the farm his father had left him, a morning's walk south of the town. He worked it the rest of his life without complaint, and without once, so far as anyone who knew him ever heard, saying a word against the council that had left him to stand a winter's watch alone and never told him why.

## IV. Of the Forty-One Barges

The barges went down that spring the way grain had always gone from the south shires to the capital, on the broad slow water of Abhainn dhomhain, low in the water and heavy, poled and towed by turns where the current ran against them, each one carrying what forty households might not eat in a season laid up against the crossing of households that would eat none of it on the island again.

On the bridge at Cathair dhearg a woman named Mairead nic Iain kept the tally that spring, as she had kept it most springs of her working life, cutting a notch in a fresh hazel stick for every laden hull that passed beneath the bridge going down toward the sea. It was not thought a hard office, nor a great one; she sat on the parapet with her legs over the water in fine weather and under the lee of the bridge-house in foul, and she had counted salmon boats and wool barges and the ordinary traffic of the river for so long that the counting had long since become a kind of restfulness to her, a thing her hands did while the rest of her watched the water and the gulls and, that spring, the strange low broad-bellied barges that she had never counted before and would, she suspected, though no one had told her so outright, never count again.

She began her tally on the day the first barge came down, low and slow under the bridge's middle arch with two men poling and a third asleep in the bow, and she kept it faithfully as the days went on and the barges followed one another down, sometimes three in a morning and sometimes none for two days together, until the season's grain had all passed under her and gone on, by the coast road of the water, past Ros àrsaidh and up the Abhainn naomh to the waiting quay at Seann Skell.

The bridge-keeper, an old man who had held the toll there longer than Mairead had been alive, came out to lean on the parapet beside her most mornings that the barges ran, and between them they made a kind of game of guessing, before each one came under the arch, whether it would ride high or low, empty going up or full going down; and it was he who first remarked, watching a barge pass with its gunwales scarcely a hand's breadth above the water, that he had never in all his years on that bridge seen so much grain moved south to north in a single season, not even in the famine years when the capital had gone hungry and the shires had sent everything they could spare. Mairead said nothing to that, only cut her notch and watched the water settle again behind the barge's wake, because it was not her place to have an opinion on what the grain was for, only to count it as it passed.

When the last of them had gone by, a week and more after the first, she cut her final notch and counted the sticks in her lap, and made the whole of that spring's grain, from the shires south of the mountain to the fleet's own stores, forty-one barges, and told the bridge-keeper so, and he wrote it in his own book, and neither of them thought there was anything more to be done with the number than to write it down.

She never learned, then or after, what had been argued over the grain at the red hall that winter, nor why the doors at Àth shean had stood barred so long, nor what word had opened them again; she only knew, from where she sat on the bridge parapet with her sticks in her lap, that the river had carried what it was asked to carry, and that forty-one was the number of times it had done so that spring, and she set that down as the whole of her own part in the matter, which, in her own reckoning, it was.

## V. Of the Roll at Seann Skell

At Seann Skell, that same season, in the moon of Pailteach when the barley was in and the last of the barges from the south had not yet reached the quay, Calum mac Nèill cut a household onto the roll of the willing that he had feared, since the day the boards were first opened, to see standing before his own board, and had put off imagining, in his own heart, for as long as he could manage it.

It was his own daughter's household: Mairead nic Chaluim, a grown woman of five-and-twenty, born ten years after the boards were first hung on their pegs, and her husband Dùghall mac Iain, a fisher of Inis àrsaidh whom she had married against no one's wishes and with everyone's blessing, six years gone. They came to the harbour-house together on an ordinary morning, no different in their dress or their manner from any of the hundreds of households that had stood before Calum's board in the years since the roll was opened, and Mairead gave her own name and her husband's and the names of their two children in the same clear voice she had used, as a small girl, to recite her letters for him at their own hearth, and watched his knife the whole time it was in the wood, the way every one of them watched it, as though the cutting were the thing that made the going real and not the choosing that had come before it.

She had brought the two children with her, a boy of four holding fast to his father's hand and a girl not yet two on Dùghall's hip, and it was the boy, more than the parents, who watched the whole business with the frank interest of a child who has been told a thing is important and means to see for himself what makes it so; he asked, in the small silence after his mother had given the last of the names, whether the boat they would sail on had a name of its own, and Dùghall told him, low, that it did, though he did not yet know which of the hulls at the quay would be theirs, and the boy seemed satisfied with that, as children often are with the promise of a thing rather than the having of it.

Calum's son Dòmhnall, who kept the boards with him still and had grown from the quiet boy who had once asked his father to close them into a quieter man who no longer asked, stood at his father's shoulder that morning and said nothing at all, and it was Dòmhnall, not Calum, who reached first for the cup of ale that was kept, by then, for every household's first entering, and set it in his sister's hands. He held her a moment by the shoulders after, the way a brother will who does not trust himself to say anything worth the saying, and let her go again, and it was the only part of that whole ordinary morning that anyone standing in the harbour-house doorway thought worth remembering afterward.

Calum wrote the household in the same hand he had used for every entry before it, steady, without a tremor that any watcher could have named, and it was only after his daughter and her husband had gone back out onto the quay, and the harbour-house stood empty a moment in the ordinary business of the morning, that his own hand, laid flat on the board where the ink was drying, was seen by no one but himself to be shaking, and he stood a while with it so, and then went on to the next entry waiting in the doorway, because the boards, as he had told his wife long before, needed their keeper, whatever the keeper himself might be feeling that morning, and the queue at the door did not know, and had no cause to know, whose name had just gone onto the wood.

## VI. Of the Hulls at the Quay

By the spring of LE 38 the fleet lay complete at Seann Skell, the whole length of the new stone quay taken up with hulls that had not existed, any of them, when the first crew of Cuan shean had walked up the river road with its tale of a far shore. The custody-book at the vein-house, when its clerk came down that season to make the reckoning, wrote the number as nineteen; a later hand, in the margin of the same leaf, made it twenty-one; and no one, then or since, has ever been able to say with any certainty which of the two counts was the truer, or why they should have differed at all over a fleet lying, the whole of it, in plain sight against one short stretch of stone.

Calum's grandson Niall, Dòmhnall's boy, was nine years old that spring and had grown up entirely within sight of the roll and the boards and the quay his grandfather kept, so that the fleet, to him, was less a wonder than a fact of the world he had been born into, the way the river was a fact, or the harbour-house. He had a habit, that spring, of running the whole new length of the quay after his lessons were done, from the old stones at the east end where the fish-boats still lay to the new dressed stone at the west where the deep hulls rode, touching each hull's timbers with his hand as he passed as though he were counting them the way his grandfather counted households onto a board, though no one had asked him to count anything at all.

He never once, that spring or the next, brought the count out even. It would begin well enough at the east end, one, two, three, murmuring the numbers under his breath the way Dòmhnall had taught him his letters, and somewhere past the tenth hull, or the twelfth, a gull would go up off a mast, or a rope-maker would call across to a wright he knew, or the light on the water would catch his eye and hold it a moment longer than he meant, and when he came to himself again he no longer knew whether he stood at the fourteenth hull or the fifteenth, and had, every time, to choose whether to go back to the beginning and start the whole count fresh, or to let it go and simply finish his run to the western stones and stand there a while, breathing hard, looking out at the whole fleet riding together against the new quay in the spring light, with the losing of the count already forgotten in the plain pleasure of having run the length of it.

Once, near the end of that spring, one of the older wrights caught him at it, hand flat on a strake, lips moving, and asked him, not unkindly, what number he had reached. Niall told him honestly that he did not know, that he had lost it somewhere past the pitch-sheds, and the wright laughed, a short surprised bark of a laugh, and said that he himself, who had helped lay four of the hulls with his own hands, could not have told the boy the true count either, not to save his life, and that Niall was in good company in his not knowing. The boy turned that over a good while afterward, walking home along the quay in the last of the light, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that it pleased him more than a straight answer would have done, though he could not then, and never later, have said quite why.

His grandfather, watching him from the harbour-house door on one such evening, said nothing of it to the boy, then or later; but it is told that Calum remarked once, to his wife Seonag, that there might yet come a year when no one on the island could say for certain how many hulls had lain at that quay, and that he did not know, himself, whether that was a thing to be sorry for.
