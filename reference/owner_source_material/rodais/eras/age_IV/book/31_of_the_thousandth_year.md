# Of the Thousandth Year

*LE 1,015 – 1,064*

## I. Of the Girl Who Kept the Tallies

Mòrag Ruadh was fifteen years old in LE 1,015, red-haired as her name said and quieter in a crowded hall than any of the old king's household thought a girl of her blood had need to be, and she had kept, since she was old enough to hold a stylus steady, a small wax-board tally of her own beside the steward's larger one: what her own chamber burned in candles through a winter month, what a new pair of boots cost against what the old pair had cost her two years before, what a horse ate in oats between one saddling and the next. She did this for no reason she could have given a questioner, only that the steward's own great ledger, kept for the whole of the red hall's household, swallowed every small thing into a sum too large to see the small thing inside it again, and she found she wanted, without knowing quite why she wanted it, to be able to lay a finger on one particular candle and say what it had cost.

The steward, an old man named Pàdraig mac Ìomhair who had kept the hall's own great ledger since before Mòrag Ruadh was born, found her at it one evening bent over her wax-board by a poor light, and asked her, not unkindly, why a girl who would never be asked to account for a single candle troubled herself so over sums a steward was paid to carry for her. She told him only that she had found, the week before, that her own tally of the winter's candles and his own great ledger's figure for the same month did not agree by some small measure, and that she meant to find, before she let the matter drop, which of the two of them had counted wrong. He laughed at her, gently, and went away to check his own figures against the chandler's own bill, and came back three days later, not laughing, to tell her that the fault had been his, a single tally-mark set down twice by a tired hand at the month's turning, and that he would be glad, from that evening forward, to have a second pair of eyes on his sums whatever the hall's own household thought of a girl who kept candle-counts for her own amusement. It is told that he never again, in all the years he served her afterward as queen, closed a month's reckoning without setting it first before her own eyes, whether she asked to see it or not.

The old king who sat the red hall in those years had sat it longer than any teller then living could put an exact number of winters to, and of the manner of his going, when it came, the custody-book sets down no more than the year. What is told is that the seven houses met at the vein-house above {{place:burg:491}} in the same season, as they had met once before to name Dòmhnall Ruadh the better part of two centuries before her, and that they sent word to Cathair dhearg within the month that the crown would go, this time, to the girl of Clann Raghnaill who kept her own small tally beside the steward's, a choosing the custody-book records in four lines and no more, giving no reason beyond her house and her blood, as though the reason had been plain enough to every house present that no house thought it needed writing down.

She was crowned at {{place:burg:19}} in {{year:III-0153}}, nineteen years old, in the red hall her own girlhood tally had been kept in under a steward's larger one, and it is told that the first order she gave as queen, before any grain ruling or any boundary plea, was to the same steward who had watched her keep her small board for four years without once asking him leave: that a proper ledger be bound for her, of good vellum and not wax, and that every measure spent on her own household, however small, be entered into it in her own hand from that day forward, so that a queen who could not say what her own candles cost had no business asking a shire to account for its grain. The steward bound the ledger before the month was out, a plain board-covered book with a clasp of iron rather than silver, at her own asking, and its first page, dated the day of her crowning, holds nothing but a single line: the cost of the feast the hall had kept that evening, set down to the last tally, with her own name signed beneath it as though she meant the entry to answer before any question was asked of it. It is the first household accounting of any ruler of Dia-thìr to survive whole from that day to its last page, and it survived, the vein-house's own scribes noted afterward, for the plain reason that a queen who wrote so exactly of candles and horses had also, without troubling herself to say so, written exactly of a great deal else besides.

## II. Of the Letters No One Could Read

By {{year:III-0154}} the moss on the old stone at {{place:marker:28}}, above Cnoc bheag, had grown thick enough over its carved face that a hawk-keeper's grandchild, sent up the hill on an errand, could no longer find the letters at all beneath the green of it, and it was a young priest of the house there, a careful man named Tòmas mac Aonghais who had kept the mews under Gormshuil's own teaching as a boy and turned, in his middle years, more to the shrine's old stones than to its birds, who asked the house's leave to clear it properly rather than let another generation grow up believing the carving had never held letters at all. He and two others of the house spent the better part of a month at it, working with wooden scrapers rather than iron for fear of the stone, and washing the face clean with water carried up from the shrine's own well a bucket at a time, until at last, on a grey morning with the whole house standing about him in a half-circle, Tòmas ran his own fingers over a face of stone as bare of moss as it had likely stood on the day it was first cut.

The letters were there, plainly enough, cut deep and old, but no man or woman of the house that morning, nor any scholar sent for afterward from Dùn thais, could put a single sound to a single one of them; they answered to no shape the schools' own tables of the letters had ever kept, and the oldest man of the house, who had climbed that hill since before Tòmas was born, said only that a stone could keep its own counsel as well as a man could, and that this one plainly meant to. Tòmas wrote the whole of it down anyway, in the shrine's own roll, tracing each letter as faithfully as his hand allowed and setting beside his tracing the plain words that no one at Cnoc bheag, that year or after, could read it; and it is told that word of the cleared stone and its unread letters reached the red hall before the season turned, carried down by the same wool-trade that had once carried Gormshuil's teaching up, and that Queen Mòrag Ruadh, hearing it read to her among the ordinary business of the hall, had the sum of Tòmas's own oil and vellum for the month's work entered into her own book without comment, and asked, of the messenger who brought the report, only whether the priests meant to try again. He answered that they did not know; that a letter which had kept its silence through however many years the moss alone could measure might, Tòmas himself had said, be owed the same silence back from those who could not answer it.

## III. Of the Nets that Broke the Salt

Caol shean's boats had fished the same stretch of the east coast, out from the mouth of the Abhainn chaol, for as long as the town's own rolls ran, taking a catch that varied, season to season, no more than any fisherman expected a catch to vary; and it was in {{year:III-0155}} that the boats came in, three days running, so heavy in the water that the men rowing them swore afterward they had never once, in a working life apiece, seen a hull sit so low and still make its own harbour without shipping green water over the gunwale. The salters of the town, who kept pans enough for an ordinary season and a little over, ran through what salt they held by the second day and sent, within the hour, to every salt-town along that coast for more; but no cart could reach Caol shean before the catch began to turn, and a foreman of the salting-sheds, a broad, unhurried man named Ruairidh mac Neacail who had kept the sheds eleven years and never once in that time seen them idle in the height of a season, made the call, on his own head and without waiting on any word from the reeve, to hang what could not be salted out in the wind instead, strung on lines the length of every lane in the lower town, rather than let it go to waste on the strand.

It dried, after its fashion; the wind off that coast was salt-laden enough in its own right to help the drying along, and Caol shean's lanes stood hung, for the better part of two weeks, with more fish than any lane in that town had ever held at once, a sight that drew gulls from three parishes and children from every house to run beneath the lines and duck the drips. Ruairidh's own granddaughter, no more than six that year, was set by her mother to walk the lower lane twice a day with a long switch and drive the gulls off before they could strip a line bare, a labour she took to with more relish than any chore given her before or since, and she was fond of telling her own children that she remembered the smell of that fortnight before she remembered almost anything else of her own childhood, a smell the whole town carried on its clothes and in its hair until the last line was finally taken down.

But dried fish, however honestly dried, sold poorly against salted, the trading houses down the coast having long since built their custom around the one and not the other, and Ruairidh's own foreman's roll notes, in a hand plainer than most of its entries, that the greater part of that year's catch, the largest the town's own rolls had ever set down, fetched less at market than a middling season's salted catch had fetched the year before. The town went back to salting the next season and every season after, laying in salt enough, from that year forward, for a catch half again the size of any it had yet taken, a lesson Ruairidh is said to have summed up to his own daughter, when she came to keep the sheds after him, in a single sentence: that a good year could cost a town more than a bad one, if the town had made no ready place to hold it.

## IV. Of the Thousandth Year

The vein-house's own count of the Diosal, kept without a break of a single year since the sunwise turn, came in {{year:III-0156}} to its thousandth year, a reckoning the custodians had watched approach for the better part of a decade and had argued over, in that time, more than any single number Mòrag Ruadh had yet heard argued in her own hall: whether the year deserved a feast, as some of the seven houses held, or a fast, as others held, or nothing marked at all beyond the count's own turning of its page, as a few of the oldest custodians held, saying that a number was a number and owed no more ceremony at a thousand than it had owed at nine hundred and ninety-nine. It was the queen herself, hearing the three views laid before her in the red hall that autumn, who judged between them: one burning, she said, on the quay the count had begun from, no feast about it and no fast either, the measure and no more than the measure, so that the year would be marked as the count was kept, exactly and without excess on either side.

She rode down to {{place:burg:489}} herself for it, in the dark of the year, a journey of two short days over roads already hard with the season's first frosts, and stood, on the appointed evening, at the edge of Cidhe an Diosail among the vein-house's own custodians and a crowd that had gathered along the whole length of the quay and back into the lanes behind it, more people than Seann Skell's own harbour-court had ever counted at one telling. The measure was brought down from the vein-house in a plain iron scuttle, no more coal in it than any single hearth of the island might burn of an ordinary evening, and set alight on the stone where the harbour scribes had once cut the first tallies of the count, thirty generations of winters before. The flame that came up off it burned, by every telling that reached the hall afterward and by the queen's own account given to her scribe the next morning, the colour of the vein itself rather than any ordinary coal-fire's yellow, a deep, steady red that held its colour the whole while the scuttle burned down and gave off, the custodians said, no more smoke than a single hearth gives off in an evening, exactly as the measure had been meant to give.

A custodian of the vein-house stood at the queen's own shoulder through the whole of the burning, an old woman of the seven houses named Catrìona nic Fhearchair who had kept the count's own weights her whole working life, and it was she who leaned close, as the last of the coal in the scuttle settled to ash, to tell the queen, in a voice pitched under the crowd's own silence, that the count would go on the next morning exactly as it had the morning before, one more year added to the tally and nothing about the count's own keeping changed by the number it had just crossed. Mòrag Ruadh asked her, low enough that only the custodian and her own scribe standing near caught it, whether anyone now living understood why the count had been begun at all, on that quay, in that first year; and the old woman answered only that the custody-book gave no reason beyond the sunwise turn, and that a keeping a thousand years old by then had needed no better reason than its own long habit to go on being kept.

The crowd that had come down every lane and cart-track of that shore to see a thousand years burn in one small fire watched it burn down to ash without, by every account that reached the red hall afterward, a single voice raised the whole while, neither in wonder nor in complaint, and went home in the same silence once the last of the flame had gone out, a silence the queen's own scribe, riding back up the coast road at her side, said afterward he had found harder to account for than any noise the crowd might have made instead. Mòrag Ruadh herself said little of it that night or after, to her scribe or to the hall she returned to; what she did say, more than once in the years that followed to those who asked her plainly what the burning had felt like to stand near, was only that a thousand years had gone into that scuttle and come out again as one evening's worth of an ordinary hearth's fire, and that she had stood close enough to feel the heat of it on her own face the whole while, and had thought, watching it, of nothing grander than how small a measure could be made to answer for so long a keeping.

## V. Of the Daughter Born at the Turn of the Year

Mòrag Ruadh's own daughter was born at Cathair dhearg the following spring, and named Beathag by her mother's own choosing before the child was a day old, for no reason the queen's book troubles to give beyond the name's own plainness; the book's entry for that week holds, beside the ordinary sums of candles and grain, one line set apart from the rest in a hand the vein-house's own scribes afterward remarked was less steady than her usual, recording only the day and the name and nothing more of what a mother might have been expected to write of a first child. The hall kept a quieter feast for it than the thousandth year's burning had asked of Seann Skell, a single evening's ale and a fire built up higher than the season strictly needed, and the seven houses sent, each in its own turn over the following month, a small gift to the cradle: a length of undyed wool from Clann Fhearchair, a carved horn spoon from Clann Mhuirich, small things a child could neither use nor remember receiving, given, the custody-book notes, more for the houses' own satisfaction in the giving than for any need the cradle had of them.

## VI. Of the Market above the Mill-Race

The hill country above {{place:burg:3}}, in the island's south-east, had traded its grain and its clay in small parcels along the roads for as long as any teller there could set a date to, each farm carrying its own cartload down to whichever town would take it that season; and it was the hill-lords of that country, in {{year:III-0156a}}, who set a single market to gather the whole of that trade in one place rather than let it scatter, as it had always scattered, along a dozen separate roads. They set it at {{place:burg:233}}, above the long mill-race of Muileann fhada, where the water ran fast enough the year round to turn three wheels at once and where a cart from any farm of that country could reach the flat ground beside the race in a single day's driving. It opened first as a small thing, a handful of carts and a morning's trading over grain and clay and honey, but it held one good beside the ordinary run of a market that no other market on the island could offer alongside it: pearls, taken by the shore-women of that same coast from the mussel beds in the shallows below the cliffs, sold nowhere else in whole parcels but at that one market above the mill-race.

A woman of the shore named Sìne nic Ruairidh, who had gathered mussels off that coast since she was a girl and had sold her pearls, before the market opened, one or two at a time to whatever trader passed near enough to ask after them, brought the whole of a season's gathering to the market's first opening rather than sell it piecemeal as she always had, and found, to her own plain surprise, that a buyer down from Cathair mhòr paid her more for the lot laid out together than she had ever been offered, in all her years of gathering, for the same pearls sold singly along the road. The buyer, a trader of Cathair mhòr who had come to that coast on other business and heard of the pearls only by chance from an innkeeper the night before, told her plainly, as he weighed the last of them into his own scale, that a buyer could judge the worth of a whole season's gathering laid out together in a way no single pearl handed across a cart-rail on a muddy road had ever let him judge it, and that she would do better, every season after, to make him wait for the market than to save him the ride by meeting him on the road herself. She told this to the other shore-women of her own stretch of coast before the season was out, and within a few years the whole of that coast's gathering came up to the mill-race market each season rather than trickle out along the roads a pearl or two at a time, so that a trader wanting pearls at all, from that year forward, learned to wait for the one market that held them rather than chase them down the coast in ones and twos. The queen's own book records, some years on, a single pearl bought at that same market and sent to the cradle at Cathair dhearg for her daughter's naming-day, its cost set down beside a cart-load of candles in the same plain hand as everything else the book ever held.

## VII. Of Baile Ghorm's Blue, and the Queen's Own Entry

The dyers of {{place:burg:3}} had worked a common blue for their own wool since before living memory, a colour that held well enough through a season's wear but faded, every dyer of that town owned plainly, a full shade paler by the second washing than it had stood at the first; and it was in {{year:III-0157}} that a dyer of that town, a patient woman named Oighrig nic Ailein who had spent the better part of ten years trying one mordant after another against the marsh plant her own mother had first shown her, found at last a mixing that held the colour fast through washing after washing without the fading her own mother's dye had always shown. She would not, by her own later telling, say exactly what turned the mixing from an ordinary blue into a fast one, only that she had changed one thing at a time across those ten years, keeping a careful board of her own beside her dye-vats much as the queen kept hers beside her household accounts, until one change at last held where every change before it had let the colour slip.

Cloth of that new blue was worn first at the vein-house's own midwinter gathering the year it was made, and it is told that every house of custody present asked, before the evening was out, where cloth of that colour could be had, so that within a few years Baile ghorm's own blue had become the feast-day dress of the seven houses entire, worn at every gathering of custody thereafter and known, wherever it travelled on a trader's cart, by the town that had first held the mixing that made it fast. Oighrig herself lived to see her own blue worn at three winters' gatherings running before she died, and taught her mixing, by hand and never by writing, to the two daughters who took up her vats when she could no longer stand at them.

Mòrag Ruadh's own book, in the same year, holds an entry for a length of that new blue bought for her own wear at the vein-house's midwinter gathering, set down beside the ordinary sums of the season in the same plain hand the book had kept from its very first page; and it is that same book, turned back by a later hand to the page for the night of the thousandth year's burning, that gives the last word this telling has to give of that evening at Cidhe an Diosail. Against the date of the burning, where another queen's book might have set down a verse or a line of wonder at a thousand years made into one evening's fire, Mòrag Ruadh had written only the cost of the coal, reckoned to the vein-house's own weights, and beneath it, in the same hand and no larger, five words and no more: one handful, nothing more.
