# Of the Treaty in One Tongue

*AE 7 – 9*

## I. Of the Letter from Baile dhìreach

In the summer of {{year:IV-0028}} a letter came up the road from Baile dhìreach to the council hall at Cathair dhearg, and lay on the table of Ceann a' Chùraim for the better part of a season.

Tormod mac Ailein was fifty-two years old that summer, and heavier than he had been when the ships came, and he had begun to feel the damp of the lake in his knees. He kept the table under the east window of the hall, where the light came in over the smoke of the smiths' lane, and he kept it clear. A custodian could lay a matter there in the morning and have his answer, or know that he would get none, by the time the lamps were lit. The clerks of the roll said that Tormod's table was the only flat place in Cathair dhearg that was never wet.

The letter from Baile dhìreach did not go from it. The council of six in that small port on the south-west coast, three of the island's people and three of the strangers, had entered a marriage in both its books: the net-maker Eilidh nic Ruairidh to the human cooper Samuel Hale. Now they asked the custodians at Cathair dhearg for a ruling on what they had done. The letter was in two hands and two tongues. Iain mac Chaluim had written the Dia-thìris half, in a fisherman's square letters, and at the foot of it the carpenter William Rook had put his own name in the strangers' writing, with a flourish under it like a wave.

Tormod read the Dia-thìris half twice. The other half he could not read at all, and he looked at it a while all the same, as a man looks at a field in a strange country, trying to tell barley from grass.

"They want to know whether it is a marriage," said Gormshuil nic Fhearchair, who had come in with the letter under her arm and stayed to watch him read it.

"They have married them. It is in both their books."

"Then they want to know whether it is a marriage to us."

Tormod set the letter down. "And what are we?"

She gave no answer, and he had looked for none. Gormshuil was five years younger than he, and in the five years since the season of words she had learned the strangers' letters from Samuel Wren, one at a time, with the patience of a woman threading beads. She could read Rook's line. She read it to him: his name, and the words *for the council*, and the date by the humans' reckoning, which meant nothing to either of them.

Tormod laid the letter in the chest under the window, with the other things that were waiting. The council did not take it up that summer, nor in the autumn. When at last a custodian of the south-west asked in the hall what answer had been sent to Baile dhìreach, Tormod said that the town had made its own compact with its own hands, and a town that could do that could keep what it had made; and the clerk wrote that no ruling was given.

He had other things on his table. He had the iron.

## II. Of Tormod's Reasons

There were three of them, and he could have counted them off on his fingers, and more than once he did.

The first was the iron. In the lane below the bridge the smiths of Cathair dhearg had learned the strangers' bar-iron in a single winter, and Gilleasbuig Gobha, the best of them, could draw a blade now that would shave the hair off a man's arm. Tormod had a hoe of it himself, for the garden behind his house, and a spade, and a pair of shears; and the old bronze hoes of the capital's fields had been melted down and cast into bells, which he heard every evening. No one in the fields wanted the bronze back.

The second was the surgeon. Jonas Tull had set the leg of a Cuan shean fisherman whom the whole town had given up for lame, and the custodian of Cuan shean had entered it in her roll as the first debt owed to a stranger. In the winter before the letter came, a back tooth had rotted in Tormod's own jaw and kept him from sleep for a month, and the capital's physician had given him willow-bark and told him to wait. Tull, passing through to the post, had looked in his mouth by the east window, asked for a stool and a cup of spirit, and drawn the tooth in the time it takes to say so. Tormod kept it in a box.

The third was the ships.

Edmund Harrow had sailed home with hides and cloth in his holds and come back with four ships in place of three. He could come back with forty, and had never needed to say so. A big man gone grey at the temples, who laughed easily, Harrow had held out an empty cask on the strand at Cuan shean and been given water, and every custodian on the island liked him for it. But Tormod had asked him once, at the post, through Thomas Pike, how many ships there were in an Tìr Thall. Harrow had thought about it honestly, then laughed and said that he could not count them, and that most of them were not his, and that the men who owned them did not always ask for water.

That was the third reason. Tormod never wrote it down.

In the autumn of that year Harrow came up the lake road to Cathair dhearg with Pike beside him to put his words into the island's tongue, and sat at the table under the east window, and asked that the terms on which his people stayed be written.

"You have your leave," said Tormod. "It is in the roll."

"It's in your roll," Harrow said. Pike gave it over word for word, with an apologetic look, as a man hands on a pot too hot to hold. "In my country nothing is so until it's written by both sides and signed by both. A man is coming who will ask for it. Better it's written now, between friends, than later between strangers."

"We are strangers," Tormod said.

Harrow laughed at that, and when Pike had given it him he laughed again. "So we are. Well. Between strangers who've eaten each other's bread, then."

He told them that the man who was coming would bear a title that Pike could not put into Dia-thìris and did not try to, and which the island came afterwards to render An t-Àrd-mhaor, the high steward; that he would speak for the government of an Tìr Thall and for no trading house; that Harrow himself had asked for him, since a ship's master was no one to be making laws; and that he would come with the spring sailing. Then he stood, and shook Tormod's hand in his fashion, and went down the stairs to the street whistling.

Tormod sat on at the table after they had gone, while the new bells rang for the evening, and thought of Oighrig nic Leòid's letter from her sickbed six years before, which the clerk had not read out: that a leave given in the roll cannot be ungiven by the next roll, and that the strangers would know it better than the council, since they would be holding it. He had thought her an old woman afraid of the sea. He read the letter again that night by the lamp, and put it back.

## III. Of the Man Who Asked for a Horse

Josiah Pell landed at Cuan shean in the early spring of {{year:IV-0029}}, off the first ship of the year to come down from the north.

The custodian of Cuan shean was still Oighrig nic Leòid, who had given Harrow water. She walked with a stick now, and kept her custody from a chair by the harbour window. She sent a rider up the valley road to the capital the same day with a letter, and the letter is in the council's roll. It says that the ship came in on the morning tide; that the man got down into the boat in a black coat, with a leather case under his arm that he would let no one else carry; that he came up the shingle dry-shod, on a plank the sailors laid for him; and that he did not ask for water. He asked for a horse. She lent him her own, the gentlest in the town, and he thanked her by name, having learned it on the ship. He was a man of about fifty, she wrote, clean-shaven, grey, with good manners and a cold, and he looked at everything once and seemed to remember it.

He rode the twenty-six miles to Cathair dhearg in a day, with two of his own people and a Cuan shean boy to show the road, and came up the hill into the capital at dusk with the rain beginning. Instead of the post, he asked for the council hall, and there for Ceann a' Chùraim; and Tormod, who had been told he was coming and had not believed he would come the same night, went down to meet him in the hall with his house-shoes still on his feet.

There was no one there that night to carry words between them. Pell took off his wet hat, bowed a short neat bow from the neck, and said a sentence in his own tongue, and then two words in Dia-thìris that he must have learned on the ship, careful and wrong in every sound: *Beannachd leibh*. It is the island's word of leave-taking. Tormod bowed back and did not correct him. Pell was given a room in the guest-house by the temple, and in the morning Wren came up from the post to put his words into the island's tongue, and the talking began.

It went on for most of a moon.

Wren sat between them at the long table in the council hall, or on fine days at the post at Ros dhomhain, where Pell laid his papers out on a board across two casks. He was thinner than he had been in the season of words and coughed in the mornings, and sometimes he stopped and said to Tormod in Dia-thìris, "There is no word of yours for this. I will say it as near as I can," and then said it. Pell's secretary, a young man with a clean hand, wrote down each day what was agreed and read it back at evening.

Tormod asked at the start that the terms be written twice, once in each tongue, as the compact of Baile dhìreach had been.

Pell heard this through Wren and said that he honoured the custom, and that at Baile dhìreach it had been well done. Only an agreement between his government and the island must go before the lawyers of an Tìr Thall, who could read only their own tongue, and a writing in two tongues was two writings, and the day would come when they did not agree. One text should govern. A copy in Dia-thìris would be made for the council's use, as a courtesy, when there was leisure to make it well.

"When will there be leisure?" Tormod asked.

Pell said: after. Wren gave it over as *an dèidh làimh*, which is the island's way of saying it, and means *after the hand*, and the council clerk wrote it down.

Six of the council sat through the talking, from the capital and the lake towns and the west; the roll gives their names, and sets down that no one of them spoke against the terms. What there was to speak against, they could hear only through Wren, a sentence at a time, and a sentence at a time the terms were fair. The strangers might dwell where they had leave and trade where they found buyers. They would keep their own law among their own people and answer to the council for harm done to the island's. The harbour dues would be shared as they were shared already. A man of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach would keep order among the humans and speak for them to the council. There was a line about goods, and the working of goods, which Wren gave twice over and frowned at.

Gormshuil was not at the table. She had gone east in the month of Bearnach with the season's copies for the loft of Beathag nic Thòmais at Muileann chaol, as she went every year, near sixty miles of hill road with a chest on a pony. When Tormod saw how the talking went he sent a rider after her. She started back the day he found her, and the rain was on the hills, and the fords were up.

On {{date:IV-0029}} the terms on which the humans stayed were set down in writing for the first time, fair, on six sheets, in the humans' tongue only. The secretary wrote them. Pell signed nothing yet, and said that the signing should be done in the council hall, before the council, as was proper; and Tormod, who had been given a great many reasons that morning and had followed all of them, found that he agreed.

## IV. Of the Line Read Twice

Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg was signed on {{date:IV-0030}}, in the moon of Bealltach, a week after the bright fires.

The fires of Bealltainn had smoked more than they blazed that year, and it rained all the morning of the signing. The rain came off Loch chrom in grey sheets and walked up the hill over the roofs, and the windows of the hall ran with it, so that the lake and the harbour and the far shore went to smears of grey behind the glass. The custodians sat in their cloaks by the two fires, and the cloaks steamed.

Josiah Pell sat on the far side of the long table in his black coat, newly come over the sea as An t-Àrd-mhaor of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, the first of them; and on the near side sat Tormod mac Ailein, in the chair of Ceann a' Chùraim, with his hands folded in front of him. Between them lay the six sheets, squared together and covered edge to edge in the strangers' close black writing.

Tormod could not read a word of it. The letters stood on the sheets like a fence of thorn, and he looked at them, and then away.

Someone read it to him. The roll keeps the name of every custodian in the hall that day, and Pell's name, and the names of the two humans who stood behind him, and the name of the clerk; it keeps the name of the boy who fed the fires. It does not keep the name of the reader. The copyists of the cellar have looked for it in every roll that came down to them, and have not found it. Tormod sat with his hands folded and his eyes on the rain and listened to the voice go down the sheets, sentence by sentence, the strangers' tongue and then the island's, and the custodians along the table listened with him.

Gormshuil had not come. Her rider had sent word from the hills the night before that the ford at the foot of the long glen was up to a horse's belly, and that she would cross it in the morning.

About the middle of the fourth sheet Tormod lifted one hand from the table.

"*Leugh a-rithist e*," he said. Read that again.

The voice went back to the beginning of the sentence and read it again. It was the line about goods: that the humans might trade in such goods as the island afforded, and in the working of them, under licence of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, and that the council would not hinder such trade. It read the same the second time. Tormod listened to it with his head a little on one side, the way he listened to a custodian of the hill country who was taking the long way round to a point, and then he nodded and put his hand down, and the voice went on.

He thought, as the voice went on, of wool from Cathair naomh and dyes for Baile Mòr mhòr, of the salt of Seann Warr, of hides and cloth. Those were the goods the island afforded; he had seen them go across Pike's counter for six years. The working of them was the spinning and the dyeing and the curing, and the strangers wanted a licence for everything. It did not come into his head that a trader might want to work anything else on Dia-thìr. The vein was the vein, and no one had ever carried it to a counter.

When the reading was done Pell turned the last sheet round on the table so that it faced Tormod, and took a pen from the secretary, and dipped it, and held it out across the table.

Tormod took it. It was a goose quill, cut fine, and it was lighter than he had thought it would be.

He looked for a long while at the place where his name should go, a space at the foot of the sheet above a ruled line. He looked at it as a man looks at a ford before he wades it: at the colour of the water, and the set of it over the stones, and the far bank, and whether the far bank is as near as it looks. The custodians along the table watched him. Pell did not move. The rain ran down the windows. Then Tormod bent and wrote his name in the island's letters, square and heavy, and gave the pen back.

Pell signed after him for Riaghaltas nan Coigreach. His name was short and went quickly, with a line under it.

The strangers had dated the sheets at their head by their own day, month and year, and the secretary read the date aloud. The custodians' clerk had the roll open at the end of the table, and when the signing was done he entered it under its name, Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg: the names of those present, the terms as near as he could catch them, and the signing. At the head of the entry he wrote the year of the count of An Diosal, and the moon of Bealltach. Then he came to the day. The humans' day was a number out of their own month, which ran by another measure than the moon, and no one in the hall could tell him how to turn the one into the other. He sat with his pen over the page a while. Then he left the day empty and went on to the next line.

It was the first page of its kind in the council's roll, and no one remarked on it. The clerk ruled off the entry and sanded it, and Pell's secretary rolled the six sheets into a leather case with a cap to it and gave the case to Pell, who put it under his arm and kept it there.

Gormshuil came in two days later, with the mud of the long glen up to her knees, and the pony lame. She went straight to the hall without stopping at her house, and found the chair of Ceann a' Chùraim empty and the clerk at his table, and she asked him for the roll, and read the entry standing. Then she went to find Tormod, and what was said between them neither of them ever told. That afternoon she asked Pell, through Wren, for a copy of the terms in the strangers' tongue, since the copy in hers was to wait for leisure; and Pell, who was courteous in all things, had his secretary make one and sent it to her house within the week, with his compliments.

She began to read it with her finger, a word at a time, the way Wren had taught her. The strangers' law had words that were in none of Wren's lists, and she wrote each one on a slip and took the slips to Wren, who was often ill that year. She did not bring it before the council, and no one asked her to. It was signed.

## V. Of the House on the Rise

That summer Pell asked the council for ground.

He wanted the rise above Ros dhomhain, the bare shoulder of hill behind the harbour where the town's goats grazed, to build on it a house of dressed stone for Riaghaltas nan Coigreach. The goat-keepers were paid in iron pots, and were glad of the bargain. The council gave the ground, with no vote needed, since it was a matter of goats.

Pell had no masons of his own, and asked for the best of the masons' lane at Cathair dhearg, who had rebuilt the council hall's east gable when Tormod was young. They were paid in bar-iron by the day, and by the humans' day, which began at midnight where the island's began at dusk, and they grumbled at it and took the iron. They floated the stone over from the old quarries across the lake on rafts, and dressed it on the rise with the wind cutting at their hands.

Tormod rode down to see it in the autumn. The house was going up square and plain, of the grey stone of the lake shore, two floors high and five windows wide, with a slate roof on it from the hills behind Cathair dhearg; and it faced the harbour. From its windows Pell would see every mast in Ros dhomhain and every boat that came up Abhainn gheal into the lake. From its door he would not see the capital. Tormod stood on the rise in the wind with the master mason and asked him why the house was turned so.

"He drew it that way," said the master mason. "On a paper he brought with him." He spat on his hands and picked up his mallet. "A good paper. You can see every stone on it before it's cut." "We could build him the other way round, if the council asked it."

The council did not ask it.

On {{date:IV-0031}} Pell went up the hill from the post and slept for the first time in the stone house on the rise, and called it by the name it had in his tongue, which the island came to call Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, the house of the high steward. A flag went up on a pole before its door. From that day the humans on Dia-thìr answered to that house.

Edmund Harrow was needed ashore no longer, and he went back to the sea.

He came up the lake road to Cathair dhearg to say so, with Pike beside him. Tormod gave them bread and cheese in the room behind the hall, and Harrow ate like a man who would be at sea again tomorrow. He said that Pell would govern now and that it was right he should; that a ship's master had no more business in a council hall than a council in a ship; that he had been ashore on this island eight years, longer than he had been ashore anywhere since he was twelve, and that it was time.

"And the post?" Tormod asked.

Harrow wiped his mouth. He and Pike, he said, were to have a licence from Riaghaltas nan Coigreach to trade: a trading house, the two of them and some men of an Tìr Thall who had put money into it. Pike would keep the books ashore, and Harrow would keep the ships at sea, and between them they would carry what the island had to sell to the people who wished to buy it. He said it cheerfully. Pike, putting it into Dia-thìris, said it more slowly.

"It's a charter," Harrow said. "Pell seals it. You'll have a copy. You'll find it's all in order."

He shook Tormod's hand at the door, and went down the stairs whistling, as he always did. He sailed on the next tide but one from Ros dhomhain, north-about, the way the ships went. Tormod did not see him again.

## VI. Of the Charter Read in Private

Harrow and Pike founded their trading house on {{date:IV-0032}}, under licence of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach and Pell's seal. It had a long name in the strangers' tongue, and the island never learned it. From the first the island called it A' Chompanaidh, since that was the word Pike used for it at his counter, and nobody asked what it was a company of.

A copy of its charter came to the council, as Harrow had said it would. It came in the strangers' tongue, on four sheets, with a red seal at the foot of the last, and the clerk entered its coming in the roll and laid it in the chest under the east window, beside the letter from Baile dhìreach. There it lay a month.

Gormshuil took it out. She had been a year at the terms by then, and read the strangers' tongue far better, though still with her finger. She took the charter home one evening at the end of the moon of Ògach and read it twice through by her lamp, and wrote something on a slip. Then she put on her cloak and walked across the town in the long summer dusk to Tormod's house, below the temple, and knocked.

He was at his supper. He had a fire lit, though it was midsummer, because of his knees; and he sent the girl who kept his house to bed and poured Gormshuil a cup of ale and sat across the hearth from her, and she read him the charter.

She read it slowly, the strangers' sentence first and then her own, and where she had no word of the island's for a word of theirs she said so. It was long. It named Harrow and Pike and the men of an Tìr Thall, and the licence, and the seal; it spoke of ships and of goods and of the post at Ros dhomhain; it spoke of tools and stores. Tormod listened with his cup in his hand. It was all in order, as Harrow had said.

Then she came to the clause, and stopped.

"There is a word here," she said. "I know it. I have known it since the season of words."

It was the word Wren had written in the window-light on the morning she tipped the basket of broken coal out on the table and named its colours: the one word, the word for the black fuel of the strangers' own hearths, with which he had tapped the whole heap to show that it stood for all of it. In the charter it had grown. It stood in a list, and the list was a long one. She read it to him in the strangers' tongue first, and then she put it into Dia-thìris as close as the island's tongue would go, word by word.

*All fuels, ores and minerals the island may yield.*

Tormod did not say anything.

"The charter gives them the first refusal of it," Gormshuil said. "All of it. Whatever the island yields that burns, and whatever is dug. Before any other trader of their people. It is sealed by Pell under the licence." She laid a second paper on her knee: her own copy of Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg, worn soft at the corners by a year of her finger. "And the licence hangs from here."

She found the line on the fourth sheet and read it to him. That the humans might trade in such goods as the island afforded, and in the working of them, under licence of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, and that the council would not hinder such trade.

He had heard it twice. He had asked for it twice.

"I thought it meant wool," Tormod said.

"Yes."

"I thought of the salt at Seann Warr. The hides. The dyes." He set his cup down on the hearthstone as though it might break. "There's Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig. No household burns more than its handful. It has been so since An Aois Naomh."

"That measures hearths," said Gormshuil, "and has nothing to say about a ship's hold. The men who made it never saw a ship go out that was not coming back." She folded the charter along its creases. "They did not ask for our word for the coal, Tormod. They never asked. Now they have written theirs down, and put a seal on it, and you have signed the paper it hangs from."

The fire had burned down. Neither of them made it up. Outside the town had gone to bed; a dog barked down by the bridge, and stopped, and the bells of the capital rang for the middle of the night, the bronze of the old hoes speaking over the dark roofs.

After a while Tormod asked her whether it could be undone.

She did not know. In the strangers' law, as far as she had been able to learn it from Wren, a thing signed by both sides stood until both sides unsigned it; and that Pell had no reason to unsign anything; and that no ship had yet come asking for coal, and none might. The charter was a list of what the strangers might want, she said, and a list was not a pit.

"Then there is time," Tormod said.

"There may be."

"Keep it between us," he said, "until I have thought."

She looked at him a long time across the dead fire, and then she said that she would; and she did. She gave him the slip she had written on, which had the clause on it in both tongues, and walked home through the dark. Tormod sat up with the slip in his hand until the window greyed. He did not bring the charter before the council that year, nor the next. There was no ship in the harbour asking for coal. There was only the list.

## VII. Of the Door at Cathair dhomhain

The first building of A' Chompanaidh was a warehouse, and it went up that summer and autumn at Cathair dhomhain, along the lake shore from the capital, where the fishing town runs down to the water between its two stony points.

Pike chose the ground. He said, when anyone asked, that it was to keep its tools and stores out of the reach of the harbour thieves of Ros dhomhain, who had had two of his axes and a coil of good rope in a single winter. The ground was a strip of shingle and rough grass above the high-water line, with a spring behind it, and it belonged to a family of Cathair dhomhain that had drawn its nets there since An Aois Naomh. They sold it to Pike for a keg of nails, two lamps and a sheet of window-glass. The custodian of the lake shore entered the sale in his roll. It was the first ground on Dia-thìr that the strangers held by sale and not by the council's leave.

The masons who had built the house on the rise built the warehouse too, of the same grey stone, long and low with no window on the landward side and two high narrow windows on the lake, like a byre; and Pike roofed it with slate.

Tormod walked out to see it on {{date:IV-0033}}.

It was the stags' moon, Damhrach, and the first cold day of the autumn. He went alone and on foot, since it was only five miles by the lake road. The road ran along the shore with the lake on his left hand, grey and still, and the geese coming down on it out of the north in long wavering lines, calling. At Cathair dhomhain the warehouse stood finished on its strip of shingle, all but its door. The doorway stood open, a square of dark smelling of new lime. There were men of the strangers inside, stacking tools: picks and bars and shovels, and heavy hammers with long hafts, and coils of rope and chain, all new, with the grease still on them. Tormod stood in the doorway and looked at the picks a long while. They were not the tools of a fisherman or a dyer or a man who cures hides.

Pike came out to him, with his tally-book under his arm and his pen behind his ear, and greeted him in the island's tongue, which he spoke well by then. He showed him the warehouse end to end, and the tally of every tool in his book, balanced to the last line.

"For the post?" Tormod asked, and put his hand on a pick.

"For whatever's wanted," said Pike. "We keep them against the day."

The door came down the shore road that afternoon on a cart from Cathair dhearg. It was a heavy door of oak planks, a hand's breadth thick, pegged and studded with iron, and it took six men to lift it down. Its hinges had been forged in the smiths' lane below the bridge. Tormod knew them when he saw them: long iron straps drawn out and folded round a pin, with the ends split and curled back like fern, which was the mark Gilleasbuig Gobha put on everything he made. The strangers had paid for them in bar-iron. Pike had asked for Gilleasbuig by name.

They hung the door as the light was going. The six men lifted it and walked it to the doorway, and held it upright with their shoulders against it while a seventh stood on a cask and guided the straps down over the pins; and it dropped onto them with a clank that went out over the lake. Pike swung it. It turned without a sound, and closed, and he took a long iron key from his coat and locked it; then he unlocked it again, to see that it worked, and locked it again, and put the key away.

"There," he said, in the island's tongue, pleased as a boy. "That's ours."

Tormod walked home along the lake road in the dusk. The geese had settled on the water for the night and were talking among themselves out in the dark, and away along the shore the lamps of Ros dhomhain were coming out one by one, and above them, on the rise, the windows of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, five across, all lit. Behind him at Cathair dhomhain there was no light at all. The warehouse had no window on the landward side. There was only the door, and the key in Pike's coat, and whatever was kept behind them against the day.

The custodian of the lake shore wrote in his roll that night that a door had been hung on the strangers' building by the water, and that it was a good door, of oak, with a smith of the capital's hinges on it. The copyists of the cellar found that line when the rolls came down to them, and kept it, and asked the reader to remember that door.
