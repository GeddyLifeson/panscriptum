# Of the Rending of Cill ghlas

*AE 136 – 138*

## I. Of the Plan and the Mark Below It

The shrine at Cill ghlas had stood through the whole of the age. It was older than that: the custodians said the cutting had been worked before the strangers came, before the mark, before the first ton was weighed at Muileann chrom, and that Brìde's lamp had burned there since a time nobody now living could put a year to. It was there the word *fuil-ghuail* had been agreed, in the year the custodians gave the thing a name, and it was there the crews still climbed on the old feast days, as they had climbed since before any gallery had a name.

In the hundred and thirty-sixth year of the strangers A' Chompanaidh came to drive new workings under the hills about it.

The engineer's name was Robert Fairley, a spare, sandy man not yet forty, who had come out from the coalfields of an Tìr Thall with a case of instruments and a habit of measuring things twice. He set his levels on the moor above the shrine in the month of am Faoilleach, in a wind that took the surveyor's flags out of the ground three times before noon, and by an Cèitean he had his galleries marked on a sheet of linen paper pinned flat on a drawing-board in the site office at Baile thais: eight headings running north under the hill, numbered, with the old cutting shown as a hatched square at the top of the sheet, and the new workings stepping down below it toward the river.

The custodians of the shrine came to him twice. The first time it was Aonghas Mòr, keeper of the lamp that year, a heavy, slow-spoken man who had held the office eleven years and never once let the flame go out in all of them. He stood in the office doorway with the smell of the moor still on his coat and asked, through the clerk who did the Company's Dia-thìris, that no charge be set where the noise or the shake of it could be felt at the shrine. Fairley heard him out, asked the clerk to repeat the words a second time so he had them right, and wrote on the margin of his plan, in a small careful hand: *Custodians ask no charge in sight of old cutting.* He underlined it once.

The second time it was Aonghas Mòr and two others, in am Màrt, when the first blasting parties had begun to come up from the coast with their crates and their careful, unhurried carters. They asked the same thing again, in the same words, because that was the custodians' way with a thing once asked: to ask it again exactly, so that no one could say later that the asking had changed. Fairley looked at his own margin note, found it still there, and told them, through the clerk, that the Company had it marked and would keep to it.

He believed, when he said it, that he meant to.

The charges that summer were set according to the depth of good coal under each heading, and the depth of good coal ran deepest where the old cutting's own diggers had worked closest to the surface, in the years before anyone measured a working by the ton. The lowest headings, numbered six, seven and eight on Fairley's sheet, came up under the shrine itself, forty fathom down and rising, where the plan's careful ink line seemed from above to set them beside it. Fairley had marked the custodians' asking faithfully. He had not marked where the ground under his numbers actually lay, because on the sheet the shrine was a hatched square with no thickness to it, and the workings below were a set of numbers with no depth drawn in beside them, and the two things never touched on the paper at all.

The powder for the new headings came in that same summer, landed not at Ros dhomhain or Cathair dhearg, where the harbour-men had grown particular about barrels that hissed when they were dropped, but at the small stone quay of Caol ìseal on the north coast, away from the crowded southern ports. It came up from the hold in casks bound with iron hoops, stencilled in the humans' letters, and carted inland under an escort of four constables who rode a little apart from the carts, as men do who have been told what is in them and would rather not be asked. The custodian of Caol ìseal, an old woman named Oighrig Ruadh who had kept that small roll for thirty years, went down to the quay herself and asked the carter what the casks held.

"Lamp-oil," the carter said, not unkindly, and helped her count the barrels onto his manifest so the number would agree.

She wrote in her roll that night: *Forty casks landed, said to be oil. They did not smell of oil when I put my hand to a stave.* It is the only line in any custodian's roll that says plainly the lie was known for a lie before it was told, and it went unanswered, as such lines mostly did, into the cellar at Muileann chaol with everything else.

## II. Of Baile thais in the Winter

Eilidh nic Raghnaill was thirty-one that winter and had led a crew at Baile thais for six years, ever since her mother died and the crew that had been her mother's would not be led by anyone from outside the family. She kept a room over the burial club's hall, two streets up from the pithead, with a window that looked down the row of grey roofs to the sea, and on the wall by the door, where another woman might have hung a carving of the Keeper or a sprig of dried heather from Brìde's shrine, she had a single sheet of paper pinned flat, copied out in her own hand from a longer one that had gone round the crews eight years before: the names of the eleven who had died under the fall at Cnoc fhiadhaich, long before her own remembering, and under them the names, added later, of everyone since whose death the crews had judged worth keeping.

Her father, Raghnall Breac, hewed at Baile thais still when she was a girl, one of the crews who had come out in the eleven days of the great strike before she was born, a boy then, and had never in all the years after let a Lùnastal pass without telling the story of the twelfth morning and the shelves. But the seam at Baile thais was old and narrow and paid by the ton at a rate that had not moved in a generation, and in the hundred and thirty-fourth year, with two grandchildren to feed and his knees gone stiff for the low work, Raghnall Breac had gone up the hill to Cill ghlas, where the new galleries paid better and a man his age could still find a heading tall enough to stand up straight in. He came down to Eilidh twice a year, at Bealltainn and at the turn of the year, and each time said the same four things: that the house was dry, that the food was fair, that the overseer left the old men alone, and that she was to mind her crew and not to worry for him.

She did not see him again after am Faoilleach of the hundred and thirty-sixth year.

It was in that winter, in the room over the burial club, that she was first asked to sit at the table where the Lamp-Watch kept its business. The Lamp-Watch had begun, twenty years and more before, as the burial club's own purse stretched a little further than the burying of the dead: a fund the crew-leaders kept among themselves for the widow of a man killed at the face, for the fine of a boy taken up by the constables, for the passage of a letter that the Company's post would not carry. By the time Eilidh was asked to the table it had become something else again, though no one at the table would have called it by any other name than what it had always been called. She was given the youngest seat and the least to say, and she went home from her first meeting with the club's minute-book under her coat, to copy its entries into a second book kept nowhere the Company's clerks would think to look, which was a woman's linen chest.

## III. Of the Ground that Spoke First

On {{date:IV-0269}}, an ordinary day of an ordinary week, the galleries under Cill ghlas were worked as they had been worked every day since the headings opened: the day crews down before light, the tubs running, the overseer's watch ticking in his waistcoat pocket at the pit-mouth as it had ticked every morning for two years.

The tellers of Cill ghlas, in the years after, all agreed on one thing before they agreed on anything else: that the ground spoke first. Those nearest the eighth heading said it was a grinding, long and low, like a quern turning under a great weight, felt through the soles of the feet before it was heard at all. Those on the surface, at the pit-mouth and in the cottages along the moor road, said the ground under their own feet answered it a breath later, a shudder that stopped the wheel on the headgear and set every dog in the district barking at once. Then, in the space of perhaps three breaths, the fault that ran unseen and unsurveyed beneath the whole of Fairley's careful sheet of numbered headings tore open along its length, and the hill above the eighth heading came down into the eighth heading, and the seventh into the seventh, and the ground above the old cutting, which had never been drawn with any thickness on any plan, went down after them into a working forty fathom below it that no one at the shrine had ever been told was there.

It killed dozens. The custodians' roll of Cill ghlas, written up at the year's end from what could be gathered of that morning, gives the number as thirty-one, and says under the number that four more bodies were never brought up at all, and were left to the hill. Raghnall Breac was among the thirty-one. He was found on the third day, with two other hewers of his heading, under a fall that had not reached the surface, in a gallery that by every account should have held for a week after the charges that opened it. The overseer who wrote his name into the roll spelled it wrong, and a later hand at Muileann chaol has corrected the spelling in the margin, and left the wrong spelling standing, as the copyists always did once a mistake had gone into the roll.

The shrine went down with the eighth heading, in the same three breaths as everything else: Brìde's lamp, the stone bench where the shrine-keepers sat their watch, the little cairn of coal from every gallery that had ever been opened at Cill ghlas, going back further than any roll now standing could say. Of the keeper on watch that morning, an old woman named Sìne nic Aonghais who had climbed to the shrine every day for nineteen years, nothing was ever found, not a bone nor a shred of her plaid.

The custodians' roll, in the hand that first set the day down, records the loss of the shrine in one line and the loss of the dead in the next line after it. A later copyist, working from the same leaves a season afterward, judged the order backward, holding that flesh must be set before stone, and wrote a second account with the two lines reversed, meaning to strike out the first. He did not strike it out. Both hands stand in the roll as it came down to Muileann chaol, one above the other on the same page, and the three households who copy there have never agreed which should be kept and which crossed through, so both are copied still, every time, the shrine before the people and the people before the shrine, side by side.

## IV. Of the Fenced Ground and the Stone on the Moor Road

A' Chompanaidh's surveyors were back on the hill within the week, this time to fence it. The district closed, by the order that came down from Ros dhomhain, ran from Cill ghlas to Àth chaol and took in every hamlet between, a stretch of hill country that had fed itself on oats and coal both for as long as anyone's grandmother could remember, and the order gave no leave for anyone to go on working any part of it, on any ground, for any wage. The fence was a poor thing, posts and three strands of wire strung by men who plainly did not expect it tested. No one tested it. The custodians read the order to their own households and then, one by one over that autumn and into the winter, the households of the closed district began to leave.

They went in twos and threes at first, and then, as the winter drew in and the last of the stored meal ran low, in whole villages together, walking the moor road south toward Muileann dhomhain with what a cart or a back could carry. The custodians of Muileann dhomhain met the eldest of them, not in the town hall, where a Company clerk sat most days on other business and might have seen who came and went, but at a standing stone on the moor road a mile short of the town, an old grey stone of a height with a tall man, that had stood there, by whose hand or for what first purpose nobody now living could say, since long before the strangers, before the mark, before any road had been metalled to run past it at all. There, at the stone, the custodians of Muileann dhomhain lent the dispossessed the poorest of their own common land, a stretch of thin ground at the edge of the fenced country that had never been worth enclosing for anything better than rough grazing. The meeting was given a name that winter, by whoever names such things: Coinneachadh a' Mhonaidh, the meeting on the moor road, and the stone has been called by no other name since.

On the ground lent them the people of the closed district raised, over the following spring and summer, a new village they called Achadh chaol, narrow field, for the shape of the ground it stood on. It was built without any surveyor of A' Chompanaidh's setting foot on it, without any Company clerk's hand in its laying out, house by house as the households that built it saw fit, on a common decided at the stone and a lease held from the custodians of Muileann dhomhain, in the old form, for the old dues, and from no one else. Of every settlement raised on the island in the years of the strangers, it was the only one built so, and the custodians who wrote of it afterward were at pains to say so, more than once, in words that were nearly the same words each time they said it.

Achadh chaol was raised through that summer in the ordinary way of a village built by people who had built villages before and had only ever lacked the ground to build one on: a well dug first, at the lowest point of the common where the water table lay shallowest, then the custodian's house, because a village without a custodian's roll to enter itself into was not yet a village at all in anyone's reckoning, and then the seven other households in the order their names had been drawn at the stone, so that no household could say afterward it had been given the worst plot out of spite or favour. Aonghas Mòr, who had kept the lamp at the lost shrine and had nothing left to keep it in, was given the custodian's house because the households that had followed him down off the closed hill trusted him more than any stranger among them, and told him so plainly enough that he could not well refuse. He opened the first roll of Achadh chaol on the day the well was finished, and its first line, in a hand still shaking a little from the digging, gives the well's depth in feet and says nothing at all of the shrine, or of Sìne nic Aonghais, or of the day the ground spoke.

Eilidh nic Raghnaill walked to Cill ghlas in the fortnight after the Rending, though there was no house of her father's left standing to walk to, and no grave with his name over it that she could find among the rows the custodians had opened in haste along the moor. She found instead the fenced posts, and the wire strung between them, and beyond the wire a slope of broken ground with the headgear of the eighth heading lying on its side in it like a felled tree, and she stood at the fence for the better part of an hour before a constable, not unkindly, told her the ground was closed and she should move along. She wrote nothing of that hour in the second book kept in the linen chest. It was the one thing in those years the Lamp-Watch's minutes never held.

## V. Of the Pumps Stopped to Save Fuel

The gallery that flooded at Muileann chrom that same season lay three miles up the valley from the barge-stage at Seann Toll, in the workings of Mòrag nic Iain's own district, under an agent new to the pit that year, a narrow, quick-spoken man named Edgar Holt who had come up from the coalfields with strict word from Ros dhomhain that the yield of every pit on the island was to hold, whatever the season asked of it.

The gallery's pumps ran on coal, as every pump on the hill ran on coal, and in the last week of an t-Ògmhios, with the Company's own stores at Seann Toll drawn low by the shipments south and a barge two days overdue, Holt gave the order that the pumps at the flooded heading should stand idle a night, to spare the fuel for the winding-gear until the barge came in. The overseer under him, a Dia-thìreach man named Fionnlagh who had cut coal at that face for twenty years, went to Holt's office himself the evening the order came down and said what a man of twenty years' standing at one face is entitled to say to any agent, however new: that the ground under that heading had never once, in his memory, gone a full night without the pumps running, not even in a dry summer, because the water came out of an old working above it that nobody living had ever mapped. Holt heard him out standing, and answered that the barge was expected within the day, that the winding-gear could not be spared its coal until it came, and that the crews would go down at the ordinary hour as they always did. The pumps were stopped at dusk.

By morning the gallery had filled to the roof for its whole length. Fionnlagh went down himself with the change of shift, to see for himself before he sent any other man in ahead of him, and so he was among the seven who did not come up again, though the roll that lists them gives his name no more weight than any of the others.

Mòrag nic Iain Maor was at the pit-mouth when they brought the seven up. She was forty-two that year, and had carried the Company's staff, the one her great-grandfather Raghnall Maor had taken from the agent's hand a hundred years before, brass-capped and iron-shod, for six years, since her father Iain Maor's death. She had stood at more pit-mouths than she could count by then, but she said afterward, to the one or two who asked her, that she had never in her life heard a silence like the one that morning, with the rain coming down steady on the boards and the seven laid out in a row on them and no sound anywhere but the feet of the women of the camp coming up the track, and no one yet weeping, because no one yet believed it enough to weep.

At noon Edgar Holt rode up from the office with four constables behind him, and stood over the row of the dead, and began, in the humans' tongue, to speak of the loss to the working.

The first stone came from somewhere behind Mòrag and took him on the shoulder before the interpreter had turned two of his words into Dia-thìris. After that the air was full of stones, and the constables were shouting one thing and the crews another, and Mòrag was shouting too, though she could not afterward say what, only that she had been trying to be heard over both. She saw a constable bring his rifle up to his shoulder before she understood what he meant to do with it. She saw the smoke of it before the sound of it reached her, which she said afterward was the strangest thing of the whole morning, stranger even than the stones: that the eye had time to see a thing the ear had not yet caught up to.

When it was over, four Dia-thìrich lay in the mud of the pit-yard beside the seven already laid out from the flood, and one constable lay with them, shot, the roll says, by his own fellow in the confusion of the crowd, though the inquiry that followed never settled whose hand had fired the ball that killed him.

## VI. Of the Inquiry and the Copy That Was Kept

The inquiry sat at Ros dhomhain in am Faoilleach of the next year, on {{date:IV-0275}}, in a long panelled room in the Residency that Mòrag had never before had cause to enter, before three commissioners sent out from the capital of an Tìr Thall for the purpose, none of whom had ever set foot at Muileann chrom nor meant to. She wore her good dark dress, the one kept for burials, and carried the Company's staff with her into the room, though nothing in the summons had asked her to bring it, because she judged, rightly as it turned out, that the sight of it in her hand would make it harder for the commissioners to pretend they were questioning some stranger off the hill and not the fourth Steward of Muileann chrom, whose family's name had stood on that staff since before any of the three commissioners had been born.

She was asked a great many questions about the stones, and fewer about the pumps, and she answered every one of them as it was put to her, in order, without hurrying to the part she had come to say. When at last a commissioner asked, almost as an afterthought, whether she knew anything of why the gallery had flooded at all, she told them: that the pumps had been stopped the evening before on the agent Edgar Holt's own order, to spare fuel for the winding-gear until a barge came in, and that the overseer Fionnlagh had told him plainly what the water would do, and that Holt had given the order regardless.

The clerk at the little table below the commissioners' bench wrote it down as she spoke it. She watched him write it, word for word, in a hand a good deal neater than her father's had ever been. Then the eldest of the three commissioners, without raising his voice or his eyes from the paper in front of him, said that the witness's account of an order she had not herself heard given was not evidence proper to the inquiry, and instructed the clerk to strike the passage from the record.

The clerk drew a single line through what he had written, not so heavy a line that the words beneath it could not still be read, and turned the page.

Eilidh nic Raghnaill was not in the room, and had no right to be, but the clerk who kept the second copy of the inquiry's minutes, the one made for the Company's own file and not for the Residency's, was a Dia-thìreach woman of Ros dhomhain whose brother sat at the Lamp-Watch's table beside Eilidh's own seat, and within the month a fair copy of Mòrag's stricken testimony, struck-through line and all, had made its way north to the room over the burial club at Baile thais, folded small inside a length of sailcloth that had nothing else remarkable about it. Eilidh copied it a second time into the linen chest, struck-through line copied as struck-through, word for word, so that whoever read it after her would see exactly what had been said and exactly what the Residency had chosen not to hear.

She was given a seat of her own at the Lamp-Watch's table that same winter, no longer the youngest with the least to say. It was am Faoilleach still, the hundred and thirty-eighth year turning over the same as every year before it, the same cold rain on the same grey roofs down to the same sea, and if anything else had changed in Baile thais that winter beyond one more woman's place at one more table, the roll that came down to Muileann chaol does not say what it was.

## VII. Of What the Two Rolls Kept

The Company's letter-books show Robert Fairley posted the following year to workings on the eastern side of the island, and there his part in the telling ends. Of Sìne nic Aonghais, keeper of the lost lamp, this alone is told: the shrine-keepers who came after her at Achadh chaol lit a new lamp without her name on it, for want of any line to carry the name from. Elsewhere in that year's telling the copyists let every doubled hand and disputed order stand, for a reader to weigh one against the other. Here they let the page stand empty, and wrote nothing to fill it.
