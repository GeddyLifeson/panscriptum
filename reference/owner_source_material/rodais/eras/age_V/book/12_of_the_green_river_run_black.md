# Of the Green River Run Black

*AE 30 – 35*

## I. Of the Stream at Doire mhòr

Seònaid Bhàn was twenty-five years old in the summer the stream stopped, and she had driven her father's cart on the hill road since she was fourteen. Her father was a carter of Cuan chrom, the little river town where the carriers' road from the capital comes down to a bend of the Abhainn fhionn and crosses it by the ford below the quay. At that quay, since An Aois Naomh, there had stood a carters' house with a hearth at either end, one for carriers going up to the hills and one for carriers coming down; and in the winter of Fiabhras nan Coigreach its roof had fallen in, and nobody had put it back. The carters cursed it as they passed, as men curse a friend who has died on them.

She was called Bhàn for her hair, which was the colour of oat-straw. She was tall and long in the arm, and the carters of the road let her sit at their fires as one of themselves, and said of her that she drove like a man and bargained like a custodian, and that the second was worse.

On {{date:IV-0078}} she took a load of lamp-oil up to the washing-floors at Cnoc fhiadhaich for A' Chompanaidh, twelve casks at so much the cask, paid in marks at the pit-head. It was a two-day haul from Cuan chrom, over the ford of Àth chiar and up into the hills. The washing-floors were new that spring. They lay in the valley bottom below the pit where the eleven had died five years before, long troughs of plank on trestles, down which a race of water ran over the broken coal to carry off the dirt; and the dirt went on down the valley in a grey stream to find its own way to the river.

She saw at once that the race was running higher than it had in the spring. She saw too where the water came from. Above the troughs, where the valley narrowed under the shoulder of the hill, there was a new bank of turf and clay faced with planks, and behind it a long brown pond with the drowned heather standing up in it. The stream that fed the pond came down off the high ground from the north, from the side of the hill where Doire mhòr stood among its oaks. It had watered Doire mhòr since there was a Doire mhòr. Now it went into the pond, and out of the pond by a wooden sluice into the race, and down the troughs over the coal.

Below the bank, in the old bed, there was a green trickle between dry stones, and a woman was kneeling there with a wooden pail, scooping.

Seònaid unloaded her casks and was paid. Then she walked up the old stream-bed towards the village. All the way up the stones were dry and the cress in the pools had gone brown, and at the top, where the path came out of the oaks into the village fields, she met a line of people coming down the slope with yokes on their shoulders and a pail at each end of the yoke. They were going down to the spring at the foot of the brae, below the oaks, which had been the cattle's spring and was now the whole village's. It was the better part of a mile each way, and a steep mile.

There were men in the line, and women, and children carrying jugs. The roll of Doire mhòr, which Beathag Dhubh kept in her own hand, names the households that carried that summer, and one of those who carried is Tormod, the son of Ruairidh who had stood behind Beathag at her door when she sent the agent of A' Chompanaidh down the hill. The roll gives him his name and his yoke and nothing more.

Seònaid slept that night in the byre of the custodian's house, and in the morning she went down to the washing-floors again with Beathag Dhubh.

## II. Of the Order That Was Not Written

Beathag Dhubh was forty-nine years old that summer, a small dark woman with a jaw like a quern-stone, and she walked down the hill with her hands clasped behind her back. She had asked Seònaid to come because Seònaid knew which of the strangers kept the book.

The man who kept the book at Cnoc fhiadhaich was Amos Tanner, an agent of A' Chompanaidh, a heavy young man with red wrists who sat in a timber office by the washing-floors with a ledger open in front of him and a stove going in the corner in the middle of summer. He spoke some Dia-thìris, badly and fast, and he stood up when Beathag came in, which was more than most of them did.

"The stream," said Beathag. "The stream of Doire mhòr. Show me the order."

Seònaid said it again for him in the carters' speech with the strangers, half of one tongue and half of the other, and he looked relieved, as a man looks who has been asked a question with an easy answer.

There was no order, he said. Nobody had ordered anything. The washing needed water and the stream was there. The bank had been thrown up in the spring by the washing gang, to hold a head of water for the dry months, and that was all.

"Then let it go," said Beathag.

Tanner said that he could not do that. The washing needed water.

"Who said you might take it?"

Nobody had said so, Tanner answered patiently. It had not needed saying. It was a stream on the hill, and the hill was worked by A' Chompanaidh under its contracts, and the water went with the working. If the custodian of Doire mhòr had a complaint, she might write it, and he would send it on to Ros dhomhain.

"To whom at Ros dhomhain?"

He did not know to whom. To the office.

Beathag looked round the timber room: at the ledger, at the stove, at the chart of the valley pinned to the planks, with the pond drawn as a neat blue shape and the stream above it as a line that came from nowhere. She put out one finger and touched the place on the chart where Doire mhòr would have been, if anyone had drawn it. Then she turned and went out without another word, and Seònaid went after her.

"He has no paper," Beathag said on the path. "So there is nothing to undo."

She wrote to the council all the same. The letter went down the road in Seònaid's cart, and Seònaid put it into the hand of a custodians' clerk at Cathair dhearg, and the council entered it; and the stream was not let go that season, nor the next.

## III. Of the Wet Harvest

The summer broke at the end of an t-Iuchar and it rained for a month.

In another year the hill villages would have taken their oats in between the showers, every hand in the field at the first dry hour. That year there were not the hands. The pits had been drawing the young men off the farms for twenty years, and faster in the six since the new Àrd-mhaor came over the sea with orders to double the yield: a son here for the ton-money, a brother there, a husband who came home at the week's end with coal in the lines of his face and slept the rest-day through. The old men and the women and the children cut what they could. The rest stood in the fields and sprouted in the ear, and in {{year:IV-0079}}, by the end of Lùnastal, the hill villages of Muileann chrom had lost the better half of their harvest.

Seònaid carried meal that autumn. She carried it for A' Chompanaidh, from the quay at Ros dhomhain where it came off the ships in sacks stencilled in the humans' letters, up the carriers' road to the store at Àth ìseal, where the camp of timber huts stood at the ford below the new workings. She was paid for the haul in marks. At the store the meal was sold, over a counter, by a clerk with a steelyard, and it was sold for marks.

The hill villages had few marks. They had oats in a good year, and wool, and cheese. The clerk at Àth ìseal would not take cheese; he had no book for cheese. So the women came down to the store with the coins their men had brought home, and when those were spent they sent the men back to the pits for more.

Seònaid stood at the store door one wet evening in an Dàmhair, waiting for her own pay, and watched a woman from the high farms above Doire mhòr count coins onto the clerk's counter. There were not enough. The clerk pushed the sack back towards the wall. The woman stood and looked at it, and then she took off the brooch at her shoulder, which was bronze with a knot on it of the old kind, and laid it on the counter. The clerk looked at it and pushed it back to her with one finger.

Seònaid put two marks down beside the brooch, and the clerk took them, and the woman took the sack and went out without looking at either of them. Seònaid drove home the next day two marks short, and her father said nothing about it, which in her father was a kind of speech.

## IV. Of the Meal-Carts from the South

The south had had its own wet autumn, and it waited on its next harvest before it gave from it. In the autumn of the next year, when that harvest was in, the villages of Doire fhiadhaich, far down in the oak country of the south, loaded carts with meal and sent them north to the hill villages. They did it as Lagh a' Bhìdh Roinnte bade in a hungry year, as it had bidden since An Aois Naomh, and nobody asked them to and nobody paid them.

There were nine carts, with oxen, and they came up by Dùn chrom and struck the carriers' road below Cuan chrom in the second week of an Dàmhair, in the rain. They came down to the ford at dusk and stood looking at the roofless house at the quay with the rain running out of its doorways, and Seònaid's father went down with a lantern and brought them up to his own byre and hearth, and Seònaid made the porridge. They talked late, in the slow broad speech of the south.

The eldest of the drivers was a man named Gille-Brìde, an oak-keeper's son, who had brought his own grandson with him to show him the road. He asked how the hill villages were keeping, and what the meal was selling for at the stores, and Seònaid told him. He sat a while with his cup between his hands.

"For coin," he said at last.

"For their coin," said her father. "The marks."

"And where do the hill folk get their marks?"

"From the pits."

Gille-Brìde nodded slowly, like a man working a sum he did not like the end of. Then he said, "Well. They will not need to buy this."

The carts went on in the morning, over the ford at Àth chiar and up into the hills, and Seònaid went with them the first day to show them the road. She turned back at the Muileann chrom fork. It was not until the next week that she heard, at the store, what had become of them.

At the ford below Àth ìseal the agents of A' Chompanaidh had met the carts on the road. They wanted meal for the store and the camp, and they offered the southern drivers marks for it, more than it would fetch at any market in the south. The drivers said the meal was not for sale. The agents doubled the price. Two of the drivers, young men with debts at home, sold their loads there on the road and went home by another way. The other seven went on up into the hills and gave their meal away, sack by sack, at the doors of the villages, as the law bade.

Gille-Brìde's was one of the seven. He came back down the road a week later with his empty cart and stopped at Cuan chrom to thank her father for the byre, and Seònaid asked him about the two who had sold.

"They were paid," he said. "The agents paid them fair. More than fair." He rubbed his face. "That is the thing about it I cannot get round."

He went away south. Seònaid did not see him again. But that was the night she began to count in her head, as she lay awake, what a cart could carry, and what it could be paid in, and who could pay.

## V. Of the Chest for the Loft

In the same season a woman in a grey cloak came up the road from the capital driving a hired pony-cart with a chest roped into the back of it, and she stopped at Cuan chrom and asked for a bed.

She was Muireall nic Fhearchair, a copyist, a lean woman of forty-two with ink under her nails. Her aunt Gormshuil nic Fhearchair had died at Cathair dhearg in the last days of an t-Sultain, in her seventy-first year. Gormshuil had kept the council's list of the island's words since the ships first came, and she had been the custodian who would not sign Puingean a' Chalaidh, and she had taught herself the humans' letters at an age when most women are teaching their grandchildren. Now she was dead, and her papers were in the chest, and Muireall was carrying them east to the loft of Beathag nic Thòmais at Muileann chaol, where the old copies were kept, because Gormshuil had asked her to and because she did not trust them to stay safe anywhere nearer Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir.

Seònaid carried the chest in from the cart herself. It was heavier than it looked.

"What is in it?" she asked.

"Words," said Muireall. "Forty years of them. Hers and the strangers'."

She would not open it that night. But the next morning the rain held on, and the road was a river, and she could not go on; and in the afternoon, sitting at the hearth with nothing to do, she unroped the chest and lifted the lid and took out, from the top, a single leaf in a wrapping of oiled cloth. It was her own copy of the first leaf she had written, she said, in the third spring of the strangers, when Gormshuil and the cleric Wren had sat across a table at Cathair dhearg with a basket of coal between them. It was Clàr nan Dà Fhichead Dath: Clàr nan Dà Fhichead Dath of the coal, each with its name, forty names in two columns, in the old strokes and in the humans' letters side by side.

Seònaid could not read either. Muireall read the names to her, down one row and up the other: the purples and the greens and the fire-colours, the white of gual sneachda, the brown-red that is almost black. Seònaid knew most of them, as every carter of the hills did, for a carter who could not tell good coal from dross by its colour did not stay a carter long. She had never heard them said in a row before.

At the bottom of the leaf there was a note in a smaller hand. Muireall read that too. It said that the strangers had one word for all the coal of the vein, and that no word of the island answered it, and that they had not asked for the island's words.

"One word," said Seònaid.

"One."

"For all of that?"

Muireall wrapped the leaf again and laid it back in the chest.

"They cannot see it," she said. "My aunt was a long time understanding that. She thought at first they would not see it. It took her years to believe that they could not." She shut the lid. "The one word is the word for what it burns like. It is a good word for that."

The rain stopped in the night. In the morning Seònaid roped the chest back into the pony-cart and saw it over the ford, and Muireall went on east alone towards Muileann chaol, three days away, a grey figure beside a grey pony, until the road turned behind the shoulder of the hill.

That winter, as the copyists count it, the mountain began to be dug by the ton. A' Chompanaidh had weighed its coal by the ton since the first contract. From {{year:IV-0081}} it cut by the ton as well: the crews were set a weight for the week, and the galleries driven to meet the weight, and the hill was worked for ends that no custom of the Dia-thìrich would have chosen. At Dùn chrom the rope-walks that had spun cable for the fishing boats turned all their hemp to winding-rope for the pit-heads, and A' Chompanaidh paid well, and the town grew by half. Seònaid hauled the rope up to the hills in coils as thick as a man's thigh.

## VI. Of the Going Down from Doire mhòr

In the spring of {{year:IV-0083}} word came up the road that A' Chompanaidh had been chartered afresh in an Tìr Thall, with new money and a new name. The name was long, and the carters tried it out at their fires and gave it up, and went on calling it A' Chompanaidh. What they did take note of was that its agents at Ros dhomhain now outnumbered the clerks of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach four to one, and that a carter with a quarrel over a haul had stopped going to the office of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach at all. He went to the office of A' Chompanaidh, since that was where the quarrel would be settled.

The second summer of the carrying came, and the stream at Doire mhòr was not let go. The pond behind the bank had grown. The washing gang had raised the bank another two feet in the winter, and the drowned heather was gone under, and the brown water lay nearly to the oaks.

Seònaid went up to the village in an t-Ògmhios with a load of salt, and found it half empty.

The households had begun to go that spring, one family and then another, with no meeting called: a man away to the pits since the winter, his wife left carrying water for a field he would not come home to cut, and at last the wife packing the kist and the pots onto a borrowed pony and going down to him. Most went to the huts at Àth ìseal, where there was work breaking coal for the children and water that someone else carried. Others went on to Muileann chrom, or down the road to the capital. Seònaid passed them on the road, sitting on their bundles in the backs of carts.

The roll of Doire mhòr sets the first households' leaving under that year, and gives their names. Tormod, who had carried water up to Doire mhòr for two summers, went down to Àth ìseal with the rest.

Beathag Dhubh did not go. She stood at the door of the custodian's house as Seònaid unloaded the salt, with her arms folded, and watched the empty cottages across the green.

"You could go down to the council," Seònaid said. "Again."

"I have written to the council three times," said Beathag. "They have entered it three times." She took a pinch of the salt from the open sack and tasted it, and nodded. "They cannot give back a thing that nobody took."

In an t-Sultain of that year A' Chompanaidh began to load barges at Cidhe Beag. Cidhe Beag was the little quay on the Abhainn uaine, below Baile thais, where the river comes out of the hills wide enough to float a boat; it had been a canoe landing since before a' Scaradh, a shelf of flat rock at the water's edge with a ferryman's hut above it, and a shrine on the rock, a grey stone the height of a child with a hollow worn in its top where the boatmen had left their offerings going out. A' Chompanaidh widened the shelf with cut stone into a quay, and built a chute down to it from the cart-road for the coal.

Seònaid brought the first loads down to it herself, and she was there when the masons came to the shrine. The foreman, a Dia-thìreach of Ros dhomhain who did not like the work, sent for the ferryman before he touched the stone. The ferryman was old and weary in the face. He looked at the stone, and at the chute, and asked where they meant to put it.

The foreman said: wherever the ferryman liked, so long as it was off the quay.

So the ferryman chose a place on the bank above, under a rowan, where the stone could still see the water; and four masons carried it up on a hurdle, with the ferryman walking in front, and set it down where he showed them, and the ferryman put a little meal in its hollow from his own pocket. Then the masons went back to their work, and the first barge was loaded before dark.

## VII. Of the Green River

The Abhainn uaine is called the green river for the colour of the water under its alders, which in the old songs is the colour of a trout's back. That winter it turned black.

The washing-floors at Cnoc fhiadhaich and Muileann chrom, and the new ones built that year, all sent their dirt down into the streams, and the streams carried it into the Abhainn uaine. At first there was a dark edge along the banks after rain. Then the pools below the landing silted, and the gravel of the fords went under a grey mud that stank when it was stirred. By the turn of {{year:IV-0085}} the whole river from Cidhe Beag to the sea ran thick and dark, and the barges went down it to Seann Toll and the river mouth on water the colour of a slate roof.

The strangers called it black. To a Dia-thìreach eye it was never only black. When the sun came through onto it at a bend there was colour in it, the colours of the coal ground to dust and gone to sludge, purple and bottle-green and a dull fire-colour, drifting in long streaks the way oil drifts on a puddle. Seònaid stood on the bridge at Cidhe Beag one bright morning with the ferryman and watched the streaks go under, and neither of them said anything about it. There was nothing to say that the other could not see.

At the river's mouth, at Ceann leathan, the fishermen had netted salmon on the spring run every year that anyone could remember. The town took its share at the mouth and let the rest go by to spawn in the gravel of the hills. In the spring of that year the run was thin. In the summer it was thinner. The last salmon netted at the mouth of the Abhainn uaine was taken that year, a hen fish of no great size, and the man who landed it is remembered by name at Ceann leathan, and he did not want to be; for he gutted it and found it empty of eggs. In the next spring no fish came in at all. The gravel where they had spawned was under the grey mud, and the fish of the sea do not go up a river that has nothing for them at the top.

The fishers of Ceann leathan went on calling it the Abhainn uaine. They said it with their mouths turned down, and the bargemen laughed at them, and the fishers did not laugh back.

Other coal went north from Cidhe Beag by the river road to Baile Mòr dhomhain, the old walled market town on its deep river, and the town grew fat on it. Its people doubled in number, most of the newcomers bargemen from the coast who slept in the lofts over its stables; and the old families complained of them at every market and let them rooms at every chance. Seònaid hauled there twice that year. It had too many inns, she said, and none of them any good.

That was the year she married. Her husband was Pàdraig mac Artair, a carter of Doire chaol, the carters' village just east of the capital, a broad slow man who sang when he drove and was the only carter on the road who could outbargain her; and she said afterwards that she married him to have the benefit of it, which may have been true. They lived in her father's house at Cuan chrom. Their son Murchadh was born there the next winter, in the room above the byre, while the roofless carters' house stood by the quay with the frost in it.

## VIII. Of the House at the Half-way

In the first month of {{year:IV-0087}} a surveyor of A' Chompanaidh went out to a valley in the hills north-west of Muileann chrom with a chain and a book of squared paper, and laid out a town. He drew it before a stone of it was set, streets set square to one another and a row of little houses all alike on either side, with a store and a pay office at the end. The camp was called Baile dhomhain, the first town of the Dia-thìrich drawn on paper before it was built. Within two seasons it was full, a hundred souls and more. Seònaid hauled timber for its roofs. She said to Pàdraig that it was a town for people who would not be staying, and he asked her where else they would go, and she did not answer.

That spring the traffic on the road was heavier than it had ever been, and every night there were carters at Cuan chrom with nowhere to sleep. Some slept in their carts, and some in her father's byre until there was no more room in it, and the rest built fires under the broken walls of the old carters' house and drank and quarrelled there; and in an Giblean one of them knocked another into the ford.

Seònaid went and stood in the roofless house the next morning. The two hearths were still there, at either end, under a litter of fallen thatch and nettles. The walls stood to the height of a man. The lintel over the western door was a single stone with a groove cut in it, worn smooth by the hands of the carriers of An Aois Naomh who had touched it going in, and of every carrier since.

She went home and told her father and Pàdraig that she meant to put the roof back.

Her father thought it a great deal of money. Pàdraig thought it a good idea, and went round the carters of Doire chaol, and came back with twenty of them who would give a week's work each for a year's lodging at half the price. Seònaid went to the custodians of Cuan chrom, who held the quay, and they gave her the house for a rent of one cheese a year, since nobody else had wanted it for twenty years and more. The timber for the roof came up from the oak country of Doire fhiadhaich, on carts she sent south for it. The slates came from the quarry at Àth chiar. The strangers' iron was in the nails and the hinges, and nowhere else.

She opened the doors on the first day of Lùnastal, {{date:IV-0088}}, on the feast, with a fire lit at both hearths and a bowl of new ale for any carter who came in; and she kept the old name, An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, because nobody on the road had ever called it anything else.

On the first night a carter from the hills came in with his crew, and asked the price of a bed and a supper, and when she told him, he put down a round cheese and a fleece on the table.

"I have no marks," he said. "Not till the week's end."

A carter of the capital, at the other end of the table, laughed and said that she would want marks. Every house on the road wanted marks now. A' Chompanaidh paid in marks and the stores sold in marks, and a cheese was a cheese.

Seònaid picked up the cheese and turned it over and smelt it. Then she weighed it in her two hands.

"That is a bed and a supper," she said. "And the fleece is two."

The carter from the capital stopped laughing. "And if I pay in marks?"

"Then it is a bed and a supper for marks. The same bed. The same supper." She put the cheese on the shelf behind her. "I will take the one or the other. I will take them at the same rate. And I will not ask which a man has before I tell him the price."

That was the rule of the house, and she had it cut on a board and hung over the hearth at the eastern end, in the old strokes, since that was what most of the hill carters could read: *Bonn no bathar, an aon bhobhla.* Coin or goods, the same bowl. She kept it all the years she kept the house, the only keeper on the road who took both; and the carters of the hills, who had cheese and wool and no marks, came to her, and the carters of the capital came to her also, since the ale was good.

In an t-Sultain the road-gangs of A' Chompanaidh finished metalling the carriers' road from the capital by Doire chaol and Cuan chrom and the ford of Àth chiar, up to the workings in the hills, broad enough for two coal-carts to pass. They laid it in crushed stone and rolled it with an iron roller that twelve men pulled. At every mile there stood an old post with the crown's sign cut in it, the posts that had marked the road since An Aois Scaraidh, and the road-gangs left them standing, since it was less trouble to go round. The carters, whose road it had been since An Aois Naomh, began to call it Rathad na Mèinne, as if it had been made the day before. Seònaid called it that too, after a while.

## IX. Of the Carters at Her Table

The carters of Doire chaol met first in her house in the winter, round the eastern hearth, because they were on the road and it was the only house on the road where they could all afford to sit down.

There were some thirty of them in the end, with Pàdraig among them, and they had one grievance, which was the rate. A' Chompanaidh paid by the ton for coal hauled from the hills to the capital, and set the rate at its office in Ros dhomhain, and had lowered it twice in two years; and a carter who would not haul at the new rate found carters from the coast who would. Each man had gone to the office in his turn. None had gone together.

They sat in Seònaid's house through the dark of the year and argued it. Some of them were for refusing the hauls outright. Some of them were for going to the custodians. Pàdraig, who was slow, and who had listened to his wife count carts in her sleep for three years, said that they should go to the office all together, all thirty, on the same day, and ask for the old rate, and that no man should take a haul at the new one until they had it. And they swore to that at her table, on the ale, with their hands on the board with the rule on it; and in an Giblean of {{year:IV-0089}} the carters' guild of Doire chaol went down to Ros dhomhain, thirty carts in a line, and stood in the yard of A' Chompanaidh.

It was the first Dia-thìreach guild of the age. A' Chompanaidh might have broken it with the carters from the coast; but those men did not know the road or the fords, and they lost wheels and loads in the ditches; and the clerks at Ros dhomhain did the sum, and found that it came cheaper to bargain with thirty men who knew the road than to break them. So A' Chompanaidh let the guild stand. It sent an agent up to meet the carters. And since the carters would not come to Ros dhomhain again, the agent came to them, to the house at the half-way stage of the road, where they met.

The agent was Amos Tanner, older and heavier now, who had been given the carriage of the coal as his charge. He came up Rathad na Mèinne on a grey horse on a hot evening at the start of an t-Iuchar, and tied the horse at the door, and ducked his head under the grooved lintel, and stood a while at the eastern end of the room reading the board over the hearth, or looking at it, since he could not read it.

"What does it say?" he asked her.

She told him.

He thought about it. "And the ale, if I pay you in marks?"

"The same," said Seònaid, and gave him a bowl.

The carters were at the long table under the window. Tanner sat down at its head, with his book in front of him, and Pàdraig sat down at its foot. The first thing Tanner told them was that the landing at Seann Toll on the Abhainn uaine had been taken for a coal stage three days before: that A' Chompanaidh had built a loading-stage of timber there below the workings, and moved the town's fishing boats to the next reach down, and that from now on a good part of the coal of the hills would go down the black river by barge to Ceann leathan, and on by ship, and never come near the capital at all. There would be less hauling on the road.

The carters had seen the barges at Cidhe Beag, and heard it without surprise. Pàdraig asked how much less.

Tanner told him. Then he named the rate for what was left.

Pàdraig named the old rate back to him. Tanner shook his head and named a figure halfway between. Pàdraig looked down the table at the others and then at his wife, who was standing at the hearth with the jug, and she did not move, and he named the old rate again.

Seònaid filled the bowls. The windows were open for the heat, and the long light lay across the table between the agent's ledger and the carters' hands. Across the room at the other hearth a hill carter was paying for his supper with a basket of eggs, and she went and took them from him, and counted them, and gave him his change in a quarter of a cheese.

At the long table they were still bargaining when the light went. She lit the lamps.
