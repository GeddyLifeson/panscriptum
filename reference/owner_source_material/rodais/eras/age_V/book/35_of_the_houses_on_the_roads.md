# Of the Houses on the Roads

*AE 141 – 146*

> *Cha tug mi dhut ach an rud a bha thu ag iarraidh nach fhaicinn.*
> I gave you only what you wanted not to see.

## I. Of the House at the Halfway Stage

Aonghas had kept An Taigh-seinnse Mòr for six years before he understood that the keeping of it had quietly become an answer to a question nobody had ever put to him straight. His great-grandmother Seònaid Bhàn had opened the house again in the first years of the digging, when the coal road was new mud and the carters still carried both coin and barter in the same pocket and would take either without a word said over it. The house had never once in three generations shut its door on a traveller who could pay. Aonghas kept that rule as he had been given it. He kept the accounts in the same ledger his grandfather had used, the covers gone soft as cloth, and he kept, without ever writing it anywhere, a second reckoning that had nothing to do with marks.

By the autumn of the hundred and forty-first year the second reckoning had grown larger than the first.

It began, as most things on the coal road began, with a man Aonghas had never met asking a favour he had not thought to refuse. A crew-leader from Baile thais came down with a Company cart of scrap iron and asked, over a cup he paid for honestly, whether the loft above the stable might hold six men a night or two, now and again, no questions wanted and none offered. Aonghas said the loft held what it held. He did not ask what the men carried, coming or going, and in time he stopped noticing that he had stopped asking. It was easier that way, and easier, he told himself, was not the same as blind.

He was not alone in it. Up the northern road, at An Leòmhann Buidhe, they took on six new ostlers in a single season, and every carter on the road remarked, laughing, that not one of the six knew one end of a horse from the other. At the singing house, An Taigh-òsta Grianach, whose songs had been Fearchar Bàrd's in the carters' grandfathers' day, they raised a wall round the yard and set a boy at the gate with a horn he had never once had cause to blow, though he kept it polished all the same. Down on the south-western road, at Am Buabhall Fortanach, Tormod Buabhall barred his gates at dusk and put a man on the roof with a glass, and once a week, on no fixed day, he sent a folded list up the road in the saddlebag of whichever rider was going that way, and never told anyone, not even the rider, into whose hand it was meant to come. Aonghas met him twice at the horse-fair at Muileann shean and never once asked him what was on the list. It was not a thing men on the coal road asked one another in those years. A house that asked too many questions of its guests soon found it had none.

He rode past An Leòmhann Buidhe himself that same autumn, taking the northern loop to see a cousin, and stopped a night for the sake of the road. The keeper there was a broad, unhurried woman named Barabal who had run the house since her husband's death and whose six new ostlers, when Aonghas walked out to look over his own horse before bed, stood about the yard in a way no working ostler ever stood: too still, too watchful, one of them holding a currycomb he plainly did not know which end to use. Barabal caught Aonghas's eye across the yard and said nothing, only tipped her head a fraction toward the stable, the smallest of gestures, the kind two keepers on the same road learn to read in each other without either one troubling to explain it. Aonghas went in to his supper and asked her, over the meal, only how her hay had come in that year, and she told him, at some length, and neither of them said another word about the six men in the yard.

The Administration called none of this by any name. There was no order posted, no notice nailed to a Company board, because there was nothing yet to forbid; six houses walling their own yards against thieves was no different in the letter of any law from An Taigh-seinnse Òir, the great house beyond Dùn dhearg, digging a well in its yard that same season and walling it round, against thieves, its keeper said. Aonghas turned it over some nights with his back to the fire and his ledger unopened on his knee, and came to call that the design of it, if design was the word for a thing that had grown up the way heather grows up a burnt hill, from no one plan and every man's smallest choice. A house could not be charged with sedition for keeping its gate shut after dark.

One night that first autumn a Company surveyor stopped at the house, a mild, tired man named Bell who drew maps of the coal seams for a living and had never in his life carried anything more dangerous than a set of dividers. He asked Aonghas, over the second cup, whether he had noticed the road-houses growing close-mouthed of late, and said he could not put his finger on why it troubled him, only that a house that had always talked freely now talked of the weather and nothing else. Aonghas poured him a third cup and agreed that the roads had grown quiet, and said no more, and watched Bell ride on the next morning no wiser than he had come, and felt, watching him go, neither triumph nor shame in it, only the tiredness of a man who has told the truth and left out the half of it that mattered.

## II. Of the Landing Below the Fortunate House

Below Am Buabhall Fortanach, where the south-western road ran down toward the sea before it climbed again into the hills, there was a harbour too small to have a name in any Company chart, though the fishermen who worked it called it Doire shean, after the stand of old oaks on the point above it. It had never shipped anything grander than herring and the odd cargo of slate, and the Company's agents, who counted every barrel that moved by water on the west coast, had long since stopped troubling to count Doire shean's.

That was its use. What the great house on the road above could not carry by cart, for weight or for the eyes that watched the road, went down instead by the old fishing path to the little harbour, and came up again by the same path in the dark of the next tide. The fishermen of Doire shean, who had fished that water three generations under three different flags of custodian and Company both, took to being paid, for certain nights' work that were never spoken of as work, in the silver guild's bar, cut small, rather than in the coin the Administration minted at Ros dhomhain. Nobody asked where the silver came from and nobody, least of all the fishermen, was fool enough to spend it where a Company clerk might see it change hands. It went, most of it, into the ground under a hearthstone or into a widow's dowry chest, to be spent, if ever, by whoever came after, without asking what nights had earned it.

Aonghas went down to Doire shean himself twice that year, on pretence of buying herring for the house, and stood on the point in the wind with old Coinneach nan Ràmh, who had fished the water since before the first pit opened at Muileann chrom, and said nothing that either of them could not have said before a magistrate. It was Coinneach who told him, without being asked, that the loft at An Taigh-seinnse Reòta on the northern road was never once, in two years, found empty by the Company's carters passing through, and never once held the same men two nights running. "They come like the tide," the old man said, "and go like it, and no man's ever caught the tide at its turning." Aonghas thought that a fine thing to say and a truer one than most things said on the coal road in those years, and he carried it home and kept it, the way he kept the ledger, without ever writing it down.

## III. Of the Post at the Far Shore and the Boys of the Ford

Word, in those years, moved slower than silver and faster than either the Company or the Administration ever managed to learn. It moved through An Sligeanach Fad' às, the post-house on the eastern road near the silver country, whose keeper took his rent, openly and by the season, from the silver guild of Muileann chiar, and who never once in five years lost a letter that was worth the losing. Through him the Lamp-Watch sent its word to the guild and the guild sent its silver back, and the Administration, whose own post rode the same road on the same days, never once thought to ask why a road-house should need two keepers' worth of trade to fill one keeper's ledger.

The letters themselves, once past the Sligeanach's door, went the last stage by boys. They came out of Àth fhiadhaich, sons and nephews of the ford's boatmen, twelve years old and thirteen, too small for a constable to trouble with and too quick on the causeway paths for a mounted man to follow. They carried nothing that looked like a letter: a fold of paper inside a boot, a line of words learned by heart and said over to a woman at a well who would say them over again to somebody else three villages on. Aonghas met one of them once, a boy called nothing worth remembering here, who came into the house's yard soaked to the knee from the ford and asked for bread as any boy might, and left an hour later having said not one word out of place, and having left, folded under the loose board by the stable door where Aonghas kept certain things, a scrap that named a date and nothing else. Aonghas burned it that night in the kitchen fire, the way he burned every such scrap, and watched it curl and thought, not for the first time, how small and how ordinary a shape a great and terrible thing can wear while it is still only beginning.

The money for it all, for the boys' bread, the Sligeanach's rent, the loft at the Reòta that fed men it never named, was kept, in those years, at Achadh mhòr, a village of no great size near the great house on the eastern road, in the roof-space of its own shrine, above the rafters where the sacred lamp burned and below the thatch where no constable's search had ever thought, or dared, to reach. The custodian there was an old woman who kept the ledger of it in a hand so cramped and so plain that a stranger, finding it, would have taken it for a list of candles bought and wicks trimmed. Aonghas never saw that ledger. He knew only that when the loft at his own house needed feeding, word came, and marks came with it, folded inside a psalter that travelled the road as innocently as any priest's book ever had.

## IV. Of the Fire Taken for Heath

The day of {{date:IV-0301}} was a dry, hard, bright day with a wind out of the north-west that had been blowing three days running and had scoured the sky to a colour Aonghas remembered afterward as harder than any blue he had seen before or since. He was in the yard at midday, seeing to a cart of coal-tubs bound down for Ros dhomhain, when one of the carters straightened and pointed east, up toward the long shoulder of Cnoc an Rabhaidh, where a thread of smoke was going up thin and steady into the wind.

"Heath fire," the carter said, and shrugged, and went back to his harness, because in that wind, on that hill, in that month, it was the likeliest thing in the world, and every man in the yard had seen a dozen such fires start from nothing more than a spark off a flint and burn themselves out by nightfall. Aonghas watched it a moment longer than the carter did. He had heard, though from whom he could no longer afterward have said, that the hill above Doire mhòr, a poor place now, its households few since it refused the Company's first contract and lost its water after, was to carry, on a day and at an hour nobody named aloud, a fire that was not heath at all, lit by the children's children of a woman called Beathag Dhubh, whose name the camps still used the way other places used a saint's.

Aonghas did not move from the yard, having no order to give and no man of his own to send, and reasoning that if the fire on Cnoc an Rabhaidh was the thing he half suspected it to be, then moving would tell any watching eye more than standing still. So he stood, and watched the smoke, and within the hour every wheel and hammer and windlass within sight and sound of the yard had stopped.

Every working the coal country held stopped in the same hour, without a single order carried by any rider the Administration's own scouts could find. At Muileann bheag, far off among the druid villages in the north-east, the pits stopped, and the custodian there wrote afterward, in words that reached even Aonghas's ears months later, that it was the first thing her town had done with the hills in a hundred years. At Àth dhomhain in the north a runner on foot brought the word rather than any beacon, and the pits stopped all the same; their crew-leaders were among those the Administration later took and never gave back. On the northern coast the loading at Ceann bheag stopped before the dockers there had even been told to stop it; the coal simply ceased to come down to them, and they laid down their hooks because there was nothing left to lift. Quarrymen at Cnoc gheal, who cut no coal and owed the Lamp-Watch no debt that any ledger recorded, laid down their tools with the rest and gave, when asked, no reason at all.

At Muileann chrom itself, and with it Baile thais, Cnoc chaol, the silver town and the eastern pits at Ros fhionn, every wheel stood still in the same hour the smoke went up from Cnoc an Rabhaidh. The Administration's own agents, writing it down afterward for the Company's ledgers, called it the first stoppage the resistance of the coal country had ever managed to hold together, and granted, in words Aonghas heard repeated on the road for years after with something close to pride, that scattered as the Dia-thìrich of the hills were, town from town and valley from valley, they could act as one people when they chose to.

The yard at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr held that day a dozen men who could do nothing but wait. Aonghas fed them all, coal-tubs man and idle carter alike, and took no coin from any of them, and a woman among the carters, a driver named Cairistìona who worked the eastern run and had never before sat down in his kitchen, asked him quietly why he gave away a day's custom for nothing. "Because a house that charges for waiting is a house nobody waits in," he told her, "and I would rather have them here where I can see them than scattered on the road where I can't." She laughed at that, the first laugh the kitchen had heard all day, and it did him more good than the coin he had given up.

## V. Of the Six Days

Aonghas kept his own house open through it, as he had always kept it open, and turned no traveller from the door, though there were fewer travellers in those six days than the house had seen in any six days of his keeping. The coal road itself stood nearly empty; the carts that would have carried coal down to Ros dhomhain had nothing to carry, and the drivers who would have driven them sat instead in his yard with cups they were slow to finish, listening for word from the hills the way men listen for weather.

At Ros dhomhain the Company's steamships lay six days at the quay with their holds as empty as the day they were built, and the agents there wrote, in letters that found their way back up the road by the same paths the silver and the psalters travelled, that they had never in their years on the coast seen the harbour so quiet, and that the quiet frightened them a good deal more than any crowd ever had. A harbour that shouts can be answered with constables. A harbour that says nothing at all cannot be argued with, because it has offered nothing to argue against.

Force ended it, and no agreement. As the year went out the constables shut the Company's stores in every district of the coal country at once, so that a man who had stopped work in solidarity found within a day that his household could not eat without going back to it. Forty crew-leaders were taken up in the same sweep, from the hills and the eastern pits and the quarries at Cnoc gheal alike. Thirty-two of them were let go inside the fortnight, the Administration having found, on reflection, no law under which to hold men for standing idle on their own ground. The remaining eight went aboard a Company ship at Ros dhomhain and were carried east, for trial, it was said, in an Tìr Thall, and no letter, no rumour, no word carried by boy or boat or silver-bought fisherman ever came back from any one of them. Aonghas asked, over the years that followed, more than once, of more than one man who might have known, and got nothing for an answer but a shake of the head, which in time he learned to give himself when others asked him the same question.

The constables burned the heather of Cnoc an Rabhaidh that same week, to deny the hill a second such beacon, and it grew back thin and late the following spring, the way burned heather does, and it was three years before anyone on that road spoke openly of the fire that had, everyone by then knew and no one by then troubled to deny, been no heath fire at all.

## VI. Of the Idle Harbours

The ships grew fewer after that, and older. No single season and no single order brought it about; it crept in the way frost creeps into a house through a door left an inch ajar, unnoticed until the cold is everywhere at once. The keepers' daybooks at the five beacons of the ring, whose only business had ever been to count the sails from over the sea, began to show, year on year, fewer ships making the crossing, and the ships that did come were older hulls, patched and repatched, as though the yards that built them had troubles of their own that the Company's letters never named outright, speaking only, when they spoke of it at all, of difficulties at home.

At Caol leathan the wool piled in the warehouses for want of ships to carry it to the humans' market, and the merchants there, who had for two generations sold nothing but by the Company's weight and at the Company's price, began quietly, against the plain letter of the law, to sell it inland again by barter, the way their grandmothers had sold it before the first ship ever came down from the north. At Caol bheag, on the northern coast, the Company's quay, built at no small cost for a trade in the eastern grain and the eastern cloth that had never, even in its best year, come to half of what had been promised for it, saw one ship in the whole of a season, and the agent stationed there, a solitary man in a solitary house at the edge of a coast that had never wanted him, wrote to Ros dhomhain that he had been forgotten, and got, so far as anyone on the coal road ever heard, no answer at all.

Aonghas heard all this the way he heard most things, from carters and drovers and the occasional Company clerk too far from home and too lonely to keep his own counsel over a second cup. He did not, at the time, think it meant a great deal beyond what it plainly said: that the humans' trade across the sea was thinner than it had been. It was Tormod Buabhall, meeting him again at the horse-fair the autumn the ships had thinned to nothing, who said the thing plainly that Aonghas had only half thought. "A house with no custom left," the old keeper said, turning his glass in his hand, "keeps its gates shut for a different reason than it used to." Aonghas asked him what reason that was, and Tormod said only that a house with nothing worth guarding guards nothing, and a house that guards everything still has, somewhere behind its locked door, something it means to keep.

## VII. Of Leave No Longer Asked

The last of it, for Aonghas, came at Ros dhomhain rather than on the road, where he had gone in the autumn of the hundred and forty-sixth year, on the ordinary business of the house, to buy salt and nails and the small iron things a road-house could not make for its own use. He found himself in the market square, more by chance than by design, on the day the Residency's notice was read aloud from its own steps, as such notices always were, to whatever crowd the harbour cared to send.

It was known already, before the notice, that Charles Merriman had gone. He had held the Commissioner's seat fourteen years, since the year Iain Maor died and his daughter Mòrag took up the staff, and he left now in health too poor, it was given out, for the climate of the island, though there were those in the crowd that day who said it low, meant only for Dia-thìreach ears near them, that a man's health failed faster under a coal country that would not stay still than under any weather the island had ever sent him. In his fourteen years the Administration had begun, by such small and unspoken steps as the walling of a well and the manning of a roof, to give its commands without first troubling to ask the leave of anyone at all, and the pretence of leave asked and given, which had stood since the council's vote in the second year of the strangers, had worn thinner every year.

A stranger read the new notice, a soldier by his bearing and his coat both, unknown to every face in the square, and his name, given at the notice's head, was Lionel Strake. Aonghas listened, as the whole square listened, to the reading of his commission from the Residency's own steps, a long document in the flat unhurried voice the humans kept for such things, naming his powers and his charge and the authority under which he held them. It named the Company. It named the Administration in an Tìr Thall. Nowhere in the whole length of it did it name the Council of Custodians, not once, not even in the old polite formula, *with the leave and counsel of*, that every Commissioner's commission before it, back to the first, had carried as a matter of course.

Aonghas stood in the square and understood, with no one beside him to say it aloud and no need for anyone to, that the pretence was over, let fall quietly with no vote and no battle to mark its falling, simply no longer thought worth the keeping up. He bought his salt and his nails and rode home up the coal road in the dark, past An Sligeanach Fad' às with its lamp lit and its post-bag hanging ready by the door, past the loft at the Reòta where a light showed and went out as he passed, and came at last to his own gate, where he stood a moment before going in, looking back down the road the way a man looks back at a house he has just left for the last time, though he had left nothing, and the house he looked at was only the coal road itself, running east into the dark toward whatever the soldier's commission, read without the council's name in it, was going to mean for all of them next.

He went in, and barred the gate, and did not, that night or for a long while after, write anything at all in the ledger with the soft covers his grandfather had kept.
