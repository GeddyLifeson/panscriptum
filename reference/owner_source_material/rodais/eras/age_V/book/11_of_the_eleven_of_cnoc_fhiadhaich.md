# Of the Eleven of Cnoc fhiadhaich

*AE 25 – 29*

## I. Of the Heading under the Wild Hill

There is a leaf in the roll of Taigh na Fèithe, copied in the cellar at Muileann chaol from a page gone brown at the edges, that holds nothing but eleven names. It has no heading. Each name stands on its own line with the name of a father or a mother after it and the name of a hearth after that, and at the foot, in a smaller hand, the day and the moon and three words for the place: under the wild hill. The letter-books of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach keep the same day in the humans' reckoning, and against it a single line from the agent of A' Chompanaidh at the workings: that a length of gallery had come down in unsound ground, and that the yield of the quarter would be short by so many tons. Between those two pages lies the tale of Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill in the years when the hill first began to take back what was taken from it, and the copyists, who had both before them, set down what the one knew and the other did not.

Raghnall was five-and-thirty in the spring of the twenty-fifth year. He had led a crew at the pit-mouth below Muileann chrom since the first ton was weighed, fourteen years before, and when A' Chompanaidh was sent away from Doire mhòr and opened its new working at Cnoc fhiadhaich instead, a short walk east along the hill, it was his crews it sent there first. He had three crews under him by then, near ninety souls, and a house at the top of the mill street with a byre and a good door, and a wife, Ciorstaidh, who had been a miller's daughter and could reckon faster than the clerk. They had one son, Dòmhnall, called after Raghnall's father, who was five that spring and followed his father to the pit-mouth whenever he was let.

The working at Cnoc fhiadhaich went into the hill on the level from a gully on its southern side, where the seam showed in the bank. The first gallery was the carters' road: a broad drift, high enough for a pony, with rails of oak laid down its floor and props of oak along its sides, square-cut and set in pairs with a cap across. That oak had come up from Cathair fhada the summer before, cartload after cartload, when Henry Maddox bought the woods about that town for the pits. Raghnall's crews had set every pair.

From the head of the drift the new gallery was driven north into the hill after the seam, and there the props stopped.

Maddox had come over the sea in the spring before with a letter that bade him double the yield, and he had read it aloud at the pit-mouth, in his own tongue and then through the young man of Ros dhomhain who put A' Chompanaidh's words into the island's. He was not thirty, with a clean jaw and a quick walk. What he wanted of Cnoc fhiadhaich was the heading: the gallery driven forward fast along the seam, so that the rooms could be opened off it on either hand and the coal come out of the hill from a dozen faces at once. The props, the agent said after him, would follow when the yield allowed. The oak was costly. It had come a long way.

So the heading went north, a yard and a yard and another yard, on the pay of the ton, with bare rock over it.

## II. Of the Colour in the Roof

Raghnall asked for timber the first time at the end of an Gearran. The heading was forty yards beyond the last pair of props, and the roof over it was a grey shale that split in leaves, and in the lamp-light, along the partings where it had begun to open, it had taken on a colour the old hewers had a word for. It was a sick yellow-green, like a bruise going off, or the underside of a toad; and every hewer's son of the hill had been taught to see it and to stop.

He took the agent in to look. The agent held his lamp up to the roof and looked for a long time, conscientiously, and then looked at Raghnall.

"Grey," he said. Then he said in his own tongue that the roof was dry and rang when he knocked it, and was the same rock as in the drift behind. That was true. The rock was the same. The colour was not, and the agent could not see it, any more than Stone and Mercer had seen the green in the sacks, fifteen years before, on the hill above the mill.

Raghnall asked a second time in am Màrt, and a third in the first week of an Giblean, when a leaf of shale the size of a door had come down in the night and been found across the rails in the morning. Ailean mac Dhùghaill, who led the second crew and had been his friend since they were boys on the mill-lade, came with him that time and stood beside him while he asked. The agent wrote the request in his book. He wrote that the crew-leaders wished for timber at the heading, and under it that the timber would be sent up when the next oak came from Cathair fhada, and he read both lines back to them through the young man of Ros dhomhain so that they should know it had been written.

"When is the next oak?" said Ailean.

The young man asked. The agent said the summer.

They went back to work. There was nothing else to do. The rate was on the paper, and the ton was on the beam, and the crews had had three hungry winters since the Law lapsed and the custodians' measure stopped paying; nobody on the hill that spring had a store of meal that would carry a family through a season with no pay. Raghnall told his crews to keep an ear to the roof and to come out if it spoke. Ailean told his the same. They set wedges of their own under the worst of it, split from old hurdles and fence-posts, which the agent did not forbid.

On {{date:IV-0071}} the roof spoke, and nobody came out in time.

It was the middle of the forenoon. Raghnall was at the pit-mouth with the clerk, arguing over a tub that the clerk said was short and he said was full, when the air came out of the drift. It came in one push, like a breath from a man struck in the belly, and it carried a cloud of dust that rolled out over the tipping-floor and went up the gully in the sun, and after it, a long way behind, the sound. The men at the pit-mouth that morning said it was like a door shut in a far room of a great house.

He was in the drift before the dust had settled, with a lamp he did not remember lighting. The props stood, every pair, all the way to the head of the drift. Beyond the last pair there was no gallery. There was a wall of broken shale from floor to roof, and the roof had gone up into a black hollow over it that the lamp would not reach, and from somewhere in the hollow small stones were still coming down, one and then another, with a sound like rain beginning.

## III. Of the Eleven

They dug for three days. Every crew on the hill came, and men from the old cutting, and farmers from the villages who had never been underground in their lives and stood in a line passing baskets back into the drift, and the women at the pit-mouth taking the baskets and tipping them and passing them back. Raghnall would let no one go beyond the last pair of props without a timber over his head. They cut the timber out of the oak rails of the drift, board by board, prising up the carters' road to prop the fall, and the agent saw it and said nothing. On the first night they heard knocking. On the second they did not.

They brought them out one by one, as they were found. Calum mac Eòghainn came first, and his son Anndra, who was fifteen and had been hurrying tubs for his father since the winter, lay a yard behind him with an arm across his back. Then Pàdraig mac Coinnich; Murchadh Liath; Iain Crùbach, who had a bad foot and could hew sitting better than most men standing; Alasdair mac Fhionnlaigh; Lachlann Beag; Fionnlagh mac Sheumais; Mòr nic Artair, who dragged the tubs for the second crew and sang while she did it; Eòghann Donn. Ailean mac Dhùghaill was the last. They found him at the very head of the gallery, where the seam went on into the hill, with his pick under him and one of the wedges he had split from a hurdle still in his hand.

Raghnall carried him out himself. At the pit-mouth there was a crowd, silent, the whole hill; and among them, at the front, a short old man with shoulders like a yoke, who had not come down to the working in fourteen years.

Raghnall's father walked past him to where the eleven were laid out on the tipping-floor under their coats. He stood at their feet and looked at them each in turn. Then he turned to the mouth of the drift, black in the bank, with the dust still hanging in the light at its edge, and he began to say the words that are said at the face before a man puts his pick into the hill. He said them slowly, the whole of them, and the old hewers among the crowd took them up. Nobody had said them at that pit-mouth since the day it was opened. Most of the young men of the crews did not know them to the end, and stood with their mouths moving, a word behind.

When it was done old Dòmhnall looked at his son once, and went home up the hill.

The eleven were buried at Muileann chrom, in the ground above the mill where the hill's dead had always lain, on the fourth day after the fall. The custodians of Taigh na Fèithe came down to them. Seonag Liath was two winters dead, and the cutting was kept now by Marsaili nic Sheumais, a tall grey woman with ink on her fingers; and she stood at the head of the row of graves with the roll open on a board held by a boy, and read the names out, and wrote them in as she read them, each with the father and the mother and the hearth. She asked Raghnall the name of Mòr nic Artair's mother, which she did not know, and he did not know it either, and a woman in the crowd called it out, and Marsaili wrote it.

Then she asked him the name of the place, and he said, "Under the wild hill," and she wrote that.

That leaf is the one the copyists found. The agent's full report was slower. It went down to Ros dhomhain in his hand when the heading had been cleared, and was entered at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir on {{date:IV-0072}}. It named no one. The agent gave the length of the fall in yards and the tons lost from the quarter, and it said that the gallery had been driven in unsound ground, and that the natives had been warned of the roof; and where it spoke of the dead at all, it called them *hands*.

Raghnall heard it read, later, by a carter who had got it from a clerk. He was quiet a long while after. Then he said that the ground had been sound enough when the oak was over it, and the carter, who had not been there, agreed that it likely had.

## IV. Of the Breakers at the Pit-Mouth

The heading was cleared by the midsummer, and timbered as it was cleared, pair by pair, with oak that came up from Cathair fhada in an t-Ògmhios after all; and the yield of the year was short, as the agent had written it would be, and nobody was paid for the days of the digging. That winter the cough came.

It began among the breakers. At every pit-mouth of the hill the coal came out of the drift in the tubs, and was tipped on the floor in the open, and there the children broke it. They sat in rows on low benches under a roof of hurdles, with a hammer each and a heap in front of them, and broke the big coal into the sizes the humans' furnaces wanted, and picked out the stone and the shale, and were paid by the basket. It was a thing that had come in with the ton. In the old cutting the custodians had never let a child near the face until he was old enough to say the words; but the old cutting worked a season in the year, and the pit-mouth worked every day there was light, and there was always more coal on the floor than hands to break it. The families were glad of the pence.

Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill went to the benches in the autumn after the fall, the month he turned six. He asked to go, because the other boys of the mill street went, and Ciorstaidh said he was too small, and Raghnall said no harm could find him on the floor in the daylight with his father fifty yards off. So Dòmhnall went, with a hammer that the smith cut down for him, and came home black every night, and was paid his first pence at the end of the week and gave them to his mother and would not eat his supper for pride.

The dust at the benches was thick on a still day. It hung under the hurdle roof in the low sun like smoke, and the children breathed it all the daylight long, and coughed, and spat black, as the breakers always had. Nobody thought much of that. Then in the new year, on {{date:IV-0073}}, a boy of nine at the Cnoc fhiadhaich benches coughed at his heap until he could not stand, and was carried home, and was dead within the week; and by the end of the month there were twenty coughing at Muileann chrom and Cnoc fhiadhaich, and most of them were breakers.

It was a dry cough. It had no wet in it and no fever, which the women said was the worst kind, because there was nothing to bring up and nothing to break. The fits came without warning, and the child would bend double with it and fight for breath with a noise like a saw in wet wood, and then it would pass, and the child would lie grey and tired and ask for water. Some had it a month and grew well. Some had it a month and did not. The hill called it An Casad Tioram, the dry cough, and had no other name for it, since there was nothing it was like.

Dòmhnall took it in am Màrt.

Raghnall sat with him through nine nights. He had the boy on his knee by the fire, wrapped in a blanket, upright, because lying down the cough was worse; and Ciorstaidh boiled water with pine-needles in it and held the steam to the boy's face, which an old woman of the mill street said would help and which did not. He would go an hour, two hours, sleeping, with his head on his father's chest and a small rattle in him like a stone in a jug, and then he would wake coughing and fight, and Raghnall would hold him and feel the whole of the small body go hard with it.

On the fifth night the surgeon of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise came up from Ros dhomhain, sent by A' Chompanaidh when the agent had counted the empty benches. He was a tired, kindly man who went round the houses of the mill street with a lamp and listened to each child's chest with an ear-trumpet of wood, and told each house through the young man of Ros dhomhain that it was a winter complaint and would pass with the spring. He left a brown bottle in every house that tasted of honey and something bitter. Dòmhnall spat it out.

On the tenth morning Dòmhnall slept through the dawn and woke without coughing, and asked for bread. Raghnall gave it to him, and went out to the byre, and stood with his forehead against the cow's warm flank for a long time before he came in again.

The spring came, as the surgeon had said, and the cough did not pass with it. It went on through an Cèitean, and through the summer, when the dust was dry and blew about the benches all day and the children came home with it in their eyes and hair. The custodians of Taigh na Fèithe asked the agent to shut the benches until the cough was done. He wrote the asking in his book. The benches stayed open, because the furnaces at the pit-mouth burned day and night now to drive the pumps, and wanted broken coal. By the end of an Lùnastal, when the first rains laid the dust and the coughing thinned, thirty-eight were dead at Muileann chrom and Cnoc fhiadhaich, and most of them were boys who broke coal at the pit-mouth, and some were their mothers and fathers, who had nursed them.

Dòmhnall lived. He was thin that year and small the next, and had a catch in his breath when he ran; but he lived, and in the autumn he went back to the benches with his cut-down hammer, and Raghnall let him.

## V. Of the Cart to Cill thais

When the cough was done there were children on the hill with nobody to keep them. Some had lost a father under Cnoc fhiadhaich and a mother to the cough after; some had lost both to the cough; and there were Anndra mac Chaluim's two small sisters, whose father and brother were both in the row above the mill, and whose mother had gone back to her own people at Muileann dhearg and not come for them. The neighbours took them in for the winter, as the hill always had. But the hill that winter was short of meal and shorter of marks, and the neighbours had breakers of their own coughing still, and by the spring of the twenty-seventh year there were fourteen children sleeping in other folk's byres whom nobody could feed through another season.

The word of it went west along the road with the carters. It came, in the way of such things, to the custodians of Cill thais, a large village on damp ground two long days west towards the capital, whose wardens kept the sacred wood; and in an Ògmhios the custodians of Cill thais sent a letter to Taigh na Fèithe to say that they would take the children in and keep a house for them at their own cost, for as long as the children needed a house. The roll of Cill thais sets it down on {{date:IV-0074}}. The custodians of the hill, on the same page of their own roll, asked A' Chompanaidh to give something toward it. A' Chompanaidh sent a crate.

Raghnall drove the cart. Nobody asked him to. He had the loan of the miller's two ponies and the long cart with the hooped cover, and he put straw in it, and blankets, and the crate, and a sack of oatcake that Ciorstaidh baked, and on a grey morning in an t-Iuchar he stood at the top of the mill street while the fourteen were lifted in. The smallest was three. The eldest was Anndra's sister Seasaidh, who was eleven and held the three-year-old on her knee and would not let anyone else take her. Dòmhnall stood at the byre door and watched them go. He had broken coal on the benches beside four of them.

They were two days on the road. The first night they lay at a farm below the watershed, where the farmer's wife brought milk and would take no penny for it. The second day they went down the long road west through the hill country, past the old carters' house at the half-way stage, roofless now, with nettles in its yard and its sign-post leaning. The children looked at everything.

Cill thais was a long street of low houses on a rise above wet meadows, with the dark edge of the sacred wood behind it, and the custodians had a house ready at the end of the street: a long house of stone with a new thatch, and beds of heather and blankets along both walls, and two women of the village to keep it, and a fire lit on the hearth, though it was high summer, because the children would be tired. Taigh nan Dìlleachdan, the village called it, the house of the orphans, and it had no other name. The warden who met the cart was an old man with a staff of black wood, and he lifted each child down himself, and asked each its name and its mother's name, and a girl beside him wrote them.

Then two of the village men took down the crate, and the warden had it opened in the street.

It was full of books. They were small books, bound in grey board, all alike, perhaps a hundred of them, and they were in the humans' letters, and in the humans' tongue; they were the primers from which Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise at Ros dhomhain was teaching the children of the humans' clerks to read. There was a picture of a lamb on the first page of each.

The warden took one out and turned the pages over slowly, the whole book, and looked at the lamb a while. Then he closed it and laid it back in the crate with the others and had the crate carried into the house and set on a shelf.

"They may learn to read them," he said. "Somebody may."

Raghnall drove home with the empty cart. On the long hill up from the half-way stage he stopped the ponies and sat on the tail of the cart for an hour with his head in his hands, and a carter coming down with a load of props from the south asked him if he was ill, and he said no.

## VI. Of the Light on the Eastern Cape

*Here the copyists leave the hills for a while, as the roll of Taigh na Fèithe does, and go east with the carters to the sea.*

In that same summer the second beacon of the island was lit, on the eastern cape at Baile ìseal, where the long slopes of the east run down out of the hills and the land ends in a shelf of black rock over the open water. There had been a fire on that cape since before An Aois Ailein, a driftwood fire in a ring of stones, kept by the households of the coast to bring the boats home in a storm. Twenty years before, Torcall Clachair had walked out to it with James Fenn and built the second of his five cairns above it; and the cairn had stood there, with the white stone on its cap, while the tower at Tobar dhearg went up in the south-west and was lit, and while the town waited for a tower of its own.

A' Chompanaidh came in the end, because its ships had begun to come round the south coast from Ceann leathan and go up the eastern shore, and two of them had touched on the rocks below the cape in the dark. It paid the masons and sent a lamp. It sent no mason of its own. The tower was built by the masons of the eastern coast under Torcall's direction, as the tower at Tobar dhearg had been, of the grey stone of the cape, and its lime was burned in a kiln on the shore with coal carted over the hills from Muileann chrom, a few loads of which, by the tally, had been cut by Raghnall's crews under Cnoc fhiadhaich in the spring of the fall.

Torcall was old by then, near seventy, and his knees had gone. He could not climb the headland. So they carried a chair out for him, a high-backed chair from the custodian's house, and set it at the foot of the headland on the flat turf above the shingle, where he could see the whole height of the tower against the sky; and he sat in it through the whole of a summer and an autumn and most of another summer, with a rug over his knees and a boy beside him to run messages up the path, and a long stick he pointed with. He could see a stone a finger out of true from the foot of the cliff, and he would send the boy up to say so.

The tower was lit on {{date:IV-0075}}. The whole town was on the cape to see it, and the households of the coast for a day's walk round, and the old fire in its ring of stones was lit beside the tower as it always had been, with driftwood. When the lamp caught at the top of the tower and the light went out over the water, the fishermen in the boats below the cape set up a shout; and Torcall, in his chair at the foot of the headland, was seen to take off his cap.

The keepers came from the fishing families. That was the first thing the custodians of Baile ìseal asked for, and they got it: the lamp would be trimmed and the stair climbed by the households who had kept the driftwood fire, and by no one else, and the families drew lots for the watches each Samhain as they had always drawn them for the fire. The second thing they asked for came the next spring, and they got that too, on {{date:IV-0076}}. On the nights the boats went out to the deep grounds beyond the cape the lamp was to be lowered, lest the fish take fright at the great new light on the water and leave the grounds. The rule was kept. When it lapsed no one wrote down.

In the spring after the lighting, when the rule was granted and the keepers were at their watches, Torcall Clachair was put on a cart to go home west. A mile out of Baile ìseal, where the road tops the first rise of the moor and a traveller going east sees the tower for the first time over the heather, he had the carter stop. He had himself lifted down, and sat by the road on a stone, and had the boy bring him stones from the ditch, one by one, and laid them himself with his own hands on the turf beside him in a small cairn, no higher than his knee, each stone on two below it and a flat one for a cap. It took him the morning. Then he was lifted back onto the cart, and went home, and built no more. The cairn is there by the road, the carters say, and they put a stone on it when they pass.

## VII. Of the Offer and the Huts at the Ford

In the hills the yield of A' Chompanaidh was short three years together. Maddox's letter had bidden him double it, and it had not doubled. It had gone down.

The reason was not hard to find. The crews of the hill were hill men. They had fields, most of them, or their fathers had, small fields of oats and barley in the valley bottoms and on the lower slopes, and a few beasts on the hill; and when the harvest came in an Lùnastal they went home to cut it, as their grandfathers had gone home from the old cutting when its season was done. They had always done so. The contract said nothing against it. The first summer after the fall, a third of the crews went home to the harvest and stayed there through the autumn; the summer after the cough, it was half. Men who had carried their friends out of the heading, or buried a child from the benches, found they could do without the pence. The agent wrote it in his book.

In the spring of the twenty-ninth year he sent for Raghnall.

He received him in the new office A' Chompanaidh had built at the pit-mouth, a room of dressed stone with a glass window and a stove in it that burned the hill's coal. The agent had been at the hill five years and had some Dia-thìris now, and he did not send for the young man of Ros dhomhain. He gave Raghnall a chair, which Raghnall had not had in that room before, and a cup of something hot and brown that the humans drank, and then he made his offer.

Raghnall's crews were the best on the hill, the agent said; everyone knew it; and Raghnall was the man the hill listened to, as the agent had seen at the fall. A' Chompanaidh would pay him, over and above the rate of the ton, a leader's share on every ton his three crews brought up, paid to him and not to the crews, if he would keep them at the face through the harvest and the winter together. The share was written on a paper, and the agent turned it round on the table so that Raghnall could see the figure, and Raghnall looked at it for a long time.

Then the agent said the other thing, in the same voice. A' Chompanaidh had been buying the foreshores of the coast for its quays, he said, at Ceann fhada and elsewhere, and a good many fishing families of the coast had nails and no foreshore now, and would come up to the pits for pay if there was a roof for them. There was talk at Ros dhomhain of building one. If the hill men would not keep the face through the year, men from the coast would; and there would be no need then of a leader's share.

Raghnall said he would think on it. The agent said, of course.

He thought on it a month, and told nobody what he thought. The letter-books keep the paper, with his mark at the foot of it and a date in an Cèitean. He told Ciorstaidh the figure, and she reckoned it out on the table with beans, in marks and in meal, the way the mill had taught her, and it came to more than a hewer of the old cutting had seen in ten seasons. He did not tell his father. Old Dòmhnall heard it at the mill a week after, and came to the door of the house at the top of the mill street, and did not come in; he stood on the step and looked at his son, and at his grandson behind him, who was nine now and black from the benches, and then went back up the hill. They spoke after that of the weather, and of the boy.

So Raghnall kept his crews at the face through the harvest of the twenty-ninth year. He did it by going to each man's door, and sitting at each man's fire, and saying what the agent had said about the coast; and most of them heard him out and stayed, because he was Raghnall, who had led the first thirty and carried Ailean out of the heading, and because a man who goes home to his oats and finds his place at the face given to a stranger has neither oats enough nor a face. The yield of the year came up. The leader's share was paid on the first market-day after, to the penny.

And the coast men came anyway.

That same season the traders' boats of the new quay at Ceann òg, on the Abhainn ìseal, had begun to run their long lane round the capes to the capital's water, down the river past Cnoc bheag to the north coast and west along it, and round the western capes and south, the lane the boatmen called Seòlaid Ceann òg; and in the autumn A' Chompanaidh used the lane the other way about. It gathered the men of the foreshores at the capital's quays and shipped them in the traders' boats round the western capes and east along the north coast and up the Abhainn ìseal to the quay at Ceann òg, and they walked the last long day over the hills with their bundles. They were fishers and net-menders and the sons of salters, and they had never been under the ground. They came in companies of twenty, with a clerk of A' Chompanaidh in front and their wives and children behind.

For them A' Chompanaidh built the camp at Àth ìseal, a few miles north-west of Cnoc fhiadhaich, at the low ford below the new workings where the ferrymen of the hill had always put the carts across in the wet season. It was built in a month, of sawn plank on sills of stone, in rows: long huts with a door at each end and a stove in the middle, and a store at the head of the rows, and a well. There was no field and no byre and no burial ground, and no shrine. The roll of Taigh na Fèithe enters it on {{date:IV-0077}} with a note in Marsaili nic Sheumais's hand that it had no custodian and had asked for none; and it was the first settlement on the island that had been made for nothing but the pit.

Raghnall walked over to see it on the evening the first crews moved in. It was raining. He stood on the rise above the ford with his hood up, where the ferrymen's path comes down, and looked at the three long huts with the smoke of their stoves going up flat into the rain, and the rows marked out in pegs beyond them for more. A man came out of the end of the nearest hut with a bucket and went to the well. He was a broad man with the rolling walk of a boat, and he stopped at the well and looked up at the hill to the east, where the workings were, and the heather going up black into the cloud; and he stood a long while at the well with his bucket, looking at it, as a man looks at a sea he does not know.

Raghnall did not go down to him. After a time he turned and walked home along the hill in the dark, past the mouth of the working at Cnoc fhiadhaich, where the drift stood dark under its oak, and down into Muileann chrom, where Ciorstaidh had kept his supper warm on the hearth over a fire of the hill's coal.
