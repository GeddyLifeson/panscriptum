# Of Gorta Baile chrom

*AE 94 – 97*

## I. Of the Empty Road

Uisdean mac Lachlainn kept the first daybook of Taigh-solais Baile chrom, and its first pages are given over to nothing but weather and wicks: so many hours burning, so much oil drawn, the wind from such a quarter. He had asked for the keeping himself, the year the ring was finished, on the notion that a keeper who wrote nothing down could never answer for what he had seen. He did not, that first winter, know what he would be asked to answer for.

In am Màrt of the ninety-fourth year he began to write other things.

The tower stood on the headland above Baile chrom, a climb of a hundred and nine steps he had counted more than once in the dark, and its gallery looked two ways: out over the water where the light he tended went round all night for ships that were not his, and inland over the fields that fed the town. That spring he stood his morning watch at the rail and saw a farmer he knew, a man called Alasdair Ruadh, walk the length of his own ploughland with a hand-harrow and no one behind him. The year before there had been six at that work, sons and cousins and a hired man from the shore. Uisdean wrote in the daybook: *Alasdair Ruadh ploughs alone. No hands for the seed but his.* It was not a thing a keeper of lights was asked to note. He noted it anyway.

The hands had gone to the pits two years before, when An Togail came down from Ros dhomhain and was read out at the market cross in the humans' tongue and then again, badly, in Dia-thìris by a clerk who stumbled over the numbers: one in five of every village's grown men and women, for two years, to work under the Company, paid in marks at the new rate. It had fallen on the coast and the hill villages round Baile chrom harder than anywhere else, for the concession farms to the north, worked by the strangers' own people, sent no one. Uisdean had watched the first draft go down the coast road with its bundles on its backs, twenty-three from a town of a few hundred, and had thought, climbing the tower that night, that it was a hard thing but a passing one, the kind of hardship a people bears and is done with.

He had not thought, that first year, to ask who would plough.

The draft had been read at the market cross of Baile chrom on a grey morning with the herring boats still out, and Uisdean, who had come down from the tower to hear it as everyone came down to hear it, remembered the silence after the clerk read the number, before the names themselves began. It fell hardest, the reading made plain, on the villages of the coast and the hills behind it; the farms of the concession to the north, worked by the strangers' own people under their own charter, sent no one, and a fisherman at Uisdean's elbow had said, low, that a law was a strange kind of net that always seemed to draw its catch from the same water. No one answered him. The clerk went on reading.

By the second spring the fields round Baile chrom stood half sown, and the ones that were sown were thin, for there were not enough hands to weed them either, and by midsummer the grain that stood was short in the ear and beaten flat in two storms that in another year would have cost a farmer a week's worry and no more. The harvest came in light. Uisdean watched the carts go up to the granary and counted them against the carts of the year before, which he had also, without meaning to, kept a tally of, and the count came out at less than half.

The winter after was hard. The one after that was worse, for it came on a country that had eaten its own reserve to get through the first.

He wrote, on a night in am Faoilleach of the ninety-fourth year, with the wind driving spray to the top of the tower and the lamp burning steady behind its glass: *Nothing crosses the road below tonight. Not a cart, not a dog. The town is shut against the weather, or shut against something else.* He read the line back before he blew out the small lamp at the desk, and thought it was not a keeper's business to write such things, and left it standing.

## II. Of the Bounds of the Hunger

The custodians who kept the count called it, before the year was out, Gorta Baile chrom, and gave it bounds: thirty-four settlements from the coast to the hill road east of the concession, worst at the centre and thinning at the edges, as a fire thins at the edge of what it burns.

At Doire ghlas, north along the coast, the hunger took the old first and then the very young, as hunger does when there is grain enough for one meal in three and a household must choose who eats it. The custodian of Doire ghlas, a woman whose name the roll gives and this telling need not repeat, walked from door to door in the last weeks of that winter and wrote down who still stood and who did not, and in the spring she sent the town's young people south to the camps for the ration the Company gave its crews, which was more certain than anything the fields could promise that year.

A woman of the town called Mòr nic Aonghais, whose son was fourteen and had never worked a day underground, walked with him as far as the hill road and no further, because the custom of the coast held that a mother did not follow her child past the boundary stone. She had baked him what bread the house could spare that morning and wrapped it in a cloth that had been her own mother's, and told him to mind the overseers and to write, though there was no one at Doire ghlas any longer who could read a letter to her but the custodian, and the custodian had her own dead to see to. Mòr stood at the boundary stone a long time after her son had gone out of sight round the bend of the road, and then she walked home to a house with one mouth fewer in it and no more grain in the store than it had had that morning. By the year's end Doire ghlas held half its former number, though half of those who were gone had only gone south and would come back, in their own time, to fields that would need them more than ever.

At Inis mhin on the north coast the second winter of the hunger found the town with seed corn in its store and nothing else, and the choice a coastal town makes in that pass is older than any custodian's roll: they ate the seed, and trusted to the fish and to what charity came, and did not plant that spring at all. The Mission's kitchen at the harbour, which had stood empty three parts of the year since it was built, fed three hundred a day the whole of that season, fish broth and biscuit brought round by sea from Cathair gheal, and the harbour-master's wife, who kept the tally at the kitchen door, told Uisdean once, years after, that she had never in her life counted so high a number so many days running and never wanted to again.

At Seann Bhrenn, east of the hunger's worst ground but inside its bounds all the same, the custodians did a thing that had not been done in living memory: they opened the grain-pits of the Age of Sinnsearachd, sunk in the chalk hill above the town in a time so old that no one now living had seen them opened, kept against a need no one now living had thought would come. The lids were levered up with bars, and the whole town stood round the mouths of the pits in the rain while the custodians went down on ropes with lamps, and came up again with nothing but the dust of grain a hundred generations gone, and a smell that the older people said was the smell of the deep earth, and no comfort in it at all. An old man of the town, who had come to watch with the rest, said afterward that he had always been told the pits held enough to see Seann Bhrenn through any winter the sky could send, and that it had never once crossed his mind, in seventy years of hearing it told, to ask through how many winters that telling had already worn thin.

Uisdean heard these things as a keeper of lights hears most things, from the boats that put in below his tower and the carters who climbed to see the lamp lit and to ask, always, whether the weather would hold. He wrote what he was told into the daybook beside the weather and the oil, because he had begun, without deciding to, to think that a record which held only the light and not what the light shone over was no record worth the keeping.

## III. Of the Ship at Ros dhomhain

Word came to the island, that same spring, that a ship of grain was crossing from an Tìr Thall, sent by the Church's own people who had heard, from the letters of their Mission, what was happening on the coal coast. It reached Ros dhomhain in am Cèitean and lay at anchor in the roads a full month with its holds unopened, for the Residency held that unloading and carriage were the Company's charge, being the party whose Levy had emptied the fields, and the Company held that famine relief was the Administration's office and none of its own, being no party to the failure of a harvest it had not sown or reaped.

Anne Colby was in Ros dhomhain that spring, back on the island after some years at Cathair gheal, and she went down to the harbour more than once to look at the ship riding at her cable with the gulls working the water round her and her crew idle at the rail. She had nursed the flux on those same quays years before and thought she had seen the worst that argument over a ledger could do to people who had none of the say in it, and found she had not. The ship's master, a quiet man who had made the crossing twice before with cargo the Administration wanted badly enough to have it off his decks within a day, told her once, leaning on the rail with nothing better to do, that he had never in thirty years at sea been kept waiting so long for a cargo nobody seemed to want unloaded at all.

She wrote to the Residency herself, once, a short letter asking only who should be told to unload the ship, and had no answer for eleven days, and when the answer came it named no one and settled nothing, and only said that the matter was under consideration between the two offices, and that Commissioner Tolley had noted her concern. She kept the letter folded small in the same case she carried her lancets in, as though it were a kind of instrument too, and showed it to no one else at all.

On the last day of that month she went down to the harbour again and found the grain still standing in the holds and the town's own children, thin already though the true want had barely begun, playing at soldiers on the quay below the idle ship. It was then, she said afterward, that she stopped writing letters and began instead to walk from office to office in person, since a woman standing in a doorway, she found, could not be answered with a note passed to a clerk.

The ship was unloaded at last, in am Ògmhios, when a clerk at the Residency and a clerk of the Company between them, and neither by any order from above, simply set men to the holds one morning without waiting for either office to finish arguing, and the grain went out by cart in the following weeks to the kitchens the Mission had by then opened at Cathair gheal and at Baile chrom. Anne Colby went north to Baile chrom herself to run the one there, since she knew the coast and the coast, by then, knew her a little.

## IV. Of the Kitchen Below the Tower

The kitchen at Baile chrom stood in what had been a net-shed by the harbour, its floor still smelling of tar under the smell of the pot, and Anne Colby kept it through the worst of that summer and into the next winter with whatever the relief ship's grain and the Mission's own store could supply, stretched with fish when the boats could get out and with nothing at all on the days they could not.

Uisdean came down from the tower most evenings that he was not on watch. The light did not need him at the kitchen door; he came because he found he could not climb his own stair afterward without having seen, once in the day, what the town below him looked like close to. He carried water for her sometimes, and split wood, and once carried a child too weak to walk the length of the quay on his own back, a boy of six or seven whose name he never learned and whose face he did not forget.

The kitchen kept no fixed hour, for the boats' luck and the cart's arrival from Cathair gheal governed it more than any bell could, and there were mornings the queue at the net-shed door stretched past the fish-curers' sheds and mornings it was three old women and no more, the young and the middling having gone, by then, south with the rest. Anne Colby ladled with her own hand at the start and end of every serving, and left the middle of it to whoever she could find to trust with the pot, saying that a person who has served the first bowl and the last knows the whole of a day's want, and everything between is only arithmetic. Uisdean thought, watching her, that she had the custodians' own habit of keeping a roll, though hers was written in faces rather than in ink, and that she seemed to hold every one of them in her memory at once, the way he held the wind and the oil.

Anne Colby knew the crews of the coast by the custodians' names before she knew them by their faces, having learned long before, in another town in another sickness, that a name written correctly in a ledger was sometimes the only thing a dying stranger was given that was truly theirs. She kept a private book of her own at the kitchen, apart from the Mission's, with every name she fed written once, and crossed through, gently, in the corner of the page, whenever one of them did not come again.

Uisdean asked her once, standing at the kitchen door with the light already lit behind them on the headland, whether she thought the Company knew what its Levy had done to the ploughing.

"They know," she said. "They have the figures. I have seen the figures."

"Then why does nothing change?"

She looked past him, up at the tower, where the beam was already sweeping its slow circle over the black water. "Because the figures they keep," she said, "are the figures for the ships, and the ships are not short of anything."

He had no answer to that, and did not try to find one.

## V. Of the Light He Did Not Put Out

There came a night in that second winter, the coldest of the two, when Uisdean climbed the hundred and nine steps with the wind trying to take the door out of his hand at every landing, and stood a long while at the lamp with his hand on the valve that fed it, and thought, not for the first time and not lightly, of turning it down and letting it go dark.

It was not a thing done from spite, or not only from spite. He had watched, that whole two years, coal go out past his light by the shipload, to hearths and forges over the sea that had never gone hungry a day in their history, while the fields that grew the food for the men who cut that coal lay half worked for want of the hands the coal trade had taken from them. It seemed to him, some nights, a plain enough account: that his light served the taking, and nothing of what it served came back. If the lamp went dark an hour, a night, a week, some ship somewhere would run onto the reef his own grandfather's generation had asked the beacon built against, and the Company's trade would falter, and perhaps, in some way he could not trace from the top of a tower in the dark, the fields would be given back their hands the sooner for it.

He stood with his hand on the valve a long time. The wind came in under the door and made the lamp gutter, then steady, the way it had steadied every night since the ring was finished, keeping its rhythm the way the tide keeps its own regardless of who is drowning or who is fed.

He thought of the nine drowned on the western reef, buried long before his time, whom the beacon had first been asked for. He thought of Alasdair Ruadh walking his empty field with the hand-harrow. Torcall Clachair had built the first of the ring and never lived to see it whole, walking the headlands once with a human navigator, in the years before either of them had reason to think the light they were planning would ever serve anyone's harm. A light built for the drowned did not choose afterward whose ships it saved, any more than a well chose whose bucket drew from it, and a keeper who let it go dark to punish one use of it would be punishing every other use along with it, the fishing boats and the coasting trade and whatever, on some night he could not foresee, might come up that coast needing it for a reason he would never be told. A ship wrecked on the rocks below would drown men who had no more say in the Levy than Alasdair Ruadh had, deckhands and boys signed on for their bread; the coal in her hold would go down with her unburned and change nothing at all in the fields above Baile chrom. The light he kept was near the wrong, he decided at last, standing there with the wind working at the door. It was not the cause of it, the way a man standing near a fire is not the fire.

He took his hand off the valve, and wrote in the daybook, in a hand steadier than his thoughts had been: *Wind north-west, fresh. Lamp trimmed at the second hour. Burning well.* Then, under it, because he had begun to think a record that held only the light was no record at all: *Thought tonight of letting it go dark, and did not. I do not know that I was right. I know only that I could not answer, after, for the ones it would have drowned.*

## VI. Of the Order That Came Too Late

An Togail was set aside in the famine districts in the summer of the ninety-seventh year, two years after the harvest that had shown what it cost, and the order was read at the market cross of Baile chrom on a morning in am Lùnastal with the same clerk who had read it out first standing in the same place to read that it was ended.

There was no crowd of the size that had gathered for its beginning. Those who came stood in the sun that had, by then, given the coast two good harvests running, and heard that the villages of the famine country need send no more of their hands to the pits, and some of them nodded, and some of them looked at the empty houses along the street, shut up since their people had gone south and not all of them come back, and said nothing at all.

Uisdean stood at the back of the small crowd that morning, having come down from the tower to hear it read, and thought that a law lifted two years after the harvest it had ruined was a strange kind of mercy, offered to fields that had already, by their own hard way, worked out how to feed themselves without the hands the Levy had never meant to give back. He wrote the day into the daybook when he climbed the stair that evening, between the wind and the oil, in the same plain hand he kept for everything: *The Levy read ended at Baile chrom this day. Two harvests too late for those it took the hardest. The custodians say it was never repealed, only set by, and that no one here expects to hear it named again. The coast has learned by now what such a word is worth.*

Cnoc mhòr, in the north, past the worst edge of the famine's bounds, had not waited for any order to reach it either way. Its people had watched two winters take what the fields could not replace, and in the spring of the ninety-seventh year, before the order at Baile chrom was even drafted, the whole of the village walked south together, the old carried and the young walking, to take the Company's ration and the Company's work rather than starve waiting for a harvest that might fail a third time. A carter who passed through some weeks after told the kitchen at Baile chrom that he had walked the length of the street at Cnoc mhòr and found every door shut but none of them barred, in the way of a people who mean to come back, and a cat sitting on a doorstep that had watched him the whole way through without moving, as though it alone had been left to keep the place. The grass came up in the street the first summer, and it was a long time, by every account that reached the tower at Baile chrom, before Cnoc mhòr stood full again.

Uisdean climbed to the gallery that last evening of the chapter's telling, as he had climbed it near every evening for four years, and lit the lamp, and stood at the rail while the beam went out over water that had drowned no one that season, over a coast where the fields, thin as they were, stood green again. Below him, past the last houses, the road that had carried away Cnoc mhòr's people and Doire ghlas's and so many others lay empty in the last light, the way it had lain empty the whole two years of the hunger, and would, he thought, lie empty a good while yet before it carried anyone home.
