# Of the Ledgers at the Adit

*Part Three: Of Silver, Steam and the Green Death*

*AE 42 – 45*

> *Chan eil an t-airgead naomh.*
> Silver is not holy.

## I. Of the Bound Book

Of all the hands in the letter-books of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, the copyists of the cellar learned Arthur Lisle's first. It is small and level and leans a little backward, like a man reading with his chair tipped from the table, and it never blots. He came to the island in the summer of the forty-first year of the strangers, a lawyer of forty-one, thin, clean-shaven, with a bad knee that he favoured on stairs, and he brought with him two clerks, a chest of books, and no letter from A' Chompanaidh promising him a share of anything. Henry Maddox had gone home rich by the same ship.

His first order as An t-Àrd-mhaor was that every agreement made between the island and the strangers since the day of the leave should be found, copied fair and bound in one book. The two clerks were at it all that winter in the long room over the stair of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, with the rain on the glass. They copied Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg and Puingean a' Chalaidh; the contracts of the pits one by one, with the crew-leaders' names spelled as the humans had heard them; the grants of foreshore at Ceann fhada, paid in nails by weight; the leases of the concession grazing; the orders about the hawk-priests; and at the back, in a separate gathering, Cairt na Companaidh. The charter had been made over the sea between A' Chompanaidh and its own masters, before any Dia-thìreach had seen it, and it bound nobody on the island; Lisle had it bound in all the same, because he wanted it where he could lay it beside the rest.

When the book came back from the binder at Ros dhomhain, in calf with brass at the corners, he read it through with a pen in his hand. The letter-books keep his notes on it, forty pages of them. Where the humans took something that no page gave them, he wrote *on what ground?* in the margin. Where the Dia-thìrich paid something that no page asked of them, he wrote *why do they pay?* He was a hard man and meant the island no particular kindness; but he could not bear an account that did not balance, and the first account he found out of balance was the northern coast.

The coal ships of A' Chompanaidh went home north-about, past the north cape, and along that coast lay fishing towns that had never been asked for a penny of harbour-due. Their boats went out to the coal ships as they passed and sold them fish and water and fresh greens, and took marks for it, and nobody had told them they owed any share of the marks to anyone. Lisle looked for the page that made them owe it and found none. So he made one. On {{date:IV-0100}} a customs house was opened at Ros gheal on the northern coast, in a hired loft by the fish-steps, with a table, a book, a brass scale and an officer from the harbour office at Ros dhomhain to keep them.

Ros gheal was a hawk town, and had been since the pupils of Gormshuil founded the second house of Òrd an t-Seabhaig there in An Aois Scaraidh. Its priests had kept the measured burning for all the country up the river to Cnoc bheag while Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig was still kept, and the fishing families there held that what the sea gave, the sea gave to the hand that went out for it. The officer had been in his loft eleven days when the hawk-faithful came down from the hawk-house at dusk, some thirty of them, fishermen and their wives and a priest with his hood up. They carried out the table with the book still open on it and set it down in the street. They carried out the scale and the officer after it. Then they walked him, not unkindly, to the last house on the road south and left him there with his book under his arm.

He came back in a month with a letter from Lisle and two clerks to bear him company, and he kept the loft through the summer. At the herring in the autumn the town put him in a boat with his table, his scale and his clerks and rowed him two miles down the coast and set him ashore on a beach with no road. The roll of Ros gheal says only that the officer "went away by water." The letter-book says a good deal more. Lisle wrote under the report, in his level hand, that the town had broken no page, since no page yet bound the town, and that he would write one.

The news went east along the carriers' roads, as news did, and came up the valley to Muileann chiar at the back end of the year, told at the smelting-floors by a carter with his hands held out to the heat. Ùna nic Choinnich heard it standing in the door of the guild hall with a ledger under her arm. The carter told it for a laugh, the fishwives and the table in the street, and the men at the floors laughed. Ùna did not.

"He reads," she said. "That one reads what he signs. Mark him."

## II. Of the Silver Town

Muileann chiar lay in the far east of the hills, on a shelf of the valley under the moor, a day's walk above the southern sea and an hour's walk from the old walls of Talla na Lasrach at Dùn ìseal. It was a small town, a few score households in stone under turf and slate, with the adit in the hillside above the last houses and the smelting-floors below them by the stream, and in those years it was the only place on the island where the smoke that went up from the work was not the smoke of coal. The guild burned charcoal from the eastern woods and peat from the moor, as the first silver-workers had done. There was a sweet, sharp smell over the whole valley that the carters said they could taste in their bread for a day after.

The silver had been found in An Aois Naomh, near the great vein but apart from it, and the first question anyone had asked of it was whether it belonged to the mountain. Muireach Liath answered it then. Silver shows no colour when it breaks, he said; it breaks grey and bright and the same all through, like water; and so it is not the mountain's. From those words Muileann chiar had been let dig as deep as it liked. From those words, too, every quarrel over the silver had begun. When Ruairidh Liath demanded one ring in five, Sliochd Fhearchair shut the workings for three seasons sooner than pay, and the road-guards went unpaid; that was Falachd an Airgid, in which nobody died, and which Coimheadaiche na Lasrach at Dùn ìseal settled by giving each side half of what it asked. The weights of Muileann chiar had been the lawful weights of every road on the island since that settlement. Twice in An Aois Scaraidh Clann Choinnich had asked the queen's council for leave to burn coal beyond the measured handful for the smelt, since the coal burns hotter than charcoal; and twice the council had refused, the second time by one voice. The last asking still lay in Taigh na Fèithe, unanswered, when the strangers came.

Ùna was of Clann Choinnich by her father and of the refiners' families by her mother, and she had been head of Comann an Airgid for eleven years. She was fifty-seven in the winter this telling begins: a short square woman with a burn-scar up the inside of one forearm, grey hair plaited and pinned flat so that it would not catch, and a voice that did not rise. As a girl of twenty she had carried the guild's rings down to the humans' counter at Ros dhomhain in the years of fair exchange, and she had learned their tongue across Pike's counter, word by word, the way the carters learned it; and later, in a winter when the snow shut the valley, a clerk of A' Chompanaidh who was lodging in the town had paid for his board by teaching her the humans' letters. She read them slowly. She read everything slowly, and forgot none of it.

The guild kept two ledgers, and they were the pride of the town.

The older was hardly a book at all. It was a stack of thin boards of ash, each notched along one edge in the stag-letters that the teachers of the grove had made out of the cutters' tally-marks at Muileann chiar long ago, bound between two planks with thongs through holes at the corners. In it was set down every member of the guild since the first, the name and the house and the year of taking-on, and every gallery driven into the seam, and who had driven it, and how far. The newer ledger was a book of the island's own paper in the island's own letters, begun in An Aois Scaraidh and kept since in a dozen hands. In it were the weighings: every bar and every ring that went out of Muileann chiar, to whom, and for what, down to the last ring traded for salt fish. Between them the two ledgers held the whole life of the seam from An Aois Naomh to that winter, and in all their boards and leaves there was not one word of the humans' tongue.

On {{date:IV-0101}} a rider came up the valley in sleet with a letter in a leather case. It was from the agent of A' Chompanaidh at Muileann chrom, and it was written in the humans' tongue with a Dia-thìris version under it in a clerk's cramped hand. The agent gave notice that A' Chompanaidh, holding under its charter the first refusal of "all fuels, ores and minerals the island may yield," would send its surveyors to the silver seam at Muileann chiar with the spring, to measure the workings and value the yield, so that the silver might be brought under the same terms as the coal.

Ùna read it at her own table by the fire, first the Dia-thìris and then the humans' version, and then the humans' version again, with her finger under the words. The rider stood dripping by the door. When she had done she folded the letter along its creases and gave him a bowl of broth, and asked him whether he had come by the Muileann chrom road or the moor road, and how the fords were running. Then she sent her grandson round the houses to call the guild.

They came to the hall in the dark, forty-one of them, in their work-clothes with the ash still on them, and sat on the benches along the walls under the hanging scales. Ùna read the letter to them in both tongues. Nobody spoke for a while.

"The charter is theirs," said Seumas mac Fhearchair at last. He was the oldest refiner in the valley, seventy-three that winter, and his house had worked the seam longer than any. "They wrote it for themselves. What has it to do with us?"

"Nothing," said Ùna. "So they will say it has everything, until someone shows them where it stops."

"And who will show them?"

"The council," said a young man by the door, and a few of the others laughed, not kindly. The council at Cathair dhearg had been shown a good many things in forty years and had filed most of them.

Ùna let them laugh. "I will go and ask it," she said, "and I will see what it has in its chest."

## III. Of the Council's Copies

She rode west in the second week of am Faoilleach with her grandson Fionnlagh, who was sixteen and had never been further than Dùn ìseal, and a pack-pony with oats and a sack of silver rings for the road. It was fifty miles and more to the capital. They went by the moor road to Muileann chrom, where the smoke of A' Chompanaidh's furnaces lay along the valley so thick that the boy put his sleeve over his mouth, and then down Rathad na Mèinne, cart-ruts frozen hard as iron, among the long lines of coal-carts going west. It took them three days. At the inns the carters talked of the Ros gheal fishwives, and the tale grew every night.

Coinneach Glas received her in the room behind the council hall, where the custodians' rolls were kept in presses along the walls. He was Ceann a' Chùraim, and had been since the death of Tormod mac Ailein twenty-three years before; he had come to the seat at forty-five and was sixty-eight that winter. He had never set eyes on a human before An t-Aiseag, the council's clerks said, and had dealt with little else since. His hair and beard had gone the grey that gave him his byname long before he was old, and now his skin was going the same way. He had a cough that he did not trouble to hide, and a brazier by his chair, and a rug over his knees. He heard her through without a word, and then sat for a long time looking at the brazier.

"I asked them to keep the tally," he said. "In the twenty-third year. I asked them to burn no more coal in a year than the hill could give. They sent me a copy of their accounts." He coughed. "It was a very fine copy."

"I have not come for the council to write to them," said Ùna.

"No. I did not think so." He looked at her properly then. "What have you come for?"

"Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg," she said. "And whatever else was signed. The council's own copies."

The clerk who kept the presses made a small noise, and Coinneach Glas lifted one hand without looking round.

"They are in the humans' tongue," he said. "Only in that."

"I can read the humans' tongue."

He had the clerk bring them: Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg on four leaves of heavy paper, sewn with green thread, with Tormod mac Ailein's mark at the foot and Josiah Pell's name beside it in a great looping hand; and Puingean a' Chalaidh, which two custodians had refused to sign; and a bundle of the pit contracts tied with tape. No promise was asked of her that she would bring them back. Coinneach Glas only said, as she was wrapping them in oilcloth for the road, that nobody in that room had read them through in a long while.

"Look for the silver," he said. "If it is there, better you find it than they do."

She read them at the inns going home, by rushlight, at the end of the long tables, with the carters singing at the other end and Fionnlagh asleep with his head on his arms. She read them the way she read a ledger, a line at a time, and when a word was strange to her she wrote it on a scrap of paper and asked the next human she met what it meant, which on that road was never long. The leaves of Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg spoke of the humans' leave to stay and to build, of the post and the harbour, of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach and the council and how each should send to the other. It spoke of the dues. It spoke of the coal, and of how the coal should be cut and paid for, at a length that surprised her. Puingean a' Chalaidh spoke of anchorages and moorings and nothing else. The contracts spoke of tons.

On the third night, at the inn below Muileann chrom, she laid the leaves down on the table and put her hand flat on them.

"What is it?" said Fionnlagh, who had woken.

"There is no silver in it," said Ùna. "No metal in it at all but the iron they paid with. Coal on every page, and not a word about anything else under the ground."

"Then they cannot have it."

"Then they have no paper that gives it to them," said his grandmother. "Which is a smaller thing. Go to sleep."

## IV. Of the Ledgers at the Adit

The surveyors came up the valley on {{date:IV-0102}}, the last day of am Màrt, in a thin cold sunshine after a week of rain. There were five of them, the chief surveyor on a grey horse and the rest on foot, with two carts of chains, pegs, staves and a tripod in a canvas bag, and a Dia-thìreach of Muileann chrom walking at the horse's head to speak for them. The town saw them from a mile off. Nobody went down to meet them.

Ùna had made her plan in the hall two nights before, and it was short. Every member of the guild would be at the adit at the hour the carts came up, man and woman and apprentice, in the leather aprons of the smelting-floors, and every one of them would hold their tongue. Seumas mac Fhearchair and old Mòr nic Fhearchair, the two eldest, would hold the ledgers. Ùna would speak, and nobody else.

"And if they will not go?" somebody asked.

"Then we will all be standing at the adit," said Ùna, "and they will have to measure round us."

When the carts came up past the last houses the whole membership of Comann an Airgid was already there, filling the yard in front of the adit and the slope above it, forty-one people in leather aprons standing in the sun. Seumas and Mòr stood at the front, one on each side of Ùna, and each held a ledger open across the forearms, the way a priest holds a book at a rite: Seumas the notched boards of the old ledger, strapped open at the first board, and Mòr the paper book of the weighings, open at the page where the old brown hands of An Aois Scaraidh gave way to the hands of the present. Behind them the black mouth of the adit breathed out its cold. Down by the stream the smelting-floors were banked but not dead, and a thread of smoke went straight up from them into the still air.

The chief surveyor was a big, red-faced man in a good coat, with a clerk's case on a strap across his chest. He got down from his horse and looked at the yard full of silent people, and then at the woman in the middle of it, and he did not seem to know which to speak to. The man from Muileann chrom said something to him low. He took a paper from his case and began to read out his authority.

It was a long paper and he read all of it, in the humans' tongue, in a loud flat voice as if reading to a crowd that could not hear: the charter, the first refusal, the fuels, ores and minerals, the seal of An t-Àrd-mhaor, the agent's instructions, the rights of entry and of measurement. The man from Muileann chrom tried to put it into Dia-thìris and fell further behind at every line. The yard stood and listened. Nobody moved. A dog barked somewhere down in the town and was hushed.

Ùna let him finish. Then, when he had folded his paper, she turned toward him the first ledger, the notched boards in Seumas's arms, and after it the second, the paper book in Mòr's; and she said to him, in his own tongue and quite pleasantly, "These are the books of the seam since it was found. Find me your name in either of them."

He looked at her. Then, because he could think of nothing else to do, and perhaps because he was an honest man in his way, he looked at the books.

He could not read the boards at all, and she did not help him. He looked a long time at the paper book, turning the leaves back and back with one thick finger while Mòr held it steady, through the weighings of the last season and the season before, through the rings for salt and the bars for bronze and the weights sent to the inns of the roads, back into hands so old that the ink had gone brown. The yard waited. The wind came down off the hill with the smell of the smelting-floors in it, charcoal and hot stone and the sharp sweet smell of the silver, and moved the hair on forty-one heads and the pages of the book.

After a while the surveyor closed his own book, the one in his case that he had been ready to write the seam into. He said something short to the man from Muileann chrom, and turned his horse, and the carts creaked round in the yard; and the men of A' Chompanaidh went back down the valley the way they had come, with their chains and pegs and their tripod still in its bag.

Nobody in the yard spoke until they were out of sight below the last houses. Then Mòr nic Fhearchair shut the paper book with a clap, and somebody at the back began to laugh, and then all of them were laughing, forty-one people in leather aprons laughing in the sun at the mouth of the adit, and old Seumas sat down on the edge of the ore-trough and wiped his eyes with his sleeve.

Ùna did not laugh. She took the ledgers from them and carried them back to the hall herself, one under each arm.

"They will come again," she said that night at her own fire, to Fionnlagh, who could not sit still. "Or somebody will come in their place with a better paper. We have won nothing yet. All we have shown them is that the paper they carried was too short."

## V. Of What the Lawyer Found

The chief surveyor's report reached Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir a fortnight later, and it is in the letter-books, in a hand that is none too steady. He wrote that he had been met at the working by the whole population of the place in a body, silent and in an attitude he could not call threatening and would not call anything else; that he had been shown two books of accounts, one in no writing he knew, and had found no trace in either of any grant, lease or payment to his masters or to any of his countrymen; that he had not thought it prudent to proceed; and that he awaited instruction.

Under it, in the small level hand, Lisle wrote one line: *Nor would I have found it. Bring me the bound book.*

He read it through again that summer, from the first leaf to the last, with the charter laid open beside it. The letter-books keep that reading too, in a long letter he wrote in an Lùnastal to the master of A' Chompanaidh at Ros dhomhain, and the copyists have kept a copy of the letter in the cellar because it is the plainest account any human wrote in all the age of what the humans on the island held and by what right. The charter, Lisle wrote, was a promise that A' Chompanaidh's own masters had made to it: that whatever the island should yield to their own people, A' Chompanaidh should be offered first. It gave A' Chompanaidh nothing on the island. It could not give what the island had not first given, and the only writing by which the island had given anything was Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg. He had read it. It was careful about coal, and spoke of coal at great length and on every page; of silver it said nothing whatever, or about any other metal or ore, or about any ground but the ground of the coal. "They gave us the hill," he wrote, "and we have been reading it as the whole island. The silver of Muileann chiar was never ours to refuse or to be offered, and a surveyor sent to measure it has no more right on that hillside than a stranger has in my garden."

The master of A' Chompanaidh wrote back angrily and at length. Lisle's answer was two lines long and is also kept. He did not mean to lose another customs officer, it said, and he did not mean to lose a war over a seam that no page had ever given him.

In the autumn he wrote to Comann an Airgid. The letter came up the valley by the same road the surveyors had taken, in the humans' tongue only. It proposed that An t-Àrd-mhaor and the guild set down together, in a compact, that the silver seam of Muileann chiar and its workings belonged to the town and its guild, and that A' Chompanaidh had no claim on them under its charter or any other paper; and it proposed that the compact be signed at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir on a day to be named.

Ùna wrote back herself, in the humans' letters, slowly, on a sheet of the guild's paper. The copyists found her letter in the letter-books, bound in between a complaint about harbour thieves and an order for lamp-oil. It says that the guild would sign such a compact gladly; that the seam was at Muileann chiar and not at Ros dhomhain, and the compact had best be signed where the seam was; and that the guild asked for it to be written in both tongues, the humans' and the island's, line against line, so that neither side should have to trust the other's reading of it.

Lisle's clerk put a question mark in the margin beside that last sentence. Lisle struck it through. He wrote under the letter, *It costs me a clerk's week and it will save me a war of glosses. Do it*, and sent word to Muileann chiar that he would come himself on Là Fhèill Brìde.

He came in a closed carriage, over roads so bad that he and his clerk walked the last three miles up the valley beside the horses with their boots full of snow-water. The town turned out to watch him come, as it had turned out for the surveyors, but this time Ùna went down the street to meet him. He was a smaller man than she had thought, and older in the face, and he limped. He bowed to her in the humans' way. She did not know what to do with that, so she bowed back.

The compact was signed in the guild hall on {{date:IV-0103}}, Là Fhèill Brìde, the first day of an Gearran, at the long table under the hanging scales, with the brazier lit and the whole guild on the benches along the walls. It was short. There were two copies, each of two leaves, each written in two columns, the humans' tongue on the left and the island's on the right, and a young man of Ros dhomhain whom Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir paid to put its words into Dia-thìris had made the island's column and Ùna had gone through it with him line by line the night before and made him change four words. It said that the silver seam at Muileann chiar and all its workings, galleries and floors were the town's, held for it by Comann an Airgid as they had been held since An Aois Naomh; that A' Chompanaidh had no claim upon them under its charter or any other writing; that the guild might sell or send its silver where it chose; and that neither An t-Àrd-mhaor nor any officer of his should enter the workings without the guild's leave.

Lisle signed both copies at the foot of the humans' column. Ùna signed both at the foot of the island's, in the island's letters, and then, because the humans set store by it, in theirs as well. Old Seumas mac Fhearchair signed as witness with a mark, and Lisle's clerk with a flourish.

Before he left Lisle asked to see the seam. Ùna took him up to the adit herself, with a lamp. He stood a long time in the mouth of the gallery, holding the lamp up to the face where the last cutting had broken it, grey and bright and the same all through, and then he said a thing that Fionnlagh, who had come behind them with a second lamp, remembered and told to his own children.

"On the coast they tell me there are colours in the coal," Lisle said. "I have never seen them. I have stood at the pit-mouth at Muileann chrom with a custodian at each elbow pointing, and I saw coal." He turned the lamp so the light ran along the seam. "This I can see."

"We see it the same as you," said Ùna. "That is why it is not holy."

He looked at her as if he would ask what she meant, and did not ask; and they went back down to the hall.

That evening, when the carriage had gone down the valley in the dusk with its lamps lit, Ùna had the compact read aloud to the guild from the Dia-thìris column, all of it, by Mòr nic Fhearchair, who had the best voice. Then it was read again, because the apprentices at the back had not heard. It was the first writing of the whole age of the strangers in which the island's side kept the better terms. Nobody in the hall said so. They did not know it yet. They knew only that the surveyors had gone down the valley and a paper had come up it, and that the paper said the seam was theirs, in their own tongue, in a hand they could read.

## VI. Of the Guild Chest

The guild chest of Muileann chiar was a box of black oak bound with silver bands, older than anyone could say, that stood at the end of the hall under the scales. It held the ledgers and the weights and the guild's seal, and it had three locks, and the three keys were kept in three houses. Ùna had the compact laid in it the night it was signed, on top of the paper book of the weighings, and the three keys turned.

In the spring word came east that Coinneach Glas was dead at Cathair dhearg. He had died in his chair by the brazier in the room behind the council hall, with the rolls in their presses round him, at sixty-nine, having held the seat twenty-four years. The clerk who wrote to Muileann chiar with the news added that Ceann a' Chùraim had heard of the compact before the end, and had had it read to him twice from the copy Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir sent the council, and had said that it was a better paper than any he had signed. The council's leaves were still at Muileann chiar. Ùna sent them back to the council by Fionnlagh, wrapped in the same oilcloth, with a bar of silver for the clerk who kept the presses, for his trouble.

The council chose Alasdair Liath after him, a custodian of forty-nine, who had sat on the council since before Puingean a' Chalaidh and had voted to sign them. The roll gives his choosing in a single line. It gives his age, and the number of voices, and nothing else. Ùna, when she heard, said that she had met him once and he had a good memory, and did not say whether that was praise.

In an t-Ògmhios the guild tried its compact for the first time. A' Chompanaidh had asked, through the agent at Muileann chrom, that the guild's silver should go to the sea by its own quays at Ros dhomhain, where it might be weighed at the customs house and entered in the books. Ùna read the compact again, the island's column and then the humans', and found that the guild might send its silver where it chose. So on {{date:IV-0104}} the first carts of bar silver went south instead, eighteen miles down the valley road to Seann Bhral on the south-eastern coast, where the oyster-beds lie in the shallows and the coasting boats had loaded Muileann chiar silver since An Aois Naomh. The guild paid the custodians of Seann Bhral for the use of their quay in the old way, in silver by the Muileann chiar weight, so much on every bar. The agent at Muileann chrom wrote to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir to complain. Lisle's clerk wrote back that it could find no ground in the compact to object, and the agent's letter and the answer both are in the letter-books, one under the other, with nothing written between them.

It was a good summer in the silver town. The floors worked from dawn to dark, and the paper book of the weighings, which had lasted the guild a lifetime, was full by the autumn and a new one begun.

In the winter came news from the other end of the island that the guild marked in its ledger, though it had nothing to do with silver. The carters of Baile gheal, in the south-west, had taken up the carrying of the brine-salt of Seann Warr round the lake to the capital, with their own carts and their own ponies, on the old roads. A' Chompanaidh had never troubled to claim the salt trade, and nobody had told the carters of Baile gheal that they might not have it; so they had it. They were paid in salt and in marks both, and their young men stayed home from the pits. A carter from the west told it at the smelting-floors in the month of am Faoilleach, in the forty-fifth year of the strangers, a week after the thing was done. He told it as a small thing, which it was. Ùna had it written into the new ledger all the same.

"Write it down," she told the weighing-clerk. "Salt at Baile gheal. Their own carts. Put the year."

"It is not silver," said the clerk.

"It is a thing they did not take," said Ùna. "There are not so many of those that we can afford to forget one."

At the end of that month, on the day the guild reckoned its year and read out the weighings, the three keyholders came to the hall with their keys, and the chest was opened, and Ùna took the compact out and had it read aloud again to the whole guild, both columns, the island's and then the humans'. She was fifty-nine that winter. She stood by the chest while it was read with her hands folded in front of her apron, and when Mòr nic Fhearchair had finished she took the two leaves and looked at them a while, at the two columns and the three names, Lisle's small level one and her own twice over and the old man's mark. Then she laid them in the chest on top of the new paper book, beside the notched boards of the old ledger, and the three keys turned, one after another, in the three locks.
