# Of the Breaking of the Staff

*AE 147 – 149*

## I. Of the Iron Bird

Mòrag nic Iain Maor was fifty-two that winter, and had held the overseer's staff of Muileann chrom for sixteen years, and had not slept a full night since the inquiry into the fight at the pithead, where she had told the Administration's table plainly that the pumps were stopped on the agent's order and had watched a clerk draw a line through the words as she said them. The Lamp-Watch had a copy of her testimony all the same, kept in the burial club's box under the false bottom with the burying-money, and she knew it, and had never asked to see it, because a thing a woman does not hold cannot be taken from her hand.

She was at the weighing-shed door on the morning the bird came over, checking the tally against the agent's book as she had checked it every working day since her father died, when the crews at the pit-mouth below her stopped their barrows all at once and stood looking up. She looked up herself. It came from the south, low over the ridge, a long grey shape with wings that did not beat, trailing a sound like a swarm too large for any hive, and it went the length of the valley and back again before it climbed away toward Ros dhomhain and was gone.

Nobody at Muileann chrom had a word for it that morning. By evening every hearth in the district had one, borrowed from nowhere and understood at once: eun-iarainn, the iron bird, a thing that flew and was not alive. The old women said a bird that did not beat its wings had no soul in it and could carry none away, which was some comfort, though none of them could say what it had come to do, only that it had counted something from the air that the ground would not have given up so easily.

Mòrag wrote it into her own book that night, under the tally, in the private hand her father had taught her that no agent ever asked to read: *a grey bird went over the valley twice and did not sing.* She did not know yet that it had gone the length of every working district on the island that season, from Doire mhòr to the eastern silver country, and that Strake, new come to the Residency, meant to have on paper what the ground would not give the Company's surveyors on foot.

Word of Strake reached the district before the bird did. Merriman had gone home sick the autumn before, an old man worn thin by fourteen years of a climate that had never once been unkind to him, and the man who came after him was a soldier, not a merchant, and did not trouble to send the custom letter asking the council's leave for anything. His commission was read out at the Residency to an empty bench where the Council of Custodians' seats had always stood, and no one from the council was there to hear it, because no one from the council had been asked. Coinneach Ruadh, who had lost his clerkship at Cnoc dhìreach for signing the second petition and had taken to riding the district roads with news nobody paid him for carrying, brought that word to Muileann chrom himself, sitting at Mòrag's table with his boots steaming by the fire.

"They've locked the hall," he said. "The whole council hall at Cathair dhearg, doors and all, and the keys gone down to the Residency in a soldier's pocket. The custodians are sitting in Macha's temple now, on the priests' own floor, because Macha's priests took them in when nobody else in the capital would risk the Residency's temper."

"The priests that blessed the coin," Mòrag said.

"The ones that didn't," said Coinneach. "There's a difference now that never used to matter. Half the order left the capital's temple years back for the forest at Cnoc bheag, and it's their own who've opened the door to the council. Blood's thicker than a blessing, it turns out, once the blessing's spent."

Mòrag thought of the staff over her own hearth, worn smooth at the grip where three hands before hers had carried it, and did not say what she was thinking, which was that a council locked out of its own hall and a staff still hanging where the Company had put it were closer cousins than either of them would have liked to own.

## II. Of the Edict of Seizure

The edict came down in the spring, printed in the Residency's own press and nailed to the counting-house door at every working on the island in the same week, so that no district could say afterward it had not been warned in time, though warning and consent are not the same thing and the edict asked for neither. It claimed for the Administration the right to take any dwelling of the Dia-thìrich that it judged needful for the widening of the workings, at a price the Administration itself would set, and it gave no appeal.

Doire mhòr was a morning's walk above Muileann chrom, close enough that Mòrag rode over herself when the first word came that the constables were there, though there was nothing in it that was hers to mend. It was the village whose custodian, Beathag Dhubh, had refused the Company's contract at her own door more than a hundred and thirty years before, and whose last few households were the first emptied now under the last edict the Company would ever write. She stood at the top of the lane while the constables carried out beds and looms and a loom-comb some grandmother's grandmother had carved, and stacked them at the roadside in the rain because the new shaft beneath the hill could not wait for dry weather any more than it could wait for leave. An old man sat on his own doorstone with his hands over his knees and would not move until two constables lifted him by the arms, not roughly, which was somehow worse than roughness would have been, because it meant they had been told to be gentle about a thing that could not be made gentle by the manner of the doing.

"Write it down," the old man said to nobody, to the rain, to Mòrag standing at the lane's head in her overseer's coat that marked her for a woman with some standing, though standing had never once turned back a cart. "Write down that they were gentle about it. Somebody ought to know they were gentle."

She had no book with her that day. She carried the sentence home in her head instead and set it down that night beside the grey bird that did not sing, in the private hand, because a thing written in two places is harder to unwrite than a thing written in one.

At Baile dhomhain, nearer still, the camp that had been laid out on the Company's own paper before a stone of it was cut, two whole streets came down within the month for a spoil-tip that the surveyors said the new galleries would need before winter. The Company offered huts at Àth ìseal and marks for the timber of the houses it took, which some families kept, weighing the marks against the winter coming, and most did not, going instead to kin in the hill villages where there was a floor to sleep on that no edict could nail a notice to. Mòrag heard the tally of it from the district's own custodian, a quiet woman named Sìneag who had never before had cause to ride to the steward's house and rode there now white about the mouth, asking whether the overseer's staff of Muileann chrom could do anything at all for a street that was not, strictly, Muileann chrom's to answer for.

"Nothing," Mòrag told her, which was true that week, though it would not stay true past the summer, and she did not yet know how soon the edict's hand would close on her own district instead of her neighbour's, or that when it did she would have an answer ready that surprised her almost as much as it surprised the Company.

## III. Of the Order and the Staff

The order came to Muileann chrom itself in the height of that summer, on the Company's own printed form with her own district's name filled into the blank by a clerk's hand, instructing the overseer to identify for clearance, within the month, such dwellings in the third and fourth rows of the camp as stood nearest the new gallery mouth, numbering not fewer than eleven households.

Mòrag read it twice at her table under the slate roof, the same table her father had sat at with the same order in a different year for a different widening, and had told the Company's man then that the staff would not do it, and had been overruled by a Company surveyor's letter that made his refusal no more than a delay. She thought of that, sitting with the paper flat under both her hands as if it might otherwise lift and fly out the window after the iron bird. She thought too of the old man on his doorstone at Doire mhòr, and the grey bird that did not sing, and Sìneag's white mouth asking for an answer the staff had not had to give.

No reply went back to Ros dhomhain. Instead she took the staff down from over the hearth, the brass-capped, iron-shod length of ash Raghnall had carried first and Dòmhnall after him and her own father Iain after Dòmhnall, its grip worn to the shape of three hands and now a fourth, and walked out of the steward's house and down through the town toward the Company's office with the staff in her fist like a woman going to a fight rather than a woman going to resign one.

The town came out behind her without being asked. A crew coming off the morning shift saw her pass the weighing-shed with the staff and left their barrows standing and followed; a woman hanging washing left the washing on the line; two boys who should have been minding a flock of half-wild goats on the slag heap left the goats to their own devices and ran ahead to be first at the office door, the way boys will run toward any commotion without troubling to learn first what it is about. By the time Mòrag reached the Company's door there were perhaps sixty people behind her in the street, not shouting, not yet, only walking at her pace the way a tide comes in, without hurry and without stopping.

The agent that year was a careful young man named Osgood who had never once, in three years at Muileann chrom, raised his voice to a Dia-thìreach worker, which the district had come, wrongly, to read as kindness rather than as the particular fear of a man a long way from home among people he did not understand and could not afford to provoke. He came to the office door at the noise and saw the staff in Mòrag's hand and understood at once, before she said a word, what she meant to do with it, because there was nothing else a woman carries a staff to a locked door to do.

"I won't clear the eleven households," Mòrag said. "Not the third row, not the fourth. Not this year, not on this order, not on any order that comes after it with the same seal on the bottom."

"The order isn't mine to withdraw," Osgood said. His hands, she noticed, were not quite steady on the doorframe, though his voice held. "You know that. I only carry what comes down from Ros dhomhain."

"Then carry this back," Mòrag said, and brought the staff down across her own bent knee.

It was old wood, dry through more than a hundred years of hearths, and the brass cap and the iron shoe did nothing to hold it, and it went with a crack that carried the length of the street and brought faces to every door along it that had not already come out on their own feet. She laid the two halves on the office step, not thrown, not flung, set down the way a woman sets down a thing she is finished carrying, and straightened, and found that her knee, where the wood had broken across it, had taken a welt she would feel for a week and never once regret.

"Send that to Ros dhomhain instead," she said, "and tell them the fourth of my line has done what the first three couldn't bring themselves to."

She walked home through the crowd that opened for her and closed again behind her, and did not look back at the office, and did not need to, because she had already heard, before she reached the top of the street, three other names called out from three other doors: Griogar Camshron of the north pit, who had held the eastern gallery's staff for six years and threw his own down in the yard within the week rather than be the one man left holding wood the Company had marked for treachery; the overseer of Cnoc chaol, whom Mòrag had never met but heard of before the month was out; and a third, at the silver country's edge, whose name did not reach Muileann chrom until the winter, whose staff, it was said, was found propped against the counting-house wall with no one there to break it, because he had simply walked away and left it standing.

The Company put humans in all four places before the season turned, men who had never held Raghnall's staff and never would, and the office at Muileann chrom kept its ledgers after that in a hand that did not know the names of the third and fourth rows it now governed. So ended, in one summer, the office that Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill had taken up more than a hundred years before believing it the only door left open to him; and the custodians who had once hoped the staff would simply lapse, unheld, when the first overseer wearied of it, got their wish at last from the fourth in his line, though not in the manner nor in the year that any of them, dead or living, had ever thought to hope for.

## IV. Of the Silver and the Forest

Mòrag was no longer overseer of anything when the silver guild's rifles came ashore, and found, to her own surprise, that she had more time for the district's true business than the staff had ever left her, and less patience for waiting to be told what that business was.

The guild at Muileann chiar had held its seam under a compact a century old, granted back when the Company still troubled to grant things rather than simply take them, and had kept its own arms in name only, an old militia roll nobody had called out since the compact was signed. That summer the guild began to buy rifles from the raiders of the eastern waters, where no Company gunboat had patrolled in years for want of coal to spare it, and paid the raiders in bar silver by weight, and entered the whole transaction in its own ledger under the heading of refining tools, in a hand steady enough that any clerk who read it would have believed the guild meant to melt the rifles down rather than load them.

Coinneach Ruadh, still riding the district roads with his unpaid news, brought that word to Mòrag's door along with the rest, and something else besides: that the custodians who had fled to Macha's dissenting priests in the sacred forest at Cnoc bheag meant to hold a council there before the autumn was out, a single council for the whole mining country, custodians and crew-leaders and priests together, the first such gathering the Company had never once been able to prevent because it had never once been told of it.

"They want you there," Coinneach said. "A woman who broke a staff in the street has more to say to that council than most who'll be sitting in it."

She went by night, on a borrowed pony over the ridge road and then off it, along tracks she had known as a girl and had not walked since, twenty-eight miles by her own count and every one of them dark, because a moon on a night like that would have shown the way to more than the council's own people. The forest at Cnoc bheag stood older than any road the Company had ever cut, oak and thorn grown up thick enough that the fires inside it, when she came to them at last past midnight, showed as no more than a red haze through the trunks until she was almost among them.

There were perhaps two hundred people in the clearing when she arrived, more than she had thought the whole quiet business of custodians and dissenting priests could have gathered without a single Company clerk hearing of it: crew-leaders she knew by sight from three other districts, priests of Macha's order with the black-and-grey horsehair of their office still worn openly for the first time in years, and a woman younger than Mòrag had expected, standing near the largest fire with her back very straight, whom she recognised from the second petition's underlined sentence before anyone spoke her name aloud. Eilidh nic Raghnaill was crew-leader of Baile thais and one of the Lamp-Watch, and had drafted the words the Residency itself had thought worth underlining, and stood now listening to older voices argue the shape of a council that had, in truth, already chosen her before the argument began, because there was no one else in that clearing whose name every district already knew.

Mòrag did not speak much that night. She had said her piece already, on a Company doorstone with a length of broken wood, and found she had little to add to voices that had spent years finding the right words for grievances she had only just begun to carry. But when the council settled, near dawn, on Eilidh nic Raghnaill to speak for the whole mining country, Mòrag was among those who raised a hand for it, and rode home in the grey light with the sense, new to her, of having put her name to something that could not be unsigned the way a staff, once broken, cannot be mended.

No human writing recorded that meeting. The Residency's letter-books, when the copyists came to them years after, held not one line about a gathering of two hundred people in a forest twenty-eight miles from the nearest road a Company cart could use, which was, Mòrag thought when she heard of the gap later, the nearest thing to a compliment the Administration had ever paid the mining country's discretion.

## V. Of the Silent Town

Strake learned of the forest council within the month all the same, by what road the district never troubled to ask, and answered it the only way a Commissioner with no council left to argue with could answer anything: with an order to seize.

The order named Eilidh nic Raghnaill first and the members of the forest council after her, and the constables who carried it went to Baile thais in the last days of am Faoilleach, five miles down the valley from Muileann chrom, expecting, by every account that reached Mòrag afterward, a house to search and a woman to take from it.

They found the house standing open and swept, the hearth cold three days by the ash in it, and the town around it silent in a way that a mining town at midday is never silent, no barrows on the road, no children sent for water, every door shut and no face at a single window, so that the constables' captain, new to the district and unused to its ways, is said to have asked aloud whether the whole place had died of the fever in a night, and been told by no one, because there was no one left in the street to tell him.

Eilidh nic Raghnaill had gone by night two days before, on word carried ahead of the order by hands faster than the Administration's own post, taking nothing from the house that could not be carried on foot and leaving the hearth swept because a woman raised to keep a house does not stop keeping it merely because she is leaving it for the last time. Where she went that night the district did not learn for some while, and did not need to, since it was enough to know she had gone, and gone whole, and gone before the Residency's hand closed on the place where she had stood.

Mòrag heard the whole of it from Coinneach Ruadh on the twentieth of an Gearran, riding into Muileann chrom at a pace she had not seen him keep in the two years she had known him, his pony blown and his own breath short, so that he had to say it twice before the words came out in an order she could follow.

"They've called it," he said. "Not the council, the district. Every district. Word's gone out from the forest since last night. The grievance is one thing now across the whole mining country, and there's no council left at Cathair dhearg to bring it before, only the Residency, and the Residency has had its answer this morning already, from Muileann chrom and Baile thais both."

Mòrag stood in her own doorway, in the house she still kept though the staff no longer hung over its hearth, and looked down the valley toward the pithead where the barrows had stood idle since dawn without her needing to ask why, and toward the ridge beyond which the coal road ran, empty now of every cart that had ever carried Company coal down it, and understood, with a plainness that surprised her less than it perhaps should have, what answer Coinneach meant: the one that comes only after a petition, and a strike, and a staff broken on a doorstone, have each in turn been tried and struck out or overruled or simply, as her own testimony at the inquiry had been, drawn through with a clerk's pen and filed away as though the drawing-through made the thing unsaid.

She did not sleep that night, the twentieth of an Gearran in the hundred and forty-ninth year of the strangers' coming, any more than she had slept the night after the inquiry, but for the first time in all the years since, the waking did not feel like grief. She sat at her father's table with her father's book before her and wrote, in the private hand, a single line under the tally she no longer had any staff to keep: *the bird that did not sing has brought the whole hill to its feet, and I broke the first wood of it myself, and do not wish that back.*

Outside, along the whole length of the coal country from Doire mhòr to the silver hills, the fires that had been lit in secret for two years were being carried openly to every beacon height the districts held, and the light of them, when Mòrag went out at last to stand in her own doorway and watch, showed her more of her own country at once than she had ever seen by daylight: ridge after ridge answering ridge, from the forest at Cnoc bheag to the coast, each fire lit from the one before it, until the whole dark shoulder of the island seemed to have taken up, in one night, the single word that no council hall, no locked door, and no iron bird flying twice the length of a silent valley had ever yet managed to carry the length of it. War had come to the hills, and the staff that had broken across Mòrag nic Iain Maor's own knee, she understood now, watching the fires answer one another into the distance, had been only the first small crack in a much larger piece of wood.
