# Of the Smoke in the Gallery

*AE 68 – 72*

## I. Of the First Winter of Achadh dhomhain

The camp had no name before it had houses, and it had houses before it had a name that anyone but A' Chompanaidh used. Beitris Bhàn came to it in its first winter with a wool blanket, a horn cup and the small knife her mother had laid out three husbands and eleven neighbours with, walking the short road up from Àth ìseal because word had gone round the huts there that a new camp at Achadh dhomhain needed a woman who knew how to close a dead man's eyes and not flinch at a wound the coal had made.

She found the camp built of itself. A' Chompanaidh had sunk its shaft into the hillside for the deep galleries, and the spoil that came up out of it, broken rock and coal-dross too poor to sell, went into walls; so that the houses of Achadh dhomhain were grey and black together, low, roofed with turf cut from the same hill, and every one of them stood a little crooked, for spoil does not lie true the way a quarried stone does. The crews called them houses of spoil without meaning anything by it, the way a man calls his own boots his boots. Beitris took the end room of one such house, next the well, and hung her mother's knife on a nail, and within the week she had work.

That first winter the camp buried nine. Two were old hewers brought down from Àth ìseal for their skill and dead of the damp before the first snow; one was a child of five crushed under a cart of spoil that a tired horse let run back on its heel; the rest went of the cough that every camp has, deeper some years than others, and that winter deep. Beitris washed them in the well water, which came up black in a bucket until it had stood a night, and laid them out on the plank table in her room with a coal set on the breast for the going-down, as the custom is in every pit town from Doire mhòr to the last camp on the eastern road, and the custodian of Àth ìseal, whose roll then still reached as far as the new camp, rode up once a season to write the names and the days.

The name Donnchadh mac Thormoid meant nothing to her yet. He was one hewer among three hundred, and his crew worked the far end of the deepest gallery, where A' Chompanaidh had run its air-shaft down from the hillside above, and men who work an air-shaft's draught keep to themselves, for the wind down there is cold enough to work in a coat where other crews strip to the waist. She had heard, as everyone in the camp had heard, that his grandfather had once stood behind Beathag Dhubh of Doire mhòr on the day she sent A' Chompanaidh's man back down the hill unanswered, and that his father Tormod had carried water up to Doire mhòr for two summers after A' Chompanaidh took the stream, and then gone down with the rest to the huts at Àth ìseal. It was said, too, that Donnchadh himself had broken coal at a pit-mouth before he could read his letters, and gone below at twelve, a beginning that raised no eyebrow in that camp, where most of the men had made the same one or a harder. She knew him, that first winter, only as a name on the custodian's roll when his crew lost a boy to the damp, and as a back going down the ladder in the grey of the morning with the rest.

The nine of that first winter were laid in a field A' Chompanaidh had not yet found a use for, above the last of the spoil-heaps, in ground too broken with old workings to carry a cart-road. Beitris cut the turf for each grave herself, since the camp had as yet no man spared from the pit for the work, and found under it, more than once, the black seam showing through where an older digging had come near the surface and been left. She set nine cairns that winter with her own hands, small ones, for the camp could spare no more stone than that, and when the custodian of Àth ìseal rode up in the spring to write the names, he stood over each cairn a while, as though counting them meant something more than the writing did.

## II. Of the Coach and the Steelyard

A' Chompanaidh put its road in before it put in anything else that eased a life. In the spring a coach began to run twice in the week between the quay at Ros dhomhain and the camps about Muileann chrom, changing its horses at the old waypoint houses that had stood empty since the post first came up the coast, and the clerks and the overseers rode in it, dry, with their ledgers on their knees, while the crews who dug what filled the ledgers walked the same road on their own feet, in whatever weather the road had that day. Beitris saw it pass twice a week for years after and never once saw a hewer's boot inside it.

The next year A' Chompanaidh raised a great steelyard on the quay at Ros dhomhain, a beam and a basket and a chain of iron links taller than two men, and from the day it was hung the island's yield was no longer spoken of in the old way, in baskets filled and carts drawn and seasons of a crew's labour, as the custodians' rolls had always kept it, but in tons and hundredweights read off a scale by a clerk of A' Chompanaidh who had never gone below in his life. The custodians' old count went on a while in the margins of the rolls, out of habit, and then it did not. Ùna nic Choinnich, who kept Comann an Airgid's own scales at Muileann chiar and had refused twice already to let A' Chompanaidh's weights anywhere near her own, said when she heard of it that a hill weighed by the ton and a hill worked by a crew's own reckoning of what a day was worth were two different hills, and that the island had let the wrong one be written down. Beitris heard the saying secondhand, as everyone in the camps heard everything Ùna said, a season late and twice removed, and thought little of it at the time.

That was also the year A' Chompanaidh opened its second coal stage, at Ros dhìreach on the southern coast, to carry off the yield of the workings south of the hills without the long haul round to Ros dhomhain. Its first cargo went down in a squall not a mile off the harbour mouth, boat and coal and two of the crew who had gone out with her to trim the load, and the fishermen whose grounds had been given over to make room for the new stage would not put out past the wreck for a season after, saying the sea there had taken against the place. And in that same year, or the year after, for the camp's memory of dates ran together the way a hewer's years run together below ground, a ship came into Ros dhomhain driven by no sail at all, with a wheel turning in a housing at either side and smoke coming off her instead of spray, and the harbour's own fishermen rowed out in a ring to look at a hull that went against the wind. It was said in the camp, by men who had seen her, that within a few years A' Chompanaidh would send nothing else, and that the sailing ships that had first come down from the north with Harrow would soon come no more.

Beitris marked these things the way the camp marked them, as weather is marked: noticed, talked over at a door on a wet evening, and then folded away under the next thing.

The road brought the camp its wonders, and Beitris listened to all of it at the well, the way the whole camp listened to everything, and weighed it against the small, unremarkable fact that a hewer at the far end of the deep gallery had stopped coughing on the night his daughter was born, which nobody outside a handful of women had yet troubled to notice at all.

## III. Of What the Wife Told the Women

It came to Beitris the way most things came to her: at a laying-out, in the small hours, when the work of the hands leaves the tongue free and grief makes people say what they would otherwise keep.

In the fourth winter of the camp, the old woman who had first taken Beitris in, and taught her the way of setting a coal on a still breast, died in her sleep with no mark of the pit on her at all, which the camp took as a mercy and marked so. The women who came to sit the night with the body were Achadh dhomhain's own: wives and daughters of hewers, two widows, and Donnchadh mac Thormoid's wife, who had never before sat such a night in Beitris's room, and who sat now with her hands in her lap and said little until the small hours had worn the room down to its plainest talk.

It was Beitris who asked, in the end, only because the woman's silence had a shape to it that Beitris's trade had taught her to know. She asked how her husband had come to lose his cough, for the camp had seen him coughing black through a winter and thinning through a spring, and had begun, as camps do, to reckon how soon they would be laying him on this same table; and then the cough had stopped, all at once, like a door shut.

The wife told it then, low, with the other women leaning in to hear, and she told it as her husband had told her, which is the only way she would tell it, for she said more than once that the words were his and she would change none of them.

He had been alone at the face, she said, in the first winter of the camp, at the far end of the deepest gallery A' Chompanaidh had opened, with his lamp hung on a nail behind him and the shaft's draught running past him steady and cold, so that his flame lay over on its side all day and the dust of his cutting drifted away toward the dark in a thin grey thread. He had stopped to spit, and turned, and seen the thread of dust part around something that was not dust.

It stood the height of a tall man, she said, and it was made of smoke, grey smoke such as a peat fire gives in a shut room, and the draught went past it on either side without so much as bending it, though it bent the flame of his lamp and carried off every grain of dust he had cut. He had heard of the smoke in the grove all his life, at his grandmother's knee, with Marbh-bheò and the white stag and the rest of the fireside tales a child puts away when he goes below; and he had put this one away too, until it stood in front of him in the dark, and he found there was nowhere to run to but past it, and he did not want to run in any case, though he could not afterward say why not.

It spoke to him. "You are Tormod's son," it said, and its voice was low and very clear, and the draught that carried off everything else in that gallery did not carry off a word of it.

"I am," he said.

"And Somhairle's, and Ailean's." It seemed, he told her, to be looking about the gallery, at the props and the black walls and the cut face, the way a guest looks about a house where things have plainly gone badly for the people in it. "They are taking the hill."

"They are," he said.

"I will not fight for the kin. I never have; that is not my charge. But I was set to keep the kin, and a kin with its hill dug out from under it is not kept." It came nearer him then, close enough that he caught its smell, which was a hearth in the morning, when the fire has been banked all night and only just stirred. "I made a promise once, in the grove, to the one you come from. Breathe."

He breathed, because it had told him to, and the smoke went into him along with the dust of his own cutting, and it was warm, he said, warm all the way down, like a coal carried home under a cloak on a cold walk.

"What is it?" he asked, when he could speak again.

"A gift," it told him. "It is not for you. You will carry it, and the carrying will not be easy. It will wake in your daughter." The draught had begun to move through it by then, and it was thinning as it spoke. "It is in the dust of the deep places now, for every one of the kin who goes down; and where it wakes, it wakes. Tell no one of it but the one who will bear the child."

Then the draught went clean through the place where it had stood, and his lamp flame righted itself, and there was nothing in the gallery but the dust, settling.

He had coughed from that day, his wife said, black, and grown thin, and Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's surgeon at the camp had listened at his chest and written him down with the miners' lung, the same dust-rot the humans brought with them from the pits of their own country, which the crews called Grodadh nan Scamhan among themselves, and had given him, the surgeon said, a year. And then, on {{date:IV-0146}}, his daughter was born to them in their own house of spoil, and that same night, his wife said, his cough stopped, and it never came back, not once, in all the years since. He had told her what he had met in the gallery the night the cough left him, and she had never told a living soul of it until this night, in this room, over another woman's body, with the coal already set on the dead breast between them.

Nobody in the room asked her the child's name. Everybody in the room already knew it. It was Aisling.

## IV. Of the Child the Rolls Gave Three Things

Beitris saw the child herself, in the way everyone in a small camp sees every child, at doors and at the well and asleep in a shawl on her mother's back while the mother queued for meal. There was nothing to see, at first, but a child. The custodian who rode up that spring wrote her down as the roll requires, a birth, a name, a father's name, and rode on to the next camp, and that was the whole of what the record ever meant to say of her.

It was the camp that said more, and it began, as most things in Achadh dhomhain began, with the women who laid out the dead. Beitris was one of them by then in her own right, and she heard it said first over a washing, in the flat voice women use for a thing they are only half in earnest about and half not: that the child was of the old king's blood. They meant Ailean Mòr, whom the gods crowned in the grove in the first years of everything, who was king four thousand years and more before he fell at Àth na Fala, and whose children, the tale ran, lived thousands of years after him. Not one woman in that room believed a word of the thousands of years; Ailean Mòr was a tale for the dark end of the year, set among the tellings by scribes long dead, and there was not a soul in the camp who had ever heard the whole of his line said out in order. But the child did not thicken through the shoulders as the other children of her year thickened, and did not lose her milk teeth when they lost theirs, and the women noticed it the way women who wash the dead notice everything about a body, early and exactly, and the whisper grew with her rather than dying the way most camp gossip dies.

They called her *nighean an rìgh*, half in jest and, as the seasons went by without her growing the way a child should, half not; and it began to be said of anyone in the camps who kept their youth past its season that they had *Aisling-aois* upon them, though there was as yet only the one Aisling for the saying to be named after. A people whose hill was going out of the island in ships by the ton took its comfort where it found one, and Achadh dhomhain found it in an old tale nobody believed and a girl of the spoil-houses who would not grow old. Nobody could prove a word of it, then or for years after, and nobody in that camp tried; a whisper that could be proved would have stopped being theirs.

Beitris herself was as much a watcher of the child as any of them, by the ordinary chance of a laying-out room standing close by the well where every mother in that row of houses of spoil came morning and evening. She saw Aisling grow as far as a small child grows. What she did not see, season after season, was the child changing the way a child changes: no new tooth lost and grown again, no stretching of the wrist past the cuff of last year's smock, no voice dropping into the huskier register a growing throat takes on. It was a stillness so gradual that no single week of it could be pointed to, and that, Beitris came to think, was why it took the women of the wash-house to notice it before anyone else did, for they were the ones who handled the camp's dying by the hand and the wrist and the throat, and knew exactly what a year should do to a body and what it should not.

Of what had passed in the gallery, and of what the smoke had told Donnchadh to keep, the women who had sat that night with Beitris said nothing outside the room where it was said. It was Beitris, in the end, who set the rule, though no one voted on it and no meeting was ever called: she told the others plainly that a thing a spirit had charged a man to keep close was not theirs to spend on the rest of the camp's talk, whatever they made of the child in their own kitchens, and the others agreed, and held to it. What was happening to the child had no name in any tongue spoken on the island, then or for years to come; it was the camp's own secret before it was anyone's mystery, kept the way the camp kept everything it valued, close, and among its own.

## V. Of Ùna nic Choinnich and the Shrines

In the child's second year word came down from Muileann chiar that Ùna nic Choinnich, who had led Comann an Airgid for near forty years and had twice turned A' Chompanaidh away from its seam, once at the adit and once by a show of hands, was dead at last in her own bed, eighty-five years old, having outlived An t-Àrd-mhaor who signed her compact. The camp had never seen her, most of it, and knew her only by the sayings that came down the road ahead of the coach; but Beitris, laying out a hewer's wife that same week, found the women of Achadh dhomhain talking of Ùna's dying with more feeling than they had shown for Àrd-mhaoir who had actually ridden through their own camp, and thought that a guild which had said no twice, and been believed, was worth more to people who had never once been asked than any office that only ever gave orders.

The camp kept its own shrines through those years as it had always kept them, A' Chompanaidh suffering the custom because it cost little enough to suffer. At Cill dhìreach, on the edge of the workings, the custodians held a small shrine where the crews of every new gallery brought the first piece cut from it and set it down before they carried the rest to the steelyard, and A' Chompanaidh let the practice stand, for a stone of coal a gallery was cheaper than a quarrel. At Cill ghlas, in the hills among the deepening pits, the custodians of Brìde's flame kept the older rite, at the mouth of the oldest cutting in those hills, tending the stone and the flame there while A' Chompanaidh's new shafts went down all around them; and of every shrine in that district it was the last the crews still climbed to on the old feast days, at Samhain and Imbolc, at Bealltainn and at Lùnastal.

Beitris went up to it for the first time in the same season Ùna's death came down the road, more from the habit of a woman who had spent a hard winter laying out neighbours than from any settled faith, for her own people had kept no particular custom of the shrine before the camp gave her one. She found the path already scored by other feet, hewers' boots mostly, going up before a shift or coming down after one, leaving nothing behind them but a nod to the custodian and sometimes a coin, sometimes nothing at all. It struck her, standing there the first time with the flame small and steady against a hard wind, that A' Chompanaidh, which begrudged a stone a gallery to the shrine at Cill dhìreach, had never once tried to close this one, whether because the cost was smaller still or because even an Àrd-mhaor who could not see the colour in the coal knew better than to be the man who put out Brìde's flame in a camp already burying its dead by the dozen.

## VI. Of the Climb to Cill ghlas

In the fifth Lùnastal of the camp, with the child three years old, Beitris climbed the hill path to Cill ghlas alone, before first light, as she had taken to doing since the night of the telling, though she could not have said to another soul why that night had put the habit in her.

The path went up past the newest of the spoil-heaps, black even in starlight, and past the mouth of the gallery where Donnchadh mac Thormoid still worked his shift, cough long gone, saying no more of what had happened to him there than his wife had said of it in Beitris's room, and by all report never once saying more. At the top the custodian was already at the flame, feeding it the last of the night's peat before the sun took over the work, and the old cutting behind her stood open and black and ordinary, a hole in a hill like any other hole a people had ever dug in search of what it needed.

Beitris set down the coal she had carried up, one piece, no bigger than the ones the crews left at Cill dhìreach, and said nothing to the custodian, who said nothing to her, for it was not a shrine that asked for words. Below her the camp was waking, doors opening onto the grey light, smoke starting from a hundred low chimneys built of the hill's own dross, and somewhere down among them a child not yet four years old.

What the camp had been given, that Lùnastal morning, Beitris could not have said, nor what the giving would yet cost it; a layer-out of the dead learns early that not every debt comes due while the one who owes it is still watching. She knew only that a promise made once in a grove, to someone whose name none of them now living had ever heard said whole, was being kept in the one way a promise so old can be kept: quietly, underground, in the dust, where nobody stood by who could put a word to it. A while longer she stayed at the flame with the custodian, neither of them speaking, and then turned back down the path to see who else the winter had left her to lay out, and did not speak of the child again to anyone outside that first room for a long time after.

Remember it.
