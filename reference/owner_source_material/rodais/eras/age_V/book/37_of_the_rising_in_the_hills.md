# Of the Rising in the Hills

*AE 149*

> *Loisc iad an leabhar, ach cha do loisc iad an cnoc.*
>
> They burned the book, but they did not burn the hill.

## I. Of the First Light at Muileann chrom

Mòrag nic Iain Maor had broken the Steward's staff across her knee on the Company's own step a year gone, and for a year after that she had carried nothing in her hands heavier than a tally-stick. On the twentieth of an Gearran she carried a pike cut from a fence-rail, and led thirty men and women up the spoil-road to the walled store at Muileann chrom before the sky over the eastern ridge had properly whitened.

She had counted the store's watch for a month by then without once being asked to, the way a woman counts the doors of her own house without meaning anything by it: eleven constables, a relief of four at the changing of the watch, a locked gate with a bar dropped from the inside and no bar dropped from the outside in all that month, because no one in Muileann chrom had ever given the Company cause to drop one. She had that from the boy who carried the store's slops out each evening and had never once been searched for it.

The gate went down under four men with a felled larch before the watch inside had properly woken. Mòrag came through it herself, because she would not send others where she would not walk, and found the eleven constables drawn up in the yard in their shirts, most of them not yet properly dressed, looking at the pikes and the felling-axes and the sheer number of the crowd behind her with the particular stillness of young men doing arithmetic they do not like the answer to.

Their sergeant was a Cathair mhòr man not five years older than the boy who had carried the slops. He held his rifle a moment as though he had forgotten what a rifle was for, and then set it down on the cobbles with a care that looked almost like an apology, and the other ten set theirs down after him without being told.

"You'll not be harmed," Mòrag said, "if you go where you're sent and go quietly." It was the only promise she made that morning that she was certain, standing there, she could keep.

She had held the Steward's staff for two years before she broke it, and had spent most of those two years learning exactly how far a promise from that office could be trusted before the Company's own ledgers quietly unmade it. She had made none of the promises herself that had been broken; she had only stood beside the men who made them, and watched the breaking, and said nothing, because the staff in her hand had told her that was her place. It told her nothing now. She found, taking the sergeant's rifle from the cobbles herself rather than leaving it to be gathered up by whoever came after her, that a promise made with an empty hand sat easier on her than any she had carried with the staff full.

They carried off the rifles before the sun cleared the ridge, and the grain of the store after them, sack by sack, down the spoil-road to the weigh-house that had not weighed a Company pound in a fortnight and would not weigh one again. An old hewer named Cailean Odhar, who had worked the Muileann chrom galleries since before Mòrag was born, stood at the empty gate afterward with his hand flat against the stone the way a man rests his hand on a horse's neck, and said to no one in particular that he had carried coal out through that gate for six-and-thirty years and never once thought to see rifles carried out through it instead.

Word of Muileann chrom reached Ceann fhada by midday, carried by a runner from the Lamp-Watch who had not slept the night before and did not sleep that one either. The Company's coal quay at Ceann fhada had loaded barges for the coast since before the first pit at Muileann chrom was sunk, and the crews who worked it had spent a generation watching coal go out and little enough come back for it. They took the quay before the light failed, and that evening they scuttled two loaded barges across its mouth with their own hands, working chest-deep in cold water to knock the plugs from hulls they themselves had caulked the summer before.

A boatman called Pàdraig Reamhar, who had built the larger of the two barges with his father twenty years gone and had loaded her a hundred times since, stood on the quay afterward and watched her settle by the stern into the mud of the harbour bed, and said only that she had been a good boat, and that a good boat sunk on purpose was a different grief from a good boat wrecked, though he could not, pressed, have said exactly how. No coal left the hills by water again that year, nor the year after.

The Company's harbour-master at Ceann fhada, a mild man named Oswin Frayne who had kept the quay's tally for eleven years without once raising his voice at a docker, was put aboard the last boat still afloat and rowed out past his own sunk barges to the open sea, with instructions from the crews to make for the nearest harbour that still flew the Residency's flag and not to trouble himself with coming back. He went without a struggle, and was heard by the men who rowed him to say, quietly and more than once, that he had never in his eleven years asked to own a hill he had not been born on, and did not know, even now, quite what it was he had done to have this done to him in return. No one rowing him answered that. It was not, that morning, a question anyone in the hills had time to answer kindly.

## II. Of the Eleven Days

At Baile thais, where the walls round the pithead had gone up within the memory of most who now stood outside them, the constables did not lay down their rifles at first light nor at any light soon after. Seumas Ciar had led the seventh shift of the Baile thais gallery for three years, since the crew-leader before him had gone to Cnoc bheag for the forest council and not come back to the pit, and it was Seumas the crews looked to when the word came down from Muileann chrom that the rising had begun.

He had expected a morning like Muileann chrom's: a gate down, a yard of frightened boys, a promise given and kept before the sun was properly up. Baile thais gave him eleven days instead.

The walls had been raised, everyone in the camp had said at the time and said again now, against exactly this: a moor country, no second gate, a well dug inside the circuit before the first stone of the wall was laid, so that whatever came against Baile thais would have to starve it rather than storm it. Seumas had a hundred men and women outside those walls and a dozen ways to fill their hands, but no way at all to fill the constables' water-barrels with anything but time.

They counted the days as the older ones among them had once counted the days of the strike a generation before, the one the town's chroniclers still called the eleven days of Baile thais, though that had been a stoppage of work and this was a siege of stone, and Seumas found himself thinking, on the fourth night, that his own grandmother had stood roughly where he now stood, waiting out a different eleven days against a different wall of the same town's stubbornness, and had come through it. He kept the thought to himself. A siege does not go better for a man telling his own people that eleven days had been survived once before; it goes worse, if the second eleven prove longer than the first.

By the seventh day the bread the camp could spare had gone to a half-loaf a household, and the seventh shift's own children, who had at first treated the siege as a holiday from the gallery and had run the whole ring of it shouting insults up at the wall, had grown quiet and stayed close to their mothers' fires instead, in the particular way children go quiet when they have understood a thing without being told it outright. Seumas walked the ring each evening with a lamp shuttered against the wall's own watch, counting his own people rather than the constables', because it was easier, some nights, to know how many of his were still standing than to guess how many of theirs still had the will to hold.

They were not longer, in the end, by more than a day. On the twelfth morning a white cloth went up over the gatehouse, tied, the watchers below said afterward, to the same pole that had flown the Company's own flag the week before. The well inside had gone to mud two days past; the last barrel had been shared out by the constables' own sergeant in cupfuls, and even a cupful, by the eleventh night, was more than the barrel had left to give.

Word had come down to Seumas from the forest council two days before that, carried by a girl of fourteen who had walked from Cnoc bheag by night roads she would not afterward describe to anyone: whatever garrison yielded at Baile thais was to be sent to the coast, whole and unharmed, under whatever guard could be spared to see it done. Eilidh nic Raghnaill's own hand was on the message, the girl said, and no one asked her to say more than that, for the hand was known.

It did not sit easily with every man in that circle. A hewer named Coll Buidhe, whose brother had died of the coughing sickness in a Company hut the winter the walls went up, stood at Seumas's shoulder as the gate opened and said, low, that eleven days of thirst was mercy enough for men who had kept walls like that one standing over graves like his brother's. Seumas did not answer him at once. He watched the constables come out into the daylight blinking and unsteady, thirty-one of them by the tally, the youngest not much past the boy who had carried word from Cnoc bheag, and he thought of the promise given at Muileann chrom that morning a fortnight gone, and of what it would be worth, the next time a wall had to be talked down rather than starved down, if this one were not kept.

"They'll walk to the coast on their own legs," Seumas said, "and every one of them will walk there whole, because Eilidh says so, and because I say so after her." Coll Buidhe did not argue further. He fell in at the rear of the guard that walked the constables east that same afternoon, and saw them fed from the crews' own stores before the road, which no one had told him to do and no one afterward thought to remark on.

The Residency's letter-books, when Strake's clerks came at last to write up the loss of Baile thais, did not trouble to record that the garrison had been spared. It was not a mercy the Residency had looked for, and it was not a mercy the Residency returned.

## III. Of the Moor and the Forge

Before ever a shot was fired at Baile thais, the crew-leaders of Baile gharbh and the pits north of it had met by night on the moor above Cnoc naomh, where the shrine ground had filled two generations before with the dead of the green plague and the field beside it had never since been ploughed. Each man and woman who came to that meeting brought a stone of spoil carried up from their own pit's own heap, and the stones were set down together in the dark and left in a cairn that stood there after, uncarved, saying nothing to anyone who did not already know what it was for. They fixed the week of their rising in an hour, by the light of a single shielded lamp, and were gone from the moor before the shrine's own watcher came up at dawn to see to the flame, so that he found only the cairn, and no one to ask about it.

Within that week the pits of Baile gharbh rose together with the posts at Muileann shean and Tobar ghorm, and took them the way Muileann chrom had been taken, at first light, with more men outside the gates than inside them. The constables of the three posts were sent south on foot under no guard at all, their own legs and their own fear judged guard enough, and every one of them reached the coal road inside two days, footsore and talking loudly, to anyone who would stand still long enough to hear it, of how the whole of the north had gone over in a single week.

It had not, quite, gone over in a week; it had been three years coming, though few outside the crew-leaders' own circle knew how long, and one of those who did know was the smith Uilleam Gobha of Muileann dhearg, who had spent most of those three years turning the Company's own blasting-powder, bought a keg at a time on a Company chit for work that was never quite finished, into something the Company had not sold him and would not have sold him if he had asked.

Uilleam was thirty-and-nine that year, a broad, quiet man who had shod more Company mules than he could count and had grown, over the counting, to dislike the Company rather more than he disliked the mules. He forged pike-heads by day out of rail-iron torn from the disused sidings of the galleries, plain honest work that any smith on the island might have been set to and no agent thought twice about; by night, in the same forge with the shutters drawn, he packed the powder into tin cut from the coal sheds' own scrap and crimped the seams by hand, a slow and unforgiving labour that had already cost one man three fingers before Uilleam came to it and was determined it would cost him none.

He tried the first of them on the open moor behind the forge on a night with no moon, alone but for his eldest son holding the lamp a good way off, as Uilleam had told him to. It went off with a flat, ugly crack that put the birds up out of the whole valley and threw a spray of rock and scorched turf a man's height into the air, and Uilleam Gobha stood looking at the small smoking hole it had left in the ground for a long moment before he said, to no one, that it would do. The rebels' council wrote afterward, in the roll it kept of that first season, that without Uilleam Gobha's forge the rising in the hills could not have been fought at all past its opening weeks, and whoever wrote the line had the right of it, though Uilleam himself would only say that a smith does what a smith is asked to do, and left the rest for others to judge.

The coal road that ran from the capital up into the hills had one house standing across its middle stage that mattered more than any fort: An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, kept these four generations by the line of Seònaid Bhàn, and kept now by her great-grandson Aonghas, who had spent the autumn before filling its loft with more men than any innkeeper's licence allowed and telling the Company's inspectors, when they asked, that the extra hands were for the harvest. On the day the word came up from Muileann chrom, Aonghas shut the gates of his own house across the road with his own hands, and the men in the loft came down armed, and the road between the capital and the hills was cut at its half-way stage and stayed cut for the whole of that year and longer. No coal came down it, and no column went up it, without Aonghas mac Coinnich's leave, which he did not give.

## IV. Of the Rising of the Capital

Word of the hills reached Cathair dhearg by the third day, carried faster than any rider by the simple fact that half the capital had kin working a gallery somewhere north of it and had been waiting, without quite admitting to themselves that they waited, for exactly this news. The custodians who had sat shut out of their own hall since the edict of seizure two years before did not wait to be asked twice. They broke the Residency's locks on the council-hall doors with a coal-hammer borrowed from the smithy across the square, and sat down again in the chairs that had been theirs before any lock was hung on them, and the roll they kept from that day forward opens without a word of apology for the two years it does not cover.

The townsfolk of Cathair dhearg held the bridge that the Company had built for its own coal-carts against the constables sent up in haste from the harbour, and barricaded it with the very carts it had been built to carry, turned on their sides and lashed together with chain from the ironmonger's own stores. It was not a fortification any soldier would have approved, and it held all the same, for three days, until the constables who had been sent to clear it were called back to guard the harbour instead and never came again.

At Cathair dhomhain the townsfolk broke open the stone warehouse the Company had raised along the lake shore in the first years of the treaty, the first building the strangers had ever owned outright on Dia-thìr and the one the old tellings had asked their listeners to remember. They carried off its tools, its rope, its nails and its stored powder for the council's own companies, and left the warehouse itself standing empty and unlocked, its doors swinging, much as the huts of Cnoc gharbh had once been left standing in the years of the green plague, though this time a reckoning long owed, and at last collected, had emptied it.

At Muileann mhin, a river port a morning's walk east of the capital on the Abhainn fhionn, the townsfolk argued for three days over a decision that the towns north and south of them had each made in an afternoon. It was said afterward, by those who had stood in the market square through all three days of it, that the argument turned less on where their sympathies lay than on where their boats did business, for Muileann mhin's living came from carrying grain and timber to whichever bank paid for the carrying, and a boatman is slower than a hewer to burn a bridge he may need again. On the third evening the town sent its boats to the council at Cathair dhearg all the same, and through that winter and the hard one that followed them the Abhainn fhionn carried food down to the capital by night as steadily as it had ever carried Company coal down by day.

At Ros dhomhain, Lionel Strake drew what remained of the Administration back inside the Residency's walls and fortified the Company's own quarter of the harbour, setting what constables he still trusted at every gate and trusting the rest to stand where he could watch them. The town below the Residency's hill, Dia-thìrich almost to the last household, neither rose against him nor lifted a hand to defend him, and it was this, more than any wall, that the letter-books afterward called the strangest siege of that first year: a garrison shut up against a silence rather than an army, in a city that went about its ordinary business at its ordinary pace on every street but the one that led up to the gate, and would not meet a constable's eye on that one.

Strake wrote to the Company's directors over the sea that autumn, in a hand the clerks who copied it afterward called steadier than the news it carried deserved, that the harbour below him held itself apart from the fighting only because it had not yet decided which side would leave it better off, and that a soldier who mistook that silence for loyalty would not keep the town a season. He asked for more men, and more powder, and a ship before winter closed the northern passage. He was given, before the year was out, rather less of all three than he had asked for, and said nothing further about it in any letter the copyists afterward found.

## V. Of the Ford of Àth chiar

It could not last, from the Residency's side of it, without an answer, and the answer Strake sent was Major Peter Ashdown, with what constables and Company guards could be spared from the harbour, marching up the coal road to open it again and relieve whatever of the hills could still be relieved.

Ashdown found the road cut at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, as every rider before him had found it that season, and turned north about it along the older cart-track that ran down to the ford at Àth chiar, where the coal road crossed the river before climbing again into the hills proper. It was there, on the far bank, that the crews who had held Baile thais a fortnight before were waiting for him, dug in along the willow scrub with what rifles the walled store had given up and what Uilleam Gobha's forge had since sent up the road in a cart with a false floor of turnips.

The ford ran deep that season with a wet spring behind it, and Ashdown's column went into it waist-high and in poor order, the guns of the rear companies held up over men's heads and the horses of the baggage train balking at the current. The fire that met them from the far bank was neither heavy nor especially well aimed, most of those who held it being hewers and quarrymen who had never fired at a man before that week, but it did not need to be well aimed to do its work in a column standing chest-deep in moving water with nowhere to go but forward or back. Ashdown chose back. He lost forty men to the river and the fire together, and the whole of his baggage besides, abandoned on the near bank in the retreat and carried off by the same crews who had held the willows.

The rebels' own dead numbered nineteen, and it was Seumas Ciar, who had brought his Baile thais men down to the ford at the forest council's summons and stood with them the whole of that wet afternoon, who carried the word back up to Cnoc bheag of who among them had not come home. The council's clerk, a woman named Fionnuala who had kept few enough happy rolls in her three years of keeping any roll at all, wrote the nineteen names down that same night by lamp-light, each with the pit or the crew it had come from, and read the whole list aloud the next morning to whoever stood near enough the hall to hear it, once, plainly, without a word added to it.

They were buried within the week on the moor above Cnoc naomh, near enough the cairn the crew-leaders had raised there in the dark before ever a shot was fired, so that the two piles of stone stood within sight of one another from that season on: the one raised in secret for a rising not yet begun, the other raised in the open for the first of what the rising had already cost. The council's roll gives the nineteen names in full, and does not say, because by the custom of that roll it was never asked to say, which was harder to keep: the stone laid down before the fighting, or the stone laid down after it.
