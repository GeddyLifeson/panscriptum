# Of the Council of Six

*AE 5 – 6*

## I. Of the Straight Town

Of all the towns of the west in the first years of the strangers, the copyists of the cellar found only one that kept two books. They were a pair of plain calf-bound ledgers, one written in Dia-thìris and dated by the moon, the other in the humans' tongue and dated by the humans' day, and they stood side by side on a shelf in the net-loft of Baile dhìreach. The pages match one for one. Where a hand in one book has struck a line through a word, a hand in the other has struck the line through the word that answers it. The rolls of the custodians are full of things done to the island by strangers. These two books hold a thing the island and the strangers did together, in a town of some fifty souls at the end of a long straight street that ran down to a quay.

Baile dhìreach lay on the south-west coast, a day's walk from the capital and a morning's sail below Cuan shean. Traders of Òrd Mhanannain had built it in the long quiet centuries, and it had kept its one street and its one quay ever since, with Òrd na Cloiche at the temple city of Inis thais a few miles east along the shore and the weather coming in off the open sea from the west. The people were fishers and net-makers and salters of herring. They had a well at the head of the street, a salt-house at its foot, a loft over the salt-house where the nets were hung to mend, and a strip of grey shingle north of the quay where no house stood, because the shingle above the wrack-line had been held in keeping for the whole town since before anyone's grandmother could remember, and nobody might build on it.

Eilidh nic Ruairidh was twenty-three that spring, and she mended nets on the shingle, as her mother had done before her and her mother's mother. The copyists knew her from both books. She was a broad, quick woman with scarred fingers who could work a needle blind. She was at her frames on a grey morning of Bearnach, the gap-moon between the last of the stores and the first of the green, when the humans' boat came round the point from the north.

## II. Of the Rule of the Shingle

It was a broad heavy boat, rowed by six men and deep in the water, and it carried timber: sawn plank, which nobody at Baile dhìreach had seen in such a quantity, stacked to the gunwales and lashed down, with tools laid on top in sacking. The humans had come ashore at Cuan shean four winters before, and every soul on the coast had seen them by now, or said so. Harrow had lately come back from over the sea with four ships where he had taken three, and carpenters in them. The carpenters had been looking up and down the coast for a beach with good shelving and good shelter, where they might mend the small craft of the post and build new ones; for the anchorage at Cuan shean was crowded, and the townsfolk there had begun to count the humans' fires.

Eilidh stood up from the frame with the needle still in her hand. The boat came in on the tide and grounded on the shingle, on the town's shingle, above the wrack. A man jumped out into the shallows and walked up to her, dripping to the knee.

"Dia dhut," he said. He said it badly, with all the sounds in the wrong places, but she knew it.

He was a big man with a carpenter's shoulders and a sunburnt head going bald at the crown, and he held his hat in both hands. Behind him the others were already handing out plank. One of them, a younger man with long arms and black hair tied back with a thong, had found a split cask in the wrack and was turning it over and looking at the staves, as a man looks at a horse he has been offered.

"Iain mac Chaluim," said Eilidh, and pointed up the street. That was all the answer she had, and it was the right one, for Iain mac Chaluim was the best fisherman on that stretch of the coast and the man the town sent to talk when talking had to be done. He was already coming down the street with half the town behind him.

So they met on the shingle, the town and the strangers, with the plank between them. The big man was William Rook, carpenter, lately of Harrow's ships. He had some forty words of Dia-thìris and the tall young man by the cask had perhaps twenty, and between them, with the help of a Cuan shean boy they had brought as far as the point and who then lost his courage and would not come further, they made the town understand what they wanted. They wanted the shingle north of the quay, for a slip and a yard, and would pay for it in iron and nails and tools, and build their own houses.

The shingle was the town's in keeping. It was nobody's to sell. Iain mac Chaluim said so, and Rook frowned, and said a word in his own tongue to the others, and they all frowned. Eilidh saw that they had no word for it. To them a piece of ground belonged to somebody or it belonged to nobody, and if it belonged to nobody a man might take it.

It was old Seonag nic Aoidh of the salt-house who settled it, on the second day of talking. The shingle could not be sold, she said. A man could be let work on it all the same, as the herring-curers of Inis thais had once been let work on it in a year when their own beach silted. The curers had kept the town's rule while they were there, and gone home when they were done, and nobody had thought the worse of them. If the strangers would keep the town's rule, then they might stay under it. And she said the rule over, the whole of it, standing on the shingle with her shawl pulled up against the wind: the well was drawn from in turn; no fire was lit on the shingle after dark in the herring season, when the boats were steering home by the lamps at the quay-head; no net was set and no hammer lifted on the night of the full moon or the night of the dark, which were the rest-nights; the dead of the sea were remembered in the black moon; the shingle above the wrack was held for the town and built on by no one.

Rook heard it through twice. He asked about the houses. They could not live in the boat.

There was a long argument then about whether a house of plank on a sill of stones was a house built on the shingle or only a tent that stood up by itself. The town decided in the end, on no grounds anyone afterwards could give, that it was a tent. Rook said that his men would keep the rule. He said it in his own tongue first, and then in his forty words, and then he put out his hand, and after a while Iain mac Chaluim took it.

The roll of Inis thais, to which Baile dhìreach sent its news, set down on {{date:IV-0021}} that the strangers had been taken in at Baile dhìreach under the town's one rule. They were seven men. There were Rook and two other carpenters, a sawyer and his boy, a sailmaker who stayed only the summer, and the young man with long arms, who was a cooper, and whose name was Samuel Hale.

## III. Of Rope and Knot

They built through Bealltach and Ògach, under a sky that stayed high and grey and let the work go on. The slip went down first, of split logs bedded in the shingle; then a long shed with one end open to the sea; then the house, which was not a house, of sawn plank set on a sill of beach stones, with a door in the middle and a window of real glass that the whole town came to look through. It stood a little crooked on the shingle, with its back to the west wind, and the gulls liked its roof. The town called it the tent, and went on calling it the tent after it had a hearth and a chimney. The copyists found it in the Dia-thìris book as *am pùball* and in the humans' as "the first house", and nobody in either book seems to have thought the other name wrong.

Samuel Hale was the first of them to be any use to the town. The herring barrels of Baile dhìreach were old, and half of them wept brine at every seam. Eilidh's father, Ruairidh, who salted herring for his living and had done since he was a boy, had a whole row of them standing idle behind the salt-house because the staves had shrunk and the hoops rusted through. One evening in Ògach he found the cooper among them with his sleeves rolled up. Samuel had taken one down to its staves and laid them out on the shingle in order, like the bones of a fish.

Ruairidh stood over him for a long time, saying nothing, and then went and got the next one.

By the turn of the summer every barrel in the town was tight. Samuel took no iron for it and no fish. He took, when he took anything, a bowl of whatever was in the pot, and he ate it sitting on the salt-house step with the barrels drying round him, and then he would take out a scrap of paper and a stub of lead and ask for words.

Most of all he asked Eilidh, because she was on the shingle most. He would come down in the evening when the light was going long and yellow over the water and sit on an upturned boat by her frames and point. *Ròp*, she told him, and he said it back and wrote it. Then he showed her his word for it. She said his word back and he laughed at the way she said it, and then he wrote hers again under his own, very carefully, with a line between.

They came to knots in the third week, and there they stayed a long time. She had a word for every knot she tied, and there were many: the knot that joins two ropes of different thickness, the knot that holds on a wet spar and will not slip, the knot that a child learns first and the knot that only the old net-makers could still throw one-handed. He had a word for every one of his, and he tied them for her in a length of line from the boat, and she found that most of his were hers under other names, and some were not. There was one of his she had never seen, a knot for hanging a weight that tightened as the weight pulled; and one of hers he had never seen, the net-maker's mesh knot of the south-west, which she could tie faster than he could follow with his eyes. He made her do it slowly, eleven times, and on the twelfth he tied it himself and held it up, and she said *snaidhm*, which is only the word for any knot at all, and he wrote that down too.

Word had come along the coast by then of what had happened at Cathair dhearg in the season of words, three years before: how the custodian Gormshuil nic Fhearchair had laid broken coal on a table and named forty colours in it, and the cleric of the humans had written one word for them all and put the coal aside to make room. The carters told it at the well. Eilidh had not thought much about it. It had seemed to her a thing that might happen between great people in a great hall.

She thought about it one evening at the end of Teasach, in her father's house, when Samuel had come up for his bowl and the rain had driven him in from the step. By the old law of the burning the house had its measured handful of coal for the hearth, and her father had laid a lump of it on the peat to catch, as he did on wet nights. It split in the heat and the colours came up at the break, the deep green under the black, and the red that grows if you breathe on it. Eilidh looked at it, as anyone would. Samuel saw her look, and looked where she was looking, and asked her what it was.

She told him. She gave him the green and the red and the grey that the old lumps have at the edge. He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and stared into the fire until his eyes watered.

"It's black," he said, in his own tongue, and then in hers, which he could manage by then: "Dubh."

"It is not black."

"I see black. I'm sorry." He took out his paper. "Say them again."

She said them again, and he wrote them, all three, in her spelling as near as he could come to it. He had no word of his own to put above them, and so he left the space above them empty, and drew his line under it just the same. Her father watched the whole business from the other side of the hearth with his pipe gone out.

That Lùghnas the humans' slip sent down its first boat. She was a small one, a coaster of the humans' pattern with a deeper keel than the boats of the south-west, and she was built for Iain mac Chaluim, who paid for her in fish and labour and in his own time on the saw-pit. The town gave her their rope. They launched her on the first evening of the Lùnastal gathering, with the fires going up on the headland behind the town, and the humans stood on the slip with their hats off while Iain's wife poured sea water over her bow and asked Manannan to keep her. Rook asked afterwards what the words meant. Iain told him, and Rook nodded and said that his own people would have said much the same to their own God, and there the matter rested.

## IV. Of the Compact Written Twice

The trouble that made the council came from the dues.

By the time of Pailteach there were three boats of the post lying off the shingle for mending and a fourth hauled up on the slip, and the town's quay had never seen so much traffic. From of old the harbour dues of Baile dhìreach had gone to the custodians at Cathair dhearg, a few coppers' worth a year in fish and salt, carried up the road by whoever was going that way. Now the post's boats paid dues too, and the post at Ros dhomhain, which kept its books to the last line, wrote to ask where its money was going, and why a harbour its own men had half rebuilt should pay the whole of it to a council that had never sent anyone to see it. At the same time the custodians of Inis thais, to whom Baile dhìreach had always sent its news, wrote to ask by what rule seven strangers were keeping a yard on a shingle held in keeping, and for how long.

Iain mac Chaluim sat on the salt-house step with both letters, one of which he could not read, and said that the town would have to write down its rule.

"Write it how?" said Seonag.

"Twice," said Rook, who had the other letter. "Once in each."

That was in the last days of Pailteach. The writing took until the middle of Damhrach, and the copyists, who knew something of the labour of putting one tongue beside another, reckoned it the hardest writing of its length in the whole age. The custodian of Inis thais sent over his clerk, a young man with ink to the wrist and a portable desk on a strap, to write the Dia-thìris book. Rook wrote the humans' book himself, in a round slow hand, with his tongue between his teeth. They sat at the long mending-table in the net-loft over the salt-house, among the hung nets and the smell of tar and old fish, with a lamp at each end of the table and the sea going on below the window. Six sat to make the compact, three of the town and three of the strangers: Iain mac Chaluim, Seonag nic Aoidh, and the pilot Murchadh mac Iain for the town, and for the strangers Rook, the carpenter Nathan Cobb, and the sawyer Peter Lusk. They did not intend to be the council. They became it by sitting.

Eilidh was fetched on the third evening, because the harbour clauses had come to moorings and nobody at the table could agree what the humans' words for them meant. The humans had one word for a rope that holds a boat to a quay and another for a rope that holds her to an anchor, and a third for the ring the rope goes through, and the town had words for all of these and for four more besides, and the clerk from Inis thais knew none of them, having been bred in a temple city where the boats belonged to other people. Samuel was fetched for the same reason from the other side. The two of them sat at the foot of the table with his papers between them, the papers that had her words written under his with a line between, and turned them over until they found what was wanted. Then Eilidh said it in Dia-thìris and the clerk wrote it, and Samuel said it in the humans' tongue and Rook wrote it, and the line down the middle, which nobody wrote, lay between them all the same.

Not everything could be found so. There was a night-long quarrel over the shingle. The humans' book wanted to say who owned it. The town's book could only say who kept it. In the end the humans' book said that the shingle was the town's and the town's book said it was kept for the town, and both of them said that no one would build on it, and Rook said that his tent would be taken down when the council told him, and wrote that in too.

There was a quarrel over the rest-nights, which the humans agreed to keep, and a matching quarrel over the humans' day of rest, which the town had never heard of. The humans kept one day in seven for their God, whose name was Crìosd, and on it they would do no work and wished that no one would hammer near them while they prayed. The town agreed not to hammer on the slip on that day. Nobody at Baile dhìreach had ever hammered on the slip at all before the humans came, and Murchadh said so, and the clerk from Inis thais wrote it into the Dia-thìris book before anyone could stop him, and it is there still, in the margin.

The dues were shared. Half went up the road to the custodians at Cathair dhearg as before, and half went to the humans' post at Ros dhomhain, and the council kept the harbour in repair out of what the town took for mooring, and nobody in either book was to pay twice.

Last of all they came to the chair. There were six at the table, three and three, and some one of them had to speak first and speak last. They agreed that the chair should go between Iain mac Chaluim and William Rook, a year apiece. Then they found that they did not agree what a year was. The town's year turned at Samhain, with the dark half, and was counted by moons; the humans' year turned in the dead of winter on a day that stood still in their calendar and wandered in the moon's. If the chair turned at Samhain, Iain would hold it for a fortnight. Iain said that a fortnight was as long as he had ever wanted to hold anything. Rook laughed, and said that a year was a year, and that the chair should turn at the humans' new year, since that at least could be written down and would be in the same place next time. So it was agreed, and so it was written in both books: that Iain mac Chaluim should hold the chair until the humans' year turned, and William Rook for the year after, and so on in turn for as long as they lived or the town kept them.

On {{date:IV-0022}} the compact of Baile dhìreach was read in both tongues on the quay, in a west wind with rain in it. The clerk read the Dia-thìris and Rook the humans'. The whole town stood and listened to both, the humans to the one they could not follow and the town to the one it could not follow, and then everyone who could write put a name in both books, and those who could not made a mark. The Dia-thìris book is dated by the island's count of years and the moon, and the humans' book by their day and month and year; neither gives the other's date, and each has a blank at the foot of its page where the other's might have gone.

The council of six sat for the first time eight days later, on {{date:IV-0023}}, in the net-loft, with Iain in the chair. They settled a quarrel over a mooring ring, sent the first half of the dues up the road to Cathair dhearg in a sack with a boy to carry it, and sent the second half round by boat to Ros dhomhain. Then they drank to the harbour, with the humans' spirit and the town's heather ale, and the clerk from Inis thais drank more of both than he should, and was put to bed in the tent.

## V. Of Her Father's No

Samuel came to Ruairidh's door on the night of Samhain.

It was the worst night of the year for it, and Eilidh told him so afterwards, and he said that he had not known. The whole town was on the headland above the quay. The fire was going up against a sky full of wet wind, and the sparks were driving inland, and the young men were running the embers along the street, sunwise about the well and down to the water. The old year was going out into the dark half and the dead were near. The humans had come up the path as far as the last cairn and stood there in a knot together, watching, with the firelight on their faces, the way men stand at the door of a house where a wake is going on and they are not sure they are bidden. Rook had taken his hat off again. Nobody had told them to, and nobody told them to put it on.

Eilidh had gone down to the house for more peat. When she came out with her creel her father was standing in the door, and Samuel was on the step below him in the rain, and neither of them was saying anything.

She knew at once what had been said, because the cooper had his hat in his hands the way Rook had held his on the shingle in Bearnach, and because her father's face had gone the colour it went when a barrel he had packed himself was opened and found spoiled.

"No," said her father, in Dia-thìris, and then in the few words of the humans' tongue he had got from the salt-house, which were mostly the names of fish: "No. Go."

Samuel looked at her past her father's shoulder. Then he put his hat on and went back down the street towards the shingle, walking carefully on the wet stones, and he did not go back up to the fire.

Her father would not talk to her that night. In the morning, when the headland was black and smoking and the gulls were at the scraps, he talked a great deal.

He had nothing against the cooper, who was a good cooper. A council, he said, was one thing and a hearth another, and he had put his mark in both books on the quay like everyone else and had meant it; the humans were welcome to the shingle and the slip and the well in its turn and the half of the dues, and none of that had anything to do with his daughter. He said that Samuel Hale had a country somewhere over the eastern water, behind Ceò Mhanannain, that nobody in Baile dhìreach had ever seen and nobody ever would, and that any man with a country might go back to it. He said that her children would be neither one thing nor the other. And then he said the thing he was really afraid of, standing at the salt-house door with his arms folded, as though it had only just occurred to him.

"When the chair goes over to the carpenter," he said, "they will have the town. You watch. A year of him, and they will have it."

"There are three of us and three of them," said Eilidh. "They will have it the way Iain has it now."

"Iain is one of us."

"Rook put his mark in our book," said Eilidh. "He wrote that he would take his house down if we told him."

"He wrote it in his book," said her father. "Who reads his book?"

She said that she did, a little. Samuel had taught her some of the letters over the summer, in the evenings on the upturned boat, and she could make out her own name in his hand and some of the words for rope. Her father looked at her as if she had told him she could fly, and went into the salt-house and banged the door.

For the rest of Samhnas she mended nets on the shingle and the cooper worked in the shed, a hundred paces off, and they did not speak. The whole town knew why. The two books, which had nothing to say about it, lay on the shelf in the net-loft.

In the black moon the council sent its bidding to Inis thais. The compact had a clause, put in at the clerk's asking, that any town of the coast might sit with them on the same terms; and Iain mac Chaluim, who had begun to enjoy the chair more than he admitted, wanted the temple city to know it was asked. Murchadh took the letter along the shore in his boat, the two books' worth of it, both tongues, and brought back word that the custodian had read it through twice and had said that he would write.

On the last night of Dubhlas the town remembered its drowned, as every harbour of the west did at the dark of the year, and set lamps along the quay for them. The humans came down from the tent and stood with the townsfolk. Samuel stood at the end of the line, and when the lamps were lit he took off his hat and said a prayer to Crìosd in his own tongue for the men he had known who had gone over the side in the northern sea on the voyage out, before ever they saw the island. Eilidh did not know the words. She set a lamp for them at the end of the quay, and her father saw her do it, and said nothing.

## VI. Of the Turning of the Humans' Year

The humans' year turned in the middle of Faolach, the wolf-moon, the storm-time at the year's dead end, when the nets were ashore for mending and the boats hauled high and the days were hardly worth the name.

The council met that morning in the net-loft with the rain drumming on the slates. Iain mac Chaluim stood up from the head of the table and moved down one place, and William Rook moved up one, and sat, and looked for a long time at the table in front of him, as if he had not expected it to be the same table. That was the whole of it. Both books enter it in a line. The humans' book gives the day and the year; the town's gives the moon and says only that the carpenter took the chair as the compact bade him, and that the fisherman gave it.

Before they had done anything else a boy came up the ladder, wet through, with a letter from Inis thais.

It was short, and it was courteous, and it was written in Dia-thìris only. The custodian of Inis thais thanked the council of Baile dhìreach for its bidding and declined it. He wished the town well of its compact. But, he wrote, one town may be a trial, and two would be a rule; and the city of Lia Fàil would not make a rule of a thing that had been tried for three moons, and would watch, and would write again if there was cause.

Rook had the letter read to him twice, once in each tongue, and at the end of the second reading he said, "That's fair," and had it laid in the Dia-thìris book, between the page of the handing-over and the page of the next matter; and the council went on to the next matter, which was a boat of the post that had dragged her anchor in the night and fouled the town's moorings, and whose master had refused to pay for the damage.

Eilidh's father was waiting at the foot of the ladder for that one, because it was his mooring.

She saw it all from the salt-house door. Rook came down the ladder with the other five behind him and walked out along the quay in the rain to look at the ring and the torn rope. He looked for a long time. He sent for the post's master, who was one of his own people, a man of Harrow's ships, and told him in the humans' tongue that he would pay the town's rate for the rope and the town's rate for the ring, and pay it in iron by weight, and pay it that day. The master argued. Rook heard him out and said the same again, louder. Then he turned to Ruairidh and told him in his forty words of Dia-thìris, most of them wrong, that he would have his rope, and asked him whether the town's rate was fair, because he had written it into the book from Seonag's saying and did not know it for himself.

Ruairidh said that it was fair.

That night the great storm of that winter came in off the west, three days of it, the worst in years. The sea came over the quay and into the foot of the street. On the second night it began to drive up the shingle towards the boats, and the whole town went down in the dark to haul them higher, and the humans came out of the tent with ropes and blocks and a capstan-bar off the slip, and nobody asked them to. Eilidh was on a line with her father and Seonag's grandsons and Samuel Hale and Nathan Cobb, heaving the coaster that the humans had built for Iain mac Chaluim up over the crest of the shingle, a foot at a time. The rain came at them level. Nobody could hear anything, and so nobody spoke anything but the one word, on the pull, which in the end they were all shouting in the town's tongue, humans and all. When the boat was over the crest Samuel threw a hitch round the post at the top of the shingle to hold her, and it was the net-maker's knot of the south-west, and he threw it one-handed.

Her father saw it. He was holding the lantern.

The storm blew itself out on the third morning. Nothing in the town had fallen down but the end of the salt-house chimney, and the humans' tent, which had lost half its roof, and which the humans put back up in a day and a half with Ruairidh's help, on the shingle where it had stood, the council having said nothing about taking it down.

The town had watched the carpenter in the chair for half a moon by then. He had sat under the same slates and at the same table, and settled what came before him by the same rule the town had always kept, and written it into his own book in his round slow hand, and nothing whatever had happened to the town that it could see, except that it had been paid for a rope.

One evening at the end of Faolach Ruairidh came down onto the shingle where his daughter was at her frames. He stood beside her for a while looking at the tent, where there was a lamp in the glass window, and the cooper's shadow going to and fro.

"Tell the cooper," he said, "that if he wants his bowl filled he can come up for it himself, like a christened man."

He meant it as a joke. He had learned the word from the humans that winter, and had no notion what it meant, except that they used it of a man who behaved as he should. Eilidh laughed so much that she had to sit down on the shingle, and she did not tell him.

That was the end of the first winter of the council of six. The two books stood on the shelf in the net-loft side by side, the one dated by the moon and the other by the day, with the letter from Inis thais between the pages of the one and nothing between the pages of the other. The lamp at the quay-head burned for the boats. The tent kept its crooked place on the shingle, with its back to the west wind and a new patch on its roof, and the gulls came back to it.
