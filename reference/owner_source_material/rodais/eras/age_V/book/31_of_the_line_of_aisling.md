# Of Sloinneadh Aisling

*AE 126 – 131*

## I. Of the Roll at Àth ìseal

Cailean mac Eachainn had been two years in Seilear Muileann chaol before he learned to stop reading the rolls he copied.

It was not a lesson anyone taught him. Seònag Bhàn, the eldest of Na Trì Teaghlaichean, told him only that a hand copies faster once it stops caring what it copies, and that a fast hand kept a place in the cellar longer than a slow one, whatever else was true of it. Cailean had taken her at her word the way a young man takes an old woman's word when he has not yet found a reason to doubt it, and by the second winter he could set down a page of births, burials, fines and shifts lost without his eye once catching on a name. The lamp smoked, and the roll in front of him went from one pile to the other, and that was the whole of the work, and he was glad of it in the way a man is glad of a thing that asks nothing further of him.

The roll of the camp at Àth ìseal reached him in the tenth month of the hundred and twenty-sixth year, bundled with three others from the eastern custodians whose rolls Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's own clerks could not be trusted to keep private, and whose copies therefore came down to Muileann chaol to be made again in a hand Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir never saw. Àth ìseal was a small camp, a hundred souls or so scraping at the thin seam above Achadh dhomhain, and its custodian had kept the new camp's people on the same pages as her own from its first winter, so that the roll ran heavier than its size would suggest: two settlements' worth of births and burials and shifts lost, pressed into one book by a hand that had grown worse with every winter it was asked to write in. Cailean did not know the custodian's name and had no cause to learn it. He set the roll on the stand, trimmed the lamp, and began.

He had copied perhaps sixty such lines that month, and read none of them, when his hand slowed over the marriages.

A camp's marriages are not written the way a town's are. Where a town custodian sets down only the two names and the year, a camp custodian writes them as the households at the ford first recited them, in An Aois Ailein, each side's line carried seven names back before the vow is called good: so-and-so, son of so-and-so, son of so-and-so, to the seventh father, and the same again for the bride, and only then the marrying. It is the oldest habit the custodians keep and, Cailean had always privately thought, the most useless one for a man paid to make a fair copy of a roll in the fewest hours he can manage; seven names back for every hewer and every hewer's wife in a hundred camps was a great deal of ink spent on the dead.

He had copied a hundred such lines that season and never once read past the first name in any of them. This one he read, and could not afterward have said why, except that the seventh name of it was a name nobody gives a child now, and something in his eye caught on it the way a boot catches on a root it has walked over a hundred times without ever quite tripping on.

*Donnchadh mac Thormoid, hewer,* the marriage began, and carried Donnchadh's line seven fathers back to a name that meant nothing to Cailean at all, and then gave the bride's line the same courtesy, and then the marrying. Cailean copied it as he copied everything, and went on, and would have thought no more of it, except that further down the same page, in the same custodian's failing hand, a birth was entered with a small mark beside it, a third mark, neither the mark for a birth that lived nor the mark for one that did not, one Cailean had seen perhaps four times in a year of copying, always against one name only, never explained in any book he had been given to copy from.

*Aisling.*

Every copyist in the cellar knew what the camps said of a child marked so. Cailean had said it himself once, as a boy at Muileann chaol, the way boys say a thing they have heard grown men say and do not trouble themselves to believe: *the girl who does not grow.* He had not thought of it in years. He thought of it now, sitting with the lamp guttering, and found that he did not want to go on to the next line until he had gone back and read the first one properly.

He read it three times. Donnchadh, hewer, son of Tormod, and Tormod's line running back seven names to one that struck him as being older than a hewer's grandfather's grandfather had any business being, in a hand that gave no sign of thinking it strange. He sat with it a long while by the lamp, long enough that Seònag Bhàn came down once to see why his pile had stopped growing and went away again without asking, because she had kept the cellar long enough to know the look of a man who has found a thing he does not yet understand is his to keep or to give away.

In the end Cailean finished the page, as the work required of him. Then he took a scrap of the cellar's poorer paper, the kind kept for false starts, and copied the seven names of Donnchadh's line onto it in his own hand, folded it once, and put it inside his coat where the ledger's ink could not mark it. He told himself, walking home along the millrace that night with the folded paper against his chest like a stolen thing, that he only meant to ask someone who would know whether such a line as Donnchadh's could truly run as old as it looked on the page, and that this was no more than any careful copyist owed to a roll he had failed to understand. A thin excuse, and he knew it, and walked on anyway. In the morning, instead of walking down to the cellar, he walked out along the hill road to Tobar dhìreach, where it was known, if a man asked at all, that the last of Clann na Ceiste who still said the old lines kept a house not far from the standing slab.

## II. Of Tobar dhìreach

It was raining by the time he reached the house, the fine grey rain that comes off the western sea in the tenth month and does not trouble to announce its coming with wind first, and he stood at the door a moment working out what he meant to say before he knocked, and found he had worked out nothing at all.

The woman who opened to him was old in the way the very old are old, past the age where a stranger can guess at the years by looking, with a face gone to folds around eyes that had not dimmed with it. She looked at him the way, he thought afterward, a woman looks at a fox that has come to her door in daylight: not afraid of it, and not glad of it either, only certain that its coming meant some wrong had been done somewhere, to bring it to her door at all.

"You'll want to come in out of that," she said, "since you've come this far in it," and stood aside.

Her name was Ealasaid, and she told it him herself when he had not thought to ask, the way the old sometimes give a thing freely that a younger person would have made him earn. The house was one room and a loft, with a fire built up higher than the day's cold quite called for, and no sign in it of the things Cailean had half expected to find in the house of the last of a house that said the old lines: no bones, no carved stones; nothing but a chair drawn close to the fire and a shelf of ordinary crockery and, over the mantel, a length of plain cord hung in loops that he did not understand and did not ask about.

He told her what he had, badly, starting twice and beginning again, until she stopped him with a raised hand and said only, "Read it me, then, and stop telling me what you think of it before I've heard it," which steadied him more than any kindness would have.

He drew out the folded scrap and read the seven names of Donnchadh's line aloud by the fire, slowly, the way a man reads a thing he is not sure he has copied without error. Ealasaid sat with her eyes shut through all of it, her hands still in her lap, and did not open them until he had said the seventh name, the old one, the one that had first caught his eye in the cellar.

"Where did you get that?"

He told her: the roll of Àth ìseal, a hewer's marriage, copied down the way the camp custodians write such things, nothing more.

"That is Somhairle's line," she said, and said it the way a woman says a fact so old it has stopped surprising even her. "The son who asked for nothing. We have kept that line since Somhairle himself, and in a thousand years nobody of it has come asking after it. They went down into the valleys and forgot us, all of them, generation on generation, until there was no one left above ground who remembered there was anything to come asking for." She was quiet a while, looking at the fire rather than at him. "Whose is the child?"

"Donnchadh's," Cailean said. "His daughter. Her name is Aisling."

He had not meant to say more than that. Something in the old woman's stillness pulled the rest out of him almost before he had decided to give it: the mark beside the birth, the whisper the camps carried about a girl who did not grow, the boy's grin with which he himself had once repeated it, not believing a word of it, the way boys repeat any strange thing that is said around them.

Ealasaid said nothing at all for so long that Cailean began to think she had not heard him, or had heard him and judged the matter beneath answering. Then, without turning her head, in a voice that had gone flat and careful, as a woman reads a thing off a wall rather than out of her own mind, she said: "When strangers hold this island, the fire will wake again in your line."

She looked at him then. "That is the end of the vigil, as we keep it. I have said it every winter of my life at the hearth on the night of the lightning, and I never once thought I would live to see what it meant." She rose, slowly, with the particular care of the very old getting up from a low chair, and Cailean understood without being told that what she meant to give him required her to be standing to give it. "The lines are said standing," she said, as if he had asked. "Sit where you are and write, if you've brought anything to write with. I'll not say it twice in a night."

He had a stub of pencil and the back of the same poor paper. She gave it him as it is given at a naming, from the child back to the fire. Clann a' Chlachair has kept the years of its dead beside their names since the mason, but it says them to no stranger except where they are strange; and so in the short form only the long lives are given their years. Cailean wrote it down as she said it, as fast as his hand would go:

> "Aisling, daughter of Donnchadh, who hewed in the deep galleries of Achadh dhomhain;
> son of Tormod, who carried water up to Doire mhòr two summers, and then went down to Àth ìseal;
> son of Ruairidh of Doire mhòr, who stood behind Beathag Dhubh when she sent A' Chompanaidh down the hill;
> and seventy generations whose names we keep;
> son of Fionnlagh, who stood on the quay at Seann Skell when the ships went out, and did not go;
> and a hundred and sixteen generations whose names we keep;
> son of Ceallach, the last of us whose years were long; and all the days of Ceallach were a hundred and forty years, and he died;
> son of Gille-Brìde, who carried a coal of A' Chiad Lasair home to the hills; and all the days of Gille-Brìde were two hundred and twenty-four years, and he died;
> and six generations whose names we keep, each longer-lived than the one after, and their days were three hundred and eight years, and four hundred and thirty-one, and five hundred and ninety-seven, and eight hundred and twenty-six, and one thousand one hundred and forty-eight, and one thousand five hundred and ninety-four;
> son of Beathan, who sat by the slab through the dark half of the year; and all the days of Beathan were two thousand two hundred and thirteen years, and he died;
> son of Somhairle, son of Ailean, who asked for no share of the kingdom and went before his father to the grave; and all the days of Somhairle were two thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven years, and he died;
> son of Ailean Mòr, the first king, whom the gods crowned in the grove, and who was king until he fell at Àth na Fala; and all the days of Ailean Mòr were four thousand six hundred and twenty-six years, and he died;
> son of Suibhne, and fourteen fathers whose names we keep;
> son of Seathan, who snapped his fingers, and the fire was in his hand; and ten fathers;
> son of Colla, in whose days they called us Luchd-ceasnachaidh a' Ghuail Dhìomhair; and fifteen fathers;
> son of Eòghann, who tasted the river; and twenty-one fathers;
> son of Cormac of the Misstrike, who bled in the grove; and thirty fathers;
> son of Brian, who buried the five like the dead; and twenty-eight fathers;
> son of Fearghas, who climbed to the top of the island; and thirty-one fathers;
> son of Aodh, who threw the seven into the sea; and eighteen fathers;
> son of Diarmad, who spoke with Coimhdeach na Fine in the grove; and thirty-four fathers;
> son of Conall, who kept the vein while it burned;
> son of Neachdan, who found the fire;
> son of an Dia-shluagh, who grew out of the island like grass;
> and the island was born of Caoran's fire."

When it was done she sat again, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the rain went on against the shutter as it had gone on the whole time, indifferent to what had just been said in the room it fell on.

"They'll say it's a tale," Cailean said at last, looking at what his own hand had written, not quite believing the numbers even as he read them back. "Four thousand years. Two thousand. Nobody will believe a word of it below."

"The years, they will call a tale," Ealasaid said. "Let them call it that. I never told you a man lived four thousand years, Cailean mac Eachainn. I told you whose son he was." She looked at him a moment longer, and something in her face eased, very slightly, as though a weight she had carried a long time without expecting to set it down had shifted onto someone else's shoulder at last. "Go home now, and write it where it will keep. That's all that was ever asked of either of us."

## III. Of the Return, and the Report that Named No One

He walked back to Muileann chaol in the last of the light with the paper inside his coat, wet through at the edges where the rain had got at it despite him, and sat up past the cellar's own hours copying the line fair onto a second sheet before the pencil could smear past reading. In the morning he did the only thing that seemed to him, then, both possible and right: he wrote the seven names, and Ealasaid's line after them as she had given it, small, in the margin of the Àth ìseal roll, beside the entry for Aisling's birth, in a hand no larger than the mark that had first caught his eye. He told no one at the cellar what he had done, and Seònag Bhàn, who saw the margin filled and might have asked, chose that year, as she had chosen other years before it, not to ask a young copyist what he did with an evening that was rightly his own.

So it was found, as far as a line of names can be said to find anything: that the girl of the spoil-heaps at Achadh dhomhain, whom the camps had whispered over since before she could walk, carried in her the blood of the first king of the island, more generations removed from him than any roll could count, and no less his for the distance. What it proved beyond that, the cellar did not ask, and Cailean, who had begun the matter, was careful afterward never to be the one who raised it again.

Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir heard of the word the custodians had agreed within the year, as Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir heard most things that were said twice within a day's ride of one of its clerks, though by what road neither the custodians nor Na Trì Teaghlaichean of the cellar ever learned. What reached it was not the line, which no clerk of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach would have believed if he had heard it, but the plainer fact: that certain children of the Dia-thìrich did not grow as other children grew, that the custodians had a word for it, and that they appeared to know a good deal more about which children these were than they had troubled to report.

The order came down at the end of the hundred and twenty-seventh year, over An t-Àrd-mhaor's own seal, and was read out to every custodian's gathering from Cathair dhearg to the coast: any child of the Dia-thìrich showing the change the custodians had lately named was to be reported at once to An t-Àrd-mhaor's office, with the child's name, place and parentage, that Riaghaltas nan Coigreach might keep its own account of a matter touching the Dia-thìrich body it had not previously thought to number.

The custodians obeyed the order in the only way most of them ever found themselves able to obey an order of that shape. They reported none. Not Aisling, not Fionnan, not any child born with fuil-ghuail in the years that followed, though every custodian's roll from Cathair dhearg to the coast went on quietly keeping its own note of them, in the plain hand the physicians had settled on, where no clerk of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir thought to look for it. A custodian at Cill ghlas, asked once by an agent of A' Chompanaidh whether she knew of any such child in her own district, is recorded as having answered that she kept no roll of miracles, only of births and deaths, and that the office in front of her was welcome to search her books for either.

## IV. Of the Third Coin

The order on the children was not the only order that year, nor, as it happened, the one that changed more households than any single ruling in a generation.

Barter had been marked down against the mark twice already, and had gone on regardless in a hundred quiet corners Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's clerks never fully reached: cloth for fish at the steps of Inis mhòr, a day's labour for a day's milk between neighbouring crofts, the old exchanges that had outlived the marks and the mint and every change of coin that came before them, because a clerk cannot fine a transaction he never learns took place. In the spring of the hundred and twenty-ninth year Riaghaltas nan Coigreach struck barter down as lawful payment altogether, and in the autumn it closed the last door as well. The barter houses at Ros dhomhain and at Cathair dhearg, where cloth and fish and every manner of goods besides had changed hands without a coin passing since before the humans first came over the sea, were shut by written order, their doors nailed, their keepers turned to other work. A clerk of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, setting the closure into the year's account, wrote that A' Chompanaidh's ledgers were now, for the first time in the island's keeping, *complete*, and meant it as praise of his own office's thoroughness rather than as anything said against the households the closing cost.

The fishwives of Inis mhòr, who had sold their catch at the river steps by barter since before any of them could say when the custom began, took no coin at all for a full week after the order reached them, giving their fish away to whoever came down to the steps rather than sell it for marks they had not asked for and did not want. A' Chompanaidh's own clerks, coming down to the steps to see what the disturbance was, recorded the week as a glut in the season's catch and set it down so in the account, and never once asked a fishwife why so great a glut had chosen that particular week to appear.

It did not last, of course; a week is a week, and a family must eat past the end of it. Within the year the hill villages that had always paid their seed and their rents in kind now had no lawful way to pay in anything but marks, and the villages around Muileann chrom, having no marks of their own to spare, began borrowing them at interest from A' Chompanaidh's own stores, the only lender within a day's walk willing to lend at all. It was a small thing at first, a mark here, two marks there, against the next year's crop. Within a few years most of the villages about Muileann chrom owed A' Chompanaidh more marks than a year's crop could answer for, and every custodian who kept a roll of debts as well as of births knew it, and none of them knew what to write against it that would change the sum.

The old market at Muileann òg, where the hill folk and the coast folk had met to trade with each other directly since An Aois Naomh, without A' Chompanaidh or Riaghaltas nan Coigreach standing between them, closed after its last poor season in the hundred and thirty-first year. No order closed it; there was simply, one spring, nothing left worth carrying to it that could not be carried more profitably to a town with a mint. The ground it had stood on, a flat green shoulder above the ford that had given the old market its name, stood empty from that year on, and the grass that grew up over it grew thicker each season, since nothing walked it anymore to keep it down.

## V. Of the Staff at Muileann chrom

Peigi Ghlas died at Cill ghlas in the spring of the hundred and twenty-eighth year, the year before the barter houses' doors were nailed shut, an old woman by then, her petition long unanswered and the pile it had joined grown taller by every year that passed without an answer coming. She had trimmed Brìde's lamp herself the last week of her life, though her hands shook so that a younger custodian stood by to catch the oil if she spilled it, and she is remembered at Cill ghlas, where such things are remembered longest, as having said, near the end, that she had asked Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir for an answer once, and meant to leave the asking there for good. She was buried within the shrine's own ground, beside the cutting the custodians still keep open, and the roll of Cill ghlas records nothing further of her save the day.

Iain Maor, third of that name to hold the overseer's staff at Muileann chrom, followed her within three years, in the autumn of the hundred and thirty-first year, having held the staff since before most of the hill's present hewers were born and having spent no small part of it, in his last years, writing letters to Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, which dismissed him for the writing of them and then reversed the dismissal and took him back, because it could find no one else the coal camps would trust to keep order among them. He died at Muileann chrom, in the same house he had held the staff from, and was buried within the week, the custom of the camp allowing no longer delay for a man whose office could not sit empty.

His daughter Mòrag took the staff from his hand before he died, by his own wish and the custodians' agreement both, the first woman to hold it in the staff's whole history at Muileann chrom. She was thirty-six years old, born the year her father first took up the letters he would spend the rest of his life writing, and she had grown up, as she told a younger cousin once, "knowing the shape of that staff better than I know the shape of my own hand, from having watched it lie across his knees at every supper of my life." A' Chompanaidh wrote to confirm her within the month, in a letter that named her, without further comment, "a safe pair of hands," a phrase the custodians of Muileann chrom repeated to one another afterward with an expression none of A' Chompanaidh's clerks would have cared to see on their faces.

Mòrag took the staff home the evening of her father's burying, through a hill gone quiet with the particular quiet that follows a funeral, past the pithead where the night shift's lamps were already moving. Where her father had set it across his own knees by the fire, she hung it over her hearth, on two iron hooks he had never troubled to have fitted, where it caught the firelight along its worn grain every evening after. There, she told the same cousin years later, she could see it without having to hold it, which was, she had found, most of what a staff of office was actually good for.

She thought, hanging it there that first night, of a thing her father had told her once and again as she grew, a thing he said he had had from a copyist at Muileann chaol who had had it, in turn, from an old woman of Clann na Ceiste out at Tobar dhìreach, though neither he nor Mòrag had ever met the woman nor asked after her name: that somewhere on a roll no clerk of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir had ever read, a girl who did not grow was written down as the daughter of a king four thousand years dead, in a line kept by people who had stopped expecting anyone would ever come asking for it. *I never told you a man lived four thousand years,* the old woman was supposed to have said. *I told you whose son he was.* Mòrag did not know, that first night with the staff cooling on its hooks and her father not yet a day in the ground, what use such a thing as a name kept four thousand years could ever be to a hill that could not keep its own coin, let alone its own children, out of a stranger's ledger. She only knew that she thought of it, standing there, longer than she thought she should have, and that she did not stop thinking of it, in the years that followed, whenever the staff caught the evening light in a particular way and she found herself, without quite meaning to, looking up at it from her chair.
