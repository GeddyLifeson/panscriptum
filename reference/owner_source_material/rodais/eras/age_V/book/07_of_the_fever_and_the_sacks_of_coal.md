# Of the Fever and the Sacks of Coal

*AE 9 – 11*

## I. Of the Carriers at Cuan shean

Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill was nineteen that autumn, a hewer's son of Muileann chrom, and he had never seen the sea.

His father sent him west with the carriers late in an Dàmhair, in the ninth year of the strangers. It was the custom of the hill households that the coal for the far hearths went down from the store at Taigh na Fèithe before the dark of the year, on the backs of ponies and young men, so much for each hearth and no more, as the law of the handful had it; and a lad who had carried it once to the coast was held to have seen the island. Raghnall had asked for three years to go. When his turn came he went with a pony called Donnag, eleven carriers, and an old custodian's clerk who could not walk fast and rode the whole way on a cart of meal, complaining.

They were five days on the road. Past the capital the country flattened and the wind got up, and it came off the west smelling of wet sand and rot, a smell he did not know, and Donnag put her ears back at it. On the fifth evening they came over the last rise and he saw the water. It lay grey to the edge of sight, heaving slowly, and a long way out on it the light ran in a white bar where the sun was going down. He stood with the pony's rope in his hand until the clerk shouted at him.

Cuan shean was bigger than any town of the hills and smelled of fish and tar. Its houses stood with their backs to the weather along a bent harbour, and in the harbour, among the Dia-thìreach boats, lay two of the strangers' ships, so high out of the water that the carriers stopped in the street to look at them. The humans had been coming to that harbour nine years by then, and the town had the habit of them.

The custodian of Cuan shean was Oighrig nic Leòid, who had given the first of the strangers water on that shingle and had been abed the winter the council gave them leave. She was old now and walked with a stick, and she came down to the custodians' store herself to see the coal counted in. She had each carrier open his baskets on the floor, and she went along the rows with a lamp, stooping, and turned the coal over with the end of the stick. Raghnall stood by his baskets with his heart knocking. She stopped at them and held the lamp low.

"Whose cutting?"

"My father's, lady. Dòmhnall's, of the old cutting above the mill."

"It is good coal," she said. "See the green in it." And she moved the lamp so that the green came up out of the black like weed under water, and Raghnall, who had broken that coal himself in the hill, felt his face go hot.

On the third day the carriers were to start home. That morning a ship came in from the north, a big hull low in the water and so crowded at the rails that from the quay she seemed to have a hedge along her sides. She had come the long way about, past the north cape and down the western coast, and she had been six weeks at sea. The humans on the quay went out to her in boats. Those who came ashore were thin and grey in the face, and some of them had to be lifted over the side. There were women among them, which Raghnall had not seen before, and children, and a man with a fiddle in a bag, who stood on the stones and wept.

Oighrig's people gave them water, as Cuan shean always did. The ship's master asked for more than water, and got it, and the town took some of the sick into the sail-lofts, where the humans said their prayers.

Within four days the first Dia-thìreach was sick. It was a woman of the net-sheds, who had carried water to the lofts. She burned, and shook, and talked all night in a voice that was not hers, and on the second day a rash came out on her chest like the marks of wet sand, and on the fourth day she died. By then there were eleven more. The custodians' roll gives the day the fever came ashore as {{date:IV-0034}}, and names it Fiabhras nan Coigreach, and that is the name it kept.

The carriers did not go home. The clerk would not have them walk the roads with the fever in the town, lest they carry it into the hills, and Oighrig said the same. So Raghnall stayed in Cuan shean through an t-Samhain and saw the whole of it.

He carried water, because the custodians asked for strong backs and he had one. He carried the dead out of the net-sheds on hurdles, two to a hurdle, with cloths over their mouths that did no good. In a week he knew the smell of the fever houses and the sound a house makes when no one inside it is well enough to answer the door. The humans died of it too, but fewer of them, and slower, and some of those who took it at sea got up again. The Dia-thìrich who took it seldom got up. The old men of the harbour sat on the upturned boats and said that the strangers had brought it in their clothes, in their blood, in their bread; and a younger man said that it made no odds where they had brought it, since here it was.

On the ninth day Oighrig nic Leòid called the custodians of the town to the store and shut Cuan shean to ships. No hull was to come in and none to go out until the fever was gone, and the boats of the town were to fish within the harbour mouth or not at all. She had them bring her chair down to the quay so that the masters of the two human ships could hear it from her. One of them laughed. The other went white and asked how his cargo was to reach the capital, and she said by the road, like anyone's.

Eight days after that a rider came in from Ros dhomhain with a letter. It was from Josiah Pell, An t-Àrd-mhaor, and it was written in the humans' tongue, on three sheets, and sealed. There was no one in Cuan shean who could read it but the humans, and so Oighrig sent for the trader who kept the humans' store by the quay and had him read it to her in the store-room, standing, with the carriers and the town's custodians round the walls.

Raghnall understood none of the words. He understood the voice. It went on a long time, level and hard, and now and then the trader stopped to put a sentence into Dia-thìris for her: that the harbour of Cuan shean was open to the ships of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach under Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg; that the custodians had no power to close it; that Pell protested in writing, and would protest again, and would hold the town answerable for the goods spoiled in the holds.

Oighrig heard it out. Then she asked the trader whether the letter said anything about the dead.

He looked through the sheets. "No, lady."

"Then write him that there are forty-one of them in this town," she said, "and that the ships may wait."

The roll of Cuan shean enters the protest, and beside it the number, which was forty-one that day and went on rising.

## II. Of the Dunes and the Road Home

They buried the fever dead of Cuan shean beyond the dunes, in the sand, where the town had never buried before. The custodians would not have the fever lie in the old ground by the harbour, among the town's grandmothers; and so the hurdles went out along the shore path past the last net-shed and up over the marram and down again on the far side, out of sight of the houses, where the wind comes straight off the open sea. Raghnall carried on that path more times than he let himself count.

It rained most days. The sand took the rain and did not hold it, and the graves were easy to dig and hard to keep, for the sides slid in. The human dead went into the same ground, a little apart, in a row of their own, because their priest asked it. He read over them from his book in the rain, and the Dia-thìreach carriers stood back with their hands under their arms and waited for him to finish so that they could fill in.

Once, on the path, the man at the other end of Raghnall's hurdle stopped and said, "Look at them," and Raghnall looked back at the town and saw the two tall ships riding in the harbour with their sails furled, and a boat going out to them with fresh water, and a man in the stern of it singing.

"They are well," the carrier said. "Most of them are well."

By the dark of the year the burying slackened, and a little before Là Fhèill Brìde it stopped. The custodians opened the harbour at the season's end. The roll of the west gives the whole count down the coast as ninety-one Dia-thìrich dead and eleven humans, and the numbers stand in the copy at Muileann chaol exactly as the custodians wrote them.

Raghnall walked home with the carriers when the roads were opened. They did not talk much. At the capital the clerk left them to report to the council, and they went on east without him, up the long road into the hills, and the country rose round them and the wind lost its salt. On the fifth evening he saw the mill at Muileann chrom with its wheel turning in the bend of the river, and the smoke going up flat from the roofs, and he sat down on a stone at the roadside for a while before he went down.

His father met him at the door and looked at him a long time and said, "You are thinner."

"I carried," Raghnall said.

His father said nothing to that. Dòmhnall was a hewer of the old cutting, as his father had been, a short man with shoulders like a yoke and black in the seams of his hands that no washing took out. He had cut the measured coal of the hill for thirty years, so many baskets in the season and not one over, and he had never been further west than the capital. Raghnall told him about the ships, and the sea, and the sand, and Oighrig's lamp, and the letter; and his father listened, and at the end he asked only whether Oighrig had liked the coal.

"She said to look at the green in it."

"So she should," said Dòmhnall.

## III. Of the Word from the West

News came up the road that year in the carters' mouths, and none of it good.

On {{date:IV-0035}} the fever reached Baile àrsaidh, a short way up the coast from Cuan shean, where the druids of Manannan kept their school. It killed the custodian of the town and both her sons, and a number of households besides, and the school shut its doors and kept them shut until the summer. The thing the custodians of the west wrote down, and the thing the carters told in the hills, was this: that no human lived in Baile àrsaidh at all. No strangers' ship had put in there. The fever had walked up the coast by itself, on Dia-thìreach feet, from house to house.

The custodians of the hills met at Taigh na Fèithe above Muileann dhearg in the spring to speak of it. Raghnall's father went, as a hewer of the old cutting had the right to do, and came back late and sat by the fire with his boots on.

"They talked of shutting the roads," he said.

"Will they?"

"No." He pulled at a boot. "The road is the meal and the salt. It is the lamp-oil from Ros dhomhain, which half the hill has got the use of now. They said a hill that shuts its road starves slower than it sickens, but it starves." He got the boot off and looked at it. "Old Seonag Liath, who keeps our cutting, said we had eaten salt with the strangers nine years and it was a little late to be afraid of their bread. They wrote that down."

So the hills did not shut their roads, and the roll of Taigh na Fèithe gives the reason in Seonag Liath's words. The fever did not come to Muileann chrom that year. Whether that was luck, or the hills' distance from the sea, or the grace of the hill, nobody in the district could agree, and they argued it at the mill all summer.

In the south-west, at Cnoc àrsaidh, where Imrich nam Pantar passes and Òrd na Cloiche keeps its oldest shrines, the townsfolk did what the hills had talked of. When the fever came down the coast towards them, on {{date:IV-0036}}, they dragged their carts across the road on either side of the town, wheel to wheel, and set men with staves behind them, and turned back every traveller for a season: carters, drovers, custodians, and the humans' traders with their packs. The fever went round them and did not come in. The carters told next, with relish, that Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had fined Cnoc àrsaidh for hindering trade, and that the town had paid it in wool, sacks of it, and the humans had not known what to do with so much wool.

Dòmhnall laughed at that, which he seldom did. Raghnall remembered the dunes, and did not.

## IV. Of the Two Prospectors

Near the end of an t-Samhain in the tenth year, when the hills were brown and the rain came across them in sheets, two humans came up the road to Muileann chrom with a letter.

They came on foot, with a pony carrying their packs, and they stopped at the mill because it was the largest house in sight. One was a broad man of five-and-thirty, bald, with a red face and a red beard and a voice that could be heard across the river; his name was Abel Stone. The other was younger and taller and stooped, with spectacles that he took off every few minutes to wipe, and a book in a waxed cover that he never let go of; his name was Hugh Mercer. The letter was from A' Chompanaidh, the trading house that Harrow and Pike had founded under licence of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, and it asked in both tongues, badly in one of them, that the bearers be given lodging and a guide into the hills, for which a fair price would be paid.

The miller read the Dia-thìris half aloud to the room and gave it back.

Nobody would go. The men of the hill were courteous to the strangers and gave them the miller's back room and a fire and a supper of mutton, and the miller's wife lent Mercer a dry shirt. But when Stone spread his hands and said "Coal?" and pointed up at the hills, and made a hammering motion with his fist, the men at the fire looked at one another and looked away. The hill was the custodians'. What a man broke out of it he broke by their leave, in its season, in the measure. No one in Muileann chrom had ever led a stranger up it with a hammer, and no one wanted to be the first.

Raghnall was at the fire that night. He had been thinking since the spring, when he thought at all, about the ships riding well in the harbour at Cuan shean and the man singing in the stern of the boat. What he thought of it he could not have said; but he was tired of the mill and the cutting and the same eleven faces, and he had seen the sea.

Stone had taken a coin out of his purse and laid it on the table, a disc of silver with a man's head on one side, stamped, and letters round the edge. The firelight ran on it. He tapped it and said a word in his own tongue and looked round.

"I will go," said Raghnall.

His father was not there. He heard of it the next morning from the miller, and he came to the mill yard where Raghnall was loading the pony and stood and watched without a word. When the loading was done he said, "You will not go into the cutting."

"No."

"Nor into any gallery the custodians keep."

"No. Only the hill."

"The hill is the custodians'," said Dòmhnall. Then he turned round and went home, and Raghnall watched his back until the corner of the byre took it.

They were eleven days on the hill. Stone and Mercer knew what they were looking for and had only a little of the tongue to ask for it, and Raghnall had none of theirs, and between them they made a talk of hands and a few words that got better as the days went on. Stone wanted water: streams, gullies, the places where rain had cut down into the hillside and bared the rock. Raghnall took them up the burns north of the mill, into the high ground towards Doire mhòr and east along the flank of the hills above the river, where the heather gave way in the stream-cuts to banks of shale and clay, and where every child of the hill knew that the black showed through.

It showed. In the second gully Stone went down on his knees in the wet and scraped at the bank with his hammer and a shout went out of him that sent a hare bolting. There was the seam, a hand's breadth of it and then an arm's length, running into the hill under the turf like a black river under a bank. Stone broke off a piece and turned it in his fingers and rubbed it with his thumb, and smelled it, and put his tongue to it, and gave it to Mercer, who wrote in his book.

Raghnall stood above them on the bank with the rain running off his hood. He looked at the broken piece in Mercer's hand. It was fresh coal of the upper seam, the sort that comes near the surface, and in the grey light it showed its brown at the edges, deep brown going into red where it had split, and under that the green, and along one face a sheen like the inside of a mussel shell. Any child of the hill could have named them. He had the words in his mouth.

"Gual donn-ruadh," he said, and pointed at the edge where the red ran into the brown. "An sin. Agus an sin, gual airgid."

Mercer looked up at him, pleasantly, and wiped his spectacles, and looked at the piece of coal, and looked at Raghnall again, waiting. Raghnall touched the sheen with his finger. Mercer nodded, the way a man nods at a child who has shown him a pebble, and said something to Stone, and Stone laughed, not unkindly, and said a word that Raghnall had heard in Cuan shean. He heard it a great many times on that hill. It was the only word either of them had for what they were breaking, and it stood for all of it alike.

They broke a great deal. They broke it at every gully where the seam showed, and they put it in sacks, coarse sacks of the humans' weaving with letters on them, a sack for each gully, and Mercer wrote the place in his book and a number, and Raghnall learned after a while that the number was the paces from the last. On the fifth day Stone took to walking the seam. He went along the hillside with his head down, from one stream-cut to the next, counting under his breath, and where it showed he broke it and went on, and where it did not show he dug until it did. Mercer came behind with the book. Raghnall came behind Mercer with the pony, and the sacks.

On the eighth day, far out along the hills to the north, where the high ground looks down on the valley of Abhainn uaine, Stone stopped on a knoll in the evening and looked back the way they had come, and then forward, and scratched his beard. He said a long thing to Mercer. Mercer wrote it down. Then Stone turned to Raghnall and said, slowly, in Dia-thìris that he had been practising, with his hand held out flat along the line of the hills:

"Black. Further than a man can walk in one day."

He seemed very pleased with it. He said it again in his own tongue, and Mercer wrote that too. Those are the words the letter-books of A' Chompanaidh keep, "further than can be walked in a day," and they are the only words of that walk the humans kept.

That night they camped under a peat-bank, and Stone and Mercer drank from a flask and talked late in their own tongue, fast and happy, and Raghnall lay in his cloak and listened to the rain and could not sleep. He was thinking of the old cutting above the mill, of the baskets his father filled in the season, eleven and not twelve; of the custodian with her lamp going along the rows; of the old words that are said at the rock face before a man puts his pick in, which every hewer's son learns before he learns to swing. He had not said them once on the hill. Nobody had asked him to.

They came down on {{date:IV-0037}}, with the pony so loaded that Raghnall carried a sack on his own back the last miles, and Stone and Mercer each carried one, and all three of them were black to the elbow. At the mill Stone paid him the coin. Raghnall took it and held it and looked at the man's head on it, and put it in his shirt.

The men of Muileann chrom stood at their doors to watch the sacks go by. Nobody said anything. The next morning the prospectors went down the road towards the capital and Ros dhomhain, with the pony and a hired cart, and the sacks in the cart.

## V. Of the Half Carried West

The custodians of the vein wrote to A' Chompanaidh before the year was out.

They wrote in Dia-thìris, for they had nothing else to write in, and the letter went down to Ros dhomhain with a carter who had orders to give it into the hand of Thomas Pike and nobody else's. It asked that the coal broken on the hill above Muileann chrom without leave be brought back, all of it, to be laid in the rock again with the proper words, as the custodians had always done with coal taken out of season. There was no threat in it. The custodians wrote as they had written to their own people for a thousand years, as if there could be no question of the answer.

The answer was a long time coming. Raghnall heard of the letter at the mill, and of the carter's going and coming, and of a second letter in the spring, and a third. Men looked at him in the mill yard and said nothing. His father spoke to him about the work of the day and about the weather, and not about the hill. Raghnall kept the coin in a crack of the byre wall and took it out sometimes and looked at it.

At the summer's end in the eleventh year a cart came up the road from the capital with a human driver and six sacks in it, coarse sacks with letters on them, and stopped at the mill. The driver gave the miller a paper. The miller could not read it and sent for Raghnall, as the one man in Muileann chrom known to have talked with the strangers, and Raghnall could not read it either. He counted the sacks. Stone had filled thirteen.

"Where are the others?" he said to the driver, in the few words of the humans' tongue he had got on the hill, and made the number with his fingers.

The driver shrugged and pointed down the road the way he had come, west, towards Ros dhomhain and the lake and the ships, and said a word Raghnall knew, and turned his cart round.

The custodians came to the old cutting three days later, on {{date:IV-0038}}. There were four of them from Taigh na Fèithe and Seonag Liath, and they had sent word through the hill, so that most of the households came up behind them, the hewers first. Raghnall's father was among the hewers. Raghnall was not asked to come and went anyway, and stood at the back, and when the custodians looked round for strong backs to carry the sacks up the last steep path to the rock face, it was Seonag Liath who looked at him and lifted her chin, and he went forward and took up the first sack.

He carried all six. Nobody helped him, and he did not ask. At the face, where the old cutting goes into the hill under an overhang of grey rock, black-mouthed and dry and smelling of the inside of the earth, the custodians had the sacks opened and the coal laid out on a cloth in the lamp-light, piece by piece; and they sorted it, as a custodian sorts, by colour and by the gully it had come from, as near as they could tell. Then the hewers took it up in their hands and carried it into the cutting and laid it back into the rock, into clefts and old workings, packing it with clay; and the custodians said the words over each handful, the old words that are said when a thing taken out of season is given back, the same words, over and over, until the words and the dark and the lamp and the slow hands made one thing.

Raghnall stood outside with the empty sacks. He could hear his father's voice in the cutting saying the words with the rest, low, and the tap of clay being pressed in.

When it was done the custodians' clerk sat down on a stone at the cutting mouth with the roll on his knees and asked how much had been taken from the hill and how much given back.

"Thirteen sacks," said Raghnall. "Six."

"And the rest?"

Raghnall thought of the driver and his pointing hand. "West," he said.

The clerk wrote it. The roll of Taigh na Fèithe enters the other half of the coal as "carried west," and it is still so entered in the copy that lies in Seilear Muileann chaol, the first coal of the vein in all the records that went out of the hill and was not accounted for. Seonag Liath, who read over the clerk's shoulder, put her finger on the line and looked at Raghnall a long while, and said nothing, and went down the hill.

## VI. Of the Contract and the First Ton

Two days after the laying-back, on {{date:IV-0039}}, A' Chompanaidh brought its first contracts into the hills.

Abel Stone brought them. He came up the road to Muileann chrom on a good horse this time, with a clerk behind him and two carts of goods: iron. There were picks with steel points, shovels, wedges, hammers, bar-iron in bundles tied with cord, and on the second cart casks of lamp-oil, more oil than the whole district burned in a year. He had the carts drawn up in the mill yard and the covers taken off, and he stood beside them with his thumbs in his belt while the hill came to look.

The contract was read in the mill in the humans' tongue by the clerk and in Dia-thìris by a young man of Ros dhomhain whom A' Chompanaidh paid to put its words into the island's. Raghnall stood at the wall. It was short, as such things went. A crew of the hill was to cut coal from the seam at a place A' Chompanaidh would name, on the hillside below the old cutting and well away from it; and for every ton the crew brought to the pit-mouth, weighed on the strangers' beam, the crew would be paid in iron tools and lamp-oil, at so much the ton, the rate written in figures on the paper. The ton was a thing of the humans' reckoning. The young man of Ros dhomhain tried three ways to say it in Dia-thìris and settled for "a great load, weighed".

Some of the older men walked out before the reading was done. Some stayed. The hill had lamp-oil to think of, and iron, and the good years of trading behind it, in which nobody had yet been cheated at the humans' counter. A bargain was a bargain. And A' Chompanaidh was careful that year: the place it named was on no custodian's lot, and the paper said nothing about the old cutting at all.

When the reading was done Stone looked round the room and found Raghnall at the wall, and grinned at him, and pointed.

"This one," he said, in his practised Dia-thìris. "He walked the hill. He knows the black. Let him lead the crew."

So Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill was made the leader of the first crew of the hill. It was thirty men, the number the contract asked for: young men mostly, sons of hewers and sons of shepherds and two from the mill, and three older hewers of the old cutting who had sons to feed and said so. His father was not among them. Dòmhnall heard the reading from outside the mill door and went home before it was over, and when Raghnall came in that night with the paper folded in his shirt beside the coin, his father was sitting at the fire with his hands on his knees and did not look up.

"They will pay us in iron," said Raghnall. "Picks with steel points. You have seen them. And oil, enough to light every house on the hill through the winter."

"I have seen them," said his father.

"The place is on no lot. It is below the old cutting. They have not asked for the cutting."

"No," said Dòmhnall. "Not this year." And then, after a while, to the fire: "Who will say the words at the face?"

Raghnall found that he had no answer, and his father did not ask again.

They opened the working on {{date:IV-0040}}. It was on the south face of the hill, a mile below the old cutting, in a gully where Stone had broken the seam the winter before; and the crew cut back the turf and the shale above it with the new shovels from the carts and went in along the black with the new picks. The steel points went through the coal like a spade through turf. Men who had swung the old picks of the hill all their lives stopped and looked at the new ones in their hands and laughed out loud. By midday there was a heap at the pit-mouth taller than a man.

Stone had the beam set up that afternoon at the pit-mouth: a great iron bar hung from a tripod of timbers, with a hook at one end and a weight that slid along the other, and a board of marks. He showed Raghnall how it worked. The coal went into a sling of chain and hung from the hook, and the weight was run along the bar until the bar hung level, and the clerk read the mark and wrote the figure in a book. The crew stood round in the drizzle and watched. It was the first weighing of coal most of them had ever seen, and they did not know yet what they were watching.

At dusk the clerk added his figures and said something to Stone, and Stone turned round to the crew and held up one finger, and shouted.

"One ton!"

The crew cheered, because he was so pleased, and because they had cut it. Raghnall cheered with them. He stood at the pit-mouth with the black to his elbows and the new pick in his hand, in the rain, and watched the clerk write the figure down in the book of A' Chompanaidh, one ton, the first ton weighed in the hills of Muileann chrom, and he was proud of it. His crew was the best on the hill, the first and the best, and he had led them, and it was all written down.

He walked home in the dark. Up the hill above him the old cutting was black against the sky. There was no lamp in it. The season of the handful was over, and his father's eleven baskets had gone to the store a month since, measured, and the custodians would not light the cutting again until the spring.

## VII. Of What the Hill Had Never Been Weighed By

*Here the copyists who set down the age from its four witnesses leave Raghnall at his pit-mouth, and look back.*

In all the years of the vein before the strangers came, from the first keeping to the last, the coal of the hill had been measured and it had been counted; but it had never once been weighed. It was measured by the handful, a hewer's two hands cupped together, which is the measure of the law, and by the basket, which is so many handfuls, and by the season, which is so many baskets. It was counted by the hearth. The custodians knew how many hearths the island had, and how many handfuls each might burn, and they cut that much and no more, and what was cut out of season was given back. The roll of every cutting is kept that way. It speaks of hands and hearths and seasons, and of colours, which it names each time, and of the words said at the face; and in all its leaves there is no ton, and no beam, and no figure written by a clerk who was not a custodian.

After {{year:IV-0040}} a second book was kept beside it, at the pit-mouth of A' Chompanaidh below Muileann chrom. It opened with a single figure in the humans' letters, under a date in the humans' reckoning, and against the figure the name of a crew-leader written as the humans heard it. The custodians' book went on counting handfuls in their season; the other counted tons.

So ended Bliadhnachan na Malairt Cothromaich, that began with a cask of water on the shingle at Cuan shean. Nobody on the island marked the day. The crew at Muileann chrom were paid on the first market-day after in picks and lamp-oil, at the rate on the paper, to the last ounce of oil; and the book of A' Chompanaidh balanced to the line.
