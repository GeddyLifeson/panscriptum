# Of Lagh na h-Aon Loidhne

*Part Five: Of the Word and the Line*

*AE 111 – 113*

## I. Of the Forty Pages and the Four Words

Iseabail nic Leòid's forty pages reached Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir at Ros dhomhain in the spring of the hundred and tenth year, and lay on a clerk's table through the summer, and were answered at last, in the winter that opened the hundred and eleventh, in four words written across the foot of the last page in Robert Haskins's own hand: *the quota stands unrevised*. The copyists who later held both leaves together in the chest at Muileann chaol remarked on nothing so much as the room the two witnesses took up side by side, the one nearly as thick as a ledger of A' Chompanaidh, the other four words and a signature, and left it at that, because there was nothing in the pairing that wanted explaining twice.

It was in that same winter, at Baile dhìreach on the western coast, that Thomas Hale first put his name to paper against Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, in a matter that had nothing to do with drought or grain and everything to do with a strip of hill ground no wider than a cart-track, and it is by his roll, and by the Ros dhomhain court's own record, kept afterward among Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's letter-books, that the copyists have told what came of it.

Baile dhìreach was a small town even by the coast's own measure, one straight street running down to a quay, and it had been, since before Thomas could remember, a town of two peoples under one roof more often than any other on that shore. His grandmother, Eilidh nic Ruairidh, a net-maker's daughter of the place, had married Samuel Hale, a human cooper off one of the early ships, in the seventh year after the strangers came, in the first of the marriages the custodians of that coast ever entered in two tongues in the one roll; and though Samuel Hale had died an old man's death long before Thomas was born, and Eilidh not many years after him, the house they had kept together still stood at the head of the street by the well, and Thomas's father, Iain Hale, had grown up in it a boatwright, mending the town's craft and building new ones, Dia-thìrich and human custom both come easily to his hands if not always to his tongue.

Iain Hale had died when Thomas was eighteen, of a fever that took three others of the town the same season, and had left his son the boatyard, the house, and a habit, which Thomas had never fully understood until he was a man grown himself, of speaking of his mother's people's ground as though it were a thing held in trust and not yet properly claimed. Eilidh nic Ruairidh's own mother's family had worked, since before any of them could say how long, a narrow cutting in the hill above the town, a seam no wider than a good man's shoulders that gave up perhaps two cartloads of coal in a good year, more often used for the town's own hearths than sold, and held by no writing at all, only by the custom that a family which has cut a seam for its grandmothers' grandmothers and asked no other family's leave to do it owns the seam as surely as it owns its own byre. Iain Hale had cut there himself, as a boy, at his mother's side, and had gone on cutting there as a man, quietly, on the days the boatyard gave him free, and had never once, so far as Thomas ever learned, thought to ask anyone's permission for it, because the question had never in his lifetime come up.

Thomas remembered his father at that cutting better than he remembered him almost anywhere else: a broad, unhurried man kneeling at the low mouth of the seam with a hand-pick worn smooth at the haft, humming some boatyard tune under his breath that had no words Thomas ever learned, and stopping, every so often, to hold a broken lump up to the light the way a man might hold up a fish to judge whether it was worth the taking. Iain Hale had never once, in Thomas's hearing, called the coal his own outright; he called it his mother's, or the town's, or simply the hill's, as though ownership of it were a question he saw no profit in asking, being a question the hill had never troubled to raise in all the years it had let the family cut there. It was only after his father's death, going through the small chest of papers the boatyard's accounts were kept in, that Thomas found the copy of his grandmother's marriage entry his father had had made in his own hand, years before, from the custodian's roll, folded small and kept at the very bottom beneath the boat contracts, as though it were a thing Iain Hale had meant, one day, to make some use of, and had died before the day came.

It came up in the hundred and eleventh year because A' Chompanaidh's surveyors, working the southern seams along a line of workable coal that ran, as such lines will, without regard for any family's custom, marked the same hill for a new gallery, and sent an agent down to Baile dhìreach with a paper naming every holding on the slope that A' Chompanaidh judged owed to it, the narrow cutting among them.

## II. Of the Paper on the Table

The agent, a young man named Pryce who had been three years in A' Chompanaidh's survey office and had learned, in that time, to read a Dia-thìrich roll well enough to find the names he wanted in it, sat at Thomas Hale's own table on a wet morning in the second month of the year and laid the paper flat between them with the particular patience of a man who has done this in a dozen other houses and expects no trouble in this one.

"Your family has worked this ground," Pryce said, "under no charter A' Chompanaidh recognises. I am not saying you have done wrong. I am saying A' Chompanaidh's claim runs beneath the district's whole, and yours does not run beneath anything at all, in A' Chompanaidh's law, unless you can show it does."

"It has been my mother's people's ground since before A' Chompanaidh had a name," Thomas said. "That is the whole of my showing."

"It may be true and still be no showing, in the court A' Chompanaidh will bring this to if you refuse the offer." Pryce named a sum, small enough that Thomas understood, hearing it, that A' Chompanaidh had already reckoned the seam worth little on its own and wanted it chiefly to close a gap in its own map. "I would take it, if I were in your place. A court costs more than it returns, oftener than not, to a man arguing custom against a charter."

Thomas did not take it that morning, nor the following week, when Pryce came again with the same paper and a slightly larger sum written beneath the old one. He had, by his own account, no clear plan in his head beyond a stubbornness he could not at the time have named any more precisely than his father's habit of speaking of the seam as a thing held in trust; it was his wife, sitting at the table's far end through both of Pryce's visits and saying nothing until he had gone, who put the plan into words for him.

"Your grandmother's marriage is in the roll," she said. "Two tongues, one page, the custodian's own hand. If A' Chompanaidh's law knows anything of lines and charters, it will have to know what a marriage entered in the register of its own Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir is, or say plainly that it does not, and I do not think it wants to say that plainly, not with every other marriage of the coast standing behind yours asking the same question."

It was, Thomas said afterward, more courage than he had felt himself in the matter, and he went that week to the custodian of Baile dhìreach, an old woman named Mairead nic Aoidh who had held the office since before he was born, and had her make him a copy of the roll's page from the second year, the marriage of Eilidh nic Ruairidh and Samuel Hale in the two tongues, sealed with the custodian's own mark as it had stood unaltered for a hundred and four years. With that copy in his coat, and no other paper to his name, he walked the two days to Ros dhomhain in the third month of the year to file his claim at A' Chompanaidh's court.

## III. Of the Walk to Ros dhomhain

The road to Ros dhomhain ran along the coast for the first day and inland for the second, through country Thomas had known since boyhood, boatyards and net-sheds giving way by degrees to the carting roads and the smoke of the hills, and he said afterward that he had spent most of the walk turning over, without ever quite settling, the question of what exactly he thought a court at Ros dhomhain owed a claim it had never in a hundred years been asked to answer. He had no lawyer, no letters of A' Chompanaidh's tongue beyond what his grandfather's house had given him, and no friend at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir to tell him what manner of hearing his paper would get once he had filed it.

Ros dhomhain he had seen only twice before that spring, both times as a boy come with his father to buy timber the boatyard could not get nearer home, and he found it, walking it now alone and on his own business, a harder town to read than he remembered: the quay stacked three deep with coal for the steamers that had begun, that year, to call there more often than the old sail ever had; Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's long grey front with its rows of shuttered windows giving nothing away of what went on behind them; and everywhere along the streets nearest the court, clerks and agents and A' Chompanaidh's men moving at a pace no coast town Thomas knew ever kept, as though every one of them had somewhere more pressing to be than the place they were walking to. He lodged the one night before his business was done at a boarding-house kept by a Dia-thìreach widow off the harbour road, one of a dozen such houses along that street that kept custodians and petitioners and claimants of every district, and she told him over a thin supper, without his asking, that she had housed men come to argue every kind of claim A' Chompanaidh's court would hear, and that in eleven years keeping the house she could not, that evening, call to mind a single one of them who had come away from Ros dhomhain with more than he had walked in with.

What the counter got him, the next morning, was a clerk's fee, four marks, paid in A' Chompanaidh's own coin over a table no wider than a door, and a date given him a full season off, the tenth month of that same year, for a hearing before the magistrate. He asked the clerk, before he left the counter, whether it was usual for a claim of this kind to take so long in the coming, and the clerk, a tired man who had clearly answered the question before, told him it was usual for every claim to take exactly as long as the court's own list allowed, and that a claim from a district with no agent of A' Chompanaidh resident in it went, as a rule, to the back of that list.

Thomas walked the two days home again with the fee's receipt in his coat where the marriage roll had been, and told his wife, and the custodian, and Pryce, when the agent came a third time with the paper and a sum unchanged from the second offer, that the matter would be heard in the tenth month, and that he did not mean to sell before it was.

The season between filing and hearing was, by every account Thomas ever gave of it afterward, the longest he had lived through since his father's death. The boatyard's work went on as it always had, and his wife kept the house, and their daughter Eilidh, thirteen that summer, understood the waiting better than her father wished her to; but Thomas himself found the hill above the town harder to walk past than he had ever found it before, the narrow mouth of the cutting sitting in the hillside exactly as it had sat in his father's day and his grandmother's, asking, he said, no question of anyone, and yet somehow, that year, seeming to ask everything of him.

## IV. Of Dunstan's Ruling

Oliver Dunstan, magistrate at Ros dhomhain, had sat on A' Chompanaidh's bench at that town for six years by the time Thomas Hale's claim came before him, and was known along the coast, so far as a Dia-thìreach could be said to know a human magistrate at all, chiefly for a manner that gave nothing away until the ruling was read, courteous to every party in a room and unmoved, so the custodians who had watched him rule on other matters said, by anything either party thought to say to him.

Thomas stood before him in the second week of the tenth month, in a room that held perhaps thirty people, most of them A' Chompanaidh's clerks with business of their own on the list that day, and had, when his own turn came, only the marriage roll's copy and his own plain telling of a seam worked without dispute by four generations of one family. Pryce spoke for A' Chompanaidh, briefly and without heat, laying out the survey's line and the charter it ran from, and did not, Thomas noted afterward, so much as mention the marriage roll at all, as though it were a paper that had not been filed.

Dunstan asked few questions. He asked Thomas once whether his grandmother's family had ever held any writing, charter or grant, naming them owners of the cutting, and Thomas said no, only the custom of the ground and the roll of the marriage, and Dunstan wrote something in the book before him and asked nothing further of it that day.

The ruling came a fortnight later, posted at A' Chompanaidh's counter along with a dozen others of that same list, and read, in the plain terms the copyists later set beside the master record of the case: that customary rights of the kind claimed descended, in Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's law, only through a line Riaghaltas nan Coigreach recognised as continuous in its own registers of holding, and that no marriage entered in a custodian's roll, however old, however honoured in the custom of a district, created such a line where none had existed before it; that Thomas Hale's claim, resting as it did on custom and on a marriage rather than on any grant of A' Chompanaidh, therefore failed; and that the cutting, together with the whole of the hill the survey had marked, stood confirmed to A' Chompanaidh under its existing charter.

Thomas read the ruling standing at the counter, with Pryce, by chance, two places down the same board reading a different notice, and said afterward that what struck him hardest that morning was not the ruling's substance, which he had half expected from the length of the wait alone, but its plainness: that a line the custodians of his own coast had kept in two tongues for a hundred and four years could be set aside in four sentences by a man who had never once, in the whole hearing, asked to see it read aloud.

## V. Of the Eleven Cuttings

The ruling named only Thomas Hale's cutting, and might, had it stood alone, have passed from the district's memory within a generation as one family's hard luck and no more. It did not stand alone. Within three years of Dunstan's ruling A' Chompanaidh's agents had brought eleven further claims of the same kind before the Ros dhomhain bench, each resting A' Chompanaidh's title on some marriage, generations back or recent, between a holder in a customary line and a human, and each decided, without exception, on the same ground Dunstan had first set out: that no such marriage created a line Riaghaltas nan Coigreach recognised, whatever the custom of the district or the age of the roll that recorded it.

The oldest of the eleven was the working at Cnoc dhubh, held by one household since An Aois Naomh, lost because a great-grandmother of that line had married a human sailor two generations back and the marriage, like Eilidh nic Ruairidh's, stood written in a custodian's roll and nowhere else. The custodian of Cnoc dhubh, an old woman who had known the family three generations deep, wrote in her own roll, when the ruling reached her, only that A' Chompanaidh had found a use, at last, for every marriage her district had ever entered in good faith, and set down no further word beside it.

A second of the eleven, decided in the hundred and thirteenth year, took the working at Seann Bhral on much the same ground: a cooper's daughter married, three generations back, to a human pilot off one of An t-Aiseag's own ships, the marriage entered in the same good faith as Eilidh nic Ruairidh's, and the cutting it had once secured passed, on Dunstan's own reasoning applied a second time by a magistrate junior to him, to A' Chompanaidh without further hearing at all, the ruling in that case running to no more than a page and citing the Hale case by name as settled law. Thomas read that ruling when a carter brought word of it to Baile dhìreach, and said to his wife, folding the notice away, that he had not thought, filing his own paper two years before, that he was writing the very sentence a second magistrate had now used against a family he had never met and a hill he had never seen.

It was Mairead nic Aoidh, custodian of Baile dhìreach, who first stopped entering such marriages in her own roll, in the spring of the hundred and twelfth year, on her own plain reckoning: a marriage written into the roll had become, under Riaghailt Dunstan, a paper a family's enemies could use against it, and had stopped, in her judgement, being a paper that protected anything at all. She told Thomas so herself, standing at her own door, and he said afterward that he had never in his life heard a woman say so hard a thing in so level a voice. Other custodians of the coast, hearing of Baile dhìreach's decision within the year, did the same, quietly, without any word passed between the districts to agree it, so that within a decade the two-tongue marriages that had once stood as the coast's own proof of a hundred years' living together had all but stopped being written down at all. The marriages themselves went on; only the writing of them had failed, because no custodian any longer trusted Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's court with it.

Thomas understood, by the second year, that his own suit, brought in good faith to save one narrow seam his family had cut for four generations, had become the very door through which A' Chompanaidh had walked to take eleven others, and he never afterward spoke of the case without some version of the same plain reckoning: that he had gone to Ros dhomhain to ask a court to honour his grandmother's marriage, and had come home having taught it, instead, exactly how to use that marriage against every family on the coast that had made one like it.

## VI. Of Faire nan Lòchran and Griogair mac Ailein

It was in the third year after the ruling, in the winter of the hundred and thirteenth, that word reached Baile dhìreach of a fellowship newly begun among the pit crews of Baile thais and Cnoc chaol, calling itself no more than a burial club, the kind of quiet society that put a little of every member's wage aside against the cost of a decent burying, of which there were a dozen along that coast already and nothing remarkable in one more. Thomas Hale, who had by then some name among the coast's custodians as a man who understood, better than most, exactly how far Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's law would and would not reach, was asked by a letter from Baile thais, carried by a carter who said little about who had sent it, whether he would come and speak for a man of the club who had died in A' Chompanaidh's lock-up at Cnoc chaol, and whose burying the club meant to see done properly, in A' Chompanaidh's own eye as well as the district's.

The man was Griogair mac Ailein, a breaker of some sixty years who had worked the sorting-floor at Cnoc chaol since before the pits there had needed a night shift, and who had been taken up by A' Chompanaidh's watchmen in the autumn of that year for a matter the letter described only as a dispute over short weight at the pay office, and had died in the lock-up before any hearing was set for him, of a fever A' Chompanaidh's own surgeon recorded as already advanced when he was taken. He had no family living to claim him, and the club, gathering what it could from its members' wages, had bought the ground and the box and asked only that someone stand at the graveside who could read, aloud, in A' Chompanaidh's own tongue, the surgeon's note releasing the body, so that no clerk afterward could say the burying had been done without A' Chompanaidh's own leave.

The man who carried the letter to Baile dhìreach, a crew-leader of Baile thais named Dòmhnall Ruadh, said little of Griogair mac Ailein beyond the plain facts of the dispute, a matter of a short-weighted cartload the pay clerk had charged against him and Griogair had refused, in words the clerk did not care to repeat afterward, to sign for; but he said a good deal, walking the last mile up to Cnoc chaol at Thomas's side, of why the club wanted the surgeon's note read openly and not merely filed. "A man who dies owing A' Chompanaidh money," Dòmhnall said, "is a man A' Chompanaidh is glad enough to forget, and forgetting is a cheap way to be rid of a debt. We mean the pay office to know he was buried honestly, and that somebody stood at the grave who could read what was owed him back." Thomas asked what, precisely, was owed a dead man, and Dòmhnall said only that a name entered correctly, and a note read aloud, and a stone that would carry his name after the club that raised it had itself been forgotten, were the whole of what Faire nan Lòchran could promise anyone yet, and that they had learned, the hard way, not to promise more.

Thomas walked the four days to Cnoc chaol in the last week of am Faoilleach, in weather no better than the winter that had carried his own suit to its ruling, and stood at a grave dug on the bare hill above the pit-head with some thirty men and women of the club around him, most of them pit crews he had never met, drawn from a fellowship he understood, standing there, to be a good deal more than a burial club, though nobody that day said so aloud and he did not ask. He read the surgeon's note in the flat, careful humans' tongue his grandfather had taught him as a boy, folded it, and gave it to the man beside him, who he learned afterward was one of those who had first proposed the club and had chosen, for reasons of his own that Thomas never asked after either, to call it Faire nan Lòchran among its own members and nothing so plain as a burial club at all, though the name it gave A' Chompanaidh's clerks stayed the same.

No one else in that company that day could have read the paper Thomas held, and he understood, standing at the grave's edge with the thin cold wind coming off the pit-head and the box already lowered, what his own hard winter at Ros dhomhain had left him. The seam was A' Chompanaidh's now, and the eleven cuttings had gone the same road behind it. What remained was a plain skill, bought at the cost of a ruling that had taught him exactly how little Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's law honoured any custom, and it was worth more, standing at a stranger's grave, to the men and women around him than any cutting on any hill had ever been.
