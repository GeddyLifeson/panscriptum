# Of the Gauze on the Water

*Part One: Of the Opening of the Sea*

*AE 1 – 2*

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Manannan, keep the sea shut.

## I. Of the Four Witnesses

This telling is set down in Seilear Muileann chaol, in the winter the rolls came down to it, by the copyists of Na Trì Teaghlaichean, who have kept the custodians' copies there through the last years of this age and now have more paper than shelf. The chests came in by cart over the frozen ruts from the west, some with their lids nailed and some with a rope round them, and some still smelling of the lamp-soot of the rooms they had lain in. There are four witnesses in them, and the telling leans on each in turn.

The first witness is the rolls of the custodians: the rolls of each town, and above them the roll of Comhairle a' Chùraim at Cathair dhearg, in the hands of a hundred and fifty years of clerks, a few fine and most of them hurried. The second is the letter-books of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, which were found on the shelves of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir when its last master went down to the water and left them behind. They are written in the humans' tongue and the humans' letters, and three of the copyists have learned to read them. The third is the daybooks of the beacon-keepers, who wrote down every sail they saw and every night the flame was lit, and had nothing to gain by lying about a ship that was not there. The fourth is the songs of the camps. They were forbidden, and for that reason somebody always wrote them down.

Where the four agree, the telling goes on without stopping. Where they quarrel, each is heard. When they fall silent, the telling says so once and goes on to the next thing it knows.

The age they speak of began in a winter like other winters. The crown had stood empty since the close of An Aois Scaraidh, and the council sat in its place at Cathair dhearg, which had worked well enough for long enough that nobody asked any more whether it should. Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig was kept: the coal of the vein was burned in the measured handful at the shrines and nowhere else, and the island's hearths burned peat and driftwood and the wood of their own hedges. Clann Choinnich's petition to smelt silver with the coal lay unanswered in Taigh na Fèithe. The north-west was nearly empty, its fields gone back to rush and its harbours to the gulls. At Seann Skell of the west eleven people had kept Deas-ghnàth a' Chalaidh Fhalaimh on Cidhe an Diosail the last time the harbour scribe troubled to count them, and the priests of Manannan went down to the river-mouth at every tide, morning and evening and in the dark between, to say their prayer:

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*

They said it because Ceò Mhanannain was thinning. For eighteen centuries it had stood on the eastern sea, where Cabhlach an Scaraidh had sailed into it and not come out, and on the north it had stood too, a grey wall a few oar-lengths off the capes, so that the sea ran into it and nothing came back. In the last winters of the old age the priests wrote that it lay thinner each year. On still mornings the fishers of the north-west could see through it to a dark line ruled across the edge of the world, which was the open sea going on. They saw it, and turned for home as the rule of sight-of-land told them, and said nothing about it in the alehouse, because there was nothing to say.

## II. Of the Gauze off the Cape

Lachlann Odhar was steersman of the larger of the two boats of Achadh àrsaidh that went out after the winter cod in the month of am Faoilleach, in the first year of this age. He was a thick, sallow man of near forty with a broken nose and a habit of talking to the tiller. Achadh àrsaidh was a small place then, a few households on the rise behind the north-west cape who farmed a little and fished more, and it kept two boats because it could not man three.

They went out in the dark with the lines baited and the lanterns shuttered, and by first light they were off the cape in the long swell, where the water shelves down from the rocks and the cod lie in the cold. The other boat worked a cable's length to the west, Lachlann's brother-in-law at her steering-oar. The wind was light out of the north and the sky clear. Behind them the land stood up black. Ahead of them, where the grey had always stood, there was no wall.

Lachlann saw it before any of his crew did, because he was the only one of them not looking at his line. The grey was there, but it had gone to gauze: a thin, hanging, shifting stuff that the light came through, and through it he could see the sea going on, and on, farther than any man of Achadh àrsaidh had been told the sea went. He watched it a long while. Old men said that Uilebheist na Mara rose in that water when it pleased, and Lachlann had half a mind that the paleness moving in the gauze was the beast's back.

Then the paleness took a shape, and the shape was sail.

It was one sail, high up, heaped on a mast taller than any tree Lachlann had ever cut or seen cut; then a second behind it and a third, and under them black hulls, wet and shining, riding higher out of the water than a hall stands on its hill. They came down out of the north slowly, with the grey tearing off their bows in rags and hanging a moment in their rigging before the wind took it. There were men along their sides. He could see the white of their faces.

Nobody in the boat spoke. One of the young men let his line run out through his fingers until it burned him, and swore, and let it go.

The first ship came near enough that Lachlann could hear her: the groan of her timbers, a block squealing somewhere up in the rigging, and then a man's voice from high on the stern, calling down.

"Ahoy the boat! What land is this? What land?"

It was no tongue that Lachlann had ever heard, or anyone at Achadh àrsaidh. He stood up at the tiller, set his feet wide against the roll, cupped his hands, and gave back the old hail of the north-west boats, which was the hail his father had given and his father before him, and which asks the stranger's harbour and his kin:

"Cò às a thàinig sibh?"

The voice on the ship called again, louder, as men do when they are not understood, and then another voice joined it, and a third, all saying something different. Lachlann hailed again. The ship's people pointed at the land, and at their mouths, and at the land again. One of them held up a wooden cask by its rope-handle and swung it so that it banged on the ship's side, and even at that distance Lachlann could hear that it was empty.

He sat down.

"Well," said his brother-in-law, who had brought the other boat in close. "What do we do?"

Nothing in all the customs of Dia-thìr told a fisherman what to do when the sea opened. There was the rule of sight-of-land, which said turn back, and there was the old law of the shore, which said that a man who asks for water is given water. The one told him to go home and the other told him to stay, and neither told him what to do with ships out of the north and a tongue that meant nothing.

"You go home," said Lachlann at last. "Tell them at Achadh àrsaidh what you saw. Tell them I've gone south to Cuan shean, and I've taken these with me, if they'll come."

"Why Cuan shean?"

"Because there are custodians there whose business it is to know. I don't know. You don't know."

His brother-in-law looked at the ships a while longer, spat over the side for luck, and put about for the cape. The boat went off into the light with her crew all turned round on the thwarts, looking back.

Lachlann put his tiller over and bore away south along the coast. When he looked back, the ships were following him.

That was the longest day of his life, by his own telling, and he told it often afterward. They ran south with the north wind on the quarter, past the headlands of the empty coast one after another, the black ships a mile astern and never nearer, shortening sail to keep behind a fishing boat as a man shortens his stride to walk with a child. Past the cape of Baile chrom, where no fire had burned for a generation. Past the mouths of small rivers where nobody lived. His crew rowed when the wind dropped and sat with their hands between their knees when it rose again. They ate the bread they had brought for the fishing and drank the water, and toward evening one of them asked whether they should give some to the ships, and Lachlann said the ships would have to wait for the custodians like everyone else.

The fishers of Achadh àrsaidh gave the place of that meeting a name, Coinneachadh nan Long ({{date:IV-0001a}}), and their boats slow there still when they pass it, as if something might come out of the north again.

It was full dark when Lachlann brought his boat round the last point and saw the lights of Cuan shean along the water, the long low rope-walks black against the town. He ran her up the shingle, and the first man who came down to see who was landing at that hour found him sitting on the gunwale with his head in his hands, and three lanterns hanging out on the sea behind him where no lanterns had ever been.

## III. Of the Empty Cask

Oighrig nic Leòid had been custodian of Cuan shean for eleven years, and in that time the hardest thing she had been asked to decide was the line of a boundary between two rope-walks. She was near fifty, tall and big-handed, a widow whose sons had gone inland to the salt-pans and the capital, and she lived in the custodian's house above the harbour with a servant and a grey cat and a roll that had nothing much written in it.

They woke her that night. She went down to the shingle in her cloak and looked at the lanterns, and at Lachlann Odhar, and heard him out. Then she said that nobody was to put off to the ships in the dark, nor light any fire on the strand that could be taken for a sign, and that she would see them in the morning; and she went home and did not sleep.

In the morning there was no seeing them. The wind went round into the west in the night and blew hard, and when the light came Na Trì Longan were standing off and on beyond the bar, well out, with their topsails reefed, the sea breaking white along the reef between them and the harbour. They kept off like that for days, through a run of westerly weather such as the west coast gets in the back end of winter, and the whole town of Cuan shean stood on the dunes in the wind and watched them. Children made up names for them. The rope-makers laid bets on whether they would come in or go. Old women said they were the ships of Cabhlach an Scaraidh come home at last, and other old women told them not to talk nonsense, and they went on saying it. At Seann Skell, half a day's walk up the coast, the priests of Manannan heard of the ships from a carter the second morning, and from that tide on they kept the prayer at every turn of the water without once missing, and a boy ran down the coast each evening to see whether the ships were still there, and ran back to tell them.

Lachlann Odhar stayed in Cuan shean. He said his boat was damaged, which was not true, and slept in a net-loft, and each morning he stood with the rest on the dunes.

The roll of Cuan shean sets the ships down first on {{date:IV-0002}}. On that morning the wind fell and the swell lay down, and the strangers' ships came in over the bar on the flood, one behind the other, feeling their way with a lead-line that a man swung and called from the bows, and let go their anchors off the town in water where no hull of their size had lain since Cabhlach an Scaraidh went out sunwise from Seann Skell and did not come back. The roar of the cables running out came over the water, and every gull on the strand went up at once.

Then a boat came off the nearest ship, with a man standing in the stern.

Oighrig went down to the shingle. She had her two fellow custodians with her, both older than she was and neither of them willing to be first, and behind them half the town, and behind the town the dunes with more people on them. She had put on her good cloak, and then thought better of it and put on her old one, and then gone back for the good one, and she was angry with herself about that all the way down the hill.

The boat grounded in the surf a stone's throw out, and the man in the stern jumped over the side into the water. It was up to his thighs and it was the cold sea of the back end of winter, and he gasped with it, and came on wading with a cask held up against his chest in both arms. Eleven men followed him out of the boat. She looked for weapons among them, and saw none, though one had a long knife at his belt such as a fisherman carries. They were thin, all of them. Two of them had to hold each other up in the surf. The one in front was a man of about thirty-five with a short brown beard gone salt at the edges, a coat that had been good once, and eyes that went quickly over everything on the beach and came back to her, because she was the one standing a step in front.

The roll calls him Edmund Harrow. He came up out of the water onto the shingle, and stopped, and set the cask down at her feet, and knocked on it with his knuckles so that she could hear it was empty.

"Water," he said. "We need water, ma'am. We've been on short allowance a fortnight and the men are sick. Water. Do you understand me?"

She heard it as sounds. He heard her silence and tried again, slower, as men do, and then gave it up and did what any child of any shore would have done: he pointed at the cask and tipped his hand to his mouth, and made a little tilting motion with it, and swallowed.

There is a law of the shore on Dia-thìr older than the custodians, older than Taigh na Fèithe and the crown, and every fisher's child knows it before she knows her letters. A man who comes up out of the sea and asks for water is given water. It asks nothing about who he is.

"Bring it," said Oighrig nic Leòid.

They brought it from the town well in two buckets, and she dipped the ladle herself and gave it into his hands, and he drank, and gave the ladle to the man behind him, and did not drink again until all eleven had drunk. She noticed that. The roll is silent on it; she said it afterward to the council in her letter, and the letter survives.

Then he pointed at the ships, and at the well, and made a gesture that took in the whole town and its wells and its fields behind, and looked at her with his eyebrows up.

She shook her head.

It was not hers to give. Water was given under the law of the shore. Anything more, a stay of a night on the strand, a sack of meal, a place for a sick man to lie, was a thing no custodian of Cuan shean had ever been asked in eighteen hundred years, and she knew it was not hers, and she knew that if she gave it the giving would be written in the roll and be a thing that had been done. She had them fill the cask. She had them fill six more that the boat went back to fetch. Then she went up the hill to the custodian's house and wrote to Cathair dhearg.

The rider left within the hour. He was a rope-maker's son who kept a good horse, and she told him not to spare it. It is some twenty-six miles from Cuan shean up the valley road to the capital, and he was at the council's door by nightfall with the horse lathered and blown, and he gave the letter into the hand of the clerk of the council, who read it standing up, then sat down and read it again.

## IV. Of the Forty Days below the Dunes

The council's first answer came back two days later. It said: feed them; keep them apart; decide nothing; the council will sit on it.

So Cuan shean fed them. The custodians would not let the strangers into the town, and would not let them stay aboard their ships and starve, and between the two they settled on the strand below the dunes, where the shingle gives way to hard sand and the marram grass holds the slope, a long stretch of beach with the town above it on one side and the sea on the other. The strangers came ashore there and put up shelters out of their spare sails and spars, and the town carried food down to them morning and evening. The roll gives it as forty days of feeding below the dunes. The roll counts the days and not the meal, which the custodians paid out of the town's own store and afterward put in the account to the council, where it was never repaid.

They were not easy days. The strangers were sick, most of them, with a sickness of the gums and the joints that comes to men who have lived too long on ship's food, and three of them died on the beach in the first weeks and were buried by their own people above the tide-line with a strange rite: a man read from a book, the rest stood bareheaded, and then all of them knelt on the sand. The children of Cuan shean watched that from the top of the dunes and could not be driven off. Afterward they played at it in the lanes, one reading out of a flat stone and the rest kneeling, until their mothers stopped them.

The fresh meat and the greens mended the rest. By the time the spring had come in, the strangers were walking about the strand like men and not like ghosts, and it was then that the town began to know them.

They could not be kept from working. That was the first thing Cuan shean learned about them. They had come down half starved and sick in a strange country, penned on a beach by people whose tongue they had not one word of, and as soon as they could stand they wanted something to do with their hands. They mended their own sails and caulked their boats. And then, because the fishers of Cuan shean spread their nets to dry along that same strand, the strangers began to mend the fishers' nets.

Nobody had asked them to. A fisher came down one morning and found a stranger sitting on the shingle with her torn net across his knees, knotting it up, and she stood over him with her mouth open until he looked up and grinned at her and held out his hand for more twine. He tied a knot the fishers had never seen, a flat hard knot that did not slip. By the end of the week half the strangers were at it. The fishers watched and learned the knot, and it is tied at Cuan shean to this day and called the strangers' knot.

Trade began the same way, out of nothing, with nobody deciding it. A stranger held out a button off his coat, a round thing of bright yellow metal with a device stamped on it, to a boy carrying a string of fish up the beach. The boy took the button and gave him a fish. By the next morning there were fishwives on the strand with baskets, and strangers with buttons, and the strangers' coats began to hang open. The custodians forbade it, and it went on, and they forbade it again, and it went on behind the dunes where they could not see. Oighrig let it be in the end. The roll notes that a good many buttons of yellow metal went into the town that spring and that the fishwives wore them on cords round their necks, and nothing more is said of it.

Word of the ships had reached Baile fhiadhaich, up the western coast, in two days, as word goes along a coast. Its custodian did not come at once, for the roads were bad and his knees were worse, but when the roads dried he rode south with two of his people to see for himself. He was an old man, a fisher's son who had held his town for thirty years and had opinions about everything, and he spent three days on the dunes and one on the strand among the strangers, feeling their ropes, looking at their blocks and their tackle, walking round their boats with his hands behind him. He asked Oighrig to take him out to the ships and she would not. Then he went home, and wrote to the council that the strangers' ropes were better than ours and their manners worse. The council kept the letter. The rope-makers of Cuan shean heard what he had said about the ropes before the ink was dry, and they took it hard, and three of them went down to the strand the next day to look at the strangers' cordage for themselves. They came back and said nothing, which in a rope-maker is a great deal.

Harrow came to Oighrig's door many times in those weeks. He learned her name, or the sound of it, and he learned the word for water and the word for bread and the word for no, which he heard more than the other two. She learned his. He brought her things: a little glass that made small writing large, which she put away in a drawer and took out at night when nobody was looking; a length of fine cloth; a knife. She sent the knife back and kept the glass. He tried to tell her, with his hands and a stick in the sand and a great deal of talk she did not follow, where he had come from, and she understood from his drawing that it was north, and then east, round some great corner of sea, and she did not like the drawing and rubbed it out with her foot when he had gone.

She was never sure afterward whether she had liked him. She said in her letter that he was a man who asked for one thing at a time and was glad of it, and who always had the next thing ready.

Through all those days the priests of Manannan at Seann Skell kept the tide-prayer. When the boy told them the strangers were on the strand below Cuan shean, eating the town's bread, the eldest of the priests said that it made no difference, and they did not miss a tide in the forty days, nor in the days after. The harbour scribe wrote down their number each week, which was nine, and the state of Ceò Mhanannain, which was thin.

## V. Of the Leave and the Margin

The council sat on it through the summer and into the autumn, and then, because nothing had been decided, sat on it through the winter. Custodians came in to Cathair dhearg from all over the island, from the hill towns and the eastern shore and the silver country and the far southern ports, some of them riding for a week, to sit in the council hall with the rain coming in off the lake and argue about ships that most of them had never seen. Ceann a' Chùraim in those years was Tormod mac Ailein, a heavy, careful man of forty-five with a lawyer's memory and a smith's hands, and he let them argue, and wrote down what they said, and said very little himself.

The strangers waited. They had long since come off the beach; the custodians had let them into the net-lofts along the harbour when the autumn gales began, since they could not in conscience leave men under sailcloth through a west-coast winter, and it was put in the roll that this was a shelter, and gave no leave. The ships lay in the harbour with skeleton crews aboard and their yards sent down. Harrow went twice to Cathair dhearg under the council's safe-conduct and stood in the hall and was looked at, and came back, and said nothing to anyone in Cuan shean about what he had seen there.

Oighrig nic Leòid fell ill at Samhain.

It began as a cough and went into her chest, and by the month of an Dùbhlachd she could not get out of her bed. The physician of Cuan shean sat with her, and her servant, and a sister of hers who came from inland; and Lachlann Odhar, who had never gone home, came up the hill one evening with a fish wrapped in a cloth, and stood in her doorway turning his cap in his hands, and she laughed at him and it made her cough for a quarter of an hour.

She knew the council was near to deciding. The custodians who passed through Cuan shean on their way up to Cathair dhearg came to her bedside, one after another, and told her so, each in the tone of a man who thinks he has brought news. She knew the way the hall was leaning. She knew that the iron the strangers carried in their ships was better than any iron on the island, that their carpenters had raised a boat-shed on the strand in four days that the carpenters of Cuan shean would have taken a month over, that their ships were the first ships in eighteen centuries, and that the ships that had brought them could come again. Every custodian who came to her bed told her one of those things. None of them told her anything else.

So she wrote to the council from her bed. The letter is in the roll of Cuan shean, copied fair by her clerk, with her own mark at the foot of it in a hand that shakes. It is short. She wrote that it was she who had given the strangers water, on the shore, under the old law of the shore, and that she had given them nothing more on her own word, because nothing more was hers to give. She wrote that she had fed them since because the council told her to, and that she had done it gladly. Last, she set down that the council should know, before it gave them anything else, that a leave given in the roll is given, and cannot be ungiven by the next roll, and that the strangers would know that better than the council did, since it was they who would be holding it.

She asked that it be read out before the vote. The clerk's note on the copy says that it was received, and does not say that it was read.

On {{date:IV-0005}} Comhairle a' Chùraim, sitting at Cathair dhearg in the crown's place, gave the strangers leave to stay, with Tormod mac Ailein in the chair. The clerk entered the vote in the roll as given with one voice. Tormod signed below it.

Later, in another ink and another hand, somebody wrote in the margin beside the entry that three custodians had been absent that day. It does not say who they were. Oighrig nic Leòid was one of them, lying in her bed at Cuan shean with the window shut against the wind. The other two are not named anywhere.

The copyists of Seilear Muileann chaol have kept that margin as they found it, with the note beside the vote. It is the first place in the record of An Aois Choigreach where what was written and what was so do not quite meet.

The news came back down the valley road to Cuan shean two days after. The rope-maker's son who had ridden up with the first letter rode down with the last, and went up the hill to Oighrig's house to tell her before he told the town, because he thought she ought to hear it first. She was asleep. Her sister told her when she woke, and she lay a while looking at the ceiling, and then asked for the cat.

She lived through the winter. She kept the custody of Cuan shean for many years yet, and never spoke of the vote to anyone, so far as the roll knows, except once, to Lachlann Odhar, and what she said to him he did not repeat.

## VI. Of the Quarter of the Sea

The leave was read out on the strand at Cuan shean by one of Oighrig's fellow custodians, standing on an upturned boat, with a stranger beside him who had learned a little of the island's tongue in the net-lofts over the winter and put it into his own as well as he could. The strangers cheered, three times, in the way they had. The townspeople did not know what cheering was, and some of them thought it was a threat, and went indoors. Harrow shook the custodian's hand, and then the hands of everyone near him, and then went out to his ship in the boat, and that afternoon the strangers began to send the ships' yards back up.

It was in those weeks, at the back end of that second winter, that the saying began to go round the harbour.

It was the old sailors who said it first, the men who sat on the bench at the foot of the rope-walks in any weather and had done so since before most of the town was born. None of them had ever been out of sight of land, since the rule forbade it, but they had fished the western water all their lives, and their fathers before them, and they knew the set of every current and the lie of every sea-mark from the north-west capes to the mouth of the capital's river. They had watched the ships come in over the bar. They had watched the strangers' lead-line, and the way the ships stood off in the westerly blow and where they stood to. Over the winter they had talked with Lachlann Odhar about the gauze off the cape and the course the ships had come down on, and they had put it all together over a good many evenings on the bench.

What they said was this: that the strangers had come in from the same quarter of the sea into which Cabhlach an Scaraidh had sailed.

That was all they said. They did not say what it meant. But it went up the valley road with the carters in a week, and it was said in the capital, and in the council hall, where at last a custodian of the hill country stood up and said it out loud before Tormod mac Ailein and the whole council. The council forbade the saying in its hall ({{date:IV-0006}}). The clerk was told to strike it from the roll. He struck it, and then, being a careful man, wrote below the striking what it was he had been told to strike, so that the next clerk should know. It is there still, and it was never answered.

Lachlann Odhar went home to Achadh àrsaidh at last in the month of an Gearran, more than a year after he had left it for a morning's fishing. On the day he went he walked down to the harbour at Cuan shean early, with his bag over his shoulder, and stood on the harbour wall to look at the ships. They lay at anchor off the town with their yards crossed and their sails loosed to dry, and there were strangers going up and down their sides and in and out of the town now as if it were their own. Three of the old sailors were on the bench below the wall, and a fishwife with a yellow button on a cord, and a boy mending a net with the strangers' knot, and a stranger with him, showing him how to draw it tight.

Lachlann stood up on the wall. He was a man who had brought Na Trì Longan down the coast behind him in a day, and he had heard that the saying was forbidden in the council hall, and he was not in the council hall.

"They came out of the quarter Cabhlach an Scaraidh went into," he said, loudly, so that the whole harbour could hear it. "I saw them come. The grey was gauze, and they came down out of it from the north, the way the old men say the old ships went. There's nobody here who doesn't know it."

The old sailors on the bench looked at him and then looked away at the water. The fishwife picked up her basket. The boy went on with the net. The stranger beside him looked up at Lachlann, hearing a man shouting from a wall, and smiled uncertainly, and went back to showing the boy the knot.

Nobody answered him. Lachlann stood a while longer on the wall with the wind pulling at his coat. Then he climbed down, and went along the strand to his boat, and put out for the north, and was a long way up the coast before the harbour lost sight of him.
