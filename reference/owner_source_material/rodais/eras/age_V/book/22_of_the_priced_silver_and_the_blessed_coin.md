# Of the Priced Silver and the Blessed Coin

*AE 80 – 87*

## I. Of the Silver Priced by Dia-thìreach Hands

At Muileann chiar in the autumn of the eightieth year Catrìona nic Eachainn weighed her first bar of guild silver alone, without Ùna nic Choinnich's hand at her elbow, because Ùna nic Choinnich had been ten years in the ground and there was no one else the guild trusted with the scale on a market morning.

She had come to the silver town a year before with nothing but the clothes she stood in and a name A' Chompanaidh had marked as one not to be hired again in any pit on the island, and the guild had taken her on as it took on anyone with steady hands and no fear of the acid vats, and had not asked what she had done at Baile thais. She learned the trade fast: how to read the ore by its weight in the hand before the fire ever touched it, how to keep the ledger that answered to no clerk of A' Chompanaidh, how the guild's own price for a bar of silver was set by the guild alone, at a hall table, by seven refiners' vote, and was the one price on the whole island that no human hand had ever moved.

It was a small thing to be proud of and she was proud of it anyway.

The town had grown around her without her much noticing the growing, the way a wall grows a foot at a time and is only a wall when you look up from your work and find you cannot see over it. Two years before there had been one street of stone houses and a smithy; now there were five streets, and a second town besides, a mile to the west, that the guild had raised for its carters and coal-breakers when the old town's lanes could no longer hold them, and which was called by the old town's own name, so that a stranger asking the way to Muileann chiar was as often as not sent to the wrong one. The custodians' rolls, when they had cause to write of either, called them the old town and the new, and did not always trouble to say which they meant.

Below the town, where the burn ran warm out of the hillside, Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had built a bath-house that autumn for its officers who came up sick from the coast fevers to take the water. The springs had been the island's own since before any custodian now living could say; the Dia-thìrich of the district had bathed there each Bealltainn and each Samhain time out of mind, for the joints and for the skin complaints of the deep galleries, and had never paid a penny to do it. Now they were let in on two days of the humans' week, and charged a penny at the door for the water that had been theirs to walk into unasked. Catrìona went once, in the second winter, to see it for herself, and paid her penny, and sat in water that scalded exactly as it always had, and came out and told the guild hall that the water had not changed at all, only the door.

Silversmiths began that year to settle along the carters' road at Muileann bhàn, half a day's walk west, working the guild's bar into brooches and clasps and buckles for the humans' own market, since a piece of worked silver crossed the sea for far more than a bar of it ever had. It was, Catrìona came to understand, turning the guild's ledgers over of an evening, the one craft of the island sold across the water under its own name and no other; every bolt of cloth, every keg of salt fish, every cask of the island's own barley beer went out under A' Chompanaidh's mark, but a Muileann bhàn brooch went out under its own maker's mark, because the humans who bought it liked to say where it had come from.

A' Chompanaidh asked, that same year and the next, whether the guild would sell its silver at A' Chompanaidh's price rather than its own, and was refused both times, and asked a third time with a paper naming a magistrate at Ros dhomhain who might be brought to rule on the question, and was refused a third time, more shortly. Catrìona was in the hall for the third refusal and watched the seven refiners vote it down without one dissenting hand, and thought, though she did not say it aloud, that this was the whole of what she had walked east over the hills to find: one table in the whole width of the island where the Dia-thìrich still set the price.

## II. Of the Seat Ten Years Empty

In the autumn of the eighty-first year Fearghas went up the hill to a council he had no vote in but had been asked to bless: the seating of a new Ceann a' Chùraim, the first in ten years.

Alasdair Liath had held the seat twenty-seven years and had been dead ten; since his burying the council had sat without a head of its own choosing, unable to agree on one, and had said among itself, in the hearing of no clerk of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, that an empty seat answered no letters and signed no papers Riaghaltas nan Coigreach might afterward hold against the whole council, and that this was not the worst thing a seat could be. Fearghas had heard Ealasaid Liath say once that ten years without a head was an answer of its own kind, though not one anybody had meant to give.

The council chose, at last, Artair mac Iain, a custodian of the eastern shires six-and-fifty that winter, a quiet man who had kept his own town's rolls without a blot for thirty years and had never once, in all that time, been summoned to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir for anything. He took the seat in the same hall where Tormod mac Ailein had signed Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg, under the same beams, and said, when he was asked for words, only that he would keep the rolls as they had always been kept, and sat down.

Fearghas said the order's blessing over him, both horses named, the dark and the light, and found the words came easier over a man than they had come over a coin, though he could not then have said why, only that a man could answer for himself and a coin could not.

## III. Of the Two Horses

Fearghas mac Mhuirich had kept the fire of Òrd Mhacha at Cathair dhearg for eleven years before Tolley's letter came, and in that time he had blessed ploughs, byres, bridges, the first-born of three custodian houses, and once, at a fisherman's asking, a boat that sank within the month with the blessing still wet on her bow. He did not think himself proud about any of it. A blessing was a word said in the right order over a thing that could not say it for itself, and Òrd Mhacha had said such words at Cathair dhearg since before Rìghrean na Cloiche were a memory anyone kept whole.

The letter was carried up from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir by a boy in a coat of A' Chompanaidh who did not know what he carried and did not wait for an answer. It asked, in the courteous hand Riaghaltas nan Coigreach used for everything it meant to have, that the priests of Òrd Mhacha bless the new coin of the realm before it was struck a second time, so that the humans' subjects on the island might take it without fear of ill luck.

The eldest of the order, a woman named Ealasaid Liath who had held the fire longer than Fearghas had been alive, read the letter twice and laughed once, without much humour in it. "They want the horses to run for them," she said. "Well. The horses have run for stranger riders."

She meant the rite of the two, Dubh Sainglenn the black and Liath Macha the grey, that stood at the heart of every blessing the order gave: the dark hour and the bright, the labour and the rest, the thing spent and the thing kept, each with its due and neither without the other. It was the oldest teaching the order had, older than any building at Cathair dhearg, and Fearghas had said the words of it over a hundred doorsteps and a thousand infants without once doubting that the two halves were equal, and that a blessing given to one alone was no blessing at all, only a wish dressed as one.

"The coin is one thing," he said. "It is spent, and it does not rest. What is the grey horse's part in it?"

Ealasaid Liath looked at him with the patience she kept for the youngest of the order, though he was forty-five that year and had buried his own teacher. "The grey horse's part," she said, "is that the coin passes from a full hand to an empty one, and comes back full again, in its season, as the tide does. That is the rest it takes. You are thinking of the box it sits in. Think of the road it walks."

He went with her to the temple on the morning appointed, {{date:IV-0175}}, with the whole order in their grey and black and Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's clerk standing at the door with a satchel of the new-struck marks, bright as fish-scale, a ship stamped on the one face and nothing of the island on the other. Tolley himself did not come; he sent word that the ordinance of an Àrd-mhaor's presence would overweight a religious matter, which those of the order who cared to be amused found a fine thing for a man to say who had ordered the blessing in the first place.

Ealasaid Liath said the words over the satchel, both halves of them, the dark and the light, in the old order, and the fire took the smoke of the burnt hazel the rite asked for, and it was done. Fearghas stood at his place in the second rank and said the words with the rest, because that is what a member of an order does, and because he could not, that morning, have told anyone in what particular the thing felt wrong. Only that when the clerk gathered up the satchel and thanked them and went, he found himself watching the door a long while after it had shut, the way a man watches a place where he has mislaid something without yet knowing what.

Three of the younger priests would not stand at their places that day and stood instead at the back of the hall with their arms folded. Nothing was said to them then. Ealasaid Liath was not a woman to make an example in front of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's clerk. But that night, over the last of the fire, she told Fearghas what she had not troubled to tell them: that a coin blessed with both horses' names was still, in the end, only a coin, and that the order's business was the rite done rightly, not the uses a stranger's hand put a rightly-blessed thing to afterward. "We do not answer for the road it walks," she said. "Only that it is a whole horse that walks it, and not half of one."

Fearghas turned that over for years before he understood he did not believe it. He said nothing of that yet. He kept the peace of his order that year, and kept it.

He kept it, too, at the cost of small things he only noticed afterward, looking back. That year he stopped walking the lower town on the mornings Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's clerks changed their coin at the money-tables by the bridge, where a queue of Dia-thìrich waited with barter goods to be weighed against the new mark and went away with fewer coins than the goods, by the old reckoning, ought to have bought. The bridge road was longer and quieter, he told himself, and left it there. He kept the fire, said the hours, blessed what was brought to him, and did not again stand at the temple door to watch a satchel of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir carried out into the town, though he heard, from those of the order less careful of where they walked, that the second and third strikings of the coin went out from that door much as the first had.

## IV. Of the Fleece and the Boats

The second cheapening of the coin fell in the year after the blessing, read out at every market cross by a crier of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir who did not trouble to soften it: the barter goods of the Dia-thìrich, wool and hide and dried fish and the rest, were marked down a second time against the mark, on a table Riaghaltas nan Coigreach alone had drawn, and no custodian's council was asked to sign it or shown it before it was cried.

Word of it reached Muileann chiar three days after Cathair dhearg, carried by a carter who had heard it cried at Baile Mòr fhionn on his way east and had thought the guild ought to know, since silver was not named in the table and the guild's own price stood as it had. He told it at the hall table over his supper, and told also what he had seen at Baile Mòr fhionn itself: the weavers standing about the market cross with their wool in baskets at their feet, working out on their fingers what a fleece was worth under the new table, and finding it was worth what half a fleece had been worth the week before.

The weavers of Baile Mòr fhionn would not sell at the table. That winter they carried no wool to the humans' market at all, and the wool sat in their warehouses through two winters until the moths had the better part of it.

At Caol fhiadhaich, a fishing town on the same coast that had never dealt in coal or silver at all, the harbour dues were called in marks that year for the first time, where they had always been called in fish or in kind, and the town could not raise the sum any way but by selling boats. It sold eleven of its two-and-twenty, at A' Chompanaidh's own asking price, which was low, since A' Chompanaidh was the only buyer on that coast with marks to spend; and the men who had sailed the eleven boats went into the pits the following season under A' Chompanaidh's contracts, because a man who has sold his boat must eat from somewhere.

Catrìona heard all this the way the guild hall heard everything, as news carried by carters and confirmed or corrected by the next carter after, and set it beside what she had seen with her own eyes at the bath-house door, and thought that she was coming to understand the shape of the thing A' Chompanaidh was building, one small cheapening at a time, the way a tide takes a shore in ten thousand waves, each so small a man standing on the sand would swear the water had not moved at all.

## V. Of the Second Asking

Riaghaltas nan Coigreach struck a new coin the year after, when the barter was marked down the second time, larger than the old and stamped, Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's letter said, so that no forger on the island might easily copy it, though there had been no forger caught in all the years of the first. The letter asked, in the same courteous hand as before, that Òrd Mhacha bless it as the first had been blessed.

Ealasaid Liath had died in the winter between, of a lung complaint that took her slowly and without complaint, and Fearghas kept the fire in her place, the eldest now among a much younger order, most of whom had not stood in the hall the first time.

He read the letter at the fire that night and did not sleep, and in the morning called the order together and told them he would not say the words over this coin, and that any who wished to say them without him might, since he held no rule over another priest's mouth, only over his own.

None went.

It was not, he told them, a matter of the humans' coin being unworthy of a blessing; a plough was no worthier and he had blessed a hundred ploughs. It was that the order had been asked, the year before, to bless the road a thing walked and not the thing that walked it, and had told itself it answered only for the horse and not the rider; and he had watched the road a year now, through the second marking-down of the barter, and did not believe that any longer. The coin did not pass from a full hand to an empty one and back again in its season, as the grey horse's part required. It passed from an empty hand to a fuller one, always in the one direction, down the same road every year the same way, and no rite he knew how to say could make a one-way road into a wheel.

"Then bless the wheel," said the youngest of them, a woman named Sìne who had joined the order the winter before, "and let the road answer for itself."

"I do not know how to bless a wheel that only turns one way," Fearghas said. "If you know, teach me, and I will say the words gladly."

She did not know either. Nobody in the hall that morning knew, and the not-knowing sat among them a long while before anyone spoke again.

Word of the refusal reached Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir within the month, as such words always did, carried by whichever clerk had cause to visit the temple on other business and found the fire kept but the coin unblessed on the shelf where it had been left. Tolley did not send for Fearghas. He had larger matters on his desk than one priest's obstinacy over a rite the coin did not in any case require to pass current; the letter asking the blessing had been courtesy, not law, and the coin was spent in every market on the island with or without it. But Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's letter-book that year notes, in a clerk's hand, that the order of Òrd Mhacha at the capital was "divided on a religious nicety," and does not trouble to say what the nicety was, which is as much as most Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's papers ever granted the order's reasons in all the years of the age.

## VI. Of the Name at the Wash-Stones

It is told from these same years, though no roll sets the day of it, that the name the washing-women of Achadh dhomhain had given the girl of the spoil-houses went out of that camp and down the coal roads, to Àth ìseal and Muileann chrom and the huts at Cnoc fhiadhaich, and was said at wash-stones where nobody had ever seen her.

By the count of the years she was near grown. By the look of her, the women said, she was not; and the women who washed the dead knew better than anyone what a year does to a body. They called her *nighean an rìgh*, meaning Ailean Mòr, whom the gods crowned in the grove and who fell at Àth na Fala; and most of those who said it did not believe a word of the thousands of years, or said they did not, which came to the same thing at supper. A king four thousand years old was, to a woman with a week's ration to stretch and a husband on the night crew, a tale for the dark end of the year. They went on saying the name all the same, because it was a good name, and because a people whose hill was going out in ships will take its comfort where it finds it.

Nobody wrote it in a roll. The custodians of the camps had learned, in these years, to keep out of their rolls whatever Riaghaltas nan Coigreach might read as a claim, and a king's daughter, even a jest of one, was such a claim.

## VII. Of the Fire under the Trees

Fearghas mac Mhuirich left the temple at Cathair dhearg in the spring after his refusal, with eleven of the order who had come to think as he did, and walked north to Cnoc bheag, where Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag had stood since before the order was founded and where a fence of oak stakes, renewed generation on generation, still kept the cattle from the old stone that no one living could read.

They were not driven out. Nobody at the capital's temple forbade them the door; the eldest of those who stayed, a mild man named Griogair who had said the second blessing without Fearghas's help and thought no worse of him for the refusing, walked with them as far as the north road and told them the fire at Cathair dhearg would keep both horses' names as it always had, and that this was no falling-out between them, only two roads to the one rite, and that he hoped they might one day agree which was the truer.

They did not agree, not then and not for years after. But the custodians of Cnoc bheag, whose own seat at the council's table had gone begging more than once in these hard years, and several custodians of other towns who had lost their seats outright to A' Chompanaidh's magistrates and Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's displeasure and had nowhere else the old forms were still kept whole, came to the dissenting priests' hall by ones and twos through that first summer, until the forest held near as many custodians without a town as priests without a temple, and the two kinds of the dispossessed began, without either quite meaning it, to keep each other's company.

On the last evening of that first spring, Fearghas lit a fire under the oaks with hazel cut from the grove, as the rite asked, and said the words of the two horses over it, both halves in the old order, the dark and the light, exactly as Ealasaid Liath had taught him and exactly as he had said them over the first coin two years before. He asked nothing of anyone to walk any particular road that night. He asked only that the fire be a whole fire, spent and kept both, as a rite ought to be; and the custodians who stood in the ring about it, most of whom owned no seat and no roll and no staff any longer, watched it burn down to embers under the old stone's shadow, and said nothing at all, which among that company had come, in these years, to be its own kind of prayer.

## VIII. Of the Staff Passed On

In the spring that Fearghas walked north, word reached Muileann chiar by the usual road of carters and market gossip that Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill Maor, overseer of the coal country these five-and-twenty years, had died at Muileann chrom in his sixty-seventh year, of no illness the guild's informant could name, only of age and the staff's long weight.

Catrìona had cause to remember him without kindness: it was his hand that had signed the order forbidding the camps to keep more than a week's grain against her own Stad-oibre Baile thais, years before she ever came east, and the granary his order had built still stood at Muileann chrom, locked of a Friday evening the way it had always been locked. She felt, hearing of his death, less than she thought she ought to feel, and told the hall table only that a hard man was gone and a harder trade would go on without him.

The staff passed, as everyone in the coal country had long known it would, to his son Iain, seven-and-thirty that year, the first of the overseer's line schooled at Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise at Ros dhomhain, who was said to keep his household in Dia-thìris and his reports to A' Chompanaidh in the humans' own tongue, and to walk the galleries himself rather than send a clerk. What that would mean for the crews under him nobody at Muileann chiar could yet say, and the guild's answer, when the news was set before the hall table, was the same answer the guild gave most tidings out of the coal country now: that they would learn it soon enough, and that until they did there was silver to weigh.
