# Of the Leaden Hawk

*AE 150 Jan – Jun*

## I. Of the Word That Came to Cuan dhearg

Coinneach Beag was five-and-twenty that winter, and had been a boatman on the Abhainn uaine since he was old enough to hold an oar against the current, ferrying barrels and once, memorably, a bull calf that would not stand still, between the two landings at Cuan dhearg. He had never carried a letter further than the length of the town. He had never fired a rifle at anything but a scarecrow, on a dare, at fourteen. When the crew-leaders of the river towns asked for seven men who knew how to keep their feet on a hill road and their mouths shut on a matter that mattered, he put his own name forward before he had properly decided to, the way a man steps off a boat onto a landing before he has quite finished thinking about the jump.

The word came to Cuan dhearg all that spring in pieces, by carter and by boat and by the mouths of men who had walked further than they meant to, and each piece was turned over at the inn's fire until it had been handled by everyone in the room. Ashdown's column, beaten at the ford of Àth chiar and its forty dead left to the river, had fallen back through Dùn chrom and lain there a night; in the morning the town had shut its gates on the soldiers who slept in its barracks, and let the column go on to the coast without its own wounded, whom the townsfolk kept to nurse or to bury as it turned out. Coinneach heard that twice, once from a carter who swore he had it from a man who had seen the gates shut with his own eyes, and once from a boatman up from the coast who swore the same thing of a different town entirely, and he learned that spring, before he learned much else of the war, that a true thing and a thing a man wanted to believe could travel the same road at the same pace and arrive looking near enough alike.

Other word he trusted better, because it came with names attached and names could be checked. The silver guild at Muileann chiar had declared for the council and put the Company's agents on the road east with their own papers under their own arms, untouched; the small post at Dùn ìseal had given up its rifles after one exchange of fire, and the whole eastern coast, from the silver hills to the sea, stood clear of any human arm that spring for the first time in Coinneach's life. At Dùn dhearg the custodians had sent grain south and read their declaration from the steps of the hall, quoting, an old man at the fire said with some pride, as though the words were his own kin's, the very sentence Fionnghal nic Artair had written against the Small-Burning Law a hundred and twenty-seven years before it mattered to anyone again. Riflemen had risen at Cnoc leathan to hold the eastern road. At Ros fhionn the crews had stopped the pumps in the same week and let the water have the lower galleries back, and the bridge on the coal road there, which Coinneach had crossed twice in his life on family business, stood silent, he was told, but for the birds.

Then in the early weeks of the year came the word that stopped the fire's talk dead for a night: a Company ship had come up the Abhainn uaine itself, not twenty miles below where they sat, and shelled the coal stage at Seann Toll to keep it from the rebels, and set the fishing town behind it burning. Three-and-twenty of the town's own people had died, and the ship had burned island coal to do it, the very coal the hills had raised and carried and weighed out for a hundred years, turned round in a boiler and fired back at the people who had raised it. Coinneach's uncle, who had fished those waters as a young man and knew every rock of the Seann Toll shore by name, said nothing for a long while after that word came, and then said only that he had not thought to live to hear of coal doing that, and went out into the yard and did not come back in until the fire had burned low.

Coinneach's own father had died at the pit face when Coinneach was nine, of a fall no one blamed on anything but the hill itself, and his mother had raised three children on what the river brought in and what the burial club would allow, and Coinneach had grown up thinking of the coal country the way a man thinks of weather that has already done him its worst: something to be watched, not fought. He had heard the talk of the Company and the constables and the marks his whole life the way he heard talk of the tides, a thing that governed him without asking his leave, and it had not occurred to him, before that winter, that a man might do anything about weather but endure it. What changed in him that spring he could not afterward put a single name to; only that when the crew-leaders asked for seven who could keep their feet on a hill road, he found he no longer wanted only to endure.

It was into this spring, thick with word half-true and word too terrible to doubt, that the crew-leaders sent for the seven.

## II. Of the Waiting at An Cupa Dàna

They were told to come to An Cupa Dàna, the inn at Cuan dhearg, on a morning when the frost had not yet gone off the roofs, and to bring nothing that would mark them for what they were. Coinneach came in his oldest coat, with a whittled knife and a pair of boots resoled twice, and found six others waiting in the inn's back room already, men of the river towns he knew by sight if not always by name: a cooper's son from further down the water, two brothers who had worked a coal barge together since boyhood, a quiet man called Gillebrìde who had been a hawk-priest's server as a lad and knew the shrine roads better than anyone else in the room, and two more whose names Coinneach set himself that morning to learn and not forget: Ailig, who talked too much when he was afraid and said so himself, and Pàdraig Buidhe, who did not talk at all.

They were given letters, sealed, and told only that the letters went north to the Hawk coast, to Ìomhar Seabhag at the shrine of Seann Skell and to whoever else along the road would hear the council's word and might be moved by it. They were given, each of them, a small thing cast in lead and shaped rough but unmistakably like a hawk stooping, and told these had been made from cartridges taken at the store at Muileann chrom that first morning of the war, melted down by a smith at Muileann dhearg who had more urgent work waiting and had done this first because someone had asked him to. Coinneach turned his over in his palm. It was heavier than its size argued for, and the edges where the mould had not quite closed were sharp enough to catch on cloth, and he thought, holding it, that he had never owned anything before that had once been meant to kill a man and was now meant only to be worn at the throat and shown to strangers as a token of trust.

They waited nine hours at the inn before the last letter came down from the council with a rider who had ridden further and harder than the road strictly asked, and in those nine hours Coinneach learned more of his six companions than he had learned of most men he had known his whole life. Ailig talked of his mother, who did not know where he had gone that morning and would be told, he hoped, only after it no longer mattered whether she worried. The coal-barge brothers argued, quietly and without heat, over whose turn it was to carry the heavier pack, an argument Coinneach understood within the hour to be less about the pack than about which of them would walk nearer the front, where the road, if it came to trouble, would find them first. Gillebrìde said little, but what he said was of the shrine roads, which ones a stranger could walk without a hawk-priest's leave and which he could not, and Coinneach set himself to remember it, for it seemed, of everything said that day, the likeliest to matter.

They left at dark, when the last letter was in Gillebrìde's coat with the rest, each man's hawk at his throat on a cord of tarred twine, and the innkeeper's wife stood in the door a moment with a lamp and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to seven men going out into a war on a road that so far existed only as a hope: the hope that the letters would find hands willing to carry them further.

## III. Of the Road by Seann Toll

The first day's walking brought them down past the burned stage at Seann Toll, and Coinneach, who thought he had prepared himself for it on the strength of his uncle's silence, found he had not. The stage itself was a black frame over black water, and the houses behind it that had caught from the fire stood roofless, their thatch gone entire and their stone walls stained the colour of the smoke that had gone up through them. A woman was picking through what had been a hearth, sorting stone from ash with her bare hands, and did not look up as the seven passed, and none of them spoke to her, for there seemed nothing a stranger could say that would not be an insult to what she was doing.

They went on that day and the next through Baile thais, where the walled store the constables had held eleven days still bore, on its outer wall, the marks of whatever had been thrown or fired at it in that fortnight, and a boy of nine or ten showed them, unasked and with more pride than the matter perhaps warranted at his age, exactly where he had stood the day the constables came out with their water gone and their hands empty. Over the hills by Achadh dhomhain the road climbed out of the coal country proper into country Coinneach had never walked, heather going to purple-brown at its roots though it was not yet the season for its flower, and down the far side by the Abhainn ìseal to Cnoc bheag, where they slept a night in a byre by leave of a farmer who asked no questions and was given no answers, which both parties seemed to find the more honest arrangement.

At every hawk-house on the road, a low building of one room where the hawk-faithful of that district kept a fire and a bench and, more often than not, little else, one of the seven stood up among whoever sat there and read the council's letter aloud. It fell to Coinneach at a hawk-house he never learned the name of, a bare room with six benches and a fire down to embers, and he found his voice did not want to carry the way he had meant it to, and steadied it by thinking of the woman sorting stone at Seann Toll and telling himself the letter was, in its way, owed to her as much as to anyone in the room. He read it through once, the council's own words about the hill country and the war and what was asked of every district the letter reached, and when he had finished an old woman by the fire said only, "Well. So it has come to that," and no one answered her, because there was nothing to answer, the letter having said as much as needed saying.

Along the Hawk coast the road ran easier, a track worn by shrine-goers rather than carts, and they came down to Inis mhin on the fourth day with their boots worn thin and their letters, Gillebrìde said, checking the packet each night by feel before he slept, all still dry and all still sealed.

## IV. Of the Hawk Coast and the Roads Off It

Inis mhin was a walled landing the humans had built and half abandoned when the trade that justified it thinned, and the seven lay twelve hours in a boat-shed there, sleeping in turns, while Gillebrìde went out to learn what could be learned of the roads ahead. He came back with word that undid, in a sentence, the ease of the last two days: Strake's constables rode the western roads out of Ros bheag every day now, in patrols of ten or a dozen, and a party of seven men on foot, however plain their coats, would be seen and stopped and searched before it had gone a mile past the town.

It was Pàdraig Buidhe, who had said perhaps forty words in four days, who found the carter willing to take them, a man of Inis mhin with a covered cart and a mule past its best years, who asked no more than a fair price and a promise that whatever was in the cart's bed under the sacking was not his business to know, a promise the seven kept to the letter by telling him nothing at all. They left the road that night and did not take it again, going by cart-tracks and field-edges known to the carter and to no map Coinneach had ever seen, and it struck him, jolting along in the dark with six other men and a mule that seemed to know the country better than any of them, that he had come further from home in four days than he had gone in the whole of the rest of his life, and that he had not, until that moment, had leisure to think about it.

## V. Of the Grass Above Tobar dhomhain

Above Tobar dhomhain the carter set them down before first light at the edge of a stretch of open grassland that ran along the ridge over the road from Ros bheag to the northern concession, and told them, with the flat certainty of a man who had lived his whole life within sight of that road, that from here they would see anything that passed on it for the better part of a day, and be seen by nothing, if they lay still and let the grass do its work.

They lay eleven hours. The grass was long enough to close over a man on his belly and cool enough, in the morning, to soak through a coat within the first hour, and by midday Coinneach's whole left side had gone from cold to numb to a kind of steady ache he stopped noticing only by giving his attention entirely to the road below. Ailig, three feet to his left, kept up a whisper of complaint for the first two hours and then, as the sun climbed and the road below began to show its traffic, fell as silent as Pàdraig Buidhe ever was, because there was, after all, something to watch.

They counted. A farm cart at dawn, going toward Ros bheag with what looked like wool. Two women on foot, going the other way, whom none of the seven could place as anything but what they seemed. And then, beginning perhaps an hour after the sun was properly up, the riders: constables in twos and threes and once a full dozen together, moving at an easy pace that told Coinneach, who had never sat a horse in his life but had watched enough carters manage tired animals to know the difference, that these were not men in any great hurry, nor men expecting trouble on this particular stretch of road that day.

By the time the light began to go, Gillebrìde's tally, kept on a strip of bark with a line for each rider and a cross for each group's leader, stood at forty-one. Coinneach checked it twice against his own count, made independently and kept in his head for lack of anything to mark it with, and came to the same number both times. What struck all seven of them, lying there in the last of the light before they dared move, was not the number itself, though forty-one riders was more than any of them had thought to see on one road in one day. It was the direction. Every rider, every cart, every single soul they had watched cross that stretch of road since first light, had been going south, back toward Ros bheag and the coast, and not one, not a single one in eleven hours of watching, had been riding north.

"They're none of them looking this way," Gillebrìde said, when they had at last crept back off the ridge into the shelter of a fold in the land where a man could stand upright and speak above a whisper. "Whatever Strake wants watched, it isn't this road. Not any more."

No one answered him at once. Coinneach lay on his back a moment in the last of the light, working the feeling back into his numbed side, and thought that a road forty-one riders had used that day and not one of them northward bound was either the safest road on the island or the most dangerous, and that there was no way to know, from where they lay, which.

## VI. Of Inis thais and the Roads of the Fianna

They came down to Inis thais on the evening of the eighth day out from Cuan dhearg, footsore and carrying, Gillebrìde reported once more, seven dry letters, and were brought before the priests of Òrd na Cloiche in the low stone temple above the harbour, where a fire of driftwood burned blue-white with the salt in the wood and the priests sat in a half-circle that made Coinneach, standing in the middle of it with the letters in his hand, feel more closely watched than he had felt lying in the grass above Tobar dhomhain with forty-one riders passing below him.

The priests heard the letter through without a word, and when it was done the eldest of them, a woman whose name none of the seven were told and none thought to ask, said that the Stone would send no men to the hill country's war, for that was not what the Stone was for. But she said also, turning her eyes from Gillebrìde, who had done the speaking until then, to Coinneach, who stood nearest the fire, that Inis thais would shut its harbour to the Company's ships from that night, and that the hawk-faithful, wherever their roads led, would find the Fianna's own ways open to them from here to the western city, a kindness she did not trouble to explain and Coinneach did not trouble to ask after, being by then too tired to do more than be grateful for it.

There the Leaden Hawk's road, as the letters had carried it, came to its end. What the seven had walked and ridden and lain eleven hours in the grass for was delivered, letter for letter, hawk-house for hawk-house, into hands that would carry its word further than any of the seven could have carried it alone; and at the western Seann Skell, some days after, when the company of the hawk-faithful was mustered under Ìomhar Seabhag's own eye, the seven of Cuan dhearg stood in its first rank and were told, by a priest who had heard already what they had done, that they were one thing now, with one name: Buidheann an t-Seabhaig Luaidhe, the Leaden Hawk.

Word reached the muster at Seann Skell all that early summer the way it had reached the fire at Cuan dhearg, in pieces, and Coinneach, now a soldier of sorts though he still thought of himself most days as a boatman lent out for a season, learned to sort the true from the merely believed as he had learned it on the river road. The coal stacked on the quays of Inis mhòr, that the Company's ships had meant to burn in their boilers, went up instead at the townsfolk's own hand rather than fuel one more voyage like the one that had shelled Seann Toll, and the ships that would have taken it put in at Ros dhomhain and burned their own stores down to the last sack. Fishing boats out of both towns called Seann Chwen, sailing by night, boarded a Company coaster at anchor and burned her where she lay, and the Company drew what shipping it had left back into the two harbours it still held and kept it there. On the concession, Cathair gheal shut its gates and raised a militia that went nowhere and did nothing, its magistrate having written to both sides asking only to be left alone, a request the council granted and Strake did not trouble to answer; and at Muileann àrsaidh the town blocked its own road with carts and kept a watch on it against both armies at once, a watch no one on either side had yet tried to break.

The southern beacons went dark that spring, one after another: Tobar dhearg first, where the keeper, a woman named Raonaid nic Dhòmhnaill of whom Coinneach knew nothing then but the name on a report read aloud at the muster, put out her own light with her own hand rather than let it go on guiding the Company's ships along a coast the council now held; and Àth dhearg and Baile ìseal followed her within the week. Two Company ships, feeling their way along that coast by night with no light to steer by, ran onto the reefs the beacons had been raised to warn them from, and were lost with what the letter-books, when they were read after, called regret and nothing more. From over the sea, in all that first half-year of the war, one ship came, bringing dispatches and a few crates of cartridges to a Residency that had asked for regiments, and sailed again inside the week, and Coinneach heard a priest at Seann Skell say, of that one ship and its light cargo, that an Tìr Thall had sent Strake exactly as much help as a man sends a drowning friend by shouting encouragement from the bank.

Of Raonaid nic Dhòmhnaill herself Coinneach learned more only much later, when the report that had been read aloud at the muster came round a second time in a fuller telling: that she had kept the light at Tobar dhearg alone since her mother's death, and had climbed the tower on the night she put it out and stood a long while at the rail before she touched the valve, as any keeper might stand before undoing the one thing a keeper's whole life was built to do. She had written nothing of it in any daybook that survived the year. The report gave only that the light went out at her hand, on her own authority, before any order reached her from the council to do it, and that when the order came, a week later, it found the tower already dark.

By midsummer, when the days over the western city ran long and pale into their evenings and the muster had settled into something less like seven strangers and a company of priests and more like an army, such as it was, the lines the war had drawn that winter had not moved. The council held the hills, the east, and the capital; Strake held the Residency, the harbour quarter below it, and the western ports; and neither, for all that had burned and all that had been counted on a strip of bark in a field above Tobar dhomhain, had found the strength that summer to break the other. Coinneach Beag, standing his watch on the road north of Ros bheag with a rifle he had been given and had not yet fired, thought sometimes of the forty-one riders, all bound south, and wondered, as Gillebrìde had wondered aloud on the ridge, what it was that Strake had judged worth watching instead of that road, and whether, wherever it was, the council's own eyes were on it yet. He did not know. No one at Seann Skell could tell him, that summer, and the watch went on.
