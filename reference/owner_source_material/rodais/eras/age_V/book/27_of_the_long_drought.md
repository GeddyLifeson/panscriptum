# Of the Long Drought

*AE 106 – 110*

## I. Of the First Dry Summer

Iseabail nic Leòid had kept the roll of Àth mhòr eleven years before the first summer the wells would not fill, and she remembered afterward that she had not known, standing at the mouth of her own well with the bucket coming up a third full of grey water and grit, that she was standing at the beginning of anything at all. A dry summer was not a strange thing on Dia-thìr. She had seen three in her time as custodian, and each had broken in its own season with rain enough to fill the cisterns before the cattle suffered for it. She let down the bucket a second time, watched the rope go slack sooner than it should, and told herself the same thing the town had told itself since Bealltainn: that the rain was late this year, not gone.

It did not come. By an t-Iuchar the burn below Àth mhòr ran in a channel a man could step across without wetting his boot, and by Lùnastal it did not run at all, only stood in pools the colour of weak tea between stones gone white with dust. Iseabail sent word east to Achadh shean, whose custodian she had known since they were girls together at the loft, and the word that came back was worse than her own: the wells there had failed a full month before Àth mhòr's, and the burn that fed the town's mill had not turned the wheel since Meadhan an t-Samhraidh.

The roll that year, when it was written up at the year's turning, named ten districts as drought country: worst at Achadh shean, at Àth uaine, at Achadh àrsaidh and at Muileann chiar, the drought reaching down as far as Cuan ruadh on the coast and west as far as Baile chaol before it thinned into ordinary dry ground. Iseabail's own Àth mhòr sat near the middle of it, close enough to Achadh shean that the two towns' custodians began that first autumn to send a rider between them twice a week, more for the company of shared bad news than for anything either could do to help the other.

The Administration kept no count of its own. Iseabail heard this first from a carter who had asked at the pay office at Ceann mhin whether the Company meant to do anything for the districts that could not feed their beasts, and had been told, in the words he repeated to her at her own door with the particular flatness of a man passing on an insult he has decided not to be angry about until later, that the yield was not affected and the office kept no register of rainfall. She wrote the carter's words into her own roll exactly as he had given them to her, because she had already begun, without quite deciding to, the habit of writing down every answer she was given, in the words it was given in.

## II. Of the Cattle Column

The second dry season broke nothing that the first had not already cracked. By the summer of AE 107 the wells at Achadh shean had been dry two years running, and the district's grazing, which had never been rich, was burned to standing straw by the middle of an t-Iuchar. Àth mhòr's own grass held a little longer, being nearer the coast and catching what fog came off the sea at night, but Iseabail could see from her own byre door that it would not hold past Lùnastal, and she called the district's herders together on a morning in the last week of an t-Iuchar and told them what she had already decided before she called them: that the beasts would be driven west, to whatever water could be found, before the grass gave out entirely and the driving became a burying.

They set out three days later, a column that the roll she wrote that autumn said was a day long, though Iseabail, walking at its head with a stick and a dog and her own two milk-cows among the hundreds of another's driving, thought afterward that a day long was too plain a way to say it, and that no word she had would say it better. Cattle low differently when they are thirsty than when they are merely being driven; she had not known that before that summer, and she knew it after in a way she never afterward lost, so that thirty years later the sound of oxen complaining at a gate could turn her stomach cold before she had thought why.

They walked four days to the first standing water worth the name, a tarn on the moor above Doire fhionn that had never in living memory gone dry, and the beasts smelled it a mile off and quickened without being driven, and Iseabail let them go, because there was no holding four hundred thirsty cattle back from water once they had scented it, and no reason on earth to try. Fewer than half of what had set out reached the tarn on its own legs. The rest had gone down on the road, in the dust, in the ochre grass at the road's edge, too weak to rise again, and had been left where they fell, because a beast that cannot walk cannot be carried, not four hundred of them, not by drovers who were themselves three days on short water. No roll required it of her, yet Iseabail counted the dead as they passed them, unable to walk by a fallen beast without marking it, the way she had begun to mark every answer the Administration gave her. She had ninety-one marks in her book by the time the column reached the tarn, and she added seven more before the drovers turned the survivors for home. She never, in all the years after, told anyone which number stayed with her longest; it was not the ninety-one of the road, but the count of calves among them, which was eleven, and which she had somehow known, walking, she would carry the rest of her life.

Doire fhionn, whose tarn had saved what was left of Achadh shean's herd, was by then sending its own women half a day's walk to a spring beyond its bounds for water fit to drink, the tarn having been fouled within a week by every district's cattle using it at once. Iseabail spoke to one of them at the tarn's edge, a woman carrying two full skins on a yoke who had walked out from the town before first light and would walk back after dark, and asked her how long the town meant to go on so. "Until it rains," the woman said, and shrugged the yoke straighter on her shoulders, and walked on, and Iseabail understood that this was the whole of the answer anyone in the drought country had, for any question that began with how long.

## III. Of Baile chaol's Choice

Baile chaol, at the far western edge of the drought country, had no moor within four days' driving that still held water enough for a herd the size of its own, and its custodian, a broad quiet woman named Ealasaid Ruadh whom Iseabail had known only by letter until that autumn, sent word round every district asking whether any had grazing to spare rather than water to reach. None did. Iseabail rode to Baile chaol herself in the last days of an Dàmhair, on the strength of nothing more than the wish not to leave the letter unanswered by a stranger's hand, and found the town's council already met and its mind already made, the kind of decision a town comes to only once it has stopped hoping there is a better one waiting a day further off.

They killed the herd over three days at the edge of the dry burn, the meat salted and smoked in every shed the town had standing, the hides scraped and stacked, and nothing wasted that a hungry district could use, and Ealasaid Ruadh stood at the killing the whole three days without once turning her back on it, which Iseabail thought, watching her, took more out of a person than the driving had taken out of her own drovers on the road to Doire fhionn. "A beast I can raise again in three good years," Ealasaid said to her that first evening, over meat neither of them had much appetite for. "A well that's gone dry three years running I can't promise anyone will fill again in ten." She did not say it as a complaint. She said it the way a woman says a sum she has already done the arithmetic of and does not need to do again, and Iseabail wrote the words into her roll that night exactly as she had heard them, the way she had begun writing down every hard thing anyone in the drought country said to her, because she had come by then to believe that the words themselves, kept whole, were worth more than anything she might have added to them.

Baile chaol ate through that winter on its own slaughtered herd and lost fewer of its people to hunger than any other district of the ten, a fact the roll records plainly and without praise, because Ealasaid Ruadh, when Iseabail rode back through in the spring to tell her so, said only that she would rather not have needed to know how to make the choice, and changed the subject to the state of the road.

## IV. Of the Spring That Did Not Fail

There was one water in the drought country that held through all three dry years, and every district that could reach it sent to it before the second summer was out: Allt an Àigh, the holy spring in the east, of which the old telling said it had never failed since the Holy Age, and did not fail now. Iseabail went there herself in the summer of AE 108, a custodian with dying beasts and a district running on borrowed water, and found the ground around the spring already trodden into mud a hundred yards out by feet that had come the same way she had.

The pilgrims of Na Seann Spioradan had begun arriving at Bealltainn that year in numbers nobody at the spring had seen before, walking in from every drought district and some that were not drought country at all, carrying water home in whatever they had brought to carry it in, and the spring's own keepers, an old brother and sister who had tended it without payment for as long as anyone could say, found themselves that season feeding pilgrims from their own stores as well as watering them, until word of the crowding reached the district Iseabail passed the news along the road, that Inis uaine, the nearest town of any size, had opened its own granary to the walkers rather than see them go hungry on the road home, a kindness the roll notes without explaining, because none was needed.

It was in that same summer that the Administration set a watchman at the spring, a young man from Ceann mhin with a ledger and a rate written out for him by some clerk who had never seen the place: so much a skin, so much a beast, payable before the water was drawn. Iseabail was there the second week he stood his post, and watched an old woman of Achadh àrsaidh, who had walked three days with an empty cask on a barrow to fill it at the one water she trusted, set the cask down at the spring's edge and refuse, in words Iseabail did not think the young man understood in either sense, to pay a stranger for water that had run free since before his people came down from the north. The watchman did not press her. He stood his post through that season and was called back before the next one began, and the roll of Allt an Àigh, which Iseabail copied that autumn from the keepers' own tally, records no reason given for his recall, only the plain fact of it, and under the fact, in the old brother's hand, the single word restored.

## V. Of the Drought-Fever

The third dry season brought a fever that the two before it had not, and it came first, as fevers of bad water do, to the towns whose wells had been dug deepest into old ground. At Baile Mòr ruadh, whose wells had gone down years before into what had once been the town's own middens, seventeen died of it before the summer's end, more than the town had lost to any sickness in Iseabail's memory of it, and the fever went on from there to Cnoc shean, where it took fewer but took them just as surely, mostly the very young and the very old, whose bodies had least water in them to spare for fighting it.

Iseabail rode to Baile Mòr ruadh in the worst of it, at the asking of its custodian, a younger woman new enough to the office that she had never yet buried anyone by the fever's own particular manner: quick at first, a griping of the belly and a thirst no water eased, then a stillness that the women who sat with the dying learned to dread more than the griping, because it meant the body had stopped fighting what it drank. Iseabail sat two nights in a row with a household that lost a grandmother and a boy of nine within a day of each other, and held the boy's hand at the last of it because his mother could not, being with the grandmother in the next room, and she wrote nothing in her roll that night at all, being for once too tired and too sick at heart to trust her own hand with it, and made up the entry the following evening instead, dry and plain, as if a day's distance could put back into the words the steadiness they had lost at the boy's bedside.

The Mission's physician at Baile Mòr ruadh, a spare young man named Coll who had been sent out only that spring and had not yet learned to hold his tongue in front of a custodian, said to Iseabail on the second night, turning a dipper of well-water in his hands as though it might answer him, that he had tested the water himself and found it foul enough to sicken a strong man on a single cup, foul with whatever had lain in the ground the well was sunk through before ever the well was dug. "The old midden," Iseabail said. "It has been the town's midden longer than either of us. Everyone knew it." "Then everyone knew, and dug there anyway," Coll said, "because the drought gave them no ground to choose from that was not one old thing or another." He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way a man says a thing he has only just understood and finds he cannot now unknow, and it was his report, sent to the Mission's house at Cathair dhearg that autumn, that named the fever's cause rightly before any other account written that year did: the deep wells the drought had driven the towns to dig, down into ground that had never been meant to give up water and gave up, along with what little it had, whatever had been buried near it in years the towns themselves had half forgotten. The drought itself had only starved.

The Mission bored new wells at Achadh shean and at Àth chrom that same season, deeper again but dug clean, away from any old ground, and those wells held, and the fever did not follow them. Iseabail entered both facts into her roll on facing pages, as the custodians of the whole drought country had begun by then to do without any of them having agreed to it aloud: the Mission's wells on one page, the Company's quota on the other, the quota unchanged through three dry years and a fever that had killed more of Baile Mòr ruadh in one summer than the pits themselves had that year, and beside neither page did Iseabail write a word, because there was no word she could find that the facing pages did not say better by themselves.

## VI. Of Achadh chaol Going Down

Achadh chaol, whose own grazing had failed with everyone else's by the second year, sent its young men to the pits that summer in numbers its custodian had never before had to record. Iseabail heard the first of it from her own nephew, her sister's boy, who came to her door one evening in the autumn of AE 108 to tell her, before he told his own mother, that he had signed with the Company's agent at Ceann mhin for a hewer's contract at Cnoc fhiadhaich, and would go down after Samhain.

"Your father kept sheep," she said, not as a reproach, because she found she had none ready to give him, only the plain fact laid beside his own plain fact the way she had lately taken to laying plain facts beside one another and letting them speak for themselves. "So did his father."

"His sheep are dead, Aunt. Mine would be too, inside the year, if I kept any worth the name." He was not angry saying it, only tired in a way she thought no boy of nineteen ought yet to be. "A hewer's wage comes whether it rains or not. That's the whole of my reasoning, and I don't think it's a bad one."

She could not tell him it was. She wrote his going into her roll along with the others who followed him from Achadh chaol that winter and the winter after, families who had kept beasts and small fields for generations going down into the galleries because a hewer's wage, paid whether the year was wet or dry, had become the only certain thing left to a household in the drought country, and she noted the count without comment beside it, because by then she had stopped believing that comment changed anything a plain fact did not already say.

## VII. Of the Petition, Begun Alone

Every custodian of the drought country petitioned Haskins that third year, each in her own hand and her own time, none of them, so far as Iseabail ever learned, having agreed beforehand to do it together, though when she came to compare the copies afterward, in the loft at Muileann chaol where such things were kept, she found that nine of the ten districts had written some version of the same three sentences, as though the drought itself had dictated the wording and left each custodian only the work of the hand. She wrote her own in the autumn of AE 109, a single page asking that the Company's quota for the drought districts be set aside until two seasons of ordinary rain had been counted, and rode it herself to Cathair dhearg, three days on a horse thinner than she liked to ride, through country that still remembered how to be green and had lost, in three years, the smell of it after rain.

The Residency kept her waiting the better part of a day in a passage she did not think had been swept since the petition before hers had waited in it, and when at last a clerk took her page from her hand he did not read it while she stood there, only set it on a pile with others she recognised by their folds as having come from Achadh shean and from Muileann chiar, and told her, in words so evenly worn by repetition that she wondered how many times that week alone he had said them, that the matter was under consideration. She asked him what matter, precisely, and by whom, and he looked at her the way a man looks at a question he has never once been asked to answer and does not intend to begin answering that afternoon, and said only that he would see her petition placed with the others.

She rode home and wrote the words into her roll, under consideration, and looked at them a long while by lamplight, and understood, as she had half understood since the cattle column and fully understood now, that the words meant nothing at all, and would go on meaning nothing at all for as long as anyone above the Residency's stair found it convenient that they should.

It was in that understanding, and not in any single grief sharper than the others, that Iseabail began, on a wet evening in the winter of AE 109 that felt, after three dry years, almost too strange to trust, to write down everything the drought had cost, district by district, in a hand small enough to fit a great deal onto a single sheet: the wells failed and when; the cattle driven and how many did not arrive; the fever's dead by name where she had the names and by number where she did not; the Mission's wells that held against the Company's quota that had not moved; the watchman recalled from Allt an Àigh and the word restored under his going; the carter's flat answer about a register that was not kept. She wrote a page and found she was not finished, and wrote another, and did not stop at ten, because by then she had stopped writing for Haskins at all and had begun writing, though she could not have said so plainly even to herself that winter, for whoever might someday want to know what three dry years had actually cost a district that the Administration's own ledgers had never troubled to measure.

Her own household thought she had taken up the writing as a kind of grief, and let her be at it evenings when there was little else to do by lamplight in a dry year, and she did not correct them, because she had not yet found words that would explain to anyone, even to herself with any confidence, what exactly she believed the forty pages were for. Nor was she a woman of letters in the way the copyists at Muileann chaol were; she had learned to write well enough to keep a custodian's roll and no more, and the work of setting down three years in full, rather than in the short entries a roll allowed, went slowly, a page some evenings, half a page on others, crossed through and begun again when a fact came back to her sharper than she had first set it down. The cattle column she wrote twice, the second time longer than the first, because on rereading the first she had found she had left out the calves, and could not let the second draft go forward without them.

Forty pages deep in it, and still writing, she stood at her own well one spring morning in AE 110 when a rider came from Achadh shean with word that the Mission's new rig, boring a third well on the moor above the town, had struck water at last, clean and cold and running, deeper than any well the town had dug before the drought and deeper, the rider said, than anyone had believed water could lie under that particular stretch of moor. Iseabail rode out to see it for herself, and stood at the rig's edge with half the town around her, children who had never in their remembered lives seen water come up out of the ground on its own account crowding close enough that the drillers had to wave them back from the pipe, and watched the water come up bright over the driller's hands, and thought, watching it, of the tarn on the moor above Doire fhionn and the ninety-eight marks in her drover's book, and of the old woman who would not pay a stranger for water that had run free since before his people came, and of her sister's boy three years gone now into the galleries at Cnoc fhiadhaich, whose contract would not be undone by a well striking water a hundred miles from where he stood.

She wrote that night, at the bottom of her fortieth page, in a hand no steadier than it had been on the first: this also happened, so that whoever reads this after me will know the drought did end, in its own place, in its own season, and not because anyone above the Residency's stair troubled to make it so.
