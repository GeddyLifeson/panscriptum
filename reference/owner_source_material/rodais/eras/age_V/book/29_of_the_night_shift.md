# Of the Night Shift

*AE 114 – 120*

## I. Of the Man Who Spoke It Badly

Robert Haskins left Ros dhomhain on a wet morning in an t-Ògmhios of the hundred and fourteenth year, over a quarrel with the Company about the price of a steamship berth, a matter so small that the custodians who watched him go could not afterward agree what it had been. He had held the Residency sixteen years and never once, in sixteen years, said a full sentence in Dia-thìris without a clerk at his elbow to mend it. He went down to the quay in a coat too heavy for the season, sixty-nine years old and glad, by every report, to be leaving, and the ship that took him stood off Ros dhomhain until the tide served and was gone by evening.

His successor came up from the same quay eleven days later, and half the town turned out to look at him, because Edward Vane was a thing Ros dhomhain had not seen before: a Commissioner who had been born there. Not born in an Tìr Thall and sent over grown, as every Commissioner before him had been, but born at the Residency, four-and-forty years before, to a mother who had crossed the water once and never again and a father who had crossed it only the once and stayed. He had a Dia-thìreach nurse as a child, the custodians said, and had run barefoot on the strand at Ros dhomhain with the fisher-children before anyone thought to stop him, and something of that had stayed in his mouth, because when he stepped down onto the quay and the custodian of the port made him the customary greeting, he answered it. Badly. The tenses came out wrong, and he put the stress on the last syllable of every word the way the humans do, and afterward opinion was divided for a season on whether he had wished the custodian a fair wind or a long life, since the two are one letter apart in the mouth of a man who has not troubled to learn the difference. But he had answered, in Dia-thìris, unprompted, with no clerk beside him, and no Commissioner had done that in living memory.

He went up to Cathair dhearg himself that autumn, which Haskins had done twice in sixteen years and both times under summons. Comhairle a' Chùraim sat for him in the crown's place, the high custodian's seat empty still, as it had been empty nine years, and Vane stood before the council in the humans' good coat with his hat in his two hands and spoke to them in their own tongue for the length of three sentences before his nerve or his grammar failed him and he finished in the humans' speech through the Mission's interpreter. What he said, in the part that was his own, was that he had come home. The council's clerk wrote the sentence down exactly as he gave it, wrong tense and all, because it was the first time in the roll's memory that a Commissioner had used the word at all, home, of the island, and whatever else Vane proved to be, the clerk thought that worth the ink.

He kept a room at the Residency that Haskins had never used, a small chamber off the counting-house with a shelf of the Mission's Dia-thìris grammars in it, worn at the spine from a boyhood's handling, and in the evenings of his first winter he was known to sit with a clerk and a lesson-book like a schoolboy twice the age of any schoolboy, going over the cases of a language he had spoken badly since the cradle and never once been made to speak well. The clerks who taught him said afterward that he learned slowly and minded being corrected less than any human they had taught before or since, which they took, in their different ways, either as a mark in his favour or as proof that a man born on the island, however he had been raised, could never quite be brought to feel the shame of it that a man sent out fresh from an Tìr Thall felt on his first mangled sentence.

He was not loved for it. A custodian of the eastern hills, an old woman past her eightieth year who had buried three husbands and outlived two Commissioners already, said within Vane's own hearing, in Dia-thìris too quick for him to follow, that a wolf raised among the sheep is still a wolf, and worse for knowing the sheep's own bleat. The saying went round the coal towns before winter and outlasted the man it was said of. But he had answered the greeting, and he went on, in the years that followed, answering more of them, and the custodians who kept the rolls could not decide, then or later, whether that made him easier to deal with or only easier to resent, since a wrong done in your own tongue by a man who has troubled to learn it cuts different from a wrong done at a clerk's remove, though it is not, in the end, a different wrong.

## II. Of the Iron Road and the Order That Followed It

Vane's first year of building was the tram-road, an iron track laid on wooden sleepers from the southern workings down to the coal port at Ros dhìreach, drawn by mule teams the Company shipped in from an Tìr Thall in crates that stank the length of the quay. It cost the Company less in a season to move coal by the tram than it had cost by cart in ten, and it cost the carters of the old coal road their whole trade inside two years; the villages that had lived on the carting, small places with no name the maps had ever troubled to learn, emptied a household at a time down toward the pits, where there was work still, of a kind. The tram-road opened without ceremony on a morning of an t-Iuchar in his second year, a string of mule-carts running loaded for the first time down the grade that generations of carters had walked with their own backs bent to it, and the custodian who wrote the day into the roll noted only the weight carried and the hour, thirty tons by the third bell, and nothing of the road it had put out of work, for a custodian's roll counts what moves, not what stops moving.

It was Vane, too, who signed the order that came down from the Residency in an Lùnastal of the year after and was read out at every pit-mouth from Cnoc chaol to Muileann chiar by an overseer standing on an upturned crate: that the digging was thenceforward to run without ceasing, day following night following day, and no season of it given over to rest at all. The order named no reason. The Company's own letter-book, which the custodians never saw, gave the true reason plainly enough, that the tram-road had made it cheap to move more coal than the day shifts alone could cut, and idle rails cost the Company money the way idle hands never had.

The custodian of Cnoc chaol, a narrow camp of new galleries in the hills above Muileann dhomhain, wrote the order into her roll in the hand she used for weather and for deaths, and then wrote under it a single line and left the rest of the page blank: *the hill is given no silence at all.* She was a young woman, new to the roll that year, and an older custodian who read it afterward said she had never seen a page left so bare on purpose, as though the custodian judged that anything more she might add would only crowd out the one true thing the page had to say.

## III. Of the Two Shifts and the Week It Lasted

The night shift at Cnoc chaol worked by coal-lamp, small clay bodies with a wick and a horn window that threw a light the crews said was worse than none at all, for it showed a man just enough of the gallery to trust it and not enough to see what the trust cost him. Two shifts split the day and the dark between them, twelve hours each, with an hour at each changeover when both crews were below together and the air in the deep galleries, thin already, had to serve twice the lungs it had ever been asked to serve before.

The crews bore it through a winter, and in the summer after they refused it. There was no vote taken that the custodian's roll records, no meeting called under any name; the day shift simply did not go down when the bell rang for the night change, and stood instead in the yard above the pit-mouth in the last of the daylight, a hundred men and women with nothing in their hands, saying nothing to the overseer who came out to ask them what they thought they were about. He asked it twice. The second time somebody at the back of the crowd, a hewer nobody afterward would name to the overseer's face, said that the hill had never yet been dug in the dark by anyone who meant to keep digging it, and the crowd took up no chant and made no threat and simply did not move, which an overseer used to threats did not know how to answer.

The Company answered it in a week with crews brought up from the coast towns under new contracts, humans mostly, dockers and out-of-work carters glad of a wage the coast no longer offered steadily, and Dia-thìrich from camps far enough off that the word of the stand at Cnoc chaol had not yet reached them plainly. They came up the tram-road in the same mule-carts that carried the coal down it, and were set to the night shift within two days of arriving, and the crews of Cnoc chaol who had stood in the yard went back to the day shift they still had, because a stand that costs a household its whole wage inside a fortnight is not a stand a household can afford to keep making, whatever it costs the hill.

Muileann dhomhain, the milling town two hours' walk below Cnoc chaol on the road to the coast, would not house them. Every door in the town, the custodian's roll says, plainly and more than once, for the custodian who kept it seems to have taken some pride in the counting: every door was shut to the new crews, the inns would not bed them, the millers would not sell them meal at the counter, and the well at the crossroads, the only water on that stretch of road that did not belong to some household's own yard, was found blocked with stones on the second morning by no hand the custodian troubled to guess at. The Company's overseers housed the new crews in barns of the Company's own outside the town, and several of the newcomers, humans and Dia-thìrich alike, went home to the coast before the season was out, unable to say afterward, when asked, exactly what had driven them, only that a town that will not sell a man his bread is a hard town to work beside for wages that were never generous to begin with.

The Lamp-Watch, the fellowship of pit crews that had begun four years before under cover of a burial club, at Baile thais and at Cnoc chaol both, kept the households of the men and women who had stood in the yard through that first week and the season after, out of a purse built a penny at a time from crews who could ill spare the penny. By the second winter the purse was empty. The society's own reckoning, kept in a hand nobody has since put a name to, closes the account for that year with a line that gives the sum remaining, nothing, and under it, in the same hand, a second line that says only that the hill was not given back its silence, which was not, whatever else it was, an accounting entry, and the custodian who copied the reckoning years later left it standing all the same.

## IV. Of the Sermon at Ros dhomhain

Matthew Dunne had held the Mission's chapel at Ros dhomhain three years when the night-shift order came down, a narrow young man of one-and-thirty with a Dia-thìris better than any minister the Mission had sent before him and a temper, his own bishop said of him once, better suited to a soldier than a priest. He had walked the coal towns in his first year the way Walter Crane had once walked the sick-rows of the Green Death, on his own feet and without an overseer beside him, and had come to know the camps better than any human at the Residency, Vane not excepted.

He preached against the order on the humans' rest-day in an t-Ògmhios of the hundred and eighteenth year, when it was two years old and the stand at Cnoc chaol a year gone; and when word of it reached Cnoc chaol and Muileann dhomhain inside a fortnight, it did not go quiet. Standing that morning in the chapel pulpit at Ros dhomhain before a congregation half of it Company clerks and half of it dockers, he said, in the humans' own tongue so that no clerk could later claim to have misheard him through an interpreter, that the Dia-thìrich were right in the thing they had not been given leave to say for themselves: that the ground the Company dug was owed a season of quiet the same as any ground the humans' own scripture called holy, and that a people who would not let their own fields lie fallow a year in seven had no right to ask a hill to give more. The whole chapel knew what he meant without his naming it.

He had written the sermon three times before he stood up to give it, his housekeeper said afterward, and burned two drafts in the grate, and on the morning of the sermon he walked the strand below the chapel for an hour before the bell, alone, in a wind off the water that took his hat twice, and came in with salt on his coat and his text unchanged from the second burning. He was afraid, he told the housekeeper once, years after and from the other side of the water, not of the Company's answer, which he had judged rightly from the first, but of standing in a pulpit built by the Mission's own money and telling the men who paid for it that they had misread their own scripture; a fear, he said, that a soldier does not carry into a battle he has already decided to lose.

The Company's agent at Ros dhomhain wrote to the Mission's own house in an Tìr Thall within the month, and the letter, which a clerk copied before it went and which found its way years later into the same roll that keeps the custodians' counts, called the sermon an interference in matters the cloth had no competence in and asked, plainly, that the minister be recalled before his words did the Company more harm than his ministry did it good. The Mission, which depended on the Company's ships for every letter and every year's stores that crossed the water, recalled him inside the season. He preached a last sermon at Ros dhomhain at the end of that same year, three-and-thirty years old, to a chapel fuller than it had ever been in his three years, and said nothing in it of the night shift at all, only the ordinary words for a minister's leaving, and went down to the same quay Haskins had gone down from four years before, in the same wet weather, though nobody who watched him go thought the two departures were alike in anything but the rain.

The Lamp-Watch had a copy of the sermon inside the week, taken down, it was always said afterward though never proved, by a Company clerk who had sat in the chapel and did not care to give his own name to the copying. It went from Baile thais to Cnoc chaol to Muileann chiar in the space of a season, read aloud in the camps of an evening by whoever among the crews could read well enough to be trusted with it, and the line the crews kept longest of the whole sermon was the one about the fallow year, which passed into the coal towns' own speech soon after as a saying that owed nothing, by the time it had been said a hundred times over, to the minister who first gave it words.

## V. Of Donnchadh's Cough

A wasting sickness of the lungs came into the night crews of Cnoc chaol and Baile thais the same winter the sermon was preached, and stayed three years. It was not the Green Death; it killed slowly, over a season or two, a cough that would not clear and a weight the sick could not keep on and a colour to the skin that was grey rather than green, and it took the humans of the crews first and hardest, the dockers and carters brought up from the coast, whose own country's pits had killed their fathers the same way before they ever crossed the water to find a hill that would kill them too.

The Dia-thìrich hewers coughed as hard through that winter, working the same galleries in the same lamp-lit dark, and the Mission's own physician, a careful man named Colquhoun who had come out after Anne Colby's years and kept her habit of writing plainly, set the same word against their names in his book that he set against the humans'. But the custodians' roll, which knew every hewer of Cnoc chaol by name and had known most of their mothers, kept its own separate count, and the count said a different thing: that of the Dia-thìrich who coughed that winter, few died of it, though the cough lasted as hard and as long as any human's.

The old women of the camps knew the telling of the hewer of Achadh dhomhain whose cough had stopped on the night his daughter was born. They called any such cough among the Dia-thìrich crews Donnchadh's cough, and would say no more. The Company, for its part, paid the widows of the dead a pension for a year and then stopped, the pension being, the agent's letter says without apology, a matter of the Company's grace and not of any debt owed.

## VI. Of Iain Maor's Letter

Iain Maor had kept the staff of the overseers at Muileann chrom two-and-thirty years, since he was seven-and-thirty, and had buried a wife and raised a daughter in the slate-roofed house above the mill that came with the staff, and at nine-and-sixty, the winter the night-shift order fell hardest on his own galleries, he did a thing no overseer of Muileann chrom had done in the office's whole history: he wrote to the Company that its own order was costing it more than it gained.

He wrote it in the humans' tongue, in the schooled hand the Mission had given him as a boy, and he did not soften it. The night shift, he wrote, was killing his crews faster than the coal they cut in the dark was paying the Company back, counting the cost of every dead man's training against a new one, the wage lost to every widow's household falling onto the parish and the Mission both, and the slower cutting of tired crews working by coal-lamp against crews that had once worked the same seams by daylight and rested. He signed it with his own name and the office's, and sent it down with the ordinary post, and heard nothing for two months.

His daughter Mòrag, four-and-twenty that winter and already keeping her father's own daybook for him in a hand steadier than his had ever been, was the one who found the answer when it came: a notice from the Residency, unlike any reply to a letter she had ever copied for him, that Iain Maor, overseer at Muileann chrom, was relieved of the staff for conduct unbecoming his office. She read it to him at the kitchen table by lamplight, and he heard it out without a word and then asked her, when she had finished, to read it again, and she did, and he said only that he had known the answer would come dressed as something other than itself, and went to bed.

He had known the letter would cost him something even as he sealed it, he told Mòrag that same evening; a man who has kept a Company's staff two-and-thirty years learns to read the shape of an answer before it comes, the way a hewer learns the sound a roof will make before it falls. She asked him why he had written it all the same, and he said only that a true thing costs the same to say whether it is answered honestly or not, and that a man who waits to speak until the cost is nothing has generally waited until the thing is no longer worth the saying.

The dismissal did not stand a month. There was no other overseer at Muileann chrom the Company trusted with the galleries, and the crews, who had heard within a day what had been done to him, worked the next fortnight at a pace so far below what the night shift demanded that the Company's own agent wrote up the road that the pits could not be run without him. The second notice came unsigned by anyone above a clerk's rank, restoring him to the staff with no mention made of the first notice at all, as though the office had simply forgotten, for a season, that it had ever taken the staff from him. Iain Maor kept both notices together in the same drawer, the dismissal and the restoration, ready to hand to anyone at his table who asked how the Company answered a true thing said to it plainly; and his letter, folded in with the dismissal that had answered it, survives in the Company's own files for that reason alone, filed by a clerk who thought the two papers belonged together and never troubled to think why.

## VII. Of the Iron Chariot

The potteries of Baile ghorm took the Company's contract for lamp-bodies in the spring the tram-road opened, thousands of clay bodies fired in a season, each with its horn window and its wick-hole turned on the same wheel that had turned bowls and pipkins for the coast trade a generation before. The potters' own book, kept as carefully as any custodian's roll though nobody at the Residency thought to call it one, notes that theirs were the first lamps made on the island for work commanded in the dark, and sets the number fired against the number of days, a column of figures that grows through that spring and does not shrink again.

Vane lived long enough to see the tram-road's true purpose finished. In am Màrt of the hundred and twentieth year the Company's iron road opened from the pits at Muileann chrom, down past the hill below Cathair dhearg, to the deep-water quay at Ros dhomhain, a proper railway of iron rail and a coal-burning engine, and the mule-drawn tram to Ros dhìreach was left, from that day, to the mules and to whatever coal the southern workings still had cause to send that way. Iain Maor stood at the pit-mouth at Muileann chrom on the morning the first loaded train went down, his daughter beside him, seventy years old and the staff back in his hand a season after it had briefly left it, and watched the engine take the grade that the tram-mules had taken at a walk, at a pace no mule had ever kept, trailing a smoke the colour of the hill itself.

The crews at the pit-mouth had no word yet for the thing. By the day's end they had made one, calling it carbad-iarainn, the iron chariot, and calling the road it ran on the coal line, and both names outlasted every name the Company's own letter-book ever gave either. Mòrag, watching the smoke go down the valley toward Ros dhomhain and the sea beyond it, asked her father what he thought the engine would mean for the crews, now that the coal could travel faster than any crew that cut it ever would again, and Iain Maor, who had buried more of those crews in two-and-thirty years than he had ever troubled to count, said only that a thing which can carry more, faster, is a thing that will be asked to carry more, faster, and that the hill had not yet been asked the last of what it had to give. He did not live to see how far that answer would run. His daughter did, and kept it, in the years after, the way she kept most of what he told her at the pit-mouth that spring: filed away, unspoken, until the day it was needed.
