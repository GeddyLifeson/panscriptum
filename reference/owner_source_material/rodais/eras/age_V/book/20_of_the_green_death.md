# Of the Green Death

*AE 73 – 76*

## I. Of the Green Acre

Beitris Bhàn had laid out the dead of Achadh dhomhain for six years before the sickness came, and she thought she knew every way a body could leave the world underground. She had washed the crushed and the drowned and the cold, the old who went quietly in a house of spoil and the young who did not go quietly at all. She had not washed anyone the colour of a candle held behind glass, and when Fearchar Iasgair's wife sent for her on a wet morning in an t-Ògmhios of the seventy-third year, and she came into the hut and pulled back the blanket, she stood a long moment with the cloth in her hand before she could make herself begin.

He was green. Not the green of a drowned man, which she knew, nor the green-white of a man dead of poison, which she had seen twice; a green under the skin, as though a lamp with a coloured shade had been set behind his face and left burning. His lips were dark. When she washed his chest the water in the basin turned grey with the dust that had come up out of him in his last hours, more dust than she had ever wrung from a living man's coughing, as if the hill itself had been washing out of him from the inside.

"He was well at Bealltainn," the wife said, from the door, not coming in. "Well enough to dance at it."

"How long was he taken?"

"Nine days. Ten." The wife's hands were working at her apron, twisting it. "He went the colour first. The overman said it was only the bad air of the new gallery and he'd come right in a week above ground. Then the cough came, and then he couldn't stand."

Beitris finished him and covered him and went out into the rain and stood a while under the eave, because that was her way; she gave herself the length of one held breath for each body before she went to the next thing. Before the month was out she had given herself that breath thirty-nine times more. The camp buried its dead that summer in a field beyond the spoil-heaps that had never been used for anything, too stony for a garden and too far from the huts for convenient burying, and by the end of an t-Iuchar the field had a name the whole camp used without anyone deciding it should: an Acair Uaine, the green acre. Beitris helped dig the first of it herself, on a day when the grave-diggers proper were laid up sick themselves, and she never after that let herself forget the particular heft of a spade going into ground that had not been turned before.

She did not know then what the sickness was, or that it had a name yet to be given it, or that within the year every custodian's roll from the coast to the hills would be keeping a tally of it under one heading. Only this she knew: that Fearchar Iasgair had been a strong man at Bealltainn and green in the ground by midsummer, and that this was the fourth summer running in which the camp at Achadh dhomhain had buried more of its own than it had the year the deep galleries opened. Beitris laid out the camp's dead and did not think, that summer, of the hewer whose cough had stopped on the night his daughter was born. The one thing had nothing to do with the other, so far as anyone could see.

## II. Of the Blank Line

It went along the coal roads the way water goes along a furrow, taking the low ground first.

From Achadh dhomhain it followed the carts to Muileann chrom, and from Muileann chrom by the same roads that carried the coal itself, to Àth ìseal and to Cnoc fhiadhaich, and from those three camps it spread wider than any of the custodians who kept the count had thought a sickness of the lungs could spread, out along the lines the ore and the timber travelled, as though it rode the carts and not the men. It fell on Ros dhìreach, on Ceann òg, on Inis ìseal, on Baile dhomhain, on Achadh fhada, on Cnoc bheag, on Muileann uaine, on Cuan àrsaidh, on Inis bhàn. It fell on Baile chrom in the hills, not the Baile chrom of the north-west, whose beacon burned coal above a different sea entirely; and the clerks of that year took a particular care, twice underlined in one hand, to keep the two apart, for they had heard already of copyists elsewhere confusing towns of one name, and did not mean to be the ones remembered for it.

The custodians' roll for that year lists twenty-six settlements with cases set down against them, in the order the word of them reached Cathair dhearg, and after the twenty-sixth name the clerk who kept the roll left a line blank. Nobody now living can say why he left it, whether by habit, meaning to fill it when the next report came in, or because he could not make himself write the page full and close it. The line stayed blank through that autumn and into the winter, and was still blank when a different hand took up the roll the following spring, by which time a twenty-seventh settlement might as well have been entered, and was not. It is there still, in the loft of Beathag nic Thòmais, a line of nothing between one town's dead and the empty bottom of the page, and every custodian who has read it since has understood it without being told what it was left for.

At Àth ghlas the custodians did a thing no other camp thought to do until it was too late for the thought to help them. When the first three died within a week of one another, the custodian of that place, a stern woman named Sìne Ruadh who had buried her own father to the fever thirty years before and never forgot the smell of a sick-hut, ordered every hut in which a death had happened burned to the ground, roof, walls, bedding and all, before the next dark. The Company's agent at Àth ghlas protested the loss of its property in writing, three times, and was answered each time with the same line copied out fresh: the hut is the Company's; the ground under it is the custodians'; and the custodians will keep what stands on their own ground safe from what killed the man in it. Sìne Ruadh burned nine huts before the summer was out. Of every camp on the coal roads, the sickness left Àth ghlas soonest, and though the roll does not say it plainly, every custodian who has since compared the counts has drawn the same line between the burned huts and the empty ones.

At Cnoc naomh there was no burning, and no early stopping. The dead there were laid first in the burial-ground of the town's old shrine, in the ordered rows the shrine had kept since it was built, and when that ground filled, in a field outside it that had never held a grave before. The field at Cnoc naomh was never ploughed again after. Fifty years on, a woman who farms the land beside it can point to where the fence turns to go round it, and will tell you, if you ask, that nothing was ever sown there because nothing would grow; and if you ask her how she knows that, since nobody has tried it in living memory, she will say only that nobody in living memory has needed to.

## III. Of Doctor Bright's Table

Nathaniel Bright had been the Administration's physician at Muileann chrom nine years by the time the sickness reached the settlements around him, long enough to have buried three of his own household to fevers that were not this one, and long enough to have grown, in the Dia-thìrich way of speaking of a stranger they had come to trust a little, "the doctor who listens." He was a careful man, thin, with ink permanently under two fingernails of his right hand and a habit of repeating a patient's own words back before he wrote them down, so that the patient could hear whether he had understood.

His study began in the second summer of the sickness, in the small room behind the Mission's dispensary at Muileann chrom that the surgeon before him had used for storing bandages. He asked the custodians' leave to open the chests of the dead before burial, and was given it, on the condition that a custodian stand by the whole time and that nothing of the body be kept afterward. This he agreed to without argument, understanding, as few of his own people troubled to, that the custodians asked it as a plain point of law and no more. What he found, he weighed. A table grew in a black-bound book, ruled in columns: the name, where known; the years worked below; how much the lungs themselves weighed; and how much, so far as his scales could reckon it, of what was not lung in them. Beside each entry he wrote, in a small cramped hand, whatever the family or the crew could tell him of how the man had died, how long it had taken, and whether the green colour had come before the cough or after.

Anne Colby came to his room once, in the second winter, carrying a list of her own: the sick-house's tally, kept by name and settlement, that she had begun keeping without being asked to by anyone in the Administration, because nobody else was keeping it in any form the living could be traced by afterward. She set her list beside his book on the table and they went down both together by lamplight, matching names.

"There is no pattern I can find in who takes it and who does not," Bright said. "A hewer at the face fifteen years may live. A boy of six months below may die of it in a fortnight. I had thought it might follow the deepest galleries, and it does not; Cnoc fhiadhaich's shallow workings have as many dead as Achadh dhomhain's deep ones, by the head."

"What do you think it, then?"

"The dust. I can find no other thing they share." He turned the book round so she could see the column of his weighings. "Every lung I have opened is dark with it, more than a man could take in from ordinary air in a lifetime of ordinary work. I mean to weigh a hundred before I say so in writing, so that no one can say I guessed from ten."

He did not weigh a hundred. He fell ill himself in the third season of the work, in the spring of the seventy-fifth year, with the same colour and the same cough he had spent two years describing in other men's chests, and his own hand grew too unsteady for the small cramped writing before the summer was half over. The last page of his book breaks off in the middle of a row: a name, the years worked, the weight of the lung, and then nothing where the weight of the dust should have been, the pen having been set down, it seems from the mark it left, and never taken up again over that line. He died at Muileann chrom the following spring, and was buried by the Mission with the honours the Administration gave its own, in ground apart from the green acre and the field at Cnoc naomh, though the colour that took him from it was the same colour, and Anne Colby, who came to the burying, thought so and said nothing, because there was no one there it would have helped to say it to.

## IV. Of the Seal and the Letter-Book

Walter Crane had been Commissioner twelve years when the sickness reached the camps, and in all that time he had walked them himself, twice a season, in a coat that grew steadily shabbier because he would not send it to be brushed while other men's coats went unbrushed for want of a wife to do it. He walked the sick-rows at Achadh dhomhain in the worst of the second summer, when the stink of the huts kept even the huts' own children playing at a distance, and he did not walk faster past the bad ones than the ordinary ones, which the custodians noticed, and which Beitris Bhàn, who saw him there twice, noticed too, and remembered afterward in his favour, the only human besides Anne Colby she ever remembered so.

The sickness caught him in the autumn of the seventy-fourth year, most likely from the very rows he had walked so faithfully, though no one could say which visit. Before the winter was out he was dead, at the Residency above Ros dhomhain, in a bed his own physician had ordered him to twice and he had twice got out of to answer letters. Martha Gale, the Company's agent, held his seal for the months there was no one else to hold it, and ran the pay office and the ore contracts and the sick-house grants exactly as Crane had left them running, changing nothing, because she said afterward that a dead man's arrangements were owed at least that much respect until a live one came to say otherwise.

The live one came over the sea in the spring of the seventy-sixth year: George Tolley, sent out with orders, set down plainly in the papers he carried and read aloud once, without apparent discomfort, to the clerks of the Residency on his first morning, that the yield of the pits must not fall on account of the sickness, whatever else was done about it. He set a quarantine at Caol mhin on the northern coast within his first month, holding every ship from an Tìr Thall offshore until her crew had been looked over by a physician sent out in a boat for the purpose, a measure so plainly sensible, and so plainly aimed at keeping the sickness from crossing the sea rather than at the men already dying of it on the island, that the custodians approved it without reservation and said so in writing, the only order of his whole rule they ever did approve.

He did not visit the camps. Not Achadh dhomhain, not Muileann chrom, not Àth ghlas nor Cnoc naomh nor any of the twenty-six named in the blank-lined roll; not once in the two years of his rule that the sickness ran its worst. He kept his own letter-book at the Residency, in a fair clerk's hand that was not his own, dictated of an evening, and in it, when at last he set down his opinion of the cause of the Green Death for the Company's directors over the sea, he gave three causes and gave them in this order: the coal dust of the workings; the custom, as he understood it from his clerks, of the Dia-thìrich sleeping close by their own hearths in ill-ventilated huts; and the custodians' shrines, which he believed, on no evidence he troubled to name, might be a means by which the sickness passed from the sick to the well through the gathering of crowds at them. Anne Colby was shown a copy of the letter some years later, by a clerk who thought she would want to see it, and read it through twice before she said anything, and what she said was not fit to be entered in any roll.

## V. Of the Feathers Over the Door

The hawk-priests had been barred from the camps since Maddox's day, an order Tolley found already standing and saw no reason to lift, and the crews had long since learned to do without them in body while keeping them in every other way they could. In the years of the Green Death, the overseers' own reports, kept with more care than almost anything else the Administration wrote down in those years, since a matter of discipline was worth a clerk's ink where a matter of dying was not, begin to list, camp by camp, hut by hut, the finding of feathers nailed above doors.

A single feather, most often; sometimes two crossed. The overseers took them down as fast as they were found and entered the finding against the household's name, a mark that in some camps cost a week's pay and in others nothing at all, depending on which overseer happened to be walking that row. The crews put them back up as fast as they were taken down. Nobody was ever caught in the act of nailing one, in six years of the sickness and hundreds of feathers found; the custodians, when asked by an overseer once too many times who had done it, answered only that a hawk drops what it drops, and a door is a door, which satisfied no overseer and stopped no report and was, so far as anyone below the rank of Commissioner ever discovered, entirely true.

There was a saying that went with the feathers, said low in the huts at night and not to any human who might carry it up the hill, though by the second year of the sickness even some of the humans of the camps, the few who had married in and stayed, had it by heart in their own halting Dia-thìris, having heard it often enough at a neighbour's door to learn it the way a child learns a hymn without being taught the words of it on purpose.

*Ithidh Mòd an neach a dh'itheas an cnoc.*

Mòd will eat whoever eats the hill.

Beitris Bhàn nailed a feather above her own door in the second winter, after she had laid out the fortieth of the season, and did not take it down when the overseer's boy found it and told his father, and paid the week's pay it cost her without complaint, and put another feather up within the month.

## VI. Of the Washing

It was in the third summer of the sickness that Anne Colby came to Beitris Bhàn's door for the first time. No one at the Mission had sent her and no custodian had asked her; she had heard, from a woman at the sick-house, that the layer-out at Achadh dhomhain had washed more of the Green Death's dead with her own hands than any physician on the island had examined with instruments, and Colby had run out, that summer, of people who knew more about the sickness than she did.

Beitris let her in without a word and without surprise, as though she had been expecting a human at her door eventually and had simply not known which one. Colby had brought nothing with her but a clean apron rolled under her arm, which she put on in the doorway before she asked leave to help, and Beitris, who had never in six years let another pair of hands touch a body she was laying out, some old bone-deep unwillingness in her giving way all at once for reasons she could not afterward have named to herself, stood back from the table and let her.

They worked without much speaking, the way two women who have each done a hard thing alone for years learn to work together the first time without needing to be told how. Colby had the physician's way of it, methodical, naming what she saw as she saw it; Beitris had the older way, the custodian's rites said quietly under the working of the cloth, a word for each part of the body as it was washed, words that had been said over the dead of Achadh dhomhain since long before the Company came, said now for a woman whose name Colby had written on a list and Beitris had known since the woman was a girl.

"You say a word for every part of her," Colby said, once, not stopping her own hands.

"My grandmother's mother said it for her grandmother's mother. There is a word for each part because each part is owed its own leaving-taking, and none is owed more than the rest." Beitris turned the woman gently onto her side to reach her back. "Do the humans say nothing over their own dead?"

"We say a great deal. Over the ones we can afford a priest for." Colby's voice had gone flat in a way Beitris had heard before, in women who had buried more than they could carry the grief of properly and had learned instead to carry the work of it. "The camps' dead get none of it. I have buried forty with no more than my own two hands and a hole dug too shallow because there was no one spared from the pit to dig it deeper."

They finished the woman together and began the next, a boy of perhaps nine, and Beitris found, without deciding to, that she had begun saying the words aloud instead of under her breath, so that Colby, working opposite her, might have them too if she wanted them; and Colby, who understood no more than a handful of words of Dia-thìris and none of these particular ones, said nothing at all, but matched the pace of her own hands to the rhythm of them, so that the two of them moved together over the small body as though the words were being said by both, which in every way that mattered to either woman, they were.

They washed the camp's dead together through that summer and the next, whenever the sick-house sent Colby to Achadh dhomhain and the toll there was more than Beitris could face alone, which by the second of those summers was most weeks. Neither of them ever spoke of it to the Administration or to the custodians as anything but what it plainly was, two women doing a hard thing that needed two pairs of hands; but the camp came, in time, to speak of it as something else, and the phrase that grew up around it, *An Dithis a Nigh Còmhla*, the two who washed together, was said in the camps for years after, though neither woman, so far as the record shows, ever heard it said to her own face.

## VII. Of the Letter-Book's Order

The sickness did not end. It slackened, the way a fire slackens when it has burned through the driest of what it was given and is left working more slowly on what is left, the deaths falling from forty a month at Achadh dhomhain to four, then to one in a season, then, in most of the twenty-six settlements, to none for a year at a stretch before another case would show and the whole camp would hold its breath until it was seen whether one case meant a new burning or only one grave. Nathaniel Bright's book, with its broken-off row, was filed at the Residency and never taken up again by any physician who came after him; his question, what the dust did and how much of it a lung could hold before it stopped forgiving a man for breathing it, went unanswered on the island.

George Tolley's letter to the Company's directors, the one setting the sickness's causes down in the order he believed them to stand, was copied at the Residency into the bound letter-book that Lisle's successor had begun and every Commissioner after kept, each adding his own year's pages to what had gone before. It sits there still, among orders about the price of iron and the state of the harbour works, three causes given in three lines: the dust of the workings first, since that at least could not be laid at any door a Commissioner might one day wish to walk through; the habit of the hearth second, since a people's own customs were always the easiest blame to give and the hardest for them to answer; and the custodians' shrines last, named without one word of evidence beside them, because a shrine was a gathering, and a gathering could always be made to sound like a danger to a man who had never stood in one and watched what was actually done there.

Of the true cause, the dust that Bright had weighed and never finished weighing, carried up out of galleries driven faster and deeper than any before them into lungs that had never been asked to breathe so much of the hill at once, no Commissioner's letter-book ever said a plain word. The camps needed no letter-book to tell them. They had the green acre, and the field at Cnoc naomh that has never since been ploughed, and the feathers that went back up over every door as fast as the overseers took them down, and the saying that outlasted every one of the men who tried to stop its being said.

*Ithidh Mòd an neach a dh'itheas an cnoc.*

They had, too, though few beyond Achadh dhomhain itself yet knew to connect the two things, a girl in a house of spoil who had been steeped in more of that same dust than any child before or since, and had not sickened of it at all, and did not age.
