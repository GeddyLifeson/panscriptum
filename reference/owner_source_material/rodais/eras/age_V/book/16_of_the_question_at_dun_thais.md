# Of the Question at Dùn thais

*AE 49 – 56*

## I. Of the Basket on the Master's Table

The house of disputation at Dùn thais, Taigh na Deasbaid, stood at the top of the town where the street gave out into oak and hazel, a long hall of grey stone with a turf roof and one door. Over the door the old question of the schools was cut in the lintel, *Carson an làmh seo?*, why this hand, and the letters had been cut so long ago that moss grew in them and the students scraped it out with their thumbnails on the first day of every year. Inside there were benches down both walls, a hearth at the far end, and a table before the hearth where the master sat. On the table lay the register. It was the third of its name. The first two, filled, stood in a press behind the master's chair, and between them they held every question that had been set in that hall since Coinneach mac Mhàrtainn first set one, with the year, and the name of the master who set it, and whatever the master had chosen to say.

In the summer of {{year:IV-0114}} the master was Aonghas mac Coinnich. He was forty-nine that year, a long lean man with a shepherd's stoop and a grey beard he cut himself with a knife, and he had sat at that table for eleven years. He was born in the last summer before the mist lifted, and his mother used to say that he had lain in his cradle through the winter the ships came, listening to the whole island ask where they were from. At eighteen he had been a student on the benches of that hall, and had heard the cleric of the humans argue there through the winter of Wren's disputation, and he could still do Wren's cough, which he did sometimes, when the students were slow.

It was the custom of the house that the master set one question at midsummer and the house argued it the year round. Most masters took a question out of the press, one of the old ones turned a little to catch the light of the year. On the eve of midsummer Aonghas came up the street with a basket on his arm, and the students on the benches saw that it held coal.

It was the coal of A' Chompanaidh, the Company. He had bought it at the weigh-house by the river, in marks, and he said so. It was broken small, the sort the carts shed on the road, and he tipped it out on the table beside the register and went through it with his long fingers until he found a lump the size of a fist that had split clean in the cart. He held the broken face up to the window.

"Màiri nic Ailpein," he said. "Tell me what you see."

She was the youngest on the benches, a smith's daughter of the town. Up she came, and looked, and told him, as any child of the island would have told him, beginning at the edge: the grey that the old lumps have where the air has been at them, then the brown like wet bracken, then the deep green that lies under the black at the fresh break and seems to go down into the lump further than the lump goes, then the red that grows when you breathe on it. She breathed on it, and the red grew. In all she gave the names of eleven colours in all, which was good for a girl of fifteen, and sat down.

"Fearchar," said Aonghas, and Fearchar Beag gave him fourteen. Seumas mac Artair, who kept the register and was vain of his eyes, gave him nineteen and was stopped.

"Now," said Aonghas. He put the lump down on the table. "There is a man at the weigh-house by the river who weighed this coal for me this morning. He is one of theirs, a decent man, and careful with his beam. I held up this same lump and asked him what colour it was, and he looked at me as though I had asked him the colour of water, and he said the word they have. He said *black*. The cleric said it in this hall thirty years ago. The keeper of the Company's books at Ros dhomhain says it, and An t-Àrd-mhaor in his house above the river, and the girl who sells ribbons off their ships. Every one of them looks at this and sees one thing." He turned the lump over. "Why?"

Nobody answered him. He had not expected an answer. He sat down at the table, pulled the register to him, and wrote the question in his own hand, in the tongue of the hall, and turned the book round so that Seumas might see it:

> *Carson a chì sinne an dath, is iadsan dall air?*
>
> Why do we see the colour, and they blind to it?

That is how it stands in the register, under the date {{date:IV-0114}}. Below it, in Seumas's hand, which is smaller and neater than the master's, is the note that has been copied into more books than the question: that when the house asked the master what the answer was, he told them it was the wrong question, and would not give them the right one.

## II. Of the Summer of Arguing

They argued it all that summer, as the custom was, on the benches in the wet weather and under the oaks behind the hall when it was fine.

Seumas mac Artair held that the humans' eyes were made differently, as a dog's are, or an owl's, and that there was nothing more to be found out than that. Fearchar Beag held that it was the burning. He had it from the old tellings of Wren's winter: when the cleric had asked the masters why the vein should be holy, they had asked him back why his fuel should be burned. A people who had burned coal by the cartload for as long as they could remember, said Fearchar, would have stopped looking at it long ago, as a man stops seeing the colour of the peat he cuts. Màiri nic Ailpein, who was fifteen and afraid of nobody, said that she had once seen a human child at Inis bheag pick up a lump from the road and turn it in the sun for a long time; and the house argued for a week about whether the child had seen anything, or had been looking at the shine, and nobody could say, since nobody had asked the child.

Aonghas let them. He sat at the table with his hands folded on the register and said almost nothing, except to stop a student who was shouting, or to send one out for more peat. When they pressed him, and they pressed him every week, he said only that the question was a poor one, and they were welcome to it.

A student from the west who had come for the summer said aloud one evening what several of them were thinking, that the master had set them a question he did not believe in so that they would find the one he did.

"Then find it," said Aonghas, and went home to his supper.

In the autumn three of them set out for Baile Mòr uaine, a long day's walk to the west over the high moor, where there was a hall of disputation of its own, older in its benches than Dùn thais and prouder. They carried the question in their heads and a copy of it in Seumas's hand, sealed. The masters of Baile Mòr uaine took it up at the dark of the year. On {{date:IV-0115}} it was put to their hall for the first time, and the argument ran through that winter and the next, and the record of it, which the students sent back to Dùn thais leaf by leaf with the carriers, runs to four hundred leaves. It is the longest argument in the press. It ends, as the question did, without an answer. The last leaf is a list of the names of those who spoke, and at the foot of it the master of Baile Mòr uaine has written that the hall adjourned because the peat was done.

Aonghas read every leaf as it came. The students saw him at it late in the hall with a rushlight, turning the leaves slowly and sometimes going back. He never said which argument he thought the best. When the last leaf came, he had the whole bundle tied up with the others, and went out, and walked a long while in the oakwood in the rain.

## III. Of the Town with No Custodian

Those were the years when the humans began to build towns of their own.

The concession country in the north-west had been theirs to graze for thirty years, and the families there had built in stone for a generation. In the spring of the year after Aonghas set his question, they founded a market town at Cathair gheal, with a stone hall for their magistrate and a chapel with a bell, and their Administration, Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, sent them up a charter under its seal. The copyists have that charter, Cairt Chathair gheal, in a fair copy from the letter-books of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir. It gives the date of the founding as {{date:IV-0116}}, and the bounds of the market, and the tolls, and the rights of the magistrate over weights and measures and the ringing of the bell; and nowhere in it, in all its three sheets, is any custodian of the island named, or asked, or thanked, or told.

That same summer farmers out of the concession cleared land beside the old village of Cnoc ghorm, up in the high country of the north-west, and put up a mill on its stream. The people of Cnoc ghorm had lived under their pillar and its saying since the first ford-gatherings, and they had seen strangers come before. They sold the newcomers grain through the first winter, at a fair price, and carried it to them on their own ponies. When the mill-wheel turned in the spring, they stopped. Nobody at Cnoc ghorm said why, and the humans did not ask; but the Mission's letters speak afterwards of "a coolness at the old village," and of a boy of the farms who went up there to buy eggs and was sold none.

On the coast a few fishing families from the concession settled at Ceann mhòr under the north-western headland, on {{date:IV-0118}}. They were the first humans on the island to fish its waters for their own living and not for the Company's table, and the Dia-thìrich fishers of that coast, who had watched the Company's boats for fifty years, watched these more closely. The newcomers' boats went out when theirs did. They learned the marks of the reefs from them, and the set of the tides round the headland; and they took, in the second summer, to planting a leaf on the sheltered slopes behind the harbour, a coarse broad plant that they hung in their sheds to dry and sold to the Company's ships. By {{year:IV-0122a}} there were whole terraces of it behind Ceann mhòr. Of all the plants the humans brought, that leaf alone took to the ground of the island.

The news of the charter came to the council at Cathair dhearg in the humans' tongue, since the Residency sent everything in its own tongue by then, and the council sat a whole afternoon over three sheets that most of its members could not read.

## IV. Of Eachann Mòr's Seat

One of those who could not read them was Eachann Mòr of Ceann àrsaidh.

He had sat for Ceann àrsaidh on the council for nine years. Ceann àrsaidh was an old market town on the roads some twenty miles east of the capital, with a stone crossing over its river and a great many opinions of itself. It had given the council Coinneach Glas, and it expected to be listened to. Eachann was fifty that year, a big slow man with a voice that filled the council hall without his raising it, and he knew every boundary-stone and every well-right in his country, and the names of the grandfathers of the people who used them. He had the old reckoning by heart, and the rolls of his town back to An Aois Scaraidh. Of the humans' tongue he had perhaps ten words, and he was ashamed of none of them. 

He stood up in the council when the charter had been read, first in the humans' tongue by a clerk who had it and then in Dia-thìris by the same clerk, stumbling. He said that a town had been founded on the island under a writing that did not know the island had custodians, and that the council ought to send the writing back. The council agreed with him. It wrote to the Residency. Some weeks later an answer came in the humans' tongue, and the clerk who read it aloud stumbled again, and nobody on the benches could say afterwards with any confidence what it had meant, except that the charter stood.

Eachann went home and told Ceann àrsaidh what he had said in the hall. He told it well. But the Company had a weigh-house on the river at Ceann àrsaidh by then, and a road-agent, and a clerk who came through twice a month to settle the carting; and in the autumn, when the households met at the crossing to choose who should sit for them, a man spoke up who had never said anything at a choosing before. He was Dùghall Òg of the carting families, who had worked five years at the weigh-house and spoke the humans' tongue as fast as his own. The town needed a custodian, he said, who could read what was sent to him. There was no heat in it. Eachann Mòr, he said, was the best man in the country, and could not read a bill of lading.

They chose Dùghall. The roll of Ceann àrsaidh gives the count of hands, and it was not close.

Eachann walked home from the crossing with his wife Mairead nic Ìomhair beside him, and his son and daughter behind. He did not speak on the way. At the door of the house he stopped, and stood a while looking at the lintel, and then he turned round and looked at his son. Calum was twelve that autumn, a thin dark boy with his mother's eyes, who could already do the old reckoning faster than his father and had a way of listening at doors.

"You will have their tongue," Eachann said to him. "Better than Dùghall has it, and better than their clerks. And from their own schools."

Mairead said nothing then. She said a great deal afterwards, in the house, through the whole of that winter; and the copyists, who had the family's letters from a granddaughter's chest, found her side of the argument only in what Eachann wrote back to her from the road. He was never a man to put a thing on paper twice.

## V. Of the Ship That Went North-about

There was a school in the humans' tongue on the island that spring, and he would not send Calum to it.

Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise, the Mission of the humans' faith, opened its school at Ros dhomhain on {{date:IV-0121}}, in a new room built on the side of the chapel. It took thirty children in its first year and taught them their letters and their catechism and their sums, all in the humans' tongue. Most of the thirty were the children of overseers, who were paid in marks and wanted their sons to be paid in more of them. Among them, the Mission wrote home with great joy, were its first converts of the island's own people.

Eachann Mòr would not have his son at a bench among overseers' sons. That was his pride, and Mairead knew it for his pride, and told him so. But he had spoken in the autumn to the Mission's cleric at Ros dhomhain, and the cleric had spoken of schools over the sea, true schools, with libraries and masters, where a clever boy might learn the humans' law and come home able to read any charter the Residency could send. The cleric wrote a letter for the boy to carry. Eachann paid for the passage in marks, and he gave Calum besides a purse of marks sewn into the lining of his coat, which was nearly all the silver the house had.

They went down to Ros dhomhain in the spring, the four of them in a cart, a long day on the road through the farms and the coal-dust. The ship lay at the quay below the Residency, a Company ship going home with coal and hides, low in the water. Her master was a short man in a blue coat who looked at the boy, and at the letter, and at the purse when it was shown to him, and then wrote a line in his book. The quay was full of carts and barrows and the shouting of the men at the weighing-beam. There was nowhere to sit. The family stood by the bollards in the rain.

Calum went aboard on {{date:IV-0119}}. He went up the plank without looking back, since his father had told him to, and then at the rail he did look back, and lifted his hand. Ciorstaidh, who was nine, waved both her arms until her mother caught them.

The pilot of Caol leathan took the ship down the Abhainn gheal on the evening tide, and at the river's mouth she stood out to sea and turned north, as every ship of the humans turned, to go up the west coast past the headlands and out past the north cape into the grey water beyond, and so round by the north to the humans' land. That is the only road. The strangers' first ships had come down by it, and the fleet of An Aois Scaraidh had gone out by it long before. Mairead said afterwards that she hated to think of him going the long way, round the whole island, when the land he was going to lay somewhere off to the east where the sun came up. Eachann said that the ship knew its road. He stood on the quay until the masts had gone down the river out of sight behind the reeds, and then he went up to the Mission and thanked the cleric, and gave him a cheese.

## VI. Of the Letters That Did Not Come

The Mission's cleric had said a letter would come by the autumn ships. None came.

Eachann went down to Ros dhomhain when the autumn ships were in, and stood at the Company's counter and asked, through a pupil of the Mission school whom he paid a penny, whether any letter had come for the house of Eachann Mòr of Ceann àrsaidh. The clerk looked through a box and said none had. The ship that had carried Calum was not among the ships in, and the clerk did not know when she would be.

In the winter Eachann wrote a letter himself. He wrote it in Dia-thìris, since he could write nothing else, and took it to Ros dhomhain, and paid the pupil to put it into the humans' tongue under his own lines; and he gave it to the master of the first ship going north in the spring, with a mark for the trouble. It went, and there was no answer to it. He wrote another the next winter. The copyists found copies of four such letters among the family's papers, each longer than the last, all of them telling the boy about the harvest and his sister and the price of oats and the dog, and none of them asking him outright to write back. Mairead's hand is on the fourth, below her husband's, one line: *Scrìobh gu do mhàthair.* Write to your mother.

In the year after Calum sailed, the custodians of Inis bhàn in the north opened a school of their own, twenty-odd miles up the road from Ceann àrsaidh, on {{date:IV-0122}}. They said openly that it was an answer to the Mission's. They taught the old reckoning of years and moons and the reading of the rolls, and the names of the hills, every hill that could be seen from the school's door and a great many that could not, with the tale that went with each name. Eachann sent Ciorstaidh there. She was ten. She came home at midsummer able to name forty hills and argue with her father about the boundaries of his own parish, and the next year the Mission complained in writing to the custodians of Inis bhàn that the children who came to its school from theirs arrived already knowing how to argue. The custodians of Inis bhàn had that letter read aloud at their gathering and then kept it, as a testimonial.

In the third year Eachann went down to the Company's counter again and asked for the ship by name. This time the clerk found her in his book. She had come in to the island twice since the spring Calum sailed, he said, and gone out again, and her master had since been given another run. Against the day Calum sailed, the book had one line, which the clerk read out and the pupil turned into Dia-thìris for the old man: a passenger, a native boy, his fare paid in full.

Eachann asked what had become of the passenger. The clerk said that was not a thing his book would know.

## VII. Of the Coal Road in the East

While Eachann Mòr waited at Ceann àrsaidh, the Company came east.

It had opened workings in the eastern hills, and it wanted a road to the sea for their coal that did not go the long way round by the capital. Up the valley from Inis bheag there was an old bridge over the river at Ros fhionn, Drochaid Ros fhionn, which Ailean Clachair had first built and whose piers the masons of An Aois Naomh had mended. The river had silted below it in the last generations, and the salt trade had gone off to the coast, and the carts had stopped crossing; the office of the bridge-keeper had been done away with, and the grass had grown up between the stones. In the spring the Company's masons came and rebuilt it for heavy carts. They left Ailean Clachair's sign in the keystone where it was, since it bore nothing up; and on {{date:IV-0123}} the first coal-carts went over.

Aonghas walked over to see them, since Ros fhionn was no great distance from Dùn thais, and he took the students with him. They sat on the bank below the bridge all one afternoon and watched the carts go over, forty in the afternoon, each with its load heaped and roped and a boy on top to hold the rope. The drivers were most of them of the island, hill-men from the camps, and when the students shouted to them from the bank to throw down a lump, some of them did, laughing. Two of the drivers were humans. The students watched them closely. The humans looked at the coal they drove as a man looks at a load of stones, and at the students on the bank as though they had lost their wits.

"There," said Seumas mac Artair to the master. "The eyes."

"Perhaps," said Aonghas, and threw the lump he was holding back up to the next cart, and the driver caught it.

The coal went down over the bridge to the river quays of Inis bheag and there onto barges for Caol chrom at the river's mouth, where the ships took it. The Company deepened the river at the quays with a gang of men and a great iron scoop worked by a horse, and to make room for the coal-stage it moved the town's fish market, which had stood on that quay since before there was a town, up the hill to a yard behind the tannery. By the winter of {{year:IV-0124}} the quays at Inis bheag could load no more, and the barges began to go on down the Abhainn bheag to Baile mhin, a short way below, whose people took the overflow and were paid by the barge. On {{date:IV-0125}} the first barge was loaded there, and within two years Baile mhin had its first houses of stone, built out of the Company's money by the men who loaded the Company's coal.

Caol gharbh on the coast had hoped for the traffic. It had taken the salt trade when the river silted, a lifetime before, and its people had watched the bridge go up and made ready. But the Company's road ran down to the river quays and passed Caol gharbh by a mile, and all Caol gharbh ever had of the eastern coal was the dust, which came over on the east wind and lay on the washing. Its custodians wrote to the council at Cathair dhearg, on {{date:IV-0126}}, that they had not known a town could be harmed by a road it was not on. The council filed the letter. Aonghas had a copy of it from a student of that country, and he pinned it to the door of the hall, beside the lintel, where it stayed until the rain took it.

## VIII. Of the Things the Clerks Could Not File

There were things in those years the clerks of the Residency never knew where to put.

In the spring of {{year:IV-0127}} the crew of a Company brig came into Ceann mhòr with their mainsail split and their master white to the lips, and said that off the north-western headlands, on a clear morning, a beast had come up beside them that was longer than their ship. It had lain on the water, they said, and looked at them, and gone down. The report went to the Residency, as all reports did, and the clerk there read it, and filed it on {{date:IV-0127}} among the complaints about pirates, having no other box it would fit. The fishers of that coast heard of it and were not surprised. Uilebheist na Mara had been seen off those capes since An Aois Àrsaidh, and had fouled their nets within living memory; they said they had wondered how long the humans would sail there before they saw it too.

In the autumn of the same year a brig bound for the northern landing with tools was caught by a gale in sea-mist off Eilean fhada and driven onto the skerries there. Her crew waited for the ebb and waded ashore through the weed, and walked along the coast in the wet to the harbour at Caol bheag, where the fishers took them in and dried them by their fires. On {{date:IV-0127a}} her master made his report. Thereafter the Company's charts marked the northern passage closed to its ships from midwinter to the end of spring, which were the months of the gales and the mist; and so, from that year, no ship came to the island and no ship went from it for a third of every year. At Ceann àrsaidh Mairead nic Ìomhair heard of the new rule from a carter and asked what it meant. When it was explained to her, she said that she would stop going to the door in those months, then, and did.

That winter the human fishers of the concession taught the boats of Àth fhionn on the north coast to go out after the whales with the hand-lance, and not wait for them to strand. The men of Àth fhionn had taken whales as the sea gave them since the first harbour there, and had thanked Manannan for each one. They went out now in the humans' way, six to a boat, and killed them in the open water, and rendered the oil in pots on the shore and sold it to the Company for its lamps. After {{date:IV-0127b}} the whale-boats went out from the north coast every summer.

And in the summer of {{year:IV-0128}} two of the Company's surveyors came up to Doire uaine in the hills above Muileann chiar with chains and pegs and a boy to carry the instruments. The hills there are cut with the old writing that nobody can read, lines of it yards long, some of it scored across by the same kind of tool that cut it; and the people of the villages under them hold that the writing speaks of a thing that walks without breath, the Marbh-bheò, which falls on anyone who lights a fire in its sight. No fire has been lit where those hills can see it since An Aois Àrsaidh. The villagers told the surveyors so, through the boy. They told them not to go up. The surveyors thanked them and went up on {{date:IV-0128}} with a tinder-box in their pack, and camped on the high ground, and were not seen again.

The Company sent a search party of eight men. They went up into the hills one morning and came back down that same evening. They had found the surveyors' last camp and their chains laid out along a ridge, they said, and a fire-ring of stones with no ash in it; and past that they would not go, not for the agent's pay nor for his threats, and not one of them would say why. The agent wrote to the Residency that the men were idle.

The villagers of Doire uaine did then what their grandparents had done after any death on the high ground. They took salt and coal to the foot of the hills and left them on a flat stone there, for the sìth. The Company's agent for that country heard of it, and sent men on {{date:IV-0129}} to gather up the coal, since he would have no coal left lying about the country to be picked up by anyone who liked, and he had it carted back to the stockpile and weighed. After that the villagers left salt alone on the stone. The Company's men came up the next season and looked at the salt and let it lie.

## IX. Of the Question Put Back

Eachann Mòr came to Dùn thais at the end of the harvest of that year, when the rowans were red.

It was forty miles and more from Ceann àrsaidh, two long days on a borrowed horse, and he was fifty-six and had grown heavy, and he came into the town stiff and grey with the dust of the coal-road. He asked in the street for the master of the house of disputation. They sent him up the hill to the hall. Aonghas was at the table with the register open, and the students were out under the oaks, and there was nobody else in the hall at all.

Eachann had not met him before. He had heard of the question, as everyone on the island had heard of it by then, from the carriers and the carters and the hearths; and he had heard, from a carter, of the salt on the stone at Doire uaine, and of the men who would not go past a cold fire-ring. He sat down on the end of the nearest bench, since nobody asked him to sit and he could not stand any longer, and told the master who he was. Then he told him about the ship.

He told it straight through, as a custodian tells a matter to the council: the choosing at the crossing, and the Mission's letter, and the purse sewn into the coat, and the quay in the rain, and the four letters, and the clerk's one line. He did not hurry it. Aonghas did not stop him. When it was done he sat with his big hands on his knees, and the rooks went over the roof, and after a while he came to what he had ridden two days to ask.

"He could see it," he said. "He could name more in a lump than I could, by the time he was eight. His mother used to set him to it for a game, by the fire." He looked at the basket, which was still on the table, with the Company's coal in it. "Five years he has been among them. Eating what they eat. Burning what they burn, by the cartload. Hearing their word for it and no other." He stopped. "When he comes home, will he see it?"

Aonghas looked at him for a long time.

Then he took the split lump out of the basket, the same one, and turned its broken face to the window, and held it out across the table; and Eachann took it, not knowing what else to do, and looked, and the colours came up out of it for him as they always had, the grey at the edge and the brown and the green under the black and the red when his breath touched it.

"You see it," said Aonghas.

"I have always seen it."

"And you want me to tell you whether your son does, in a country none of us has seen, among a people I cannot see into." He took the lump back and laid it in the basket. "Seven years ago I set this house a question. Why do we see the colour, and they are blind to it? The house has argued it ever since, and so has Baile Mòr uaine, four hundred leaves of it. You have ridden two days to ask me the same question turned round. I told my students it was the wrong question. I will not answer it for you any more than I would for them."

"Then what is the right one?"

"I do not know," said Aonghas. "If I knew it I would have written it down."

Eachann Mòr sat a while longer. Then he got up off the bench, heavily, and thanked the master for his time, as a custodian thanks a man who has told the council nothing, and went out into the yellow evening and down the hill to find his horse.

Seumas mac Artair, coming in from the oaks, met him at the door and asked the master afterwards who the big man had been. Aonghas told him, and told him why he had come. Seumas asked whether he should put it in the register.

"Put in that a man came from Ceann àrsaidh to ask the question," said the master, "and that the master did not answer it."

Seumas wrote it down. It is the last entry in Aonghas mac Coinnich's hand of that year, or rather in Seumas's under his dictation, dated at the end of the harvest of {{year:IV-0129}}. Below it the rest of the leaf is empty. The basket stood on the table in the hall through the winter, with the Company's coal in it, and the students took lumps from it to argue over and put them back, and none of them was ever burned.
