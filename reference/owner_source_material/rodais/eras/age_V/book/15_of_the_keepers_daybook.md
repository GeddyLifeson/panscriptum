# Of the Keeper's Daybook

*AE 45 – 48*

## I. Of the Fire-Shelf and the Tower

Of the four witnesses in the chests at Muileann chaol, the copyists say the daybooks of the beacon-keepers are the dullest to read and the easiest to trust. A custodian writing to the council wanted something of it. So did a clerk of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach writing home. A keeper on a headland wanted oil for the lamp and a dry wick, and what he wrote down was the wind, the weather, the hour he lit the flame and the hour he put it out, and every sail that went by his light. A keeper has nothing to gain from a ship that is not there. So the copyists have leaned on the daybooks wherever the other witnesses quarrel, and the first daybook of them all was begun at Àth dhearg, on the north-eastern cape, by a mason named Tormod mac Pheadair.

The fire on that cape was older than any roll. The households of the coast had kept it on a shelf of rock near the top of the headland since before An Aois Naomh, and had cut a stair up to it from the shore, turning back and forth across the face of the cliff, a hundred and more steps, each narrower than a man's boot. Forty years before, the mason Torcall Clachair had sat on that shelf with the human navigator James Fenn, counting the chisel-cuts, and had raised a cairn at its end where the rock was broad enough to bear a tower. The custodian of that day had made them go round every hearth of Àth dhearg to ask leave, and it had taken eleven days.

The cairn stood a long while with nothing beside it. The beacons at Tobar dhearg and Baile ìseal were lit, both of Torcall's building; then Torcall died, and A' Chompanaidh, which paid for the towers, let the third wait while it paid for props and pumps and winding-rope in the hills. When at last it sent word up the coast that it would pay for a tower on the north-eastern cape, it sent no mason with the word. Its masons were building at the pits.

So the custodian of Àth dhearg called the hearths together again, in the long room over the net-store where the town held its councils, and put it to them. The strangers would pay half the cost in marks, their coin with the ship on one face, and would send the lens and the oil; the town would find the stone and the hands. The other half of the pay, she said, was a promise: that the tower would bring home the boats of Àth dhearg as the old fire had done, and better. Some in the room laughed. A promise, said one old fisher, was a poor thing to put in a pot. But the households of that coast had lost boats on the reef below the cape in every generation, and nobody in the room had to be told whose, and the town said yes.

Tormod mac Pheadair was among the first to put his name down. He was twenty-nine that winter, a mason's son, broad and slow-moving, with a burn-scar across the back of one hand from a lime-kiln and a way of looking at a wall for a long while before he touched it. Byres and sea-walls he had built all along that coast, and he had worked on the new quay at Seann Bhral, where he first saw the humans' figures chalked on a stone to be cut. The custodian's clerk had taught him his letters as a boy, and he wrote a square, careful hand, like a man laying courses.

They built through a year and more. The stone came from the landward side of the headland on sledges. They raised the tower over the end of the fire-shelf, and Torcall's cairn they took down stone by stone and set into the footing, facing out to sea, because that was what Torcall would have done. Inside the tower they turned a stair in the thickness of the wall, so that no keeper need climb the old stair in a gale again, as the town had been promised forty years before. The old stair they left as it was.

In Teasach of the forty-fifth year the lamp-room was roofed, and the lens came up the coast road in a crate packed with straw, with a human of A' Chompanaidh riding beside it who would let nobody else touch it. He set it on its iron frame, polished it for most of a day, and then asked who would keep the light. The keeper would be paid a little, in marks, and must keep a book: his masters wished to know how many ships went by the cape, and whose, and when, and which of their own captains kept to their sailing-days. Most of the household elders could not write. Among the masons of the tower there was one who wrote a fair hand and knew the humans' figures besides.

So Tormod mac Pheadair was made keeper of Taigh-solais Àth dhearg. He said afterwards that he had only gone up the stair to fetch his hammer.

## II. Of the First Line

The lamp was lit at dusk on {{date:IV-0106}}. The whole town came out along the cliff-top to see it, and most said it was brighter than the old fire. The man of A' Chompanaidh noted the hour by his watch. In the morning he rode south, and Tormod was alone in the tower with the lamp, the oil-casks, a bundle of wicks and a book of the humans' paper, bound in grey board, left on the table.

He sat a long while before he wrote in it. The paper was better than anything the custodian's clerk had, and he did not like to spoil it. In the end he wrote as he would have cut a line in stone, slowly, pressing hard. The copyists found the book among the first chests from the north-east, its grey boards warped by the damp and its first leaf gone brown at the edges, and the first line reads:

> *Air a lasadh aig ciaradh. Gaoth an iar, beag. Cha robh long ann.*
>
> Lit at dusk. Wind west, light. There was no ship.

Under it, in the same hand, stands the night of the moon: the tenth night of Teasach. The humans' day stands above the line, in a different ink.

The man of A' Chompanaidh came back at the end of Lùghnas with a cart of oil and read the book through at the lamp-room table, and was not pleased, for he could not tell from the nights of the moon on what day any ship had passed. He sat Tormod down beside him and wrote out a list of the humans' months and days to the end of their year, each against its night of the moon as well as he could reckon it, which was not well. From then on Tormod wrote two dates at the head of each entry, the humans' day above in their figures and the night of the moon beneath in words, and went back and wrote the humans' days over the entries already made. Some of them he got wrong, and the copyists have set them right from the letter-books.

The entries of that first autumn are short and much alike. The lamp was lit and put out. The wind went round. Fog came in off the sea on the nineteenth night of Pailteach, and he wrote that the lamp was lit and did no good. The fishing boats of the coast went in and out, and he left them out of the book, for the man of A' Chompanaidh had said *ships*. Then, on the fourth night of Damhrach:

> *A ship of the strangers, three masts, going south. Deep in the water. She showed a lamp at the stern.*

She went by within two miles of the cape with every sail set, and Tormod watched her out of sight from the gallery. He did not know her name, or her cargo, or her port. He wrote down what he had seen.

By the end of the humans' year he had written down nineteen of them. By the end of the next, forty-four.

## III. Of the Boats Brought Home

That winter the masons went on working, though the lamp was lit. The outer face was half-pointed when the weather closed in and the keeper's house at the tower's foot was unroofed, and Tormod slept in the lamp-room on a pallet by the oil-casks and wrote by the light of the lamp, the best light he had ever written by. Few ships came round the north coast in the dark months, and the daybook shows them late on their sailing-days, and early never.

On the second night of Bealltach, in the forty-sixth year, a gale came up out of the north-east in the afternoon, when four boats of Àth dhearg were out on the long lines beyond the reef. Tormod saw it coming from the gallery: a dark edge on the sea far out, and the gulls coming in ahead of it. He lit the lamp an hour before dusk and stood in the wind and rain watching the grey water below the cape, where the reef showed white when the swell drew back.

The first boat came in at dusk. The second came an hour after, low in the water and baling. Then nothing came for a long time, and the women of the four households stood on the cliff-top with lanterns, as the women of that coast had always stood in a blow, under the shelf where the old fire had been fed by hand. There was no fire on the shelf now. The tower's light went out over their heads to sea.

The third boat came in near midnight, from the wrong side of the reef, having run north past it in the dark and come round. Her steersman said afterwards that he had not known where he was until he raised the light, and then he had known exactly, because it could be nowhere else. The fourth came in at first light, a man short. He had gone over the side in the afternoon, before the gale reached them, where no light could have helped him.

Tormod wrote it all down, which was more than the man of A' Chompanaidh had asked: the four boats by name, the hour of each, and the lost man and his name. It is the only entry in the first daybook that names anyone of Àth dhearg. The copyists think he wrote it so that the promise could be seen to have been kept.

On {{date:IV-0107}} the work was done. The keeper's house was roofed and the outer face pointed, and the man of A' Chompanaidh came up the coast road with a clerk and a strong-box and paid the masons in marks from a table set out on the grass at the tower's foot. There were eleven of them, and Tormod came down the stair and stood in the line and took his pay with the rest. The custodian's clerk wrote the payment into the town's roll, and wrote beside it that the masons of the tower had been the town's own men, and that the other half of their pay had been made on the second night of Bealltach, in four boats and three of them home.

## IV. Of the Glow in the South

In the autumn of that year a ship of A' Chompanaidh went south past the cape:

> *A ship of the strangers, two masts. Coal. Going south close in, with the wind behind her.*

He knew her for a collier by the way she sat low and square in the water, and because as she passed below the cape the wind brought him the smell of her cargo, the smell of broken coal that nobody on the island mistook. At that distance he could not see the colour in it. He could smell it.

Two nights after, at the change of the watch, he went out onto the gallery and saw a redness low in the south, under the cloud, where there should have been nothing but the dark coast running away towards Ceann leathan and the islets beyond. It was too steady for lightning and too far off for a fire on any hill he knew. He watched it grow, and towards morning sink.

> *A light in the south under the cloud, red, from the middle watch until near dawn. I do not know what it was.*

Four days later a carter came up the coast road with salt and the news. Raiders in low black boats had come out from the islets off the eastern shore in the night and taken the collier, and stripped her of what they wanted, and set her afire, and she had burned to the waterline with her coal in her. Her crew had been put in their own boat and set rowing for the land, and came ashore alive at a fishing village near Ceann leathan, and the village fed them. The copyists set the burning at {{date:IV-0108}}, which is the day the letter-books give.

The raiders of the eastern islets had taken Dia-thìreach boats in every generation since An Aois Naomh, and nobody on that coast was surprised that they had taken a stranger's at last. Within the month its agents were saying at Seann Bhral and Ros dhomhain, and writing to Arthur Lisle, An t-Àrd-mhaor, that Comann an Airgid had paid the raiders, out of spite for the surveyors turned back from the adit at Muileann chiar.

The guild said nothing. The copyists have looked for a word from it in every chest that came to the cellar, and have found none.

In the dark days of Dubhlas a clerk of A' Chompanaidh rode up the coast road to Àth dhearg alone and asked to see the daybook. He had some Dia-thìris and could read the humans' dates, and he found the collier and the red light, and went over them with his finger. Then he asked Tormod whether he had seen any boats go south along the coast in the days before the burning. Low boats, black-painted, with many oars.

Tormod said that he had written down every ship he saw.

The clerk said that such boats were hardly ships, and Tormod might have seen them and thought them not worth the writing. There was room at the foot of the page.

Tormod looked at the page for a while. Then he said that he had seen no such boats, that if he had seen them he would have written them down, and that he would not write down a boat he had not seen, for his own people or the clerk's.

The clerk did not argue. He thanked the keeper for the use of the lamp-room fire and rode south in the morning. The daybook says nothing of his coming. His report lies in the letter-books of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir among the papers on the burning of the collier, and says that the keeper at Àth dhearg was "civil, but of no assistance," and that his book "throws no light on the matter"; and somebody at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir has underlined the words about the light.

## V. Of the Gunboat and the Cove

The next summer A' Chompanaidh sent a ship of war.

Tormod saw her come round the north coast on a clear morning in Teasach of the forty-seventh year. She was smaller than the colliers and lower and a great deal faster, two-masted, painted black with a white band along her side, and there were more men on her deck than any trader carried. Through the glass A' Chompanaidh had given him for reading sails he made out the gun in her bows under a canvas cover, and wrote her down with more care than he had given any ship before:

> *A ship of the strangers, two masts, black with a white band. A gun in the bows under canvas. Many men. Going south along the coast.*

On {{date:IV-0109}} the gunboat found a cove near Ceann leathan with boats drawn up on the shingle and the smoke of a fire among the rocks above. Her master wrote in his report that he had tracked the raiders there. He put a boat's crew ashore with torches under cover of his gun, and there was nobody on the shingle to stop them. They burned every boat drawn up there, eighteen of them. The gunboat stood off through the day, waiting for the raiders to come down out of the rocks, and nobody came.

Most of the boats were the fishermen's. The households of Ceann leathan drew up their small boats in that cove in the summer, when the lining was good off that shore, and slept in the bothies above the rocks, and it was their fire the master had seen. When the torches came up the shingle they had run into the rocks with their children, having heard what a gun could do, and from the rocks they watched their boats burn. The raiders were not found. Nobody on the coast believed they had been there.

Tormod wrote the gunboat going back north on the third day after, running before a southerly wind:

> *The gun-ship, going north. The gun uncovered.*

The news came up the coast road behind her, with a Ceann leathan man who walked nearly forty miles north with two of his sons to ask Àth dhearg for the loan of a boat. The town had boats to spare that year, since the masons had been paid in marks and two households had bought new boats with the money. The households talked it over into the night in the long room over the net-store, and gave the loan of two, to be returned when the Ceann leathan men had built their own. The man and his sons sailed them south in the first days of Lùghnas, and passed under the cape on the way, and Tormod saw them go. He left them out of the book, as he left out all the boats of the coast.

## VI. Of the Wall on the Shingle

That same Lùghnas, on the other side of the island, the council of Baile dhìreach broke apart.

It had sat for forty-two years, and every year it had sat on less. When the customs house went to Ros dhomhain six years before and took the harbour dues with it, the council had cut its members from six to four. William Rook and Iain mac Chaluim gave up their seats then, being old men, and sat on the salt-house step on fine evenings with their pipes and watched the others go up the ladder to the net-loft without them. The two books stood on the shelf there side by side, and most months there was little to put in either.

Iain Hale was thirty-eight that summer, the son of the marriage entered in both books on the quay, of the net-maker Eilidh nic Ruairidh and the human cooper Samuel Hale. He had been born in the tent on the shingle and grown up in the yard, and he was a boatwright. The carpenter Nathan Cobb had taught him the humans' way of setting up a keel, and his grandfather Ruairidh the salter had taught him the town's knots before he could walk. He spoke both tongues as a child speaks them, without choosing. He had his father's long arms and his mother's broad hands, and people of both peoples in the town were a little shy of him, as they were of the tide-line, which is neither sea nor land.

In the spring of that year the yard-master, Daniel Lusk, the sawyer's son who had come ashore as a boy with the first seven, came back from Ros dhomhain with a promise from A' Chompanaidh of oak and pine by the shipload, more than the yard had ever had, if the yard would build for it. He had nowhere to stack it. The yard stood on the shingle north of the quay by the town's leave, and above the wrack the shingle was held for the whole town and built on by nobody; so both books said, each in its own words.

Lusk wanted to build a wall.

He put it to the council in the net-loft on an evening in Lùghnas, with the rain on the slates. A wall of dry stone, he said, no higher than a man's chest, from the foot of the slip up the shingle to the old post at the top. It would keep the timber in and the town's children out. It would not be a house, and the compact said nothing against walls.

Eòghann mac Iain, Iain mac Chaluim's son, who sat for the town, said that the compact did not need to. A wall was a building, and the first thing that anybody would ever have built on that shingle, and a wall had two sides.

The other town member was Mairead nic Aoidh, Seonag's granddaughter, who kept the salt-house as her grandmother had. She said that before there could be a wall somebody had to say where the line was, and the town had never said that the yard went this far and no further. The yard was on the shingle by leave, as the curers of Inis thais had been in her grandmother's grandmother's day. A wall would make it a piece of ground.

"It has been a piece of ground for forty years," said Lusk.

The fourth member was Nathan Cobb, seventy and deaf in one ear, who had sat on the council since the night the compact was written. He took the humans' book from the shelf, opened it at the compact, and read out the clause about the shingle in the humans' tongue: *The shingle north of the quay is the property of the town.* If it was the town's property, he said, not unkindly, then a wall would show where the town's property ended and the yard's began, and that was the whole use of a wall.

Mairead asked for the other book. Iain Hale was sitting at the foot of the table, having been asked to carry the talk from one tongue to the other when either side stuck, and it was he who took down the Dia-thìris book and found the page and read it out: *The shingle north of the quay is kept for the town.*

Everyone at the table looked at him then, from both sides, and waited for him to say which of the two books was right. He had read them both, the only man at the table who could.

He closed the book, put it back on the shelf, and said nothing.

They argued for four nights. While they argued Lusk had the footing of the wall laid, a line of big stones from the slip half-way up the shingle, and on the third night somebody of the town took the stones up and carried them below the wrack-line and left them in a heap. Nobody on the council would say who. On the fourth night, {{date:IV-0110}}, Lusk stood up and said that he would not sit with people who pulled down a man's work in the dark, and Eòghann mac Iain said that he would not sit with people who built on the town's ground by daylight, and Lusk went down the ladder. Cobb sat on a while, looking at the table. Then he got up and followed.

The council of Baile dhìreach never met again. On the last written page of the Dia-thìris book a line in Eòghann mac Iain's hand says that the council rose without its business done. The humans' book has nothing on its last page but the date, in Cobb's shaking hand.

## VII. Of the Yard That Went East

The human families began to go before the harvest was in. There were not many of them now, and most were the families of the yard. Two went up the coast to Ros bheag, the citadel harbour of the west, where there was a human chandler and a chapel. The rest went to Ros dhomhain, where A' Chompanaidh had its yards and counting-houses and work for any man of their people who could hold an adze.

Of the households that were of both peoples it is not told which went and which stayed, save one. The Hales stayed, and said nothing about it. They went neither to the quay to say they were staying nor to the salt-house to say they had taken the town's side. They kept to their house at the head of the street by the well, where they had lived since the tent grew too small for them; Samuel Hale mended the town's barrels as he had for forty years, and Eilidh mended nets on the frames outside the door, being too stiff in the knees now for the shingle. The humans who were going came to their door to say goodbye, and the Hales gave them bread and a jug of ale. When the town spoke of the wall in their hearing, they spoke of other things.

In the first days of Samhnas Lusk came to find Iain in the yard, where the men were breaking up the stacks and caulking the yard's own boats for a voyage. The yard was going east, he said, to Inis chrom, a day's sail along the coast: a walled harbour of sailors on its bent island in the mouth of the river, with ground to spare, where A' Chompanaidh had promised the timber and the town would take them in and say nothing about walls. A' Chompanaidh wanted boats, and would want more every year. Lusk needed a foreman who could set up a keel of the humans' pattern and talk to the island's timber-men in their own tongue, and he offered Iain the place, for more marks than the yard had ever paid.

Iain asked for a night to think. He went down that evening to the shingle and sat on the heap of footing-stones below the wrack-line and looked at the yard: the long shed open to the sea, the slip of split logs bedded in the stones, and the tent. It stood where Rook's men had set it up in the first summer, a little crooked, with its back to the west wind and a lamp in its window of real glass. Rook had written in the compact, in his round slow hand, that the tent would be taken down when the council told him. Now there was no council to tell anybody anything.

His father found him there after dark. Samuel Hale was sixty-nine and walked with a stick, and he came down the shingle slowly and stood beside his son and looked at the tent, as his father-in-law had looked at it forty years before, when the town was still deciding whether it was a house.

"Your mother says you'll go," he said. He said it in Dia-thìris, as he always did in the town. His Dia-thìris had grown little better since the day of the wedding, and in forty years the town had come to hear it as the way the cooper talked.

"And you?"

"I said you'd stay." Samuel poked at a stone with the end of his stick. "We didn't wager on it. She wouldn't."

After a while Iain asked his father whether he had ever thought of going back over the sea. Nobody in the family had ever asked him.

Samuel laughed. He had a sister over there somewhere, he said, if she was living, who would not know him, and a town that would not know him either. When the men of the yard talked of home they meant a place; when he thought of home he thought of a bowl of whatever was in the pot, on the salt-house step. And he was not the one being asked.

In the morning Iain went to Lusk and told him no.

The yard sailed for Inis chrom on {{date:IV-0111}}, three boats deep with tools and timber and families, on a cold bright morning with a north wind. Half the town came down to the quay to see them off, which was more than anyone had expected. The last boat out was steered by Nathan Cobb, deaf and upright at the tiller, and he did not look back.

When they had gone round the point Iain climbed the ladder into the net-loft. The humans' book lay on the long mending-table where Cobb had left it on the last night of the council, open at the page with the date and nothing under it. Iain closed it and set it back on the shelf beside the Dia-thìris book, the two side by side as they had always stood, and the copyists found them there in that order.

That winter he took over the slip with the town's leave and under the town's rule, and built the town's boats on it, a keel at a time. The tent stayed where it was on the shingle. Nobody told anybody to take it down.

## VIII. Of the Dry Year and the Seed

At Àth dhearg the daybook went on as before. The forty-seventh year gives fifty-one ships of the humans going by the cape; the forty-eighth gives fewer colliers and more ships bound for Seann Bhral and the eastern harbours with goods from over the sea. Tormod had a wife by then, Ciorstaidh nic Phàdraig of Baile ruadh, a red-haired woman who laughed at his handwriting and could not read it. They lived in the keeper's house at the tower's foot, and in the evenings she climbed the inside stair with his supper and stood on the gallery with him and named the boats of the coast as they came in.

In the spring of the forty-eighth year the rain began to fail.

The daybook does not say so at first. It says, night after night, *wind west, dry*; *wind south-west, dry*; *no rain*; and the copyists noticed the word before they noticed the season. A keeper writes down the weather because the weather is what brings ships ashore. By Ògach he was adding what he saw from the gallery: the fields inland going yellow before midsummer, and a heath fire on the hills to the south that burned three days. In Teasach he wrote that the burn below the town had stopped running, which it had never done in anyone's memory, and that the women were carrying water from the spring on the landward side of the headland.

The farming country south and east of the cape, the old fields of Achadh shean and the red-earth country beyond, lived by its grain. The rain failed over all of it through the summer. It rained a little in Pailteach, and the daybook marks the night with a word it had not used for four months, and then the rain stopped again. On {{date:IV-0112}} the custodians of Achadh shean wrote to the council at Cathair dhearg that the harvest of their country had failed for want of rain, and that the granaries of the custom of An Aois Naomh would carry them through the winter. The council's roll enters the letter. No answer is entered.

Baile ruadh had sown its winter corn early, in the last days of Lùghnas, trusting to the rains of Pailteach as the farmers of the red-earth country always did. The rains came for three nights and stopped. The seed swelled and sprouted and withered in the dry ground, across the whole of the town's fields. By Damhrach there was nothing to be done but sow again, and no seed left to sow; the granary had gone to feed the town through the summer.

Ciorstaidh's brother Pàdraig sat on the council of Baile ruadh, and he sent a boy up the coast road to the tower. Tormod left the lamp in the charge of the old clerk's grandson, who had learned to trim the wick, and walked south-east over the brown hills to Baile ruadh, a long day. At the council's table in the custodian's house sat a clerk of A' Chompanaidh from its store at Seann Dunn, a thin young human with a ledger and an ink-pot, and beside him a Dia-thìreach of Seann Dunn to put his words into the island's tongue.

The store at Seann Dunn had seed. A' Chompanaidh had brought it over the sea in grain ships, some of which Tormod had written down going south past his cape, and it would lend enough to sow the fields again before the frosts, at a price to be paid back in grain over five harvests. The terms were written out in the humans' tongue and the figures in the humans' figures. Nobody at the table could read them. The custodian of Baile ruadh, who was old and had a cough, turned to Pàdraig and asked whether it was true that his sister's man could read the strangers' numbers.

So Tormod read them. He stood at the custodian's elbow and put his finger on each figure in turn and said it aloud in Dia-thìris: the measure of seed lent, the measure of grain to be paid back at each harvest, and the number of harvests. He read them twice, as he would have read a line on a stone twice before he cut it. The figures were what the man of Seann Dunn had said. The price was high, and there was nothing in the paper that the council had not been told, and he said so.

The council talked a little, not long, since there was nothing else to talk about. Then the custodian made his mark on the humans' paper, and the clerk sanded it and blew on it, and on {{date:IV-0113}} the town of Baile ruadh borrowed its seed corn from A' Chompanaidh. The custodians' roll of Baile ruadh enters the debt under the night of the moon, with the measure and the five harvests. It is the first debt in grain to the strangers that stands in any roll of the island. Beside it the clerk of the roll has written, in a smaller hand, the name of the man who read the figures.

The seed-carts came up from Seann Dunn three days later, and the town sowed its fields again in the dust and waited for rain.

Tormod walked home the next morning. He came up the coast road in the late afternoon and saw the tower on the headland ahead, grey against a grey sky, and Ciorstaidh on the gallery. The boy had lit the lamp early, since there was cloud coming in from the west, and Tormod climbed the inside stair in the last of the light and read over what the boy had written while he was away, which was *lit* and *no ship* and *lit* again.

Before midnight the rain came, and a ship went by below the cape, going south. He knew as well as anyone on that coast what the deep ships going south carried that autumn, and to whose store. He wrote down what he saw, with the humans' day above and the night of the moon beneath, and it is the last line on that leaf:

> *Wind west. Rain before midnight, a small rain. A ship of the strangers going south, deep in the water.*
