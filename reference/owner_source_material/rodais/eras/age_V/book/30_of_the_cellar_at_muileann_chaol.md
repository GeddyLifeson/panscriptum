# Of the Cellar at Muileann chaol

*AE 121 – 126*

## I. Of the Second Birth

In an Gearran of the hundred and twenty-first year a second child was born who did not take after the way of children, and word of it went up the east road in a season, from mouth to mouth, the way such word goes among a people who cannot write it down themselves and must trust it to carriers. His name was Fionnan. The custodians who kept the eastern rolls set down the birth and no more beside it, for there was no more that could yet be told; but every one of them who had heard, fifty years before, what the camps said of a girl called Aisling understood, on the reading of that single line, that the thing was no longer a wonder of one house. A wonder that happens once is a wonder. A wonder that happens twice is a question, and a question, once it is asked, does not go quietly back to sleep.

At Muileann chaol, where this telling is set down, the news reached the cellar in the ordinary way that all news reached it: folded small inside a roll of births and burials that a rider brought in on a wet evening, indistinguishable to look at from the fifty rolls before it and the fifty after. Nobody in the cellar that year thought to mark the page. It was only later, when the word had a name and the name had a use, that the copyists went back through the year's rolls to find the line again, and were a long while finding it, because it had been written the same size as every other line on the page.

## II. Of the Town and the Loft

Muileann chaol stands on the eastern slope where the mill road crosses the burn twice within a bowshot, a narrow town that had made the island's first paper from rags in an older age, and had gone on making it in a small way ever since, more from habit than from any great custom for the stuff. It was known east of the capital chiefly for one thing that had nothing to do with paper at all: the loft over the old mill, where a mill-owner's widow named Beathag nic Thòmais had once stacked the copies she bought up from houses that no longer read them, and left the loft and all that was in it to the town on the single condition that nothing kept there should ever be burned. The town had kept that condition longer than any of the families now living in it could trace their own descent, and since the strangers came the custodian houses of the east had grown into the habit of sending to the loft anything they wished to be certain of, since the Residency's writ did not reach into an old woman's roof-space over a paper mill, whatever else it reached.

Cailean mac Eachainn had grown up under that roof without ever once climbing the ladder to it. His father cut turf on the hill above the town; his mother wove the coarse grey cloth that Muileann chaol sold at the coast markets before the barter went out of the old towns' hands. He was a plain hand at his letters, no better and no worse than any boy who had sat through the winter months at the custodian's fire learning them, and he had thought, as far as he had thought about it at all, that he would cut turf as his father did, and grow old on the same hill, and be buried, in the end, under it.

What changed that was not the loft, which had stood over the town all his life and asked nothing of him, but the cellar, which had not.

## III. Of the Three Households

In am Màrt of the hundred and twenty-fourth year, at the end of a winter that had closed the coast road with drift, three families of Muileann chaol, the mac Ailpeins, the nic Rìghs, and the household of an old widow named Seònag Bhàn, took in more work than they had ever taken before, and it came from the east.

The custodians of the eastern hills, from Cnoc thais down to the shore below Dùn thais, had begun that winter to send copies of their rolls to Muileann chaol for the keeping, over and above whatever they had always sent to the loft. It was not, at the start, spoken of as anything more than good housekeeping: a custodian's roll, kept in one thin book in one damp custodian's house, could be lost to fire or flood or the plain wearing of the years in a way that three copies, kept in three different houses in a town the Residency had no cause to visit, could not. But a roll copied is a roll read, twice over, once by the hand that first wrote it and once by the hand that copies it fair; and three households reading, winter on winter, the whole record of the eastern hills, every birth and burial, every marriage with its seven names, every fine and fever and every shift lost to the Company's pits, came in time to know that record better than any single custodian who kept only his own corner of it ever could.

They worked in the cellar under Seònag Bhàn's house, because it was the driest of the three and had a chimney that drew well even in the worst weather, and because Seònag herself, being past the age for close copying, kept the peat going and the ink from freezing and fed whoever was at the table, which by the second winter was five or six people at a time. It became, without anyone deciding it should, the place the work was done, and the loft above the mill the place the finished copies went when they were done with; and the town, which had one word for the whole arrangement long before the Residency ever heard of it, called it simply an Seilear, the cellar.

Cailean was four-and-twenty when the copying began, a turf-cutter's son with a plain hand at his letters and no other prospects, when Seònag Bhàn's own eyes began to fail her past the point of close work, and the three households, needing a hand that could be trusted with rolls no stranger's eye was meant to see, sent for him.

He remembered afterward that he had gone to the cellar the first evening expecting to be asked whether he could write a fair hand, and had been asked instead whether he could hold his tongue. That he could do. They gave him a corner of the table, a good pen, and the roll of a village he had never heard of, and told him to copy it exactly as it stood, mistakes and all, for a copy that corrected its original was worth less than the paper it was written on.

He was slow, that first winter, and made the ink run twice, and Seònag Bhàn scolded him for it in a voice gone thin with age but no gentler for that. By the second winter he was as fast as any hand at the table, and had learned, the way every copyist learns without being told, that a roll of births and burials read a hundred times over stops being a list and becomes something closer to the shape of the place it comes from: which families were dying out, which taking in strangers, which custodian's hand was failing along with his eyes, which village had gone three years without a marriage worth the name.

## IV. Of the Count of the People

Two years before the copying began, word went round the east that the Administration had ordered a count made of every soul on the island, the first such count in the memory of anyone then living, and that its officers were riding out from Ros dhomhain with books and questions to every district in turn.

The count that came back, when the copies of it reached the cellar the following year among the Residency's other business that the custodians thought worth keeping a record of, was a strange document to read fair. For the humans of the coast towns and the concession farms it gave a number to the household, close as a tally of sheep: so many souls under this roof, so many under that. For the Dia-thìrich of the same district it gave a number rounded to the nearest hundred, and further inland, where the hill villages ran together in the Administration's own maps into districts with names its officers had half invented, rounded to the nearest thousand.

"They count us the way a man counts trees," said Fearchar mac Ailpein, whose household had taken in the count among the rest of that winter's copying, laying the sheet down on the cellar table. "Near enough for the timber. Not near enough for the names."

Cailean, copying beside him, thought of the roll he had just set down, the roll of a village of forty-one souls that the Administration's own count gave as a round hundred, and said nothing, because there was nothing in it worth saying that the sheet had not already said for itself.

The same officers, riding the west and the outer islands that same season, had set the whole of the far coasts, Eilean bhàn, and the wind-scoured shore below the great moss that no chain of theirs could cross, and the harbour town of Seann Vell on Eilean ruadh where their revenue boats lay, into a single district ruled from Seann Vell itself, for want, the custodians' letters said, of anywhere closer that the Administration cared to build an office. The cellar's copies of that letter noted, in a hand Cailean came to know as Fearchar's, that the surveyors had walked the edge of the moss, found no way across it, and left the rest of the district's map blank; and that the custodians of those coasts, being no readier to be counted by a stranger's chain than the moss itself, had let them.

## V. Of the Death of the Last Teacher

In an Lùnastal of the hundred and twenty-third year, word came down from Dùn thais that Seònaid nic Dhùghaill was dead, in her seventy-eighth year, and that the school she had kept was closed with her.

She had been, for longer than anyone at the cellar's table could remember hearing otherwise, the last master of the Deer to teach openly in that age: the last to sit under the trees at Dùn thais with pupils in a ring around her and speak the philosophy of the schools aloud, in the hearing of whoever wished to stop and listen, as the schools had always taught before the Residency and the Mission between them made open teaching a thing best done quietly or not at all. She had gone on doing it for years after the last hall was shut and the last public disputation forbidden, on the grounds, she was reported to have said, that a philosophy that could not survive a Commissioner's displeasure had never been worth the keeping in the first place.

Cailean had never heard her teach. He had only heard of her the way a young man in a cellar hears of most things, at second hand, through a roll that another man's pen had already made history of before it reached his table: her death entered in the Dùn thais custodian's hand, plain as any other death that year, and beneath it, in the same hand, a line that said the register of her school ended with her name and would not be continued, there being none left of her house or her pupils willing to keep it in the open.

"They'll take it up again," said Fearchar, "when the strangers are gone."

"When will that be," said Cailean, not really asking, and copied the line as it stood, for a copy that corrected its original was worth less than the paper it was written on, and the register's silence was as much a part of the record as anything ever written in it.

## VI. Of the Copies Gone Astray

It was in the spring of the hundred and twenty-fifth year that the cellar first learned there was a second Muileann chaol.

It stood some hours to the north along the same eastern hills, a smaller place by every account, its whole population not so many as one good village of the coast; but it shared the name letter for letter, and more than one rider coming down from the northern custodian houses with a chest of rolls for the cellar had, over the years, asked the road to the wrong town and been told it plainly enough by carters who saw no reason to ask why a stranger with a locked chest wanted either.

Fearchar found the first of the misdirected copies by chance, going through a bundle newly arrived and finding among it a receipt, in a northern custodian's hand, for a delivery the cellar had never made and a roll the cellar had never received. He took it round the table without a word and let each of the others read it in turn. Seònag Bhàn, past copying but not past thinking, said only that a mistake three years old was a mistake worth mending before it became four years old, and looked at Cailean.

He went north on foot in am Cèitean, with a letter of the three households' own seal and nothing else, for there was nothing to carry until he found what there was to carry. It took him the better part of a day to reach the smaller Muileann chaol, along a road that climbed twice as high as it needed to and came down again for no better reason than the shape of the hill, and he arrived footsore and in no mood to be careful of anyone's feelings, custodian or carter.

He need not have worried. The custodian who met him at the northern town's door, an old man with a squint and a temper that had gone soft with disuse, showed him into a room no larger than Seònag Bhàn's own cellar and produced, from a chest under a bench, every roll the cellar at the true Muileann chaol had been missing for the better part of three years, sorted by year and tied with a cord, dry, unburned, and kept, the old man said with something close to pride, every bit as well as they'd have been kept anywhere else, mistake or no mistake.

"You could have sent word," said Cailean, not unkindly, for the old man plainly meant it as a kindness done rather than a wrong admitted.

"To who?" said the custodian. "Nobody told us we had what wasn't ours. We only knew we had rolls, and a roll's a roll. You keep it dry and you keep it whole and you wait for someone to come and say it's theirs." He tied the last cord himself, with hands that shook a little at the work, and handed the bundle over. "Well. Someone's come."

Cailean carried the whole of it back south on his own shoulders in two days, sleeping the one night at a hill farm that took him in for a story of the road, and reached the cellar at Muileann chaol with his feet raw and the chest of copies unopened, exactly as he had received it, for a copyist who broke a seal that was not his to break was no copyist the three households wanted at their table. Fearchar broke it for him, that same evening, and the two of them and Seònag Bhàn went through three years of the north's own record by lamplight, matching what had been missing against what the cellar had pieced together from other hands in the meantime, and found, to their considerable relief, that nothing of any weight had been lost after all. It had only been kept in the wrong cellar for a while.

"It is a strange thing," said Fearchar, when the last roll was matched and put away, "to think how much of what we call lost is only misplaced, and how much of what we call found was never really gone."

Cailean, whose feet hurt too much that night for philosophy, said that he would rather it were all in one cellar to begin with, and went to bed.

## VII. Of the Change, and What the Years Would Not Do

Through all of this the copyists at the cellar table went on doing what copyists had always done with the thing that had no name.

It had been in the rolls, here and there, since before any of the three households were born to the work: a birth entered like any other birth, and beside it, in one hand or another, a mark, or a word set apart, that meant a custodian had noticed something in the child that he did not have a word to set down properly. Cailean had copied such lines himself, that first winter and every winter since, and had learned, as every hand at the table learned, that the custodians who wrote them wrote around the thing rather than at it, the way a man walks around standing water in the dark rather than say plainly that he cannot see the bottom of it. *The child does not grow as she should.* *He is much as he was at his naming, though four winters are past.* *She is called by the camp nighean an rìgh, though on what ground the roll does not say.* Cailean had heard the camps' own word for it too, from carters who had it from other carters, the kind of word boys use for a thing they are half afraid of and wholly certain will never touch them: Aisling-aois, they called the state of her, the age of Aisling, though nobody at the cellar's table had ever set that down in any hand that mattered.

It was, by the year of Fionnan's birth, thirty years and more since the first child had shown it and been written round in that fashion, and now there were two, and the custodians who had gone on writing round the thing for thirty years began, in ones and twos, to say in their letters to the cellar that a thing seen twice wanted a name a physician could enter in a column, whatever a teller might call it by the fire.

Cailean read a dozen such letters that winter and the next, in a dozen hands, each circling the same want from a different side, and none of them settling it. He asked Fearchar once, late at a table with the peat gone low, whether the three households ought to put a word of their own forward, since they had read more of the record than any custodian living. Fearchar said no, and said it hard enough that Cailean did not ask again. "We copy what is decided," he said. "We do not decide it. A cellar that starts deciding what the rolls should say stops being a cellar anyone can trust."

## VIII. Of the Call to Cill ghlas

The letter that settled it came to the cellar in am Faoilleach of the hundred and twenty-sixth year, from the custodian of Cill ghlas, a woman named Peigi Ghlas whose hand Cailean knew well from years of her own eastern roll, small and upright and given to underlining the word that mattered most in any sentence she wrote.

She had called, the letter said, every custodian within two days' walk of Cill ghlas to meet at the shrine there on the night of the coming full moon, at the mouth of the oldest cutting on the hill, where Brìde's lamp had burned since before any of them had a roll to keep; and she asked, further, that the cellar send a hand who could write a fair copy on the spot, since the custodians who would be there were, in her own words, more used to arguing than to agreeing, and a decision arrived at in the dark by candlelight and not written down clean before the company dispersed was a decision that would be argued over again within the year.

Seònag Bhàn looked at the letter a long while and then looked at Cailean, who was the youngest hand at the table and had the steadiest by then, and said that it was his to carry, if he was willing.

He was willing, though he did not say, walking west the following week with a folding writing-board strapped to his back and a stock of good paper wrapped against the weather, quite how much he minded the cold of a hill shrine in am Faoilleach, or how far Cill ghlas lay from anywhere a fire could be had along the way. He slept the one night at a custodian's house on the road and reached the shrine the next evening with the light already failing and the wind coming hard off the moor.

They came up the path in ones and twos through the last of the dusk, custodians he knew by their hands and had never before seen by their faces, cloaks steaming where the walk had warmed them and the cold struck through again the moment they stopped: an old man from Cnoc thais who had to be helped the last stretch of the climb; two sisters from villages on either side of the same burn who had walked separately and arrived within a minute of each other and seemed put out by it; Peigi Ghlas herself, last of all, small and upright as her hand, with a lantern that she set by the lamp at the cutting's mouth so that the two flames stood together, one old beyond telling and one carried up the hill in her own fist.

The oldest cutting still stood open in the hillside behind the shrine, black-mouthed, the timber at its lip gone soft and silvered with age, and all around it in the dark, under the wind, Cailean could hear the pumps of the Company's newer galleries lower down the slope, working on through the night the way they worked every night now, for the hill, since the order went out, was given no season of rest at all.

He set his board on his knee at the edge of the group, near enough to hear, far enough not to be in anyone's way, and dipped his pen, and waited.

## IX. Of the Naming

They sat on the benches of the shrine with their cloaks steaming and argued, as Peigi Ghlas had promised they would, for the better part of two hours before anything was decided at all.

Some wanted an old word, one out of the tellings, a word with weight already in it, so that whoever bore it would be seen for what the tellings said such children were. Peigi Ghlas herself spoke hardest against that. "An old word carries an old story with it, whether we mean it to or no," she said, "and the story it would carry is one we have not finished telling ourselves, let alone a child of five winters." Others wanted no word at all, arguing that a name is a door, and that a thing without a door could not be walked through by the Residency's clerks or the Mission's physicians or anyone else who might, given a word to hang a report on, hang more on it than the word was ever meant to carry. The man from Cnoc thais, who had said little on the climb up, said that a physician would write a word down whether the custodians gave him one or not, and that it was better the word came from a shrine than from a stranger's ledger.

It was full dark by the time the arguing narrowed. In the end it was one of the sisters from the burn villages, a woman Cailean never did learn the name of, who put forward the word that held: the plainest word there was, she said, fit for nothing grander than a physician's column, with no song in it and no story attached that any teller could make more of than the two words themselves already said. Fuil-ghuail. Coal-blood.

There was a silence after it long enough that Cailean, waiting with his pen above the paper, began to think it had failed, the way a proposal fails in a room grown tired of arguing, simply by not being taken up. Then Peigi Ghlas said that she, for one, could enter it in her own roll tomorrow without shame, and asked for a show of hands.

Cailean counted them by lamplight as they went up, dark shapes against the paler dark of the hillside, and there were more raised than not, though not every hand in the company went up, and nobody asked those that stayed down to account for themselves.

"Fuil-ghuail," said Peigi Ghlas, when the hands were down again, the way a custodian repeats a decided thing to fix it in the record. "Coal-blood, for the humans' books, if they must have it in their own tongue. Let it be entered so."

Nobody moved to argue it further. The pumps went on working somewhere below them in the dark, and the wind came round the mouth of the old cutting and made the two lamps gutter together and steady again, and one by one the custodians got up from the benches, stiff with the cold and the sitting, and began the walk back down to whatever fire waited for them at the bottom of the hill.

Cailean stayed a moment longer than the rest, because Peigi Ghlas had asked him to, and because the thing was not yet finished until it was written.

She dictated it to him there at the mouth of the cutting, by the two lamps, in the plain words a physician's register wanted and none other: that on that night, at that shrine, the custodians present had agreed a name for the change that had come twice now upon a child born of the pits, and that the name was fuil-ghuail, and that it was to be entered so in every roll that had cause to speak of it from that day forward, in the custodians' hand or, where the Residency's clerks required it, in the humans' tongue as coal-touched.

He wrote it in the fair, careful hand the cellar had taught him, the ink slow to dry in the cold, and read it back to her once before she would let him close the board. She listened with her eyes on the old lamp rather than on him, and when he had finished she said only that it would do, and that he had best get down off the hill before the paper froze in his hand and the pen with it.

He walked back to Muileann chaol over two days with the board strapped to his back exactly as he had carried it up, and gave the leaf, when he reached the cellar, to Fearchar to copy fair a second time for the loft, as every leaf of any weight was copied fair a second time before it was trusted to the years. Fearchar read it through once, in silence, and then read it again, and said nothing at all about the word itself, only that the hand was good and the ink had held despite the cold, which was, from Fearchar, as near to praise as a copyist's work ever got.

It was Cailean's own hand, in the end, that carried the word into the physician's column: a small, upright entry, no larger than any other line on the page, set down among the year's other births and burials and fines and fevers as though it were no more than any of them, though every copyist who came to that page afterward, in the cellar or the loft, would know exactly what it was, and would read it, whatever else the roll around it said, a little more slowly than the rest.
