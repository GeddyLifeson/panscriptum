# Of Raghnall Maor

*AE 36 – 41*

## I. Of the Barges Going Down

In the winter of the thirty-sixth year the coal of the hills began to go to sea by the river.

A' Chompanaidh's stage at Seann Toll stood finished below the workings, three miles down the valley from Muileann chrom, a loading-stage of timber with a crane on it, where the town's boats had been drawn up for as long as the town had stood. The barges loaded there. They went down the Abhainn uaine on the current, and at Ceann leathan, where the river comes out through the flats into the southern sea, A' Chompanaidh's ships lay at the mouth and took the coal aboard in baskets. From there the ships went out by Slighe-mhara Caol fhiadhaich, west along the south coast to the wild strait and the open sea, and round, the long way, north-about, for home.

The fishers of the south coast had worked that lane for five hundred years. On {{date:IV-0090a}} the custodians' roll of Ceann leathan enters that a coal ship coming out on the ebb ran down a fishing boat of the town off the river bar, and that the two men in her were taken out of the water alive by their neighbours, and the boat was not. The harbour-men said afterwards that she was too big to stop, and that likely nobody aboard her had seen the boat at all. Within a season the boats of Ceann leathan had learned to keep inshore of the ships' track and to lie to at the bar when a hull came down.

Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill walked down to Seann Toll the week the first barges loaded, to see it for himself. He stood on the new stage in the rain among A' Chompanaidh's men, and watched his own crews' coal go into the hold of a barge and the barge go off round the bend of the river with the current under her. The water of the Abhainn uaine had been green when he was a boy. It ran the colour of slate now.

He was forty-five that winter, and had led crews on the hill since the first ton was weighed. He had three crews under him and a leader's share on every ton they cut, a son grown tall, a wife who could reckon faster than the clerk, iron picks for every man he worked with, and lamp-oil to spare. As he walked home up the valley, a hewer he had known all his life stood aside for him on the stones of the ford, which had never happened before.

## II. Of the Staff

The agent sent for him in an Cèitean.

The agent of A' Chompanaidh at the pits of Muileann chrom in those years was Enoch Pratt, a narrow, bald, careful man who had come up from Ros dhomhain the year before the fall under the wild hill and had not been down again since. He kept the office of dressed stone at the pit-mouth, with a stove in the corner that burned the hill's coal day and night, and he used his Dia-thìris sparingly, as a man spends coin in a strange market.

This time Pratt came round the desk and shook Raghnall's hand, as the humans did with each other. Then he took up a thing that had been leaning against the stove, and held it out across his two palms.

It was a staff of ash, a little taller than a man's shoulder, straight and pale and new, with a cap of brass at the head and a ferrule of iron at the foot. On the brass were cut the letters of A' Chompanaidh, and under them a small ship with three masts.

"For you," said Pratt in Dia-thìris. For the rest he sent for the young man of Ros dhomhain who had put A' Chompanaidh's words into the island's at the first contract, and who was not young now.

A' Chompanaidh, the interpreter said, had watched the crews of Muileann chrom since the first ton. Raghnall's crews cut more than any on the hill, and other men listened to him, and its agents could not stand at every face. It wished to set one man of the island over all the crews of the hill of Muileann chrom: to see the work done, to keep the tally of the tubs, to put men on and put them off, and to answer for all of it to the agent. For this he would have a wage paid in marks, weekly, whatever the tubs came to; a house, to be built at A' Chompanaidh's charge; and this staff, so that any man on the hill might know him.

Raghnall looked at the staff for a long time. He did not take it.

"Over the crews," he said at last. "Over the hewers of the hill. Over my own people."

The interpreter put it into the humans' tongue. Pratt answered without any pause.

"Over the work," the interpreter said. "The agent says, over the work. The men are your people still."

Raghnall knew what the old men would say, and what his father would say, standing on the step and not coming in. He knew too what the hill had been these last years: the eleven under the fall, the boys coughing at the benches, the agent's men with their watches shouting at crews who had not the words to shout back. He thought that if there must be a man set over the crews it had better be a man who could say the words at the face and knew where the ground was bad. Then there was the wage in marks, every week, in rain or drought; and there was his son. Afterwards, when men asked him why, he gave each of these reasons at one time or another, and every one of them was true.

On {{date:IV-0091}} he put out his hands and took the staff, and the clerk wrote it down. Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill was forty-six years old.

It was lighter than it looked. He walked home with it down the mill street in the evening, holding it a little away from his body, as a man carries a thing he has been lent. The men on the bench outside the smithy stopped talking as he came by, and looked at the staff, and then at the ground. At his own door his son Dòmhnall was sitting on the step mending the strap of a coal-basket, and he looked up, and his hands stopped.

"What is it?" the boy said.

"It is A' Chompanaidh's," said Raghnall, and went in, and stood the staff in the corner by the hearth where the peat-spade stood, and it was taller than the spade.

Ciorstaidh asked him what the wage was, and he told her, and she did not reach for the beans to reckon it. She said it was a fair wage, and set the supper on the board.

Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill was sixteen that spring. He had broken coal at the benches from the autumn he turned six, the year of the dry cough, and lived, and at twelve had gone below into his father's crews. He was a long, rawboned lad with his grandfather's shoulders and his mother's quick eyes and a way of watching before he spoke. All through supper his eyes went to the staff in the corner with the firelight on its brass, and he did not ask about it again.

## III. Of the Slate Roof

The house went up that summer on a shelf of the hillside above the mill, where the ground had been common grazing, with A' Chompanaidh's masons from Ros dhomhain to build it and A' Chompanaidh's carts to bring the stone. It had two rooms below and a loft above, glass in the windows, and a roof of slate, the first in Muileann chrom, where the hill roofed with heather and rushes. When the roof was done it stood out above the mill from as far off as the ford, and threw back the light like the water of a loch.

On {{date:IV-0092}} the roll of Taigh na Fèithe enters, in the hand of Marsaili nic Sheumais, that Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill of Muileann chrom has taken A' Chompanaidh's staff and a house with a roof of slate, and is set over the crews of the hill. It is the last entry in that roll to give him his father's name alone.

It was the miller's wife who said the other name first, or so the hill told it. She was a sharp woman, and she had known Raghnall since he was a boy stealing her apples. The week the family moved into the new house she stood at the mill door and watched him go by up the road with the staff, and said to the women at the well, loud enough to carry, "There goes Raghnall Maor to his castle."

The women laughed. The name went down the hill before nightfall. By the end of the summer it was the only name the crews used.

The Dia-thìrich had called the strangers' governor at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir An t-Àrd-mhaor since the first of them came, the high steward. A maor was the lesser thing: a bailiff, a man who held another man's keys and gathered another man's rents. The name said whose keys Raghnall held.

He heard it before the summer was out. He was going along the heaps at the pit-mouth with the tally-board, counting tubs, and one of the boys at the benches said it behind him, not meaning him to hear. Raghnall turned round. The boy went white. Raghnall looked at him, and then went on counting, and did not turn round again; and that night he sat long at the fire in the slate-roofed house and listened to the rain on the roof. It made a harder, thinner sound than rain on thatch, and he found he did not like it.

## IV. Of the Number in the Roll

A' Chompanaidh did not stop at one.

Within the year there were overseers at Achadh dhomhain, where the new galleries were being opened into the hill, at Àth ìseal among the timber huts, at Cnoc fhiadhaich over the old fall, and at the pits along the valley towards Seann Toll; and at each of them a man of the island with an ash staff and a brass cap, answerable to the agent.

Marsaili nic Sheumais had entered Raghnall in the roll by name. She entered the second overseer by name, and the third, and the fourth. Then she laid down her pen, and closed the roll, and walked over the hill to Taigh na Fèithe to put it to the custodians of the vein.

What the custodians said to her is not in the roll. What the roll holds is the entry of {{date:IV-0093}}, in Marsaili's hand, which reads: "Over the crews of the hill, set by the strangers, overseers: seven." There are no names. The next year the number is nine, and the year after that it is twelve, and the roll goes on so, a number under the date, through all the years after. Asked why, she said that the roll was for the vein and for those who kept it.

Raghnall could not read, but he knew what was in the roll, as everyone on the hill knew, and one evening at Taigh na Fèithe, where he had gone with the tally for the custodians as crew-leaders had always done, he asked Marsaili to read him the line.

She read it. "Overseers: seven."

"And my name?"

She turned back the leaves to the summer before and found it and read it out: Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill of Muileann chrom, the staff, the slate roof. Then she turned the leaves forward again and closed the book.

"That is the last of it," she said. "There will be no other."

Raghnall stood a while in the doorway of Taigh na Fèithe with his staff. Then he said good night, and Marsaili said good night, and he walked home in the dark over the hill and did not light his lamp.

Raghnall was good at the work, and that was the worst of it. Knowing where the roof was rotten, and which roof had the sick colour in it that no human could see, he could tell the agent so, and sometimes the agent listened. That year there was no fall on the hill of Muileann chrom, and the tonnage went up, and Pratt wrote to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in praise of the first overseer of the island.

What he minded was the singing. The crews had always sung below, the old slow songs of the vein that go with the swing of a pick, and he had sung with them for twenty-five years. Now he would come along a gallery with his lamp and his staff and hear the singing ahead of him in the dark, and it would stop before he reached it, and the men would be working without a word, and would look up and say "Raghnall," civilly, and wait for him to go by. When he had gone by, after a while, the singing began again behind him.

His son was in one of those crews. Dòmhnall said nothing about it at home, and Raghnall did not ask.

## V. Of Seumas Dubh

Seumas Dubh had come into Raghnall's crews as a lad of fifteen, in the fourth year of the pit.

He had been born in the winter before the ships came down from the north, a black-haired hewer's son of the valley below Muileann chrom, strong and quick and given to laughing. When A' Chompanaidh opened its new pits at Cnoc mhòr, a morning's walk south of the hill over the moor, and asked Raghnall for a man to set over them, Raghnall gave Seumas's name; and Seumas took the staff and went south in the spring of the thirty-seventh year with a cart of tools and his wife and his two children, to a crew of the new men of Cnoc mhòr who had never worked a seam before.

The pits at Cnoc mhòr were driven down a slope, and the coal came up out of them in tubs on a haulage, a rope of hemp from the rope-walks of Dùn chrom running over a drum at the top that a horse turned. A' Chompanaidh wanted the tubs hurried. Maddox's orders were still to double the yield. Seumas hurried them. He ran the tubs up three at a time on the one rope, which the crews said it would not bear, and set the boys to run the empties down the slope so that the horse need not stop.

On {{date:IV-0094}} the rope parted with three full tubs on it. They came back down the slope and through the gallery-mouth, and a boy was coming up the rails with an empty and could not get out of the way. His name was Coinneach Beag and he was eleven. The custodians' roll of Cnoc mhòr has his name and his age, and the name of his mother; A' Chompanaidh's report of the day gives "a boy" and the cost of the rope.

What happened then happened below, in the dark, and the men who were there did not tell it the same way twice. Seumas came down the slope with his staff when he heard the tubs go, and went into the gallery-mouth, where the crew had the boy out from under the iron and were kneeling round him. Somebody said something. Seumas said something back. It is told that he said the boy should have jumped, and it is told that he said nothing of the kind and only asked whether the tubs were broken; and either way a hewer stood up with a pick-helve in his hand. When the agent's man came down the slope with a lantern there were two dead in the gallery-mouth, the boy and the overseer, and the crew standing about them, and nobody running. Seumas Dubh was thirty-eight years old.

Before that month was out A' Chompanaidh hanged two men at Ros dhomhain for the killing of him.

Maddox ordered the overseers down to see it. Every man of the island who held A' Chompanaidh's staff was to be at Ros dhomhain on the day and stand in front. Pratt told it to Raghnall in his office without looking at him, and then the interpreter told it again in Dia-thìris.

Raghnall went, thirty-five miles by the road in A' Chompanaidh's cart with the four other overseers of the hill, and none of them said much. They lay a night at the carters' house. Seònaid Bhàn served them and took their marks and said nothing, but the carters at the other end of the room drank up and went out.

They hanged them on the open ground between the lake shore and the rise where Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir stands, on a frame of new timber put up the day before, with soldiers of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach round it in a square. It was a still, bright day at the end of summer, and the lake behind the gallows lay flat as a board. A great crowd had come, humans and Dia-thìrich both, and they made almost no sound. The overseers were put in a row inside the square, in front of the crowd, with their staffs, where the crowd could see them. There were fourteen of them, from all the workings of the island. Raghnall stood at the end of the row nearest the gallows.

The two men were brought out of the guardhouse under the rise with their hands tied. One was a big man with a cut over his eye, and the other was young. The roll of Cnoc mhòr gives their names, Eòin Ruadh and Fionnlagh mac Aonghais, hewers of that town, and the day, and that they were carried home and buried in the town's ground on their neighbours' shoulders; the letter-book of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir gives "two of the crew." A clerk read the sentence in the humans' tongue from a paper, and nobody put it into Dia-thìris. A chaplain of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise said a prayer, which nobody put into Dia-thìris either.

As they were brought past the row of overseers the young one, Fionnlagh, turned his head and looked along them. He looked at every face, one after another, as though he were counting them, and when he came to the end of the row he looked at the staff with its brass cap in Raghnall's hand, and then at Raghnall. He did not say anything. Then they took him up.

Raghnall watched it. He had been ordered to, and he found afterwards that he could not have looked away if he had tried. He had carried the fever dead of Cuan shean and dug out the eleven, and had thought there was no way of dying left that he had not seen. This was slow. The crowd made one long sound when the young man's feet left the cart, and then was silent, and in the silence Raghnall could hear the water of the lake lapping at the shore stones behind the gallows, and a gull.

The roll of the custodians of Ros dhomhain enters that day as the first on which a man of the island was put to death by the strangers' law on the island's ground. It enters too, in a line of its own, that the overseers of A' Chompanaidh stood in front.

On the road home they lay again at Seònaid's house, and one of the overseers of the hill, a man of Àth ìseal, left his staff leaning against her wall when he went up to bed. In the morning it was gone. He told the agent he had lost it, and A' Chompanaidh gave him another.

Raghnall kept his. He carried it into the house above the mill and stood it in the corner by the hearth, and sat down at the fire with his hands on his knees, as his father had sat on the night of the first ton, and did not speak all evening. Ciorstaidh put his supper by him and took it away again cold. Dòmhnall, who was eighteen now, sat across the fire and watched him. At last the young man got up and made up the fire and went to bed. On the stair to the loft he stopped and said, without turning:

"The men at the face are saying that you gave Seumas Dubh his staff."

"I did," said Raghnall.

Dòmhnall waited, but there was no more, and he went up.

## VI. Of the Priest at the Gate

A few weeks after the hanging Maddox barred the priests of the hawk from the camps.

The order came down from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir on {{date:IV-0095}}, and the overseers read it at every pit-mouth on the hill. It said that the priests of Òrd an t-Seabhaig were not to come within the bounds of any working or any camp of A' Chompanaidh, for their preaching slowed the work and put the crews in fear. It said nothing of the chaplains. The chaplains of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise, kept their chapels of plank in every camp as before.

The hawk had many friends in the camps. Men who worked below found the old teaching of Mòd, An Seabhag Acrach, easier to believe than most things: that the land feeds whoever eats in measure, and the hawk starves whoever eats faster than the land can feed him. The hawk-priests came up from the west in ones and twos, with a hawk's feather bound in the hair behind the ear, and preached at the pit-mouths at the change of shift.

Several of the overseers were hawk-faithful themselves. The roll of Taigh na Fèithe says so, under the number, which is the only thing it ever says about them.

Raghnall was at the gate of the camp at Àth ìseal the week the order came, with Iomhar Bàn, the overseer of the huts there, a heavy grey man of the coast who had worn the hawk's feather behind his ear at every feast since he was a boy, and had taken it out when he took the staff. A priest came along the road from the west in the evening. He was old, with a bag on his back, and the crews coming off the shift began to gather at the gate.

Iomhar went out into the road to meet him. Raghnall stayed at the gate, and heard it.

"You cannot come in, father," said Iomhar. "It is A' Chompanaidh's order."

"I have not come for A' Chompanaidh," said the priest.

"The crews are A' Chompanaidh's while they are inside the fence. I will lose the staff, father. I have children."

The priest looked at him a while. Then he put down his bag in the road and sat on it. "Then the crews may come out," he said.

They came out. They came out through the gate past Raghnall and Iomhar, forty and fifty of them, black from the shift, and sat down in the road round the old man in the dusk, and he preached to them there, outside the fence, where the order did not run. When the sermon was done and the men were going back in, he went out into the road himself, and knelt in the mud, and the priest put his hand on his head.

Raghnall did not kneel. He had never been much given to the hawk. He stood at the gate and watched, and when Iomhar came back past him with mud to his knees neither of them said anything about it, then or after. Pratt heard of the sermon in the road and wrote to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir that the order had been obeyed, as it had.

## VII. Of the Spotted Pox

In the autumn of the thirty-ninth year a ship of A' Chompanaidh came up the Abhainn gheal into the lake and tied up at the quays of Ros dhomhain with sickness aboard, and on {{date:IV-0096}} the custodians of Ros dhomhain entered the first of their dead of A' Bhreac Bhallach, the spotted pox.

It did not stay on the quays. It went up Rathad na Mèinne as the coal came down, stage by stage, and in the last days of an Dàmhair it came into the camps.

Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill turned twenty that autumn. He was a hewer at the face in the gallery below Cnoc fhiadhaich, and his crew was the first on the hill to lose a man, a carter's son of Àth ìseal who had fetched a cask up from the ford. The fever came first, then the spots, which filled and broke. Some lived and were marked for life. More died, the children most, in the huts at Àth ìseal where the families lay six and seven to a room, and at Achadh dhomhain, which was the worst on the hill.

A' Chompanaidh did not stop the pits. The ships lay at Ceann leathan waiting for their loads. It was the overseers who went round the huts each morning with the agent's list to see which men were fit for the face, and stood in the doorways, and saw what was inside, and went on to the next. Dòmhnall went down to the face every day that winter with the men who were left, and in the evenings he carried with the others. They buried the pox dead in new ground below Achadh dhomhain, stony ground, dug by lamplight.

Twice that winter Dòmhnall saw his father at the burying ground with the staff in his hand, standing at the edge, where the agent's clerk stood with A' Chompanaidh's book. He did not go to him. Once, coming up from the pits with a hurdle, he found his father at the door of a hut at Àth ìseal where a whole family was down, a hewer and his wife and four children, with no one to fetch water; and Raghnall had put the staff down against the wall and was carrying the water in himself, two buckets at a time from the ford, with the brass of the staff shining by the door where anyone going by could see it. Dòmhnall stopped. His father looked up and saw him, and said, "Take the other buckets," and Dòmhnall took them.

Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise kept a count. The chaplain at Àth ìseal, a young man lately come over the sea, went among the huts with a vinegar cloth over his mouth and wrote down every dead man, woman and child he could find, in a fair hand; and his list went down to Ros dhomhain with the lists of the other chaplains, and the chaplains' count of the whole sickness was four hundred and six.

The custodians kept a count also. Marsaili nic Sheumais went round the hill in the spring with her book, from hearth to hearth and camp to camp, and wrote down the dead as the households gave them to her, the ones the chaplains had not found and the ones who had gone home to die among their own people. Then the custodians of the hill asked the chaplains for a copy of their list, and the chaplain gave it to them readily, for he was proud of it; and they wrote their own names in the margin of it, down the whole length of both sides, in the old letters, with the total at the foot. They sent it back to Ros dhomhain, where it was filed.

The pox went on south from the harbour through the winter and into the next year. On {{date:IV-0097}} it reached Baile chiar, a day's walk down the country from Ros dhomhain, and the villages about it. The carters brought the news up the road in the spring of the fortieth year, and with it the news of what the custodians of Baile chiar had done: they had set the town's masons to cut the names of every one of their dead into the stone lintel over the door of the council house, the whole length of it, so that no one might go in to the council without passing under them.

At Muileann chrom the pox burned out with the spring. In an Cèitean the pits were full again, with new men from the coast in the dead men's places.

## VIII. Of the Last Door at Doire mhòr

In the dead of the next winter news came up the road that Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had moved its customs house from Baile dhìreach to Ros dhomhain, on {{date:IV-0098}}, and that the harbour dues the little town had shared with the strangers since the fifth year would go now to the strangers alone. The mixed council there, left with half an income, cut itself from six to four. Seònaid Bhàn said at her table that it was the first time the strangers had taken back a thing they had shared.

In the spring of the forty-first year Beathag Dhubh died at Doire mhòr.

She was sixty-one years old. She had been custodian of Doire mhòr on the day A' Chompanaidh's agent climbed up with the first contract under his coat, and she had heard him out at her door and sent him down the hill again, and the road had gone round her fields. Then the stream had been dammed, and one household after another had gone down. By the fortieth year there were only a few hearths left lit on Doire mhòr, and the bed of the stream below her door was dry stones with nettles growing in it. She would not go down. The households that had gone sent meal up to her, and peat, and she took it.

The word came down to Muileann chrom on a wet morning of an Giblean. By noon the hill was walking up to Doire mhòr, many of them households who had lived on Doire mhòr and gone down; and the crews came up from the galleries with the black on them and did not stop to wash. Pratt did not stop them. He looked out of his window at the road and said something to his clerk, and the clerk did not write it down.

Dòmhnall went up with his crew. At the bend where A' Chompanaidh's cart-road curved wide round Beathag's fields, the people left it and went straight up over the old walls, the way the path had gone before the road was made, through grass unmown and wet to the thigh.

She was laid out in her own house on the board, in her dark gown, with coal of Doire mhòr's own cutting at her head and her feet, as the custodians of the hill have always been laid out. The house was too small for the people who came. They went in by the door and out by the back in a slow line, the whole of the afternoon, and each one as they went by her laid a hand on the board, or on the coal. Outside they stood in the wet among the empty houses, some hundreds of them, and waited.

Dòmhnall was in the line with his crew when he saw his father.

Raghnall had come up by himself, late. He did not have the staff. He had left it, Dòmhnall learned afterwards, leaning in the corner of the slate-roofed house, and had walked the eight miles in his old coat and his hewer's boots; and he stood now at the back of the crowd by the wall of the last empty cottage, bare-headed in the rain, where the people could see him and could choose not to. Nobody spoke to him. Nobody moved away from him either. He did not go into the line, and he did not come forward when they carried her out to the burying-ground under the oaks; but he stood where he was, at the back, until the last of the earth was in, and then he was gone down the hill before anyone else had turned for home.

The roll of Doire mhòr that Beathag Dhubh kept in her own hand ends a few leaves after the year the stream stopped, with the names of the households that went down. The last leaf of it is in another hand, and gives her name, her age and the day, and that the hill came up. Marsaili nic Sheumais entered the same in the roll of Taigh na Fèithe, and a line more: "The last door of Doire mhòr that did not open."

## IX. Of the Bound Book

Henry Maddox left Dia-thìr that summer, in the seventeenth year of his office, a rich man.

The hill saw his going only in the carts. For a week the carts went down Rathad na Mèinne loaded with his goods, and a clock taller than a man lashed upright in a cart of its own; and at the quays of Ros dhomhain it all went into the ship that had brought his successor. The carters who hauled it said that he had come out with one chest.

Arthur Lisle came to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in his place. He was forty-one years old, a lawyer, thin and clean-shaven, with a bad knee and a dry voice, and he came up the Abhainn gheal to the lake in the grey weather with two clerks and a chest of books and no letter in his pocket from anyone.

On {{date:IV-0099}} his first order went out from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in his own small hand. Every compact, charter, contract and agreement made between the strangers and the island since the leave was given was to be found, wherever it lay, and copied fair, and bound in one book.

A rider of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach came up the hill to Pratt's office in the week after with a satchel and a letter, and carried down to Ros dhomhain the contract of the first thirty and the paper of the leader's share with Raghnall's mark at the foot. He asked too for the agreement by which the staff had been given; and Pratt found that there was none, and the rider wrote that down.

Raghnall Maor carried his staff through all of it, at the heaps and the gallery-mouths and the huts, in the forty-first year of the strangers and the fifty-first of his own age, and the crews stopped singing when he came by. He had learned to walk a little faster through the galleries than he used to, so that they might begin again the sooner. Dòmhnall was twenty-one that summer, a hewer at the face in his father's district, and he took his turn with the others at the singing.

Down at Ros dhomhain, on the evening the rider came in, Lisle had the papers of the hill laid on the table in his room at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir with the rest, under the window that looks down across the gallows-ground to the lake. The clerks had carried up the old letter-books of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir from the cellar that week, Pell's and Maddox's, more than thirty years of them, and stacked them against the wall. The first of them had Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg copied into it, in Pell's hand, on its opening leaf.

Lisle sent the clerks away. Then he sat down, and lit the lamp, and drew the first letter-book towards him across the table, and opened it at the first leaf, and began to read.
