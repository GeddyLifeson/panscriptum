# Of the Door at Doire mhòr

*Part Two: Of the Hill Sold by the Ton*

*AE 12 – 16*

## I. Of the Shortest Roll

Of all the rolls of the hill villages that came down to the cellar at Muileann chaol, the roll of Doire mhòr for these years is the shortest, and the copyists have handled it more than any other. It is nine leaves stitched with linen thread, in a square black hand that presses so hard on the pen that the letters stand up from the back of the page like the marks of a nail. The hand is Beathag Dhubh's. She was custodian of Doire mhòr through the years of the first contracts, and she wrote the roll as she said everything, with as few words as would carry the thing and none to spare.

Beside it stands the second witness. In the letter-books of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, among the reports sent down from the hills to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in the fourteenth year of the strangers, there is a sheet in the humans' tongue from an agent of A' Chompanaidh, who climbed to Doire mhòr with a contract under his coat and came down again without it. It is the only place where her answer to him is set down. The telling that follows is made from the nine leaves and the one sheet, and from what the carters of the hill remembered.

Doire mhòr stood on the shoulder of the hills north of Muileann chrom, a morning's walk above the mill and the bend of the river. Its name was the great oak grove, and in those years the grove still stood: old oaks on the slope behind the houses, broad and low and crooked from the wind, with a stream coming down between them out of the heather and running past the doors to the fields. There were seven households, stone-built and turf-roofed, with their byres at their backs. They grew oats and barley in small walled fields along the stream, and in the season of the handful their young men went down to carry at the old cutting above Muileann chrom. The custodian's house stood highest of the seven. Its door faced down the hill, and from the step on a clear day you could see the smoke of the mill, and past it the green line of the Abhainn uaine going away between the hills towards the sea.

Beathag was some thirty-two years old in the year the quay was built below her. She had black hair, which gave her the byname, and black brows that met, and a way of standing with her arms folded while other people talked that made the talkers talk faster. She had been custodian since her mother's death, and she kept the roll and the count of the village's hearths, and went to the council at Cathair dhearg when it called the custodians of the hills. Children she had none, and said when asked that she had seven households, which was enough.

## II. Of Nails by Weight

In the summer of the twelfth year she walked down to Ceann fhada to buy salmon, and found A' Chompanaidh building on the shore.

Ceann fhada was a hamlet of river-fishers and pilots on a long spit where the Abhainn uaine ran slow and deep below the hills, a short hour's walk under Doire mhòr. The foreshore of the spit, where they drew up their boats and dried their nets and split their fish, was held by the hamlet in common, as a thing no one household could buy or be sold. Beathag came out of the trees on a hot morning and saw the spit covered with men.

There were masons from over the lake at the capital, and human overseers in shirt-sleeves, and carts of cut stone drawn up on the turf where the nets had hung. A wall was going out into the river on a bed of rubble, square-ended, straight as a rule. At its root, where the fish-shed had stood, a clerk sat at a table under a sailcloth awning with a book open before him and a steelyard hung from a tripod at his elbow, and before the table the men of Ceann fhada were standing in a line.

She stood at the back of the line and watched it for most of the morning. Each man came to the table in his turn, and a human with a measuring-chain paced out on the turf the strip of foreshore where that man's boat had always lain, and called the figure to the clerk. The clerk wrote it. Then a boy tipped nails out of a keg into the scoop of the steelyard, and the clerk ran the pea along the arm until it hung level, and the nails were poured into the man's cap or his shirt or his two hands, and he stepped aside. So many pounds of nails for so many paces of shore; and the next man came up.

An old pilot near the front had his nails poured into his hat and stood looking at them.

"What does a man do with these," he said, "who has no house to build?"

Nobody answered him. The old man carried his hat down to the water's edge and sat on a stone with it on his knees. Beathag went and sat beside him.

"They are good nails," he said after a time. "I bit one." He turned one over in his fingers. "My father's boat lay on that shore. And his father's. Now there is a wall on it, and I am paid for it by the pound." He looked at her sidelong. "You are Beathag of the oaks. Tell me, custodian, whose shore was it, that they could buy it off me?"

"It was the hamlet's," said Beathag.

"Then why did they pay me?"

"Because you were standing in the line," she said, "and the hamlet was not."

That evening the men of Ceann fhada took their nails to the fish-shed that had been moved up the bank, and weighed them again, each man's share, on their own old beam that they weighed salmon on, to see whether the strangers had cheated them. The strangers had not. Every man had his weight to the ounce. The copyists found it in the roll of Ceann fhada, in a pilot's hand: that the weight was true, and that the men were ashamed of having doubted it, and ashamed besides, though of what the roll does not say.

The quay was finished on {{date:IV-0041}}. Beathag was not there. But in the autumn she went down to the edge of the oaks on her own hill and watched the first carts of coal from the new pit at Muileann chrom come jolting along the river track and down onto the spit, and tip their loads into the first flat barge moored against the new wall. The barge went away downstream with two pilots of Ceann fhada at the sweeps, since nobody else knew the river.

The coal in the carts was all colours. She could see it from where she stood, even at that distance, the way anyone of the island could see it: the black that is green at the edge, and the black that holds a blue in it like the back of a mussel, and the rust and the plum and the grey that go purple in rain. The two carters walking at the horses' heads were humans, and they looked at the road.

## III. Of Three Words in a Grammar

Samuel Wren finished his grammar of the island's tongue at Ros dhomhain in {{year:IV-0042}}, in the last weeks of summer, and sent a fair copy to the council at Cathair dhearg with his compliments. He had been at it ten years, since the season of words at the capital. The council gave it to Gormshuil nic Fhearchair to read, because she had learned the humans' letters for that purpose, and she took it home and read it through, and at Samhain, when the custodians of the hills came down to the council for the winter sitting, she brought it into the hall under her arm.

Beathag had not met her before. She found a woman past fifty with a long grey face and ink on her thumb, who sat by the fire in the side room with the book on her knee and a candle at her shoulder, reading out whatever anyone asked for, and putting it into Dia-thìris as she went.

"It is a good book," Gormshuil said. "He has the verbs right. He has most of the words for the weather. He has all the words for the sea, because he came over it and was frightened of it." She turned a leaf. "He has one word for the coal, which is his own, and he has put it where ours should be. And he has this." She put her finger on a line. "This is what he calls us. You and me and every custodian of every cutting on the island."

"Read it," said Beathag.

Gormshuil read it out in the humans' tongue, slowly, three words, and then put it into Dia-thìris. It came out as *the keepers of the coal*.

"That is near enough," said a custodian from the east, and one or two others nodded.

"It is near," said Gormshuil. "Ask what a keeper is, in his tongue." She shut the book on her finger. "I asked him. I sat in his room at the mission-house with this on the table and asked him, what is a keeper, and he told me, a man who keeps a thing. So I asked him what kinds of keepers there are over the sea. A keeper of sheep. A keeper of a gate. A keeper of a shop, which is a man who has goods on his shelves until somebody pays him for them. An innkeeper. A storekeeper. He was pleased to be asked. He gave me eleven." She looked round at them. "In his tongue a keeper of the coal is a man who has the coal."

Nobody spoke. The fire settled.

"Then when they read this over the sea," said Beathag, "they will think the coal is ours to sell."

"Or they will think we are standing in the way of it," said Gormshuil, "which is what a storekeeper does, until he is paid."

Beathag went home to the hills in the snow before the sitting was over, and the copyists find the thing in her roll that winter in two lines. The first says that the strangers' book of words calls the custodians keepers. The second says: *We do not keep it. We keep it back.*

The grammar went out over the sea on the next ship, and copies of it were made at the mission-house for the strangers' own use on the island. In the summer of the thirteenth year, on {{date:IV-0043}}, a hawk-priest came down to Ros dhomhain from Cnoc bheag, a thin man in a brown cloak with a feather in his hair, and asked at the door of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise for a copy for the priests of his shrine, so that they might read what the strangers wrote of them. The mission-house gave him one, and was pleased to. He carried it home a day's walk on his back in a satchel of oiled hide.

That copy came down to the cellar among the hawk-priests' papers, and it has more notes in its margins than any other copy the copyists have seen. Where Wren writes of the gods the hawk-priests have written corrections, and where he writes of the hawk they have written a good deal. And beside the three words on the custodians, in a small hand of the old kind with a hawk's head drawn after it for a signature, somebody has written in Dia-thìris: *He writes as a man counts sacks.*

## IV. Of the Bell before the Fleet

The council called the custodians of the hills again at the dark of the thirteenth year, for the contracts were beginning to come up from Muileann chrom into the villages round about, and the council wished to know what the hills made of them. Beathag walked down to Cathair dhearg in the frost, two days on the road with a pony and a bag of oatcake, and sat through four days of talk in the hall. The council heard that iron was good and lamp-oil was good and the young men liked the look of the picks, and made no ruling; and on the last evening of the sitting word came up from the lake shore that the strangers' chapel at Ros dhomhain was finished, and that they would hang their bell in it and ring it in the morning for the first time.

Some of the custodians went down to see it, Beathag among them. Ros dhomhain was two hours' walk below the capital along the shore of the lake, and they went in the dark before dawn, because the strangers' service was to be at first light, and stood with the fishers on the cold stones of the quay on {{date:IV-0044}}.

The chapel was of timber, on the quay itself, with its back to the water: a long plain hall of new planks, tarred black, with windows of the strangers' glass down each side and a little open tower at the landward end. In the tower, hung on an oak beam, was the bell. It hung against the grey of the sky like an upturned cauldron. It was the first bell of iron that had ever hung on the island.

The fleet of Ros dhomhain was going out. The lake-fishers were loading their boats along the quay in the way they had always loaded them, with lanterns shuttered and no word spoken. The rule of that quay was an old one. What is said on the stones before the boats are out is heard on the water, and the water keeps it; so the men went down to their boats without a word and cast off without a word, and a boy who laughed aloud on the quay in the dark was sent home. Beathag stood among the silent men and was careful not to cough.

Then the strangers rang their bell.

It was a great noise. It went out over the black lake and came back off the far shore, and rolled back again, and went on ringing, twenty strokes, thirty; and every man on the quay stopped where he was, with a net or an oar or a basket of line in his hands, and stood with his head down as though he had been struck. One boat had cast off already. Its men lay on their oars in the dark water and did not row. Then an old man on the quay put down the net he was holding and walked home, and then another, and one by one the crews unloaded their boats again, not hurrying, still without a word, and went up the quay and away into the town. Only three boats went out on the lake that morning out of more than twenty. The bell rang on over the empty quay until the service began.

The custodians went up to the chapel door when the strangers came out of it. The minister was a young man with a cold, and Samuel Wren was with him, thin and grey and coughing into a cloth. Wren put the custodians' words into the strangers' tongue.

The custodian of Ros dhomhain did the speaking. He told them the rule of the quay, and that on that quay a bell over the water before the boats were out was the bell for the drowned, and that the men had gone home because they would not go out on a lake that had been told to expect them. He asked that the bell should not be rung again before the fleet was out.

The young minister listened to Wren with his head on one side, and looked at the empty quay, and was troubled; and it is to his credit, and Wren's, that he agreed at once. After that morning the bell of the chapel was rung at Ros dhomhain only when the last boat was clear of the harbour mouth, and the chapel held its first service late.

Beathag walked back up to the capital with the others. The sun was up by then, and the three boats that had gone out were small on the lake behind them.

"He was a decent young man," said one of the custodians. "He did what we asked."

"He did," said Beathag. "And the morning is gone all the same."

## V. Of the Paper at the Hearth

The contract came up to Doire mhòr at the end of the winter of the fourteenth year. A boy of Muileann chrom carried it up in oilcloth inside his shirt, and gave it to Beathag at her door. It was written in the humans' tongue, two pages of it, close, with a space at the foot for marks. Beathag could not read a word of it. The next morning she walked down to the mill at Muileann chrom with the paper and found the young man of Ros dhomhain who put A' Chompanaidh's words into the island's tongue, and made him read it to her twice in the mill-house, sitting on a sack.

It was the same paper the hill had heard read at the mill in the autumn of the eleventh year, save in the one thing that mattered to her. The place it named for the working was the south slope of Doire mhòr's own hill, under the oaks, where the stream came down.

"They have found the black there," the young man said. "The prospectors found it in the stream-bank four winters back. There is more of it under your fields, they say, than under all the rest of the hill together."

"I know where the black is," said Beathag. "My grandmother knew."

She took the paper home and put it on the shelf by the door, and did not put a mark on it, and did not send it back. On {{date:IV-0045}}, the day A' Chompanaidh had set for the marks, the boy came up the hill again for the answer, and she gave him none and sent him away with an oatcake. That was the first refusal of the age. Other villages of the hills had put their marks to the contracts, and one or two had haggled first; but none had let the day go by with the space at the foot of the paper empty. The clerks at the pit-mouth entered it in their book as a delay.

That same evening she called the seven households to her hearth.

They came in out of the rain and sat round the fire on stools and on the floor, with the children at the back on the heaped fleeces. She had the paper read to them in her own words, since there was nobody on the hill who could read it in the strangers' own. Then she let them talk.

They talked a long while. Some of the young men wanted the iron: a spade that did not bend, an axe that held its edge through a winter's wood. One of them said that the pit at Muileann chrom paid better than oats. One of the women said that the strangers had been fair at every counter on the island for ten years, which was true. An old shepherd asked where they would put their sheep when the hillside was a heap of shale, and nobody knew. Another asked what A' Chompanaidh would do if they said no, and nobody knew that either.

Last of all the oldest woman of the hill spoke from her stool by the door. She was near ninety and had carried baskets at the old cutting since before any of them were born, and she did not say much. She said:

"Chan ann leinn an cnoc."

*The hill is not ours.*

Then she said that the custodian should answer for the hill, since that was what a custodian was for, and she went home through the rain, and after a while the others followed her. The roll for the night has a single line: that the households were called, and that they gave the answer to the custodian to give.

## VI. Of the Door

The agent of A' Chompanaidh climbed to Doire mhòr himself on {{date:IV-0046}}.

It rained. It had rained on that hill for eleven days, and the agent came up through it from the mill on foot, because no horse would take the last of the path. He was a man of forty or so, well-fed, in a good dark coat that was mud to the knee by the oaks, with the contract buttoned inside it and a hand held flat over the place as he walked. The young man of Ros dhomhain came behind him with a satchel and a face that said he would rather be somewhere else.

By the time they reached the stream the doors were full of faces, and nobody came out.

Beathag was at the top of the village, at her own door, with the door open behind her and the fire lit inside. She had been told they were coming. Ruairidh of Doire mhòr stood behind her in the doorway, and said nothing.

The agent stopped at the foot of her step and took off his hat, and the rain ran off his hair. He was civil. The copyists allow him that; everything in his report is civil. He had a good deal of the island's tongue for a stranger, got at the counters and the pit-mouths, and what he did not have the young man of Ros dhomhain gave for him. He told her who he was, and whom he spoke for. He told her of the contract and the rate and the iron: picks and spades and bar-iron, lamp-oil by the cask, more than the hill had ever had. The young men at Muileann chrom, he said, had new boots. There would be a road up the hill, so that no one of Doire mhòr would ever have to carry a load on their back again. He told her the seam under her fields was the best A' Chompanaidh had found, and that it was a sin to leave it where it did nobody any good. He said it all in order, and then he said some of it again.

It is the custom of the island, and was in those years a custom older than any law, that a stranger who comes to a door in rain is asked in to the fire. Oighrig nic Leòid, who had no liking for the strangers, gave their captain water on the shingle at Cuan shean because he had come with an empty cask; the humans had lived on that custom for thirteen years. Beathag Dhubh let the agent stand on her step in the rain until he had said all he had come to say, and she did not ask him in.

When he had done she answered him. She spoke a few words, and then she shut the door.

What she said at the door, in her own tongue, the roll of Doire mhòr does not set down. It lives only in the agent's report, which he wrote that night at the pit-office by the mill, by the light of A' Chompanaidh's lamp-oil, in the strangers' tongue, and sent down to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir with the week's figures. The copyists give his words as they stand in the letter-books:

> The woman who is custodian there heard me through at her door, in the rain, and did not ask me in. Her answer I set down as near as I have it: that the hill was not hers to sell, nor mine to buy. Then she shut the door. I judge that nothing is to be had of that place by persuasion at present, and recommend that the working be opened elsewhere.

He went back down the hill with the contract still dry inside his coat. The young man of Ros dhomhain went with him. At the stream, the carters used to say, he stopped and turned round and looked back up at the shut door for a long time, and then went on.

## VII. Of the Road round the Fields

Doire mhòr was not punished. The hill talked of it all that summer: that the custodian of the oaks had shut her door on A' Chompanaidh, and nothing had come of it, and the boy of Muileann chrom did not come up again with any papers. Beathag, when she was asked why, said she did not know.

In the fifteenth year, on {{date:IV-0047}}, A' Chompanaidh opened its next working at Cnoc fhiadhaich, below Doire mhòr on the south, where a wild hill rose over the road to the river landing and nobody lived but a few households of hunters; and it began a cart-road to carry the coal of both its pits down to the quay at Ceann fhada.

The road came up from the south by the shortest way, and the shortest way lay over Doire mhòr's fields.

The surveyors came in am Màrt with a chain and a staff and a bundle of white-painted pegs, two humans and a boy, and walked straight up the hill from Cnoc fhiadhaich with the staff before them. They crossed the stream at the lower ford and went on across the barley ground, and every forty paces the boy knocked a peg into the earth. By evening there was a line of white pegs up the middle of the village's three best fields, from the stream to the edge of the oaks, and on over the shoulder of the hill towards the river.

Beathag walked the line of pegs the next morning with her arms folded. She did not pull them up. She sent to the custodian of Muileann chrom to say that the pegs were on the fields of Doire mhòr, which were not in any contract, and she waited.

Five days later the surveyors came back. They pulled up their white pegs one by one and put them back in the bundle, and they went down to the lower ford and started again; and this time the line went east along the foot of the fields, outside the field walls, round the whole of the village ground in a long bend, and joined the first line again on the far side of the oaks where the hill fell away towards the river. It was a mile longer that way. The carters complained of it from the first day to the last.

They built it that spring and summer with a crew of hill men paid in iron, bridging the stream with timber at the lower ford, and finished it at the end of the summer. The first carts came up it from Cnoc fhiadhaich in the autumn, heavy with coal, the horses leaning into the collar on the bend below the fields, and went on round the oaks and down to Ceann fhada and the barges.

Beathag let the carters water their horses at the stream where the road crossed it. They asked her leave the first time, in bad Dia-thìris, and she gave it, and they did not ask again, and she did not mind. In wet weather they ate their bread under the oaks before the last pull down to the river. The children of Doire mhòr went down to the road to watch the carts pass, and learned the strangers' words for horse and wheel and whip before they learned their letters.

In the autumn Beathag went down alone to the bend of the road below her fields and walked the length of it from one end to the other. The road was well made. It ran in a wide smooth curve round the foot of the field walls, never more than a stone's throw from them, and at every point along it she could see her own barley stubble over the wall, and beyond it the roofs of the seven houses, and above them the oaks. Nowhere did it go away from the village; the whole way round, it kept as close as it could go.

She stood at the far end, where the bend joined the straight road again, and looked back along it. It seemed to her a road made by people who had marked a thing and meant to come back for it. They had not gone another way. They had gone the same way, and left a space in the middle of it the shape of Doire mhòr, and made the space as small as they could.

## VIII. Of the Light at Tobar dhearg

*That same spring, far off on the southern cape, the first of the new beacons was lit; and since the keepers of the lights are the third witness of this age, the copyists give its beginning here, as the keepers tell it.*

Torcall Clachair and James Fenn had walked the five headlands of the old driftwood fires, and the first of the towers went up at Tobar dhearg, the village of the red well on the southern cape, over the stump of the old fire-tower the village had kept since An Aois Naomh. A' Chompanaidh paid for it, since its ships would use it most. Torcall built it. He was past fifty and stiff in the knees: a round tower of grey stone with a stair inside it going up in the thickness of the wall, and a room at the top with windows on the sea. Fenn set the lens: a great glass of the strangers' making, ground over the sea and carried up from Ros dhomhain on a cart packed in straw, which nobody in the village was allowed to touch.

It was lit on {{date:IV-0048}}. The village stood on the headland in the dusk and watched the light come up in the room at the top of the tower and go out over the water in a long beam, and the old people said it was a poor small thing beside the driftwood fires of their youth; and then a fisherman came back from the open sea after dark and said he had seen it three leagues out, and nobody said anything more against it.

The keeper was Anna nic Dhòmhnaill of Tobar dhearg, a fisherman's widow of twenty-five or so, whose husband had been lost off the cape two winters before. The village chose her. It held the light as a trust, the way it had held the old fire, and it chose the keeper from among its own families as it always had; and it chose Anna because she lived nearest the headland and had sat up nights for a boat that did not come home, and knew how long a night was. The council confirmed her in the spring of the next year. After that the keepership stayed in the families of the village.

She climbed the tower twice a night, once at dusk to light the lamp and once in the black middle of the night to trim it, and she went up and down that stair every night that she lived. She could not write. So she kept her count on the wall of the stair, at the turn below the lamp-room: one stroke cut into the stone with the point of a knife for every night the light was lit, and a stroke across every thirty, like a gate across a field. The copyists have not seen that wall. The keepers who came after her say that the strokes low down are cut deep and straight and the highest are shallow and crooked, and that there is not one night missing.

It was the keepers of Tobar dhearg who first said that a keeper has nothing to gain from a ship that is not there. They meant that the count is true. The light at Tobar dhearg was paid for by A' Chompanaidh so that its coal ships should come round the southern cape by night; but the tally on the stair counts nights, and not ships.

## IX. Of the Brig and the Bend

The word of the southern light came up to the hills with the carters in the summer, and they were pleased with it, since it meant that the barges could go out of the river mouth and round the cape without waiting for daylight. Beathag heard them say so at the stream, and understood what the light was for, and said nothing.

In the sixteenth year, on {{date:IV-0050}}, a brig of A' Chompanaidh coming down from the north with a cargo of iron tools for the pits was boarded off the north-west coast, in the empty waters beyond the capes, and stripped of everything in her hold. Her crew were put in their own boat and came ashore at Cuan shean, cold and whole. The raiders were never known. Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir wrote to the council that the custodians of the coast should have prevented it. The custodians of the coast wrote back that those waters had always kept their own law, and that the island had never asked the sea for leave.

The answer of the coast came up to the hills in the spring, copied by the council for every district and read out at the mill at Muileann chrom by the custodian of the district, and the hill men laughed at it for a week. Beathag heard it read. She did not laugh; but the copyists find that she went home and wrote it into the roll of Doire mhòr word for word, which is more room than she gave to anything else that year.

Then she went down to the bend.

It was a clear spring evening, the first dry one after a wet month, and the carts had stopped for the day, and the road lay empty round the foot of her fields in its long smooth curve, with the ruts of the winter filled with light. The barley was up green behind the walls. The stream ran loud at the ford. Up the hill the smoke of the seven houses went straight up into the still air, and the oaks stood black above it. Down the valley the smoke of two pits went up now instead of one.

She stood at the bend a long time. She had shut her door on A' Chompanaidh and A' Chompanaidh had gone round her; and in two years it had not come back. The hill thought that a custodian's word at a door still counted against paper, as it had counted against everything else since An Aois Naomh. Beathag stood on the new road and looked along the bend of it, and knew better.

She went up to her house in the dusk and took down the roll, and under the sixteenth year, after the brig and the answer of the coast, wrote the road into it: the road round the fields, made in the fifteenth year, a mile the longer for Doire mhòr, well built. Then she wrote two words more, pressing hard enough on the pen that they stand up from the back of the leaf, and they are the last words she wrote that year.

*For now.*
