# Of the Grey Goose and the Dockers

*AE 132 – 135*

## I. Of the New Àrd-mhaor

Edward Vane died at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir above Ros dhomhain late in an Lùnastal of the hundred and thirty-second year, in the bed he had been born in sixty-two years before, the only Àrd-mhaor Dia-thìr ever gave a birth as well as a death. He had spoken the tongue of the island badly to the end, in the flat clipped way of a man reciting a lesson he had never quite trusted himself to feel, and the custodians who came to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in the old form to acknowledge the change of stewards found, as they always did with Vane, that they had liked him rather more than they had meant to and could not now say precisely why.

Charles Merriman came out from an Tìr Thall within the season, and with him came thirty men in a grey coat nobody on the island had worn before, each with a rifle slung and a look of having crossed a great deal of water to be told, on landing, that there was nothing yet for them to do. The custodians who received the ship at Ros dhomhain asked what the soldiers were for, and were told, courteously, that they were constables under An t-Àrd-mhaor's own commission, sworn to keep the harbour and the roads, and that this made all the difference in law, whatever it looked like standing on a quay with a rifle slung.

"It is the first standing force Riaghaltas nan Coigreach has kept here," said Mòrag nic Iain Maor, who had ridden down from Muileann chrom to see the new Àrd-mhaor land, more from a wish to look at his face than from any hope of learning anything from it. "A hundred and twenty years A' Chompanaidh kept order with an agent's signature and a locked storehouse. Now it keeps it with rifles."

"A hundred and twenty years A' Chompanaidh had no cause to do otherwise," Merriman said. He was a broad, sandy man who did not raise his voice, which the custodians came in time to find more unsettling than a man who did. "I hope, madam, to give it none now either."

He built his first post within the month, at Muileann shean on the northern coal road: a low stone house with a yard for horses and a flag that flew from a pole cut, the townsfolk noted without much surprise, from timber A' Chompanaidh had felled and never paid the custodian for. They called it the humans' second Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, and did not mean it kindly, and the name outlasted every other name anyone tried to give the place. A second post went up that same autumn at Tobar ghorm, nearer the northern workings, where the custodian of the village was paid a rent for the ground it stood on and used the money, with a thoroughness that made A' Chompanaidh's agent write two letters of complaint to Ros dhomhain, to buy the village clear of every debt it owed A' Chompanaidh's own store.

Mòrag rode home from the landing with the picture of the thirty grey coats fixed in her mind the way a burn scar fixes, and told her crew-leaders only that the staff she held was the same staff her father had held, and that nothing about the water she had just seen them carry ashore changed what it was for.

At Tobar ghorm the constables who took up the new post found, in their first week, that the village gave them nothing to do at all: no theft reported, no quarrel brought for judging, no crowd gathered anywhere near the stone house with its yard and its flag. The custodian who had let the ground to them, an old man named Uilleam Glas who had buried two sons in the same gallery three years apart, told the sergeant set over the post, when the man asked him plainly why the village kept so quiet, that Tobar ghorm had learned long since to settle its own quarrels before any stranger with a badge could hear of them and offer to settle them differently. The sergeant wrote as much in his first month's report to Merriman, who read it twice and told his clerk that a quiet post was either a well-kept one or a lying one, and that he did not yet know Tobar ghorm well enough to say which.

## II. Of Hannah Webb

Hannah Webb had come to the hill country behind Baile chrom at nineteen, in the year Slighe a' Ghuail opened, following a brother two years younger who had heard there was steady field work to be had among the concession's tobacco slopes and steadier pay than either of them had known on the crossing. She was thirty-two now, broad through the shoulder from twelve years of stooping to the leaf, unmarried, with a face the sun had weathered past what her years alone would have done, and she kept, in the small room she rented from a tenant-farmer of A' Chompanaidh, a single object of any value: her mother's clasp-knife, brought over the sea and never once used for anything but cutting bread.

She had gone down to Ceann mhòr in the spring of that same year, on an errand for the farm that had nothing to do with what she came home carrying. The harbour under the north-western headland held perhaps forty houses of human fishing families, the first of their people to draw a living from the island's own sea rather than A' Chompanaidh's table, and among them an old woman named Ruth Pallant who mended nets on a bench outside her door and talked, without much needing to be asked, to whoever sat down beside her.

"You keep late hours, out on the field paths," Ruth said, when Hannah admitted, more from politeness than confession, that she often walked at night rather than sleep through the heat that hung on even after dark in the growing season.

"There's nothing to see by night but the dark."

"There's a deal to see by night, if you know to look for it." Ruth's needle did not stop. "My grandmother's people fished these waters before there was a Ceann mhòr to fish them from, and they kept a watch some nights, to hold the sea shut against whatever the dark lets loose on it if nobody watches. We learned it from the island folk, who've kept it longer than anyone can say, though Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise would have it we invented it out of our own fright at a strange coast. A grey goose flies over the watch, they say, and if you keep it faithfully and in silence, the goose carries the watching up, and nothing comes ashore that oughtn't."

"What do you say, keeping it?"

"Nothing at all. That's the whole of it. You keep still, and you keep quiet, and you let the goose do the saying." Ruth looked up from the net for the first time. "You've the look of a woman who doesn't sleep well. Try it, some clear night. It costs you nothing but the hours you weren't using anyhow."

Hannah kept the vigil for the first time in the last week of an t-Iuchar, alone, in the field behind the tenant-farmer's byre, sitting on an upturned crate with her knees drawn up and her mother's clasp-knife closed in her fist for no reason she could have given, watching the sky go from the last green of evening to a black so thick with stars she could not find, at first, the constellations Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's calendar named for her. She did not know, doing it, that she was keeping anything with a name; she knew only that the stillness asked for stopped, for the first time in twelve years, the endless small counting of her days that ran under everything else she did: the tally of what was owed and what was short and what her brother had said at breakfast that she ought to have answered better. Toward the middle of the night she saw, or thought she saw, low over the field, a long pale shape crossing east to west with its neck out straight before it, and she did not move, and it passed, and she went in afterward and slept the whole night through for the first time since she had come to the island.

She kept it again a week later, and then most weeks, telling no one, until a field hand named Colm Ashe, walking home late from a different farm, saw her sitting motionless in the dark by the byre two nights running and told the story at Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's rest-day meeting more from wonder than from malice. Within a month half the human labourers of the concession's edge had heard of it, and a handful had tried it themselves, and by the autumn there were nights when six or seven of them kept the field together, spaced apart and silent, each fixed on the same patch of sky.

## III. Of What Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise and the Priests Said of It

Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise at Cathair gheal took the matter up formally the next summer, when its minister, a careful and not unkind man named Obadiah Renn who had buried more of his flock to fever than he had married, asked Hannah to sit with him in the chapel's cold vestry and tell him plainly what she did and why.

"We keep the sky," Hannah said. "That's the whole of it. Nobody taught me a word to say. There isn't one."

"You call it a prayer."

"I don't call it anything. Others have started to."

Renn turned his pen over twice before he wrote anything. "The scripture knows the dove, and it knows the raven Noah sent out and the one that did not come back. It does not, so far as I have ever read it, know a goose." He looked at her not unkindly. "You have done nothing wrong that I can name. But I cannot find you in the book, and a minister who cannot find his flock in the book grows uneasy, whatever the flock itself feels keeping the field. So I mean to write to Cathair gheal, plainly, that the practice is irregular, and unaccountable, and very likely picked up, however it came to you, from the island folk's own customs."

"It was an old woman of Ceann mhòr taught me the way of it. She's no more island folk than I am."

"Then she had it from someone who was, once, however many years back, and the water it has passed through since does not change the spring." He wrote his letter that week, and it went, as such letters did, up the chain to Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's house at Cathair gheal and from there, in time, into a report that named the vigil irregular and its origin uncertain, and asked that no minister encourage it while it remained unaccountable to the scripture.

Baile chrom's own Dia-thìrich, when the thing came to their ears, for little stayed long unheard between the town and the fields about it, wanted no part of it either, though for reasons of their own. "We keep no watch with An Eaglais," said the custodian of Baile chrom's lower row, a stiff, careful woman who had buried enough of her own people in the hunger years to have no patience left for anyone else's borrowed grief. "If the field folk want a goose in their sky, let them keep it themselves and leave our name out of the keeping of it." The saying went round the town within the week, and the human labourers who had begun, hopefully, to call their watch a thing shared between the two peoples learned instead to call it, among themselves only, their own.

It was Manannan's priests at Ceann mhòr who, of everyone who heard of it that year, came closest to naming the thing rightly, and liked it least for having named it. An old priest of the harbour temple, hearing from Ruth Pallant herself what she had taught the field woman, walked the width of the concession to Baile chrom to see the vigil kept with his own eyes, stood at the field's edge through one whole silent night without once being asked or asking to join it, and told Hannah afterward, in the grey hour before dawn, that she had the form of the watching near enough right and its meaning hardly at all.

"The goose you keep you take for an Gèadh Fiadhaich, the bird of an Spiorad Naomh, your own, and your minister cannot find it in his book, and Baile chrom will have none of it. Your minister is half right. The watching under the goose is ours, out of our own harbours: the night prayer to keep the sea shut. Your fisher-wife's people learned it from mine, to keep the sea shut against what the sea lets in unwatched, and somewhere between her grandmother and you the sea got left out of it, and what's left is a good and a true thing, kept faithfully, that is nonetheless not the thing it started as. It has gone astray of us. I will not say it has gone wrong."

"Will you tell your own people to keep it with me, then?"

"I will not. It is not, any longer, entirely ours to keep, and I would not have my house's people standing silent in a field to a prayer that has wandered as far from its beginning as yours has. I have told Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's man at Cathair gheal, though, that it is not for him to stop it either, in words he did not much care to hear." He looked out at the field, empty now in the daylight, the crate Hannah sat on nothing more than an upturned crate. "Keep your watch, Hannah Webb. Whatever it is you are keeping, by now, is your own."

## IV. Of the Dockers of Ros dhomhain

Hannah's brother, whose name was Daniel, had left the tobacco fields for the quays at Ros dhomhain two years before, saying he had had enough of another man's leaf under his own nails and would rather break his back for wages he could see counted out to him at the week's end than for a crop A' Chompanaidh priced after the fact. He worked the coal berths, loading the steamships that had begun, since Slighe a' Ghuail opened, to carry more coal in a season than the sailing ships had carried in five, and he worked them beside Dia-thìrich dockers who had been loading the same berths since before he was born, men he had learned, over two years, to drink with and argue with and, on the one occasion a beam had come loose and nearly taken a Dia-thìreach docker's leg, to haul clear with his own hands fast enough that the man had thereafter called him, half in jest and half not, *a bhràthair*, brother, which was not a word given lightly on either side of that harbour.

The docker he had pulled clear was named Tòmas Ruadh, a broad quiet man twice Daniel's age who had worked the Ros dhomhain berths since the pit at Muileann chrom first sent coal down to them, and who took to bringing Daniel a share of whatever his wife had put up that week, a habit neither of them ever remarked on aloud. It was Tòmas who told him, on an evening in the spring of the hundred and thirty-fourth year, that A' Chompanaidh's agent had posted a new tariff on the loading-house door, a toll on every ton counted at the scale that had not been there the week before and that no docker of either people had been asked about.

"They'll say it pays for the new stone on the quay," Tòmas said. "It pays for the quay right enough. It does not pay us anything at all."

"Then don't load it."

"Easy said by a man who can be put on a ship home if it goes badly." Tòmas looked at him without heat, only the flat weighing look of a man deciding something. "But you're not wrong that it wants saying. We have never once, in all the years this harbour has stood, put down our hooks with the humans standing next to us instead of over us. It might be worth seeing what that looks like."

The berth charges went up that spring, a new toll on every ton loaded that A' Chompanaidh's agent said was owed the harbour works and the dockers said was owed nothing but A' Chompanaidh's own accounts, and the two peoples of the quay, who had never in the whole history of the port struck together over anything, put down their hooks on the same morning and did not pick them up again.

"You're mad to stand with them," a clerk of A' Chompanaidh told Daniel Webb on the second day, not unkindly, meaning it as a warning. "They'll strike you eleven days at Baile thais and forget you the day after. You're not one of them and never will be."

"I'm not striking for them," Daniel said. "I'm striking for the toll, same as they are. It happens we're both on the same side of it this year." He said the same, in rather fewer words, to Hannah when she walked down to see him on the fourth day of it, finding him thinner already and cheerful in a way she distrusted on sight, the way she had learned to distrust his cheerfulness their whole lives, since it generally meant he had decided on something before he had thought all the way round it.

The stoppage held nine days, the whole harbour idle, the coal piling on the quayside faster than any store could take it, before Merriman's constables came down from their new posts on the tenth morning and stood in a line the length of the quay, rifles slung and shown but never fired, enough of them that the dockers who had held nine days without a blow struck went back to their hooks within the hour rather than find out what the tenth day of standing against thirty grey coats might cost. A' Chompanaidh took the names of every human docker who had struck, and within the week a ship stood off Ros dhomhain to carry them over the sea. The passage was offered once, in a notice read to the men on the quay by A' Chompanaidh's agent himself, courteous and plainly worded and impossible to mistake for anything but what it was: it would not be offered twice, and no man there needed to be told what the ship's leaving without him might mean for the season's work he had left.

Hannah stood on the quay the morning the ship took on its passengers, in a crowd of families who had come to see off husbands and brothers and, in two cases, wives, and watched Daniel go up the gangway with a single sack over his shoulder and his back straight in the particular way she knew meant he would not turn to look at her again before the ship cleared the harbour mouth, because he had never in his life been able to say a proper goodbye and had settled, instead, on not making one. He did turn, once, at the rail, and lifted a hand, and she lifted hers, and that was the whole of it; the ship's whistle sounded, and it stood out past the headland with the morning tide, and she did not know, standing there, whether she would ever again in her life see the water this particular colour of grey without thinking of that sail going small against it.

The Dia-thìrich dockers of Ros dhomhain went back to their hooks that same week, having no ship offered them and nowhere else on the island to be sent. At Seann Vell, the neighbouring harbour, where no human had ever worked the berths, the dockers heard of the shipping and refused, on their own account and without a single human among them to strike beside, to load the coal that had been meant for the vessels the strike had held back. When the stoppage broke elsewhere they were not shipped over the sea, for there were none among them to ship; but neither were they hired again that season, nor easily the season after, and the harbour-master's rolls at Seann Vell show, for two years running, a quay standing half idle beside a coal-yard standing full, which no one at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir troubled to explain in writing, and everyone at Seann Vell understood without needing it explained.

## V. Of the Two Births

Hannah kept her watch through that winter and the next, in the field behind the byre, alone some nights and joined by two or three of the concession's own on others, the shape of it settled now into something Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise had stopped writing letters about and Baile chrom had stopped troubling to disown, since a thing left long enough alone in its own field becomes in the end too small a thing for anyone to keep minding. She wrote to Daniel twice that year, letters that took the better part of a season to cross and be answered, and learned, from the second answer, that he had found work on the docks of a great river port of an Tìr Thall, loading ships bound, as it happened, nowhere near Dia-thìr, a fact he set down without comment and she read with more feeling than she let herself write back.

That same spring, on {{date:IV-0265}}, a third child of the coal-blood was born, Lorccan, the first of them born in a mining town and not in a camp; and the custodians, who had written the word *fuil-ghuail* in their columns for eight years by then against two names only, knew from it that the thing had outgrown any one place. Within a year, on {{date:IV-0266}}, a fourth was born, Cian. Never before had two of the coal-blood come so near together. The custodians who wrote the two births wrote them as the rolls required, the name and the day and the word in its column, and nothing more, and reported neither to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir.

Hannah heard nothing of either birth, keeping her own watch in her own field far from both, and knew nothing of any such children at all. The custodians' rolls kept the four names, and Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's register, which asked for them, kept none.
