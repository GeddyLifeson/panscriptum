# Of Forty Words for the Colours of Coal

*AE 2 – 3*

## I. Of the Land Beyond

By the back end of that second winter Comhairle a' Chùraim had given leave to a people it had no name for, and the clerks of the roll were the first to feel the want of one.

In the forty days below the dunes the clerks had called the newcomers the men of the ships, and Harrow's men, and once, in a hand afterwards scored through, the wet men. That did well enough while they were a wonder on a beach. Leave to stay is another matter. It is given to people who come from somewhere, and the clerk who entered the leave on {{date:IV-0005}} left a gap after the words *the people of*, meaning to fill it when he knew what to put there. He never filled it.

The strangers had told their country's name to the custodians of Cuan shean many times over, slowly and loudly, as men talk to the deaf, and nobody on the island ever learned to say it so that its owners knew it for theirs. No scribe tried to write it down. What the island called the place in the end came up the valley road from the harbour with the carters, and it was the fishers' way of naming a thing they could not reach: *an Tìr Thall*, the land beyond. On {{date:IV-0007}} the clerk of the council wrote it into the roll for the first time, and after that he wrote nothing else, and neither did any clerk after him. The people of it the rolls called the humans, as if the island had never had any of its own, and so they are called in this telling.

Gormshuil nic Fhearchair read that entry in the fair copy the morning after it was made. She sat on the council for Clann Fhearchair, as her father had before her, and she was in her forty-second year that winter, a tall woman with a long plain face and grey coming in at her temples, who wrote a better hand than any clerk in the hall and was known for it. When the leave was put she had given her voice for it, thinking of the cask Harrow had held out on the shingle at Cuan shean, empty and turned upside down, and of what it must be to be so far from home that you have to ask a stranger for water with your hands. Beyond that she had not thought much. Few had.

The news that came up from Cuan shean in those weeks was mostly of small things: sails mended on the strand, fish bought with buttons, singing on the ships in the evenings that carried over the water and set the dogs howling. On the day they kept for their god, which came round every seventh morning whatever the moon was doing, the strangers did no work at all, and when the rain came on they asked the town, by signs, for a roof to pray under.

A sailmaker of Cuan shean lent them his loft. The custodians of the town set down what they saw there, and theirs is the first entry on the island to tell of the humans at prayer under a roof. The strangers knelt on the sailcloth among the bolts of canvas, the captain with the rest. One of their priests stood before them with a book. They lit no fire and burned nothing. They sang, and the priest read, and at the end they all said a word together that sounded like a door shutting. Two of Manannan's priests of the harbour went and stood on the loft stair to watch, the roll says, and came down again without a word, and went to the water's edge to say their own prayer at the turn of the tide.

The strangers had one god, and no other, and they called him by a name the island heard as *Crìosd*. Their faith they called by a word their priests used of the whole company of their believers, and the Dia-thìrich made of it *an Eaglais*. Those two words went into the roll that season, the first of the humans' tongue that the island took and kept.

It was in the month after, when the carts were back on the roads and the rain had turned soft, that Tormod mac Ailein sent for her.

Ceann a' Chùraim was a heavy, slow man with a beard that had gone white before his hair, and he kept a room at the top of the council hall with a window on the lake. On the table before him lay a letter in the humans' hand, which he could not read, and one from the custodians of Cuan shean, which he had read twice.

"They want words," he said. "Their priests do. They cannot teach their god to people who cannot understand them, nor trade with them either, and they would like to do both. They have asked Cuan shean for somebody to sit with them at a table and trade words as a man trades fish, one for one, until each side has enough to buy bread with."

"And Cuan shean sends to us."

"Cuan shean has sent to us for everything since the first ship," said Tormod. "Oighrig nic Leòid is still in her bed. The priests will come up to the capital, and the council wants a custodian to sit across the table from them and keep our side of the list."

"The council wants a clerk."

"The council wants somebody who will not give away more than she is paid for." He moved one hand. "You have the best hand in the hall. You have more patience than I have. And you are not afraid of them."

"I have never been near them."

"That is what I mean," said Tormod mac Ailein.

So the council gave the keeping of the word-list to Gormshuil nic Fhearchair, and the roll enters it in a line, between a dispute over a mill-lade and the price of lamp-oil for the temple. She went home that evening and told her brother's daughter Muireall, who had been copying the roll for her since the autumn and had ink worked into the skin of her wrists that no washing would lift. Muireall was thirteen. She wanted to know at once whether she might come to the table, and whether the strangers really had hair on their faces as the carters said.

"You may come to the table," said Gormshuil. "You will sit at the end of it and make the fair copy, and you will not speak unless I tell you."

## II. Of Cup and Bread

They came up from Cuan shean at the beginning of the seed-time moon, which the old folk call Sìolach: two priests and a boy, on three ponies of the town, with a custodian's man walking at the head to show them the road. It is a long day's ride from the harbour to Cathair dhearg, and the priests were no horsemen. They came over the bridge at dusk soaked through, the older of the two sitting his pony like a sack of meal, and the younger coughing.

The younger was Samuel Wren. He was a thin man of about thirty, with long hands and a narrow face and a courteous way of bending his head when he was spoken to, as if to bring his ear nearer the words. The damp of the lake country did not agree with him. He coughed that first night in the house the council had lent them below the temple, and through most of the spring after, a dry catching cough that he would turn away from the table to finish, with a hand up, as if apologising to the room. The older priest was a broad red-faced man with the sea still in his walk, who slept a great deal.

The council gave them a long room on the east side of the hall, with a table under a window of thin-scraped horn, a shutter that could be taken down on fine days, and a peat fire at the far end. On the first morning Gormshuil was there before them. She had set her pens and her ink on her side of the table, and a stack of the grey rag paper they made at Muileann chaol, and Muireall sat at the end with the same.

Wren came in and bowed to her from the door. She inclined her head. He sat down opposite and took out of a leather case a quire of paper so white that Muireall gasped aloud and was frowned at; it was more paper than the council used in a moon. Then he looked across at Gormshuil, and she looked at him, and for a long while nobody said anything at all, because there was nothing either of them knew how to say.

In the end he picked up the cup at his elbow, which had water in it, and held it up between them, and said a word.

She dipped her pen and wrote the word in the stag-letters of the island, as near to the sound as the letters would go. Then she reached for the loaf that the hall's kitchen had sent up, and broke a piece off, and held it up in her turn.

"*Aran*," she said.

He wrote it in his own letters, slowly, with his lips moving, and said it back to her. He said it badly. She said it again. He said it better. And so it went, all through that morning and the mornings after: cup and bread, and water, and table, and hand, and knife, and window, and door; the one holding up a thing and giving its name, the other writing and saying it back; and the older priest asleep in his chair by the fire with his chin on his chest, and Muireall at the end of the table, copying every word fair, in both hands, as best she could manage the strangers' letters by the shape of them.

The things that could be held up were the easy part.

For three days Wren had her word for *finger* written down as the word for *that*, because she had pointed with her finger when she said it, and nobody could find the mistake until Muireall, copying, saw the same word against two things that were not the same. A whole morning went on *go* and *come*, with Wren walking to the door and back a dozen times while Gormshuil watched him. He pointed at the rain running off the sill and she said *uisce*, and he wrote it; then he pointed at the jug and she said *uisce* again, and he looked from one to the other, and laughed out loud, the first time she had heard him laugh, and wrote something long in the margin. Afterwards she learned that his people had one word for the water in the jug and another for the water that falls from the sky, and could not think how a people managed with only the one. She could not think how a people could need two.

He learned quickly. She learned more quickly than he did, though she was careful not to let him see it. By the end of Sìolach he could ask *Dè seo?*, what is this, with his head on one side, and she could ask him the same in his own tongue, and the table went faster.

There was one morning in that spring that the copyists set down because Wren's list still keeps what came of it. He had drawn a great wheel cut into twelve like a cake, with a word in each piece, and he tapped the pieces one after another and pointed out of the window at the sky. It took her most of the morning to understand that these were the months of his year, fixed to the sun, and that he wished to know what the island called them.

The island had no months of that kind. It had the moons. She drew them for him: twelve moons of thirty nights and twenty-nine in turn, each begun on the night of the full and turning at the dark, from Samhnas at the year's beginning, through Dubhlas and Faolach and Gearras, through the seed-time and the gap and the bright fire, round to the stags' moon at the year's end. He set his months against her moons one by one, as near as they would go, and wrote the moon's name beside each piece of his wheel. It went well enough until she drew the thirteenth.

She told him its name, *Eadrach*, the between-moon, and showed him with her hands how the keepers of the reckoning put it in at the year's end every thirtieth moon or so, so that Samhain should not come round before the summer was over. He understood at last what it was for, and could find nowhere on his wheel to put it. In the end he wrote it in the margin outside the wheel altogether, with a mark after it that Muireall copied without knowing what it meant, and which Gormshuil learned afterwards was the humans' sign for a question.

One morning in seven he did not come. The first time, Gormshuil thought him ill and sent Muireall down with a crock of broth, and the girl came back with the broth and said the priests were kneeling on the floor of the front room with the shutters closed, and the boy had shut the door on her. After that Gormshuil counted the mornings on her fingers. The island reckoned its time by nights, from dusk to dusk; the humans counted by days, and by these sevens of days, and nobody on the island had ever divided time by seven for any reason at all. She wrote the seven into the list with their names as he gave them, and beside each the thing she knew of it, which for six of them was nothing.

The peat fire at the end of the room smoked when the wind was in the east. On the worst of those mornings Wren's cough came on so hard that he had to get up from the table and go to the window, and Gormshuil had Muireall bring a brazier of the fire's embers up to his end of the table. He held his long hands over it. Then he pointed at the embers and raised his eyebrows.

"*Mòine*," said Gormshuil.

His face lit up. He said his own word for it and made a digging motion with both hands; then he made the motion again, deeper, and said a different word, and pointed away east over the lake towards his own country. This we have too, he meant, tapping the brazier; and this other, which we burn at home, the stone that is dug from under the ground. It was black, he showed her, holding up the ink-pot, and it burned hot and long, and his people burned it in every house.

Gormshuil wrote the word down as he said it, and beside it, in the Dia-thìris column, she wrote what he had told her: *the black stone the humans dig in their own country and burn on their hearths*. It was one line among two hundred, and she thought nothing of it.

By the dark of the year there were some hundreds of words on each side of the table. On {{date:IV-0009}} the council's clerk came in with his own ink to enter in the roll how far the work had come, and found both lists lying side by side, Wren's in his white quire and Gormshuil's on the grey paper of Muileann chaol, and counted the leaves of each, and wrote that they were near enough even. The roll puts it no more warmly than that. For a season's work between two people who had begun it with a cup and a loaf, it was a good deal.

## III. Of the Basket of Coal

It was in the wolf-moon, when the island's hearths burn their hottest and the high passes are shut, that she did the thing the age remembers her for.

She had been thinking of it since before the dark of the year. Some hundreds of words lay between them now: cup and bread, rain and knife, a boat and its sail and every line that works a sail, and the moons and his months. They had peat, and the black stone of his own country. And in all those moons of pointing and naming, Wren had never once pointed at the coal of the vein, nor asked what it was called, nor seemed to know there was anything there to ask about.

He could not have lived three months in the capital and missed it. The shrine at the temple door kept the measured handful burning under its hood day and night, as Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig allowed, and the carriers came down from Taigh na Fèithe with their sealed baskets on the white road, and every child in the town knew to stand aside for them. He had watched them go by, she knew, because she had watched him watching. He had never asked.

So on a still morning of thin winter sun she went up to the hall early, before the priests were out of bed, and had the shutter taken down from the window so that the light came in clear. She took from the council's store the basket that the carriers had brought down for the shrines of the capital, broken fresh from the vein that autumn, and she chose from it with her own hands a double handful, the best-broken pieces she could find. It broke no law. A custodian may carry the coal and hold it and show it; it is the burning that is measured. She set the basket on the table under the window and sat down behind it and waited.

Wren came in coughing, with the older priest behind him and the boy with the ink. He bowed. She inclined her head. Then she stood up, took the basket in both hands, and tipped it out on the table where the sun lay, and the coal ran out across the boards and came to rest in the light.

It was black as a wet crow, and bright all over with a sheen like oil on water. Where the pieces had split, the colours lay along the edges.

Gormshuil began to name them.

There was the green that lies under the black like deep water under weed, and she laid her finger on it and said *uaine*, and then the word for that green and no other, which is not the green of grass nor of the sea. There was the red at a fresh break that deepens if you breathe on it, and she breathed on it and showed him, and gave its word. She gave the grey-violet of the old cuttings, that no dyer has ever matched; and the brown that shows the piece came from near the surface; and the blue that runs along a split edge like the light on a mackerel's back. She gave a colour like the heather in Lùnastal, and one like the dark of a blackberry, and one like rust on a nail left out in the rain. There was the colour of an ember just before it goes grey, and there was the colour of the ember after. There was a hair-line of something like gold that ran through one piece and out the other side, and a sheen on another like a pigeon's neck, which changes as you turn it; and she turned it in the sun and gave him the two words for the two colours it showed, and then the third word, for the piece that shows both.

She laid her finger on each piece as she came to it and gave each colour its own word, plainly and without hurry, as any child of the hills could have done, and as her mother had done for her with a double handful on the kitchen table when she was four years old. Muireall at the end of the table wrote them as fast as her pen would go. Wren wrote too, at first. He wrote the green, and the red at the break, and the grey-violet, and several more, and said them back to her with his head bent in the way he had.

She gave forty. There were more. She stopped because Wren had stopped writing.

He had put down his pen. He reached out and picked up one of the pieces, a large one with the green deep in it and the red at one end, and held it up to the window and turned it in the light, with his eyes narrowed, the way a man looks at a coin he suspects. Then he looked at her across the table. There was nothing unkind in the look at all. It was puzzled, and gentle, and a little sorry for her, as a grown man is sorry for a child who has told him a long story about the faces in the fire.

He laid the piece down with the rest and said one word. Then he took up his pen and wrote one word, and tapped the whole heap with the end of the pen, lightly, once, to show her that his word stood for all of it.

It took her a breath or two to know the word. When she did she sat without moving.

It was the word he had given her in the spring over the brazier, with the digging motion and the finger pointing east. It was the black stone of his own country, that his people dug out of the ground and burned in every house.

The sun had moved a little along the table. She could hear the older priest breathing by the fire, and a cart going down the hill outside, and Muireall's pen, which had stopped. She waited for him to ask for her word. There is an old courtesy on the island, when one has given the name of a thing and had the other's name back: *Agus thu fhèin?* And yours? It was in her mouth to say, if he would only ask the question to which it was the answer. She waited for him to ask what the island called the whole heap, the one word for all of it, since he had given her his.

He smiled at her. He moved the coal aside with the flat of his hand, gently, to make room on the table, and he reached behind him to the shelf and took down a candle, and held it up.

"*Coinneal*," said Gormshuil nic Fhearchair, after a moment.

He wrote it down. The morning went on.

When the priests had gone at noon she gathered the coal up a piece at a time and carried the basket down to the store herself, and saw it counted back in against the carriers' tally, not a crumb short; and it went up the hill to the shrine at the temple door with the rest of the capital's measure, and burned there under the hood in its turn, as it was meant to.

Muireall walked home with her that evening and did not say anything until they were over the bridge.

"He could not see them," she said then.

"No."

"Not any of them. Not the green. I watched his face. He looked and looked."

"He looked," said Gormshuil.

"Then how do they know what it is?" said Muireall. "When they burn it. How do they know what they are burning?"

Her aunt had no answer to give her, and gave none.

## IV. Of the Note under the First Leaf

The list was bound in the gap-moon, when the stores run low and the first green has not yet come. Gormshuil had kept it through the year on loose sheets in a box, as the words came, and now that the season of words was plainly done she gave the sheets to Muireall to copy fair into a book for the keeping of Clann Fhearchair, and to be copied again after that for whoever else might want it.

She gave one order about the copying. The colours were to go first.

Muireall looked at her. The sheets began with cup and bread, as the work had. That was the order of the table, and it was the order the council's clerk had counted.

"The colours first," said her aunt. "Before the cup. Put them on the first leaf by themselves, and leave room at the foot."

So the first leaf of the fair copy holds forty words for the colours of broken coal, in Muireall's hand, which was young and careful and larger than it later grew, and nothing else on it but the room at the foot. The copyists of this cellar know that leaf well. It has been handled more than any other leaf in the loft where it lies, and the corners have gone soft as cloth.

The list had two columns, the island's word on the left and Wren's on the right. For the forty colours Muireall came to the right-hand column and stopped with her pen in the air. There was one word she might have written in it forty times over. There was no other.

She went to her aunt with the leaf and asked her what she ought to put.

"Put what happened," said Gormshuil.

"All of it?"

"As much as goes at the foot of the leaf."

Muireall took it back to the table by the window and sat over it most of an afternoon. What she wrote at the foot of the leaf, on {{date:IV-0010}}, is short, and it is in the girl's hand and the girl's words, and it runs like this. The humans have one word for the coal of the vein. It is their word for the black fuel they burn on the hearths of their own country. No Dia-thìris word, in either list, answers to it. And they did not ask.

She brought it to her aunt, and stood while it was read.

"Should I have written that he was kind?" she said. "He was. He was kind about it."

"You have written what happened," said Gormshuil. "Leave it."

It was about that time, perhaps the same week, that Gormshuil went across the table to Wren with a request of her own.

She had been turning it over since the morning of the basket. He would not learn her colours, and she had given up waiting for the question; the word he had written for the heap was all his eyes could give him, and no patience across a table would put another in its place. Then she would learn his letters. There would be more of these people, and more paper, a great deal more paper, she thought; and a custodian who could not read what was written about the island in its own capital would be a poor sort of custodian.

She put her finger on a line of his writing, and then on her own breast, and then made the motion of a pen.

Wren understood her before she had finished, and was delighted. Since his god's book was written in those letters, he had been looking for a year for somebody on the island who would learn them, and here was the council's own custodian asking. From his white quire he took a clean sheet there and then, and wrote across the top of it the letters of his tongue, all of them, in their order, large and plain; and under them he wrote them small; and then, underneath, as a gift, he wrote her name the way he heard it.

She looked at it a long time. It was a long name in his letters, with more of them in it than it had sounds, and it looked to her like somebody else's. She let it stand. She copied it out underneath, stroke by stroke, the first thing she ever wrote in the humans' letters, and he clapped his long hands together, and the older priest woke up and asked what had happened.

After that there were two lessons at the table. In the mornings they went on with words, more slowly now, for the easy ones were done; in the afternoons Wren taught her his letters, and she learned them as she did everything, without fuss and faster than he expected. By the end of that spring she could spell out a line of his list with her finger under it, and she did.

She found the page of the coal. It was in his white quire with the rest, and she read it through with Muireall at her shoulder. He had written the green at the top of the page, as near to her word as his letters would go, and the red at the break under it, and the grey-violet, and a few more down the page in his careful hand. Then there was a line drawn under them. Under the line was his one word, and a stroke of the pen that joined it to all the others above it, and then nothing more on that page at all.

## V. Of the Chest for Muileann chaol

The fair trade began that summer, at the moon of the bright fire, and for the first time Gormshuil saw from her own window what the season of words had been for.

The strangers' goods came up from Cuan shean on the carts, iron knives and pots, window glass packed in straw, lamp-oil and white paper; wool and hides and salt fish and dyed cloth went down the other way; and in the market below the hill men of both peoples stood over the same bales and haggled with the words that had come out of the long room on the east side of the hall, and got them wrong, and laughed more often than they quarrelled. Gormshuil went down one morning on purpose, and stood among the stalls, and watched a hill shepherd give a fleece for a knife, and the stranger who took the fleece weigh it in his hands and nod, and the shepherd test the knife's edge on his thumbnail and nod. She could find nothing unfair in it anywhere. She looked.

The council entered the first of those bargains in its roll, fleece for knife, cloth for glass, with both prices in the island's reckoning, so that anyone who came after could see that the strangers had given good measure. It was good measure. The copyists set that down plainly, since much of what they must set down later is otherwise. The island had been alone for eighteen centuries and lacked a great deal that the strangers had, and it was glad of what it got.

Wren bought wool too. Gormshuil's sister knitted him a pair of stockings from the fleece of the hill ewes, grey and thick, for the damp; and he gave Gormshuil in return a quire of his white paper and a steel pen, the first she had held, which never needed cutting. She wrote with it for twenty years.

It was the paper that decided her.

Through that spring she had watched him use it as if there were no end to it. A word he did not like cost a fresh sheet. A page spoiled with a blot was crumpled in his long hands and, when the brazier was lit, dropped on the embers to burn. Paper came in his ship's hold by the bale, and he meant nothing by it, any more than a baker means something when he throws a burnt loaf to the gulls. But she saw him do it, again and again, and she thought of the fair copy of her list, and of Clann Fhearchair's book-chest, which stood against the wall of a timber house in a town full of timber houses, and of how the strangers' ships had come down out of a sea that had been shut for eighteen centuries, and what else might come down out of it now that it was open.

There was one place on the island where nothing was burned.

Muileann chaol is a narrow mill town in the eastern hills, where they made the first paper on Dia-thìr from rags, and in it stands the loft of Beathag nic Thòmais. Beathag had been a mill-owner's widow in An Aois Scaraidh who bought up the old copies of Leabhar a' Chùraim and the harbour rolls from houses that no longer read them, and stacked them in the loft over her mill; and when she died she left the loft and all that was in it to the town, on the one condition that nothing in it should ever be burned. The town had kept that condition since, and the custodian houses sent copies there when they had a thing they wished to be sure of.

So Gormshuil had Muireall make a second fair copy of the list, the whole of it, the colours first and the note at the foot of the first leaf, word for word as in the first. It took the girl the rest of that moon. When it was done Gormshuil read it through against the first and found two mistakes and had them put right. Then she wrapped it in oilcloth, and laid it in a small chest of oak with straw about it, and sealed the chest with the seal of Clann Fhearchair, and wrote on the lid in the stag-letters where it was to go.

The carriers going east to the hills left the capital two days after, with the chest in the bottom of a cart under a load of the strangers' iron pots. It is some three days' road from Cathair dhearg to Muileann chaol, and longer with a laden cart.

Gormshuil and Muireall walked down with the cart as far as the bridge on a bright morning with a wind off the lake. At the far end of Drochaid Cathair dhearg the cart went on up the eastern road, and they watched it out of sight between the hedges, and Muireall asked her aunt whether anybody at Muileann chaol would ever read it.

"Somebody will," said Gormshuil. "It is a long time, in a loft."

Then they went back up the hill, because Wren would be waiting at the table, and it was the afternoon, and the afternoons were for his letters.

The copy came safe to the loft, and the loft keeps it still. Gormshuil's own list, the one she kept at the table, is lost; Wren's lies in the humans' letter-books in his own hand, in a good black ink that has not faded. The copyists of Seilear Muileann chaol have the two before them as they set down this age, side by side as they lay on the table in the long room. They have set the first leaf of the copy, and the note at its foot in the careful hand of a girl of fourteen, at the head of the whole age.
