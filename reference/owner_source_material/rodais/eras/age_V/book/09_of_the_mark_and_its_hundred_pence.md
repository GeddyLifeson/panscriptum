# Of the Mark and Its Hundred Pence

*AE 17 – 20*

## I. Of Carragh Cnoc bheag

Samuel Wren went up to Cnoc bheag at the end of the dark of the year, in the seventeenth winter since the ships came, with a pony, a boy from the clergy-house to lead it, and a satchel of white paper wrapped in oilcloth against the rain. He was near fifty then. The damp of the island had never agreed with him. He coughed on the lake road out of Ros dhomhain and on the long rise north through the sheep-country, and at night in the houses where they lodged he sat up against the wall to cough, so as not to wake the household, and woke it anyway.

He had been asked. Some years before, the hawk-priests of Cnoc bheag had written to Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise for a copy of his grammar, to read what the humans wrote of them, and had been sent one. Now they wrote that there was a stone near their town with writing on it older than any hand on the island, and that a man who wished to know the island's tongue from its roots should see the oldest thing in it.

The priest who met him on the road was a lean man with a hawk's feather pinned in his cloak and a voice much bigger than his chest; Wren's day-book calls him only the priest of the feather. He talked the whole of the last miles in the clipped Dia-thìris of the north, and Wren caught one word in three and nodded at the rest.

At the priest's house that night, after they had eaten, the priest brought out Wren's own grammar.

Wren knew the binding, but not what had become of the pages. Every margin was written in, in two hands and three inks, in the stag-letters of the island, running up the edges and between the lines; there were small crosses beside some words and a drawn hawk beside others; and on the leaf where Wren had put the name of the vein's custodians into his own tongue as *keepers of the coal*, someone had written the island's word in the margin three times over and scored a line beneath it.

"You have it wrong there," the priest said, with great good humour. "A keeper keeps a thing in a box. The custodians have the care of it. As a man has the care of a sick child, or a ewe at lambing." He tapped the page. "We wrote it in so that the next one who reads your book will know."

Wren asked whether he might copy the notes.

"Copy all of them," said the priest. "That is what they are for."

He sat up by the priest's fire until the candle was a stub, copying, and coughing.

The stone stood some four miles off, south and west of the town, on a green shoulder of ground above a burn. The town had fenced it long ago with a ring of oak stakes against the cattle, and each generation renewed the stakes as they rotted, so that the fence was always new and the stone always old. It was a little taller than a man, grey, narrowing to the top, and it leaned as if it had been listening to something in the west for a long time. The rain came across the shoulder from the sea, fine and cold, and Wren stood inside the stakes with his paper pinned to a board under his cloak and looked up.

On the upper face ran a single line of marks. They were cut deep and cleanly, with the edges still sharp after all the weather, and they were like no letters Wren knew in any tongue of his own world or any tongue he had learned on the island. Lower down, cut with a narrower tool, shallower and less sure, were four words he could read.

"*Bha teine sa chloich*," he said aloud.

"There was fire in the stone," said the priest. "That is the easy part."

The custodian of Cnoc bheag had come out with them, an old woman on a stick who had said nothing all the way from the town. Now she laid her hand on the top of a stake and told him what the custodians of that town had told every asker since the stag-letters were made. The stone was older than the Dia-thìrich. The scholars of the old schools had carried their own letters to it when those letters were new, and found that they matched nothing. Somebody of the island had cut the four words below, later, knowing no more than they did. None living could read the rest.

"Then who can?" Wren asked her.

"Nobody here," said the custodian. "Perhaps nobody anywhere. You may try."

He tried. He copied the line mark by mark, three times over, with his back to the wind and the boy holding the ink-horn in both hands to keep the rain out of it; he measured each mark with a rule and wrote the measures under it; and he copied the four words also, and the lean of the stone, and the stakes. It took him the whole of that day, which was {{date:IV-0051}}. When he came away his fingers would not close on the pony's rein.

He sent the best of the three copies over the sea on the first ship of the spring, north-about, to the scholars of an Tìr Thall who kept such things, asking what the line was and who had cut it. Through that summer and the next he asked the master of every ship that came in whether there was a packet for him. No answer came. The custodian of Cnoc bheag, when she was told, is said to have laughed.

## II. Of the Ship on the Coin

While Wren was in the north the coin came in.

On {{date:IV-0052}} Wren's clerk, making up the quarter's accounts, found that the stores of A' Chompanaidh at Ros dhomhain would take his notes of hand no longer, but only coin: the humans' own, shipped from over the sea in small iron-bound kegs and brought in beside the old barter of the island, as the proclamation of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach put it, for ease. Nobody on the island had asked for it. The post went on taking wool and fish that year as before. The iron began to cost coin, and the fish went on costing fish.

That summer Riaghaltas nan Coigreach set up a striking-house on the quay at Ros dhomhain below Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, a long stone shed with a press brought over in pieces and bolted to a floor of new oak, and silver and copper came over the sea for it in bars. On {{date:IV-0053}} the first coins of the island's own striking came out of the press into a wooden tray: the mark and its hundred pence.

Wren was there, because Josiah Pell had asked the clergy to send somebody to bless the press, and they had sent the man who spoke the island's tongue best, in case any of the island came to watch. A dozen fishers stood in the shed door with their caps in their hands, and a knot of children who had been told to go home and had not. Wren said a short prayer over the press in his own tongue and then said it again in theirs. The press came down. The workman lifted the first mark out of the die with a pair of tongs and dropped it in the tray, and it rang.

Pell turned it to the light from the door and showed it round. On one face was a ship under full sail, very finely cut, with every rope drawn. On the other were letters in the humans' tongue that gave the coin's worth and the year of the humans' own reckoning. There was nothing of Dia-thìr on either face. No hill, no hawk, no word.

Wren said so, afterward, in Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, over a glass of the strangers' wine. He said that it might be well to have some mark of the island on the island's coin, since it was to pass from hand to hand among the island's people, and they would look at it.

Pell said the dies had been cut over the sea. He said it pleasantly, and poured Wren more wine, and asked him about the stone at Cnoc bheag.

Wren kept one of the first marks. At the end of that month he walked up the hill to Cathair dhearg with it in his coat, to the long room on the east side of the council hall where Gormshuil still kept her papers, and where he had sat across a table from her fifteen years before and pointed at cups and bread; and he put it on the same table, in the same window-light, ship-side up.

Gormshuil picked it up. She was grey by then, and wore spectacles of the strangers' glass, which she did not like and could not do without. She held the coin close to them a long while, both faces, and weighed it in her palm.

"Which ship is it?" she asked.

Wren said it was no ship; it was a ship. A figure for all ships.

"No," said Gormshuil. "It is the ship that came." She put the coin down between them. "Where is the island on it?"

He had no answer to give her, and gave none.

Muireall was at the far end of the table, copying. She was a woman near thirty by then. She took the mark from her aunt without asking and laid a sheet of thin paper over it and rubbed it with a soft lead until the ship came up grey on the white, every rope of it, and then did the letters. Under the rubbing she wrote the island's year and moon, and the humans' day in the humans' figures. Under that she wrote: *the island is on neither face.*

The rubbing went east that autumn with the season's copies, in the chest for the loft at Muileann chaol. Gormshuil gave the coin itself back to Wren. She said she had no use for it yet.

## III. Of the Two Books

From the first day of the next year the market of Cathair mhòr, the greatest market below the hills east of the capital, would not take the mark at all.

The custodians of that town met in their hall on {{date:IV-0054}} and gave their ruling in a sentence: what was sold in Cathair mhòr would be sold as it always had been, thing for thing, by the old measures, and a man who wanted iron could bring wool. The tool-sellers of A' Chompanaidh were told it at the bridge-foot and took it back to Ros dhomhain, and Pell wrote to Tormod mac Ailein, and Tormod put the letter in the chest under the east window of the council hall.

At the capital there was no ruling. There it happened stall by stall.

Wren saw it that first winter after the striking, on a market-day at the end of am Faoilleach, when he went down to buy stockings. The market lay below the hill along the lake road, and the lake wind set the awnings cracking. At the bridge end stood the tool-sellers of A' Chompanaidh with their carts, and their prices were chalked on boards in the humans' figures, in marks only. At the other end the cloth-women and the fish-wives sat as they had always sat, and a fleece was worth so many ells and a creel of herring so many loaves, and a stranger with a purse of new pence had to find an islander to change them, at a loss, before he could buy anything.

Between the two ends, under the arch of the old gate, sat the custodians' market-clerk with two books open in front of him.

He was a sharp-nosed man called Tadhg. In the left-hand book he entered the market's dues by the old reckoning, so many fleeces and so many creels; in the right-hand book, ruled in the humans' manner, he entered the tool-sellers' dues in marks and pence. They would not pay in fleeces. The cloth-women would not pay in pence. At the end of each month he must make the two books into one figure for the council, and there was no figure that would do it.

He showed Wren the foot of the left-hand page. Under the last tally he had written, in the stag-letters: *This book cannot be added to the other.* Then he turned to the foot of the right-hand page and showed him the same sentence, written again, laboriously, in the humans' letters and the humans' tongue, with one word misspelled.

"So that both sides will know," said Tadhg.

Wren bought his stockings at the far end for a fleece's worth of goods he had brought in a bag for the purpose, having learned. On the bridge one of the tool-sellers called out to ask whether it was true that the strangers' priest had gone to Cnoc bheag to read a stone, and Wren said that it was true he had gone, and not true that he had read it.

## IV. Of the Winter at Dùn thais

That summer a letter came from Dùn thais, in a beautiful hand in the stag-letters, so courteous that it took Wren and his clerk a week to be sure what it asked. The masters of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the school of the deer, had read the grammar also, and heard of the stone. They would receive the man who made the grammar for the dark of the year, when the school sat, if he would come and dispute with them; and they would give him a bed near the fire, since they had heard he coughed.

He went east with the carriers at the end of an t-Samhain, with a trunk of books on the cart, over the capital road through Cathair mhòr and on into the hills, where the road narrowed and the carriers walked at the horses' heads and sang to keep the pace. They were five days on the sixty miles, in the rain. He came down into Dùn thais on the last afternoon with the light going: grey houses at the head of a long valley, smoke lying flat over the roofs, and a dark wood on the hill behind.

The disputation began on {{date:IV-0056}}, in the hall of the school: a long, low room with a hearth at each end and rushes on an earth floor, where some twenty masters sat on benches down both walls in undyed wool and the students sat on the floor between. The eldest of them was a woman so old that she had to be helped to her bench, and so sharp that the others looked at her before they answered anything.

Wren put his question on the first evening, standing, in his best Dia-thìris, with the fire loud behind him.

He asked why the vein should be holy.

He meant it honestly. In seventeen years on the island he had seen the custodians go up to the cuttings in their season with their baskets and their words, and seen the handful set down at the shrine and burned there, measured, and the rest left in the hill. He had seen a people who were cold every winter keep a fuel under their feet and not burn it. He had written it into the grammar and never understood it. In his own country, he said, God had given men the earth to use, and the black stone in it with the rest, and a man who froze beside a coal-seam for want of leave would be thought a fool.

The old woman heard him through. Then she asked him, in her turn, why his fuel should be burned.

He began to answer, and found he did not know how to begin. In his own country nobody had ever asked it. The fuel was there; it burned; the house was warm. She asked him what the fuel had been before it was fuel, and he told her what the philosophers of his country said, that it was the wood of old forests pressed under the earth for ages. She nodded, as if she had expected it, and asked him whether he burned his grandmother's house because it was made of wood.

So it went all that winter. Each side put its question and the other put its question back. They sat in the long hall through the short days with the rain on the roof, and argued in two tongues, with a young master to interpret when Wren's Dia-thìris failed him, which was often when they came to the old words for the vein. The school's way was to hold a question up to the light like a piece of coal and look for the colours in it. Wren's way was to answer, and write the answer down. By the turn of the year the students had taken sides, and argued on the floor after the masters had gone to bed.

Neither telling of that winter names a winner. The register of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh sets down the two questions, and under them a line the students took up among themselves: that it is a poor question that can be answered in one winter. Wren's letters call the masters the most learned company he had met on the island or off it, and the old woman a person he would not willingly face again across a table.

On a night near the year's end, when the talk was done, the masters told stories at the hearth, and the old woman told one of a voyage west. A holy man of the island's first days, she said, put out from the western shore in a boat of hides with a few companions to look for the isle where the blessed live; and they came to many islands, one of sheep and one of birds that sang the hours, and landed on a great fish's back and lit a fire on it, until it moved; and at last they came to the isle of the blessed, and were shown it, and sent home to tell of it.

Wren sat without moving while she told it, and afterwards asked her to tell it again, and wrote it down as she spoke.

In the last days of the year Wren wrote home to the house of his order over the sea, and sent it by the packet of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, so that the letter lies in the letter-books of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach still. It tells of the school and the questions, and then tells the story as the old woman had told it, and says that it was very like the telling in an Eaglais of the voyage of Naomh Breandan, a saint of his own country's coasts, who sailed west in a boat of hides to look for the land of promise and found the fish and the birds and the isle; told from the other shore, as though the people at the end of that voyage had watched the boat come in. Wren did not know what to make of it, he wrote, and should be glad of any light his superiors could give him.

That letter, too, had no answer the island ever heard of.

He went home at the end of the dark season, west over the hills, coughing, with a second trunk of copied pages and a gift from the school, a small deer carved of black bog-oak.

## V. Of the Bridge and the Carriage

Cathair mhòr held out its whole year. All that year the tool-sellers came to the bridge-foot on market-days and went away with wool. When the year turned they came no further; they stopped their carts on the far bank of the river and would not cross the bridge, and a man of Cathair mhòr who wanted a spade or a pot had to walk out over the bridge to them with coin in his hand. By the second month of the new year the custodians of Cathair mhòr had given way, and entered it in their roll without comment, and the market took the mark like everyone else.

Gormshuil read that entry to the council. She was the only custodian of the council who could read both the island's letters and the humans' without help, and the chest under the east window, where the strangers' papers went, had come to be hers in all but name.

That spring the masons of A' Chompanaidh came up to rebuild Drochaid Cathair dhearg.

The bridge had carried the capital's road over the Abhainn dhomhain on the same piers time out of mind, and the carts of the hill country crossed it every market-day with their wheels a hand's breadth from the parapets. A' Chompanaidh wanted it wide enough for coal-carts to pass two abreast, and wanted it in dressed stone. The work began on {{date:IV-0057}}. The masons took down the old parapets course by course and stacked the stones on the bank.

Gormshuil went down on the second morning with a single stone in mind. On the downstream parapet near the middle of the bridge was a block with a name cut in the old letters, much worn: the name of Queen Sìleas, who had widened the bridge in her day for two carts to pass. The children of the capital were taught to find it with their fingers on the way to market. Gormshuil had found it so herself.

She found it already down, lying on its face in the mud of the bank. The master mason was a big, red-faced man from over the sea with a voice like a gravel-cart, and he came up the bank wiping his hands, and heard her in his own tongue, which surprised him, and asked her what she wanted with one old stone out of three hundred.

She told him whose name was on it.

He had the block turned over, and could not read it, and she read it for him. He said the new parapet would be squared true and this block would never sit square with the others. Gormshuil said it had sat square with the others for some hundreds of years. In the end he had it dressed on its back and sides and left the face as it was, and set it again in the downstream parapet near the middle of the new bridge, a little proud of the new stones and a little darker. The children found it again by midsummer.

The bridge was finished by the autumn. On {{date:IV-0057a}} Pell drove over it.

The wheelwrights of A' Chompanaidh had built him a carriage over the summer: a closed black box on springs, with a glass window in each door and a pair of greys shipped over the sea to draw it. It was the first carriage on the island. He drove in it from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir along the lake road and over the new bridge and up the hill to the council, with a coachman on the box and a boy up behind, and the whole of the lake road came to its doors to see it go by.

Tormod mac Ailein was walking to the council that morning with Gormshuil beside him. He walked slowly by then, with a stick in each hand because of his knees, and they were halfway over the bridge when they heard the wheels behind them and stood in to the downstream parapet, by Sìleas's stone, to let it by. The carriage drew up level with them and stopped, and Pell let down the glass.

He greeted them both courteously, in Dia-thìris, and offered Tormod a seat as far as the hall.

Tormod thanked him. Then he said, in the island's tongue, so that the coachman and the boy and the people on the bridge could hear it: "*Coisichidh sinne.*" We will walk.

Pell bowed, and put up the glass, and drove on. Tormod and Gormshuil walked up the hill after him, with Tormod's two sticks knocking on the new stones, and reached the hall long after the carriage had been put up in the yard. The roll of that day notes that An t-Àrd-mhaor came to the hall in a carriage, and that the custodians came on foot, as they had always come. The custodians said it aloud often after, to anyone who would listen.

## VI. Of Puingean a' Chalaidh

Puingean a' Chalaidh, the articles of the harbour, came before the council at the end of that year.

They came as Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg had come, in the humans' tongue alone, eight sheets with Pell's seal at the foot, to be signed with the council on {{date:IV-0058}}. This time the council had the sheets a month beforehand. On the first evening Gormshuil took them out of the chest and carried them home, and read them twice by her lamp, with her finger and her spectacles, and made a slip of every clause.

They were articles for the harbour of Ros dhomhain. They fixed where a ship might lie in the lake and what she must pay for lying there, and the dues on goods landed and taken off. A harbour-master of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach was to be set over the quay, with the keeping of the anchorages. At every turn the dues fell in favour of A' Chompanaidh, whose hulls lay at the best berths for a third of what any other boat paid; and every boat that anchored off Ros dhomhain paid something, the boats of the island's own lake towns among them, which had anchored there free since there had been boats.

Gormshuil went to Tormod's house with her slips. He heard them by the fire, as he had heard the charter ten years before, with a cup in his hand and his feet on a stool. When she had done he was silent a long time.

"We need the quay," he said at last. "We need what comes to it."

"So we do," said Gormshuil. "We have had the quay since before there were strangers. What we are asked to sign is that it is theirs."

"Does it say so?"

"The harbour-master is to be theirs. That comes to the same."

He asked her what she would do. She said that she would read the articles aloud in the hall, in both tongues, before anyone signed, so that every custodian would know what was on the sheets; and that she would not sign them herself. He let her be. He was tired.

On the day the hall was full. The rain came in off the lake as it had on the day of Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg, and Pell sat at the far side of the long table in his black coat with Wren at his elbow to carry words. When it came to the reading, Gormshuil stood up.

She read the articles through, every clause, first in the humans' tongue as it was written and then in Dia-thìris as close as she could bring it. It took most of the morning. Where she had no island word for a word of theirs she said so, and turned to Wren and asked whether she had the sense right; and Wren, who had not been asked for this, said each time that she had, and each time Pell looked at him. When she came to the clause on the anchorages, a custodian at the lower end of the table, Ailean Ruadh of Baile ghlas, whose town's boats had lain free off Ros dhomhain every season of his life, stood up and asked to hear it again. She read it again.

Then she sat down.

Tormod signed first. He wrote his name slowly, looking a long while at the place before he put the pen to it, and the custodians signed after him one by one down the table, with Pell watching each name go on.

When the sheet came to Gormshuil she did not take up the pen. She passed the sheet on to the next place. When it came to Ailean Ruadh he did the same.

Pell asked, through Wren, whether the custodian of the capital was unwell.

Gormshuil answered him herself, in his own tongue. She said that she was well. She said that she had read the articles, which none at that table but she had done, and that she would not put her name under what she had read. Ailean Ruadh said, in the island's tongue, that he would not put his name under anything that charged Baile ghlas to lie where Baile ghlas had always lain. Pell heard them out. He thanked them. Then he signed, for Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, below the names of those who had signed, and the articles stood.

The council's roll enters the signing, and the names under it, and says that two custodians refused: Gormshuil nic Fhearchair of Cathair dhearg, and Ailean Ruadh of Baile ghlas. It was the first time since the strangers came that any custodian had refused to sign a thing the strangers put before the council. Muireall, who copied that leaf for the loft, drew a line round the two names.

Walking home in the dark, Tormod said to Gormshuil that it had done no good.

"No," she said. "It is written, though."

## VII. Of Grazing, and Chimneys

Puingean a' Chalaidh took effect with the new year, and the harbour-master took up the keeping of the quay, and by the autumn after, on {{date:IV-0059}}, he was charging anchorage.

The fishermen of Baile ghlas paid it once. Then they talked it over on the shingle and sent word up the lake that they would not pay it again. They took their catch to their own small harbour under the town, a stone jetty and a slip with room for a dozen boats, and sold it there off the boats to anyone who would walk the seven miles out from the capital, and to anyone at all but A' Chompanaidh. Gormshuil walked out one bright morning at the end of an Dàmhair and found the jetty crowded with the capital's fish-wives and their creels, and the herring going from hand to hand for pence and for loaves both, and Ailean Ruadh at the top of the slip with his arms folded, very pleased. The letter-books complain that the salting-house of A' Chompanaidh had herring that season only from its own boats.

A month later the council granted Riaghaltas nan Coigreach grazing.

There were human families on the island by then who had come over the sea to work for A' Chompanaidh and would not go down the pits; they had sheep and cattle of their own breeds and no land. The council gave them, on {{date:IV-0060}}, summer grazing on the empty uplands of the north-west about Muileann àrsaidh, where the fields had gone back to rush in the last age. The grant was for grazing. Gormshuil had the word read back three times, in both tongues, before anyone signed, and noted on her slip that the humans' word meant the eating of grass by beasts and nothing else. She signed that one.

Before the winter a short letter came down to the chest under the east window from the custodian of Muileann àrsaidh. The families on the grazing had built houses, the custodian wrote. They had built them of stone: good stone, quarried from the hill and squared, with chimneys and slate from somewhere, and walls a man could not kick down. The shielings of the island were built of turf and wattle, to stand a summer and be let fall. These would stand a hundred years. Did the council mean it so?

## VIII. Of Tormod's Burying

Tormod had been ailing since the harvest, in the chest and then in the heart. When Gormshuil brought the letter from Muileann àrsaidh he was in his bed, propped on bolsters, with the fire made up and the girl who kept his house on a stool by the door. He asked her to read it to him.

She read it. When she came to the chimneys he began to laugh, and the laugh turned to coughing, and the girl brought him water.

"Grazing," he said, when he could.

"Grazing," said Gormshuil. "I had it read three times."

"I know you did." He lay back on the bolsters. "You had it right. It meant grazing when we signed it. It is only afterward that their words grow." He was silent a while. "Write to them that the council meant summer grazing, and shielings. Write it in both tongues. They will not pull the houses down."

"No."

"Write it all the same."

She wrote it that night, in both tongues, and sent it north in the morning. Tormod died on {{date:IV-0061}}, in the dark of the year, in the house below the temple, with the fire lit and the girl at the door and Gormshuil asleep in the chair by the bed. He was sixty-five years old. He had sat at the head of Comhairle a' Chùraim, in the crown's place, for longer than most of the council had been grown, and he had given the strangers leave to stay, and signed Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg and the harbour articles, and there were those in the capital who had not forgiven him for it, and came to his wake anyway.

They waked him three nights in his own house, by the old custom, and on the second night the council chose Coinneach Glas of Ceann àrsaidh after him, a broad, slow-spoken man from twenty miles up the east road, who had never set eyes on a human before An t-Aiseag and had dealt with little else since, and the choice was made in an evening. The roll gives it as unanimous. It was.

They buried Tormod on the fourth day, in the ground behind the council hall, on a cold wet morning with the cloud down on the hill. Six custodians carried the bier out of his door and down through the town, with the council walking after, Coinneach Glas at their head and Gormshuil among them in her grey cloak, and the bells of Cathair dhearg rang for him all the way, the old bronze of the hoes. At the grave they went round sunwise, each with a stone from the lake shore, and laid the stones on the turf one by one, and said the words.

Josiah Pell came. He left his carriage at the bridge-foot and walked up the hill in the rain behind the custodians, bareheaded, and stood at the edge of the ground through the whole of the burying, and went away at the end without speaking. At the back of all the crowd, by the gate, stood Samuel Wren in his black coat. He had walked up from Ros dhomhain in the rain, as he always walked, and he did not come nearer. Through the words and the stones he stood by the gate with a handkerchief to his mouth, and coughed, and tried not to, and coughed again; and when it was done and the crowd went down the hill he was still there, and Gormshuil, passing, stopped, and they went down together.
