# Of the Burning of the Ledgers

*AE 150*

> *Loisc iad an leabhar, ach cha do loisc iad an cnoc.*
> They burned the book, but they did not burn the hill.

## I. Of the Grain from Seann Warr

Dùghall Dubh had counted carts before, in the years he weighed coal for A' Chompanaidh at the Ros dhomhain scales, and he counted them now for the council out of the same habit, standing at the harbour quarter's upper gate with a tally-stick and a stub of chalk while the grain train from Seann Warr came in by the eastern road under a sky the colour of wet slate.

Nine carts, then eleven, then a gap the length of a held breath before the twelfth came round the bend with a wheel binding badly enough that the carter walked beside it, easing it over the ruts by hand. Dùghall let the tally rest a moment and watched the wheel. He had not eaten a full meal since the fourth day of the month and could not have said, if asked, whether it was the wheel or the hunger that made him watch it so closely.

Seann Warr lay two days east by the good road, a farming town in the flat country that had never seen a pit-head in its life and had sent grain to the hills every month since the winter the lines were drawn, by carts that went the long way round to keep clear of Strake's patrols out of Ros bheag. The council's roll in the yard behind Dùghall kept the count of every load: how many sacks, whose oxen, which nights the carters slept rough rather than risk a farm too near the coast road. It was a poor sort of harvest to be grateful for, twelve carts against a capital's hunger, but Dùghall had learned in a year of siege that a poor sort of harvest was still a harvest, and he thanked the carter by name and sent the last cart on to the sharing-yard with the rest.

The sharing-yard stood behind the custodians' hall, where Cathair dhearg's grain had been measured out by household since Strake shut himself up behind the harbour's gates the spring before: a scale, a clerk, a line that did not shorten by evening. The custodians themselves, Dùghall had heard it said more than once and had seen enough to believe, took no more from the scale than any weaver's widow in the line before them, and the saying had done more for the city's temper through that hard winter than any speech from the hall steps could have. Dùghall did not go often to hear speeches. He went to the sharing-yard the one day in five it fell to him, took his household's share, and went back to the harbour quarter, where the work that mattered to him was done with the hands and not the mouth.

That work, through the first year of the siege, had been watching. The harbour quarter of Ros dhomhain sat below Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir and above A' Chompanaidh's quays, close enough to the humans' lines to hear their bells and their drums and, on a still evening, the creak of their ships' timbers at anchor, and it had sat there a year now neither wholly for the council nor wholly against it, a narrow strip of stone houses and net-lofts caught between two powers that each wanted its gates and neither had troubled, yet, to take them. Dùghall's own house stood on the quarter's upper street, three doors from the counting-house where A' Chompanaidh had kept its tithe rolls since before he was born, and he had grown up close enough to that building's clerks to know most of them by their coughs before he knew them by their faces.

He had weighed coal at the counting-house scales for nine years before the war closed them, standing the same six hours of every working day on the far side of a steelyard from a clerk named Prentice, an easy-spoken man who kept a paper twist of peppermints in his waistcoat and offered one to every man whose load he entered against, Dùghall among them, without ever once asking whose account the entry would fall on come the year's reckoning. Dùghall had liked Prentice, in the plain way a man likes another he has stood beside for nine years without either learning much of the other's life outside the scales, and it was a strange thing, that summer, to find himself weighing in his own mind what he owed a man's peppermints against what the ledgers that man had kept owed the whole of the hills, and to find the second sum so much the larger of the two that the first could not be set against it at all.

He had not, until that summer, thought of the counting-house as a thing that could burn.

## II. Of the Roofs in Stocking Feet

The word to move came down from the council's captains in the first days of an t-Iuchar, carried as a question rather than an order, put to men who knew their own streets better than any captain could: could the harbour quarter reach the counting-house without a shot fired, and if it could, would it.

Dùghall put the question to six men he trusted before he answered it, all of them born within sight of the counting-house's slate roof, and all six said the same thing in different words: that they had watched A' Chompanaidh's clerks carry the tithe books past their doors for thirty years, entering against every settlement in the hills the debt the hills would spend the next thirty paying, and that a shot fired would only tell Strake's men where to look, while a fire lit quietly would tell them nothing until it was too tall to answer.

They went over on a night of low cloud with no moon in it, in their stocking feet, up the outside stair of the net-loft that backed onto the counting-house yard and along its roof-ridge on hands and knees, six men and Dùghall a seventh, each with a garret window he had marked in daylight and a rope coiled at his shoulder for the drop inside. Dùghall's own window stuck at the sash, swollen with the damp of a wet summer, and for the length of ten held breaths he worked it with the flat of his knife while below him, through the slates, he could hear the counting-house's night clerk moving about at his desk, entirely unaware that the roof over his ledgers had seven men crouched along it.

The sash gave without a sound worth the name. Dùghall went down the rope into a garret stacked with old tally-boards and out through a door that had not been locked in living memory, because no one in thirty years had thought there was a way in from above; and at the stair-head he found Fearchar Beag already waiting for him, having come in by the loading hatch on the ground floor with two others, the night clerk's lantern taken and turned down and the night clerk himself sitting on the counting-house steps in his shirt with his hands laced over his head, looking, Dùghall thought, more relieved than afraid, as though some part of him had been waiting a year for this night to come and was glad, now, to have it over.

The tithe books stood where they had always stood, in the long room above the loading floor, calf-bound and ranked on their shelves like a wall built of pressed hide, each one heavy enough that a man could not carry more than two at a stretch without his arms failing him before the yard. Dùghall had weighed coal by the ton for A' Chompanaidh once and thought he had lost the trick of being surprised by weight; the tithe books surprised him. It took the seven of them the better part of an hour to carry the whole wall down the stair and out into the yard, armful by armful, stacking them on the cobbles the way a mason stacks stone for a wall not yet begun, and by the time the last shelf stood bare Dùghall's shoulders ached in a way the coal scales had never made them ache, because the coal, at least, a man set down knowing it would be picked up again.

He struck the light himself, from a lantern-wick and a twist of oiled rag, and stood back while it caught along the bottom row and climbed. The sentry at the counting-house's front door, posted there every night of the siege against a raid from the water side, never turned round until the whole yard behind him stood lit like a hearth in a house with the roof off, and by then there was nothing left for him to do but blow the alarm bell and watch six men and their leader go back over the roofs the way they had come, unhurried, because a fire that size buys its makers more time than they need.

Dùghall stood a while at his own garret window afterward, watching the light of it redden the underside of the low cloud, and thought, without much satisfaction in the thought, that thirty years of a man's life could be made to weigh less than a garret full of old tally-boards, provided enough of it was burned at once.

## III. Of the Word from Ceann àrsaidh and Muileann chrom

The word of the other two fires reached the harbour quarter three days apart, each by a rider who had ridden hard enough to arrive gasping, and Dùghall heard both accounts standing in the same doorway where he had heard the first report of his own men's work, so that the three fires came to him afterward as one telling rather than three, the way a man remembers a single bad winter though it held a dozen separate storms.

The tithe-house at Ceann àrsaidh burned second, and the rider who brought word of it, a boy of Fearchar Beag's own age with Ceann àrsaidh mud still on his boots, told it with a care that made plain he had turned the telling over more than once on the road. The tithe-house held the portion owed by every settlement of the hills, entered against each in its own column since the first schedule was drawn, a record older by twenty years than the counting-house's tithe books and kept, by all report, more exactly. The Ceann àrsaidh companies had let the humans' clerks go unharmed before they carried the ledgers into the square, and it was there, the boy said, at the last, that Aonghas Ruadh's own successor in the custodian's chair had asked that one ledger be spared, to keep the record entire rather than soften the debt it named, so that in a settled year to come the hills might show by their own hand what had been asked of them and what had been paid. His own townsfolk had refused him. They had carried that one ledger to the pile with the rest, and a woman the boy would not name had said, loud enough for the custodian to hear it, that a debt kept on paper could always be raised again by whoever next held the paper, and that the only sure way to close an account was to leave no account to close.

The weigh-house at Muileann chrom burned third, and by the time that word came the harbour quarter had already had two days to grow used to the smell of old smoke on the wind off the eastern road, so that this third telling landed lighter than the first two, though the loss it named was, by every measure Dùghall knew how to take, the greatest of the three: the steelyard ledgers in which the coal of the hills had been set down by the ton since the earliest contract, the whole long reckoning by which A' Chompanaidh had known, to the ton and the shilling, what the hill had given it. The rider who brought that word, older than the first and less careful with his telling, said only that the rebels' own chronicler at Muileann chrom had written the fire burned green, from the damp in the old timbers or from something in the ink itself, nobody could say which, and that the camp folk who watched it were already making a verse of it before the ash had cooled.

Dùghall did not hear the verse whole until a week later, from a woman selling fish at the quarter's lower gate who sang it under her breath the way market women sing whatever is going about, without knowing or much caring where it came from: *they burned the book, but they did not burn the hill.* He bought no fish that morning and stood a while at the gate all the same, listening to her sing it twice through, and thought that whoever had made those eight words had done in eight what he had failed to do in a week of turning the Ceann àrsaidh woman's saying over in his own head.

## IV. Of Cnoc ghorm

Strake answered the fires the only way left to him, which was with the guns he still held inside his shrinking lines, and it cost him, by the harbour quarter's later count, the last strength he had to spend on the hills at all.

Word of Cnoc ghorm came to Dùghall as smoke on the wrong quarter of the sky rather than any rider's telling, a grey smear rising beyond the eastern ridge where no fire of the council's setting had any business standing, and for the better part of a morning the harbour quarter went about its work not knowing whether the smoke marked an advance of A' Chompanaidh or a defeat of A' Chompanaidh. It was Fearchar Beag, sent up to the net-loft roof with a glass borrowed from a fisherman, who brought the answer down at noon: A' Chompanaidh's guards and constables, sallied out from the capital's harbour under cover of the night to try the eastern road one last time and win back the way to the mining country, driven off at Cnoc ghorm in the hills beyond the city with losses the loft-roof glass could plainly see though it could not count them, riderless horses coming back down the road alone before the men who had ridden them.

It was the last such attempt Riaghaltas nan Coigreach made in the whole of that war, though no one in the harbour quarter knew it yet for a last thing; they knew only that the guns had gone quiet on the eastern road by evening and had not spoken again, and took what comfort a quiet road gave, which in a year of siege was considerable.

## V. Of the Lower Gates

Eight weeks after the counting-house burned, the harbour quarter did the thing Dùghall had watched it circle for the better part of a year without doing, and opened its lower gates to Buidhnean na Comhairle waiting beyond them.

It was not put to a vote, in the end, nor settled by any one man's word, though afterward more than one teller would claim the deciding of it. It was settled, as Dùghall came to understand it, by the plain arithmetic of a quarter that had fed Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's kitchens for thirty years and could no longer feed its own children while doing so, and by the counting-house yard standing burned the whole summer in full view of every window on the upper street, a black scar that made the old bargain look, each morning a family woke to see it, less like a bargain and more like a debt nobody now living had agreed to. Dùghall stood at the lower gate himself when the bar came off it, having asked to be there though the honour of the drawing had not fallen to him, and watched Buidhnean na Comhairle come up from the quays in good order, their captain a woman of Muileann dhearg he did not know by name, who thanked the gate-wardens plainly and asked, before anything else, where the wounded of the counting-house sentry's alarm bell had been carried, which struck Dùghall as a stranger's sort of first question and a better one than he had expected.

That same night, with the gates open and the companies bedded in the lower streets, men of the quarter went up to A' Chompanaidh's shed above the town where the flying machine had stood idle the whole length of the siege, grounded from the first month of the war for want of the spirit its engine burned, and burned the shed and the machine together. Dùghall did not go up with them; he had never much liked the look of the thing, an iron bird that had never once flown in his sight and had cost, by the tithe rolls he had weighed against in his working years, more than the whole harbour quarter's grain for a season, and he felt no want to stand over its burning the way he had wanted to stand over the tithe books'. He heard only, from his own street, the second glow of that night rising over the roofs to join the first, and thought that nothing had flown over Dia-thìr since Ceò Mhanannain first thinned that was not, in the end, a bird.

## VI. Of Cuan uaine and the Water Road

With the harbour quarter lost to him and the eastern road shut at Cnoc ghorm, Strake had one road left, and it ran by water: the Abhainn gheal down from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir to the sea, and along it the river quays of Cuan uaine, a morning's row east of the capital, through which what little still reached him from the coast had been passed all that year.

Cuan uaine opened its own quays to the council's boats within the week, on terms Dùghall heard told two ways and believed neither wholly: that the town's boatmen had simply stopped turning the council's craft back, one morning, without a word said either way, the way a door left unbarred is not quite the same as a door thrown open, and is opened all the same. Whichever way it went, Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir after that could be fed, if it could be fed at all, only by what a boat could carry past a watch that no longer troubled to look away.

## VII. Of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir Stripped

Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir fell to Buidhnean na Comhairle without a shot on the morning of the twenty-second of an t-Sultain, and Dùghall was among the first up from the harbour quarter to see it, walking the same streets he had weighed coal in half his working life, now empty of every uniform of A' Chompanaidh but for a handful of clerks too old or too frightened to have gone with Strake down the river the night before.

The house itself, when he came into it, told its own story before any clerk could tell it him: rooms stripped to the floorboards, presses standing open and bare, the great hall's long table left with nothing on it but the marks where other things had stood, dust-shadows in the shape of what a year of siege had taught its household to carry and what it had not. Strake had taken his officers, Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's clergy and A' Chompanaidh's own agents down the water road in the night, by every account Dùghall gathered that morning, and had taken with them everything a careful man judges worth the carrying: plate, papers of account, the small close-held things a household gathers over thirty years and cannot bear to leave for an enemy's hands.

What he had not taken, because in his haste or his judgement he had not thought them worth the room in the boats, were the letter-books: shelf on shelf of them in a lower room off the hall, bound in the same calf as the tithe books that had burned three times over that summer, but filled with something the tithe books had never held, Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's own correspondence back to an Tìr Thall since the first Àrd-mhaor's day, every dispatch sent and every answer, or silence, received.

He sent word to the council's clerks that evening, and by the next morning a table had been set up in the same stripped hall where A' Chompanaidh's long board had stood, with the first of the letter-books laid open on it under a good lamp, and men who had never in their lives been asked to read a stranger's hand bent over the close, foreign script with the same patience Dùghall had once given a scale that would not balance. He did not stay to read them himself, judging that he had done the part of that summer's work that was his to do, and went back down through the empty streets to the harbour quarter, where the counting-house yard still stood black three streets from his own door.

Dùghall passed it slowly on his way home, as he had passed it every day since the fire and would go on passing it, he supposed, until someone built something else there or the rains wore the black down to grey. A child of the quarter had chalked something on the standing wall in the days since, letters Dùghall could not fully make out in the falling light, and he did not go closer to read them; he had heard enough words made of that fire already, the Ceann àrsaidh woman's and the fish-seller's both, and judged that whatever a child had found to say of it belonged to the child and not to him. Prentice, he had heard from a clerk taken at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir that morning, had gone down the river with Strake's household after all, peppermints and all, to whatever account was kept of him at Ros bheag or beyond it; and Dùghall found, standing in the last of the light with the black wall in front of him, that he could wish the man no particular harm and no particular good, only that the ledgers Prentice had kept so evenly for nine years were ash now on the same cobbles where he had once handed a boy his coin for the day's weighing, and that whatever came after the war would have to be reckoned by some other hand than that.

For the first time since the fourth day of the month he found, when he reached the sharing-yard, that there was bread enough to feed every household in the line before evening fell.
