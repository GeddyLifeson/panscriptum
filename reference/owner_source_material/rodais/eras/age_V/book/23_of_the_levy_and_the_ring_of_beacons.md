# Of the Levy and the Ring of Beacons

*AE 88 – 91*

## I. Of the Reading at Caol mhòr

The camps under the hawk-feather burned their fires that winter as they had burned them the winter before, and the Administration's clerks at Ros dhomhain wrote in their ledgers that the yield still climbed. It was George Tolley's thirteenth year as Commissioner, and by then he had learned what the island's soil would give up and what its people would, and had stopped troubling to ask which was which. On a hard morning at the turn of the year he set his seal to a single sheet and called it An Togail, the Levy: from every farming village of the west and north, one soul in five of the able grown folk, taken for two years, to go down into the pits where the Company's coal was cut. The villages would be paid for it, in marks, at the rate the second reform of the coin had lately fixed; a rate that bought less bread each season it was reckoned in, though the ledger did not say so.

Word of a law travels a country faster than the law's own officers, and slower than the fear of it. It reached Cnoc thais as a rumour before it reached Caol mhòr as a list, and at Caol mhòr, in the far north-west where the strangers had first raised the land, it came as both together.

The officers rode in on a market morning, four of them and a wagon, with a guard of the Company's men the town had not asked for. They set up at the steelyard by the quay, where the fish were weighed, and one of them stood on the weighing-stone with a paper and read from it in the humans' tongue, flat and fast, a list of names that were not the names anyone in Caol mhòr answered to. The custodians of the town stood below him with their own clerks and wrote each name down again as it was said, guessing at the shapes of it, and only when the whole list was read and the officer had gone back to his wagon for a cup of something did they go from house to house with their own paper and match a guessed name to a face. Twice they guessed wrong and took the wrong son from the wrong door, and it was three days before the mistake was mended, by which time the right son had already been marched a day's walk east.

The custodian who kept that day's roll, an old woman with a bad hip who had held the post since before the Levy was thought of, wrote at the end of it a single line that the Administration's own reader, when the roll crossed his desk months later, let stand unremarked: that a people may be counted twice, once by those who take them and once by those who love them, and that the two counts never come out the same.

The wrong son was found on the third day, sitting under guard in a barn a day's walk east with eleven other taken men, none of whom had thought to say, until a rider from Caol mhòr caught them up, that the paper's name and his own were two different shapes that had only looked alike to a guessing clerk. He was sent home, and the right son was fetched in his place, and the officer who had made the error entered it in his own tally as a delay of one day, with no further word, so that the roll of Caol mhòr and the roll of the Administration disagree to this hour on how many men that town gave up, by exactly one.

It was the first tally taken under the new law, and Caol mhòr's grief was loud and short, as a first grief is. Grief travels a country too. By the time the officers' wagon had gone from three villages to thirty, the households of the hills had stopped weeping where the officers could hear it, and had begun instead the older, quieter work of hiding what could be hidden and choosing, among those who could not be hidden, who should go. Some sent their eldest, on the thought that the eldest was hardest to spare and so ought to bear the hardest thing; others sent their youngest, on the thought that a young back mended faster than an old one; and there was no counsel from any custodian's roll, and none from the Council at Cathair dhearg either, on which of the two thoughts was the kinder.

## II. Of the Two Tallies

The Levy's writ ran over the whole west and north, but it did not run evenly, no more than rain runs evenly over uneven ground. It fell heaviest about Baile chrom and the province the humans called their northern concession, the upland grazing of the north-west that the council had granted for the humans' flocks two generations before, and that the Administration had since leased whole to its own farmers. The concession's fields were sown that spring as they had always been sown, by the same hands; only the hands that worked them now were fewer, for every Dia-thìreach household within the concession's bounds paid the Levy in full, while the human farmers who held the leases paid nothing at all. The officers' own list, when the custodian of Baile chrom's roll got sight of it, gave the reason in one line: that the concession's leaseholders were tenants of the Administration and its charter, and the Levy touched the Administration's own subjects only by the Administration's own leave, and that leave had not been given.

At Baile ìseal the tally took forty-one, the largest of any single town that year, out of a town of some seventy hearths. Nineteen came home again when their two years were done. The custodian's roll names the other twenty-two, town by town among the camps they were carried to, and against four of the names writes nothing at all, neither camp nor date nor word, only the name and a line drawn under it, which the copyists have kept exactly as it stood, understanding it no better than the roll's own keeper had.

Muileann naomh tried what no other town on the list is known to have tried, and its trying is remembered for the manner of its failing. Its custodians met the Levy's officers before the reading and offered silver, a purse weighed out on the town scale in front of witnesses, that the tally might be cut by half. The senior officer, a heavy, patient man who had done this work in a dozen towns before Muileann naomh and would do it in a dozen more after, took up the purse himself and weighed it a second time on his own small scale, as though a custodian's weights might not be trusted, and only then said that the town's offer was noted and would be honoured in full. The custodians took this to mean the tally would be halved, and stood by while the whole list, uncut, was read to the end. When the eldest of them asked, before the townsfolk, what had become of the silver, the officer said that it had been honoured as an offering toward the Company's granary, and that the Levy answered to no offering a town cared to make, and rode on. The complaint that Muileann naomh sent after them to the Residency at Ros dhomhain was received, logged, and filed, and no answer to it has ever been found, though the custodians kept a fair copy against the day one might come.

The tale of it went ahead of the officers into every town they had not yet reached, and did the Administration more harm, in the way such tales do, than the silver was worth to lose; for after Muileann naomh no other town on the list is recorded to have offered a bribe, custodians having learned in one hearing what Caol mhòr had learned in one reading, that there was no bargain to be struck with a list, only a length of road between the reading of it and the leaving.

Dùghall Clachair heard all of this the way a man hears weather from over the next hill, as talk carried by carters and by the officers themselves, who liked well enough to tell what they had done in one town when they rode into the next. He was three-and-thirty that spring, a mason of Cnoc thais, master of a crew of six who had cut and set stone together since he was a boy carrying their water. He heard the talk and thought, as every man in every village thought that spring, that his own door would not be spared for long, and went on cutting stone, because there was nothing else a mason could do about a law.

## III. Of the Shut Halls and the Trees of Flidais

In the same season, and by the same Commissioner's hand though under a different seal, the Deer hall of the druid school at Dùn thais was shut. Its offence, as the order that closed it gave it, was a lecture the masters had let stand unanswered: whether the humans' plain blindness to the colours of the coal, the blindness Gormshuil nic Fhearchair had first met across a table two generations before, was a kind of sickness in its own right, and if so, whose. Tolley's clerk who drew up the order did not trouble to say which answer had offended him. He shut the hall on the strength of the question alone.

The masters of Dùn thais did not disperse. They went on teaching in their own houses, in rooms that grew smaller each year as more households thought it wiser not to send their sons, until at Doire chiar, on the eve of Bealltainn, they held the last open disputation the schools held in the old way, in the open air on the village threshing-floor, before whoever cared to stand and listen. Folk came from three parishes to hear it, more than the floor could well hold, and stood in a ring beyond the stones with children on their shoulders. Its matter was whether a question might rightly be forbidden at all, and it was argued the whole of one long evening by two masters who had taught together thirty years and did not agree at the end of it any better than they had at the start, which the old men in the crowd took as proof that the matter had been well chosen. The Administration's clerk who was sent to note it down sat apart on a cart-shaft with his own lamp, and wrote in his own report, without seeming to feel the weight of writing it, that it might.

After that the masters who would still teach took their pupils under the trees, to the sacred forest of Flidais near Muileann chiar, where the white stag was said to walk and where the Administration had no hall to shut, having never troubled to learn that a grove could serve the office of one. There was no threshing-floor there and no cart-shaft for a clerk to sit on; the pupils sat on roots and stones in a rough half-ring, and the master who spoke walked as he spoke, so that on a wet day the lesson moved from tree to tree with the shelter. It is told that the first lesson given under those trees was the very question that had closed the Deer hall, taken up again from where the order had left it standing, and that the master who put it that day answered it herself before any pupil could, saying that a question forbidden by one hand could always be asked again under a roof that hand had not thought to look for.

Dùghall's own younger brother had gone to Dùn thais as a boy to learn his letters from the masters there, in years before the hall was shut, and it was from him, home for a season between one crew's work and the next, that Dùghall first heard the tale of the threshing-floor at Doire chiar. His brother told it standing in the doorway with the light behind him, the way a man tells something he has decided not to be afraid of, and Dùghall, who set stone for a living and had never in his life stood up in a hall to ask a forbidden question, thought his brother the braver of the two of them, and said so, and his brother laughed and said a mason's hands asked questions too, only slower.

## IV. Of the Masons of Cnoc thais

The officers came to Cnoc thais in the high summer of the ninetieth year, with the same wagon and the same list, and Dùghall's name was on it, and the names of his whole crew besides: Ailean, the two brothers Cailean and Seumas, old Pàdraig who had taught Dùghall to read a stone's grain, and the two youngest, Anna and her cousin Murchadh, who had been apprenticed to the crew not two years before and had not yet cut a stone worth the name of a mason's work. Dùghall stood in his own doorway and heard his name read from a paper by a man who could not have said it back to him if his life had hung on it, and understood for the first time what the custodian of Caol mhòr's old roll had meant, that a people may be counted twice.

He thought, as the officer moved on down the list, that they were bound for the pits like every other village's chosen, and had already begun in his head the reckoning a mason makes of any road, how many days, what weather, what he would need to carry. It was two days into the march east, on a stretch of the coast road above Baile ìseal, that the guard captain rode down the line and called for any among them who knew stone-work, and Dùghall and his whole crew put up their hands together, for there was no sense in six masons pretending otherwise to one another's faces. They were turned out of the line then and there and marched north instead of east, along the headland road toward Baile chrom, and it was three days more before any officer troubled to tell them why: that the fifth and last of the coast beacons was to be raised on the headland above the town, the ring of towers that had stood four strong since builders older than any roll now kept, and that masons were wanted for it more than diggers were wanted underground.

It did not feel, to Dùghall, like a mercy, and he came in time to think it was not meant as one. The fields of Cnoc thais that his crew would have worked between commissions, the small plots that kept a mason's household fed in a lean season, stood untilled that whole year for want of the very hands the Levy had taken to build a light that would serve no plough and no byre of theirs. He set that thought aside most days, because a man cannot cut good stone while nursing a grievance, and took it up again most nights, because a man cannot help what comes to him lying still in the dark.

"You should be glad of it," the guard captain told him, on the second evening of the march, meaning glad to be spared the pits, and waited as though a man ought to thank him for the saying. Dùghall said nothing, because there was no way to tell a guard-captain that a mason parted from his stone-yard felt the parting no less than a hewer parted from his own hill, only that the shape of the loss was different, a field going to weed instead of a family going hungry for want of a wage; and a captain with a wagon to keep moving had no patience for a difference of shape. He touched his cap instead, which cost him nothing, and the captain rode on satisfied, and Dùghall walked the rest of that evening beside old Pàdraig, who had heard the whole exchange and said only that a man who explains himself to a guard has already lost the argument he meant to win.

They were housed, the six of them, in a hut of turf and driftwood on the headland above Baile chrom, alongside crews taken the same way from four other villages, so that within a month the camp held above sixty masons and their apprentices, none of whom had chosen one another's company and all of whom now shared one fire, one well, and one quarry. The work went by the light there was, longer in summer than any of them had worked before, shorter in winter than any of them had hoped, and the officers who oversaw it kept a tally of stone cut that mattered to them more than any tally of men fed. Anna, who was seventeen that first winter and had never before slept a night away from her mother's hearth, wept the first three nights in the hut where she thought no one heard her, and was heard by all of them, and on the fourth night old Pàdraig without a word got up from his own straw and mended the gap in the turf wall nearest her sleeping-place, through which the wind had been finding her all along, and after that she did not weep where they could hear it again, though Dùghall never once believed she had stopped.

The tower rose through that autumn and the winter after, coarse grey stone from a quarry the officers had opened on the headland, so that the masons need not even be spared the labour of hauling it far. Dùghall set the courses and taught Anna and Murchadh to read the lie of a block before they touched a chisel to it, the way old Pàdraig had taught him; and Pàdraig, whose hands had grown too slow for the finer cutting, sat most days on an upturned barrow and told the younger ones what he could no longer show them, and the crew came in time to work as well by his voice as by his eyes. Cailean broke a finger that winter and would not leave off working for it, and Seumas, his brother, quarrelled with him about it every evening and dressed the finger every morning, and the two of them were never once heard to agree, on that or on anything else, in all the time Dùghall knew them.

The worst of that winter was a gale out of the north-west that took two days to blow itself out and stripped the scaffold from the tower's third course as easily as a man strips bark from a green branch. When it had passed, Dùghall climbed what stood of the walls alone, before he would let any of the others up after him, to judge whether the stone had shifted; and standing in the wind that still gusted hard enough to lean a man sideways, with the sea below him white to the horizon and no ship anywhere on it, he understood for the first time in his own body why a light was wanted on that headland at all, and forgave the tower, a little, for the fields it had cost him. He did not forgive the Levy. A tower, he thought, climbing down again with his hands raw from the cold stone, owed nothing to the law that had raised it, any more than a child owed anything to the manner of its getting.

By the second summer the walls stood to their full height and the crew began the finer work at the top: the stair turning always to the right, hand over hand up the cold stone, and the lamp-room, no bigger than a byre, lit on every side by narrow windows against the weather. It was slower work than the coursing below, and Murchadh, who had grown in a year from a frightened boy to a mason who could be trusted with a door-jamb, cut the last of the window-sills himself, under Dùghall's eye and without a single stroke wasted, and Dùghall told him so in front of the others, which was the only wage of that kind either of them ever got for the Levy's taking.

They built the tower to a height a man could not see the top of from its own foot without stepping well back, round and tapering. It was Dùghall who cut the lintel of that room, the stone that would stand over whoever kept the light for as long as the tower stood, and it was Dùghall, on the last evening before the tower was done, who took a chisel he had no order to take and cut into the underside of that lintel, where only a man climbing the last step and looking up would ever see it, the names of his crew: Ailean, Cailean, Seumas, Pàdraig, Anna, Murchadh, and his own beneath them, each letter as clean as any he had ever cut for a custodian's hall or a dead man's stone. It is the only writing on any of the five towers of the ring, and no order asked for it and no order struck it out.

## V. Of the Ring Made Whole

The ring of coast beacons was finished in the summer of the ninety-first year of the strangers: Taigh-solais Baile chrom last of the five, standing to Baile ìseal, Muileann bheag, Àth dhearg and Tobar dhearg as a hand's fifth finger stands to the rest, so that a ship might now run the whole coast of Dia-thìr by night and never lose a light from sight before the next one rose to take its place. The Company's clerks at Ros dhomhain called it the completion of the coast trade and entered the date in three ledgers. The keepers' own daybooks, kept in a plainer hand, called it the end of the dark crossing, and the two names meant the one thing.

What it meant for Cuan ruadh, on the eastern shore, was the end of a trade the town had kept since before any beacon stood at all: the old coasting boats that had carried goods by day from harbour to harbour, hugging a shore too dangerous to run by night, put up their sails for the last time within a few years of the ring's completion, for the Company's steamers no longer needed to put in anywhere along that coast, and ran the whole circuit of the island without touching Cuan ruadh's quay from one season's end to the next. A master of one of those boats, a woman named for the same red headland as the town, sold her hull for firewood the winter the last cargo failed to come, and is remembered to have said, to whichever custodian's clerk thought the sale worth entering, that a light built to keep ships from wrecking on a coast had wrecked her trade instead, and no less surely for doing it slowly. The town's custodian entered the last coasting sailing in her own roll with no comment beyond the date, which the copyists have judged, from the small unevenness of the ink, was written with a hand that trembled.

The Levy held Dùghall's crew at Baile chrom until the tower was lit, and they would have stayed for it in any case; a mason likes to see a thing finished that he has raised with his own hands. The keeper appointed to it was a young man of the headland villages named Uisdean mac Lachlainn, thirty years old that autumn, chosen for the post, the Administration's letter said, on the recommendation of the very masons who had built the light he was to keep; which recommendation, though the letter does not say so, Dùghall had himself written out and signed, along with Ailean and old Pàdraig, in a hand none of the three would have called fine.

On an evening of an t-Sultain, with the last of the year's warmth still in the air and the sea below the headland gone the colour of hammered lead, Uisdean climbed the turning stair to the lamp-room for the first time as its keeper, with Dùghall and what remained of his crew behind him, for they had asked to be there and no one had thought to refuse them. Ailean carried the coal up in a leather sack himself, saying that a light built by six masons' hands ought to be lit with coal carried by at least one of the same six, and Anna, who had grown a head taller in the year since she first slept in the turf hut, went up last of all and touched the newel of the stair as she passed it, the way she had seen old Pàdraig touch a finished stone before he would call it done.

At the top of the stair Uisdean stopped under the lintel, as every keeper after him would stop there without knowing why the stone at that particular step seemed to want a man's eye lifted to it, and asked Dùghall, since he stood nearest, whether the mason who had cut it thought it plumb. Dùghall said that he did, and did not say that he was the mason, and Uisdean, who had signed his own name to the tower's finishing without ever learning that the recommendation for his post had come from these six hands, struck the light.

It caught on the second strike, a coal flame steady behind glass, and threw its first beam out over water that had never in the island's whole memory carried a light so far. Below on the darkening sea, and for a distance beyond guessing, the beam found nothing to answer it that night; but the keepers' daybooks would show, within a very few years, the Company's sailings past that headland doubled, and doubled again, ships running by the new light at hours no master would once have risked the coast at all.

Dùghall stood at the foot of the lamp-room, under the lintel he had cut, and looked up at the names no order had asked for and no order had struck out, and thought that a tower raised by taken hands might yet be made to answer, in its small way, to the hands that raised it, though the light, once lit, answered only to the ships it was built to serve, and asked no man's leave to shine for them and not for him.
