# Of the Petition on the Bench

*AE 98 – 99*

## I. Of the Word from Ros dhomhain

Peigi Ghlas was forty-eight years old and had kept the shrine of Cill ghlas for eleven of them when the word came down from Ros dhomhain that An t-Àrd-mhaor Tolley was going home.

It came the way most word came to Cill ghlas: by a carter who had it from a carter, three days old and worn smooth by the telling, so that by the time it reached her at the shrine door she could not have said which parts were the letter and which were what men had made of the letter on the road. Tolley was old, the carter said, and rich, and going home with honours, whatever honours were, to the country across the mist that none of them had seen and none of them missed. A new man was coming to sit in his chair at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, and this one, the carter went on, straightening up over his empty cart with the pleased look of a man delivering good news, had let it be known that he meant to ask the custodians' council what it thought before he set anything down.

Peigi heard it out, and thanked him, and gave him water for his horse, and went back into the shrine and trimmed the lamp, because it wanted trimming, and because her hands wanted occupying while the rest of her turned the news over.

She had been eleven years at Cill ghlas by then, since her mother died of the wasting cough the year before the Green Death was given its name, and in all that time she had watched three things come down from the Residency wearing the shape of good news, and none of the three had kept it past the first winter. She kept this from the carter, and told the flame instead, that night, the only thing at Cill ghlas old enough to have heard it all before her and unlikely to repeat it.

Cill ghlas was a small place even by the reckoning of that country, a scatter of stone houses and byres along a burn under the shoulder of the hill, with a shrine no bigger than a good barn set into the slope above the last house, where a flame that the roll called Brìde's had been kept since before anyone now living could say by whom. It stood a long day's walk from Baile thais, where the Company's galleries went deepest and the walls had lately gone up round the pithead, and it had given men to those galleries the way every village of that country gave men, in ones and twos, and had them come back, when they came back, grey in the face before their time. Peigi had buried four of her own kin out of the pits in eleven years, and had blessed the coal a household kept back from the vein for the shrine, and had gone up the road herself in her season to lay the custodian's handful at the door and burn it, small, the way it had always been burned. She was custodian in a line that was never written down; nobody had appointed her; the flame had simply been hers to keep since her mother's death, as it had been her mother's before her, and the village had never thought to ask whether there was another way of coming to it.

She had also, since the famine years, kept something else: a slate by the shrine door on which she scratched, in the stag-letters her mother had taught her from an old primer bound in sheepskin, every household of Cill ghlas that had lost someone to the Levy, the fever, or the two winters when the granary at Muileann chrom had been full and the village had not. The slate was short. Its weight had nothing to do with its length.

The letter that named the new order of things came a month later, in the last week of an Dàmhair, carried by a rider sent specially, a young man of Ceann àrsaidh with a good horse and a look of a man pleased with his errand. He asked for the custodian by name, which told Peigi before she had broken the seal that whatever was inside had been thought about by more than one person.

It was from Aonghas Ruadh, custodian of Ceann àrsaidh, and it said, in the plain hand of a man more used to counting than writing, that Robert Haskins, the new An t-Àrd-mhaor, had sent word round every district that the council of custodians would be asked its mind on the tithe before anything was fixed, as a mark, the letter he had sent said, of the new order's good will; and that within the month, before any custodian outside Ros dhomhain had so much as replied to the asking, the tithe had gone up on the boards at every Company store from Baile thais to the coast, fixed, at the new rate, with the seal of Riaghaltas nan Coigreach at the foot of the notice and no mark anywhere on it of any custodian's mind.

Nowhere did Aonghas Ruadh write that this had surprised him. He wrote instead that it should not, in his judgement, be let pass unanswered a second time the way the granary order had been let pass, and the Levy before it, and the asking about the drowned wells at Achadh àrsaidh before that; and that he had it in mind, if enough of the custodians of the east and the hills gathered with him at Ceann àrsaidh, to draw up one paper in one hand that could not be filed apart, settlement by settlement, the way single letters always were. He named a day in am Faoilleach, and asked Peigi to come. Cill ghlas was small, he wrote plainly enough, but its roll of who had been lost was better kept than most, and that was worth more to him than size.

Peigi read the letter twice by the lamp, and then went out and stood a while at the shrine door in the last light, looking down the burn toward the road that went, eventually, everywhere.

## II. Of the Store at Seann Dunn

She was not the first to arrive at Ceann àrsaidh when the day came, nor the last. She had walked it in two days, sleeping the one night at a cousin's house at Muileann dhearg and coming down into the walled town on the second afternoon with her feet sore and her good cloak, the one she kept for burials and for this, rolled tight against the rain in a bag on her back.

Ceann àrsaidh stood behind its old wall on a rise above the eastern road, a town of some size for that country, with a market square inside the gate where the custodians of the nineteen settlements that answered Aonghas Ruadh's letter had begun, by the second day, to make a kind of camp: fires in the lee of the wall, horses picketed along the inside of it, and everywhere the low talk of people comparing what had been done to them, as if putting it side by side might show the shape of it better than any one of them could alone.

It was there, on the second night, over a fire shared with three others, that Peigi heard properly of Seann Dunn.

The custodian who told it was a spare, quiet man named Coinneach Òg, from a fishing town on the far eastern coast that Peigi had never seen and would likely never see again, three days' ride past Ceann àrsaidh by his own account and further than that on foot. He did not tell it as a grievance. He told it the way a man tells a thing he has watched happen slowly enough to have stopped believing it could be stopped.

Haskins, in his first year, had opened a customs house at Seann Dunn, on the coast below Coinneach Òg's own town, and a Company store beside it, the first store the district had had. It came in, Coinneach Òg said, as help. The east had no smith to speak of and no source of iron nearer than a week's cart-journey, and the store sold seed against a bad year and tools against a broken one, on the Company's note, to be paid back in fish or wool or coin when the year turned. Most years the debts had been paid. But there had been a bad year, and then, close behind it, the drowned wells of Achadh àrsaidh and the two winters of the granary, and by now, Coinneach Òg said, turning a stick in the fire without looking up from it, the store's ledgers held the debts of near half the district, entered against named households, generation to come reckoned in against the last.

"They will tell you it is owed fairly," he said. "It is owed. I will not say it was not lent." He looked round the fire at the others. "But there is no year now in which the debt grows less. Only years in which it grows more slowly."

Nobody at the fire had an answer to that, and nobody tried to give one. Peigi thought of her own slate at the shrine door, the names scratched into it, and thought that Coinneach Òg's district must have a slate a great deal longer, and said so; and he said that it did, and that it was not his to keep, since Seann Dunn had no shrine and never had, being new-founded on land the Company had had of the crown for the harbour alone.

It was past midnight before the fire burned down and the talk with it, and Peigi lay that night under her cloak with the wall's stones cold at her back and did not sleep well, turning over the shapes of it: the granary, the drowned wells, the store at Seann Dunn, the Levy that had taken hands from every field in the west and left the harvest to rot standing two winters running at Baile chrom, and now the tithe fixed before the asking about it had so much as reached half the men it was fixed on. She had thought, walking down from Cill ghlas, that she was coming to speak for eleven households and a dead mother's flame. She began, that night at the fire, to understand what Aonghas Ruadh had meant by one hand.

## III. Of the Nineteen at Ceann àrsaidh

They drew it up over three days in the hall behind the market square, a low stone building the town used for its own councils, with a fire at one end and windows too small to let in much of the grey light of that am Faoilleach, so that most of the work was done by lamp, at noon as much as at night.

There were, in the end, custodians of nineteen settlements, from Ceann àrsaidh itself out to the coast where Coinneach Òg's town lay and up into the hills as far as Àth fhionn in the south-east; and it was, so far as any of them could say, and several of the older ones tried to say it, the first time custodians of that many places had sat down together in one room to speak with one voice at all, rather than each writing alone to the Residency and each being answered alone, or not answered, which had been the way of it since the first years of the treaty.

It did not go smoothly. Peigi had thought, walking down, that the grievance would come plain once enough people were in a room with it; she found instead that nineteen people who agreed entirely on what had been done to them could argue for the better part of a day over how to say so. One custodian, an old man from a hill village north of Ceann àrsaidh, wanted every death named, every year, every settlement, on the ground that a list could not be argued with the way a sentence could. Aonghas Ruadh, who had called them and who took, without anyone quite deciding it, the chair of the drafting, said that a list a hundred pages long would be filed unread where a single page might, just possibly, be read; and that they should say the true thing once, plainly, and let the naming wait for another day if it came to naming. The old man was not satisfied, and said so, twice, and in the end put his name to the shorter paper anyway, because there was nothing else on offer to put it to.

The paper that came out of the three days was not long. It set down, in a page and a half of the stag-letters copied fair by Aonghas Ruadh's own clerk, that the tithe had been fixed without the asking that had been promised; that the Levy, though set aside in the districts the harvest had failed in, had never been repealed and could be laid on again at any Commissioner's word; that the granary built of the Dia-thìrich's own coal money had in two winters been the only store between the west and starving, and had been the Company's the whole time; and that the custodians who signed it asked, in the humans' own phrase that Aonghas Ruadh's clerk had found for it in an old letter of the Residency's own, for their council to be consulted before, and not after.

They read it through together on the last evening, each of the nineteen in turn, aloud, in the hall by the lamp, so that no one could say afterward he had not heard every word of what he put his name to. When it came Peigi's turn she read her part with her thumb pressed white against the edge of the sheet, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she had thought it would.

Then they signed it.

It was Ruairidh mac Thormoid, custodian of Àth fhionn, who signed last, and slowest, and it was he, of all the nineteen, that Peigi remembered longest after. A small, bent man, with a hand that shook a little at rest and did not shake at all once the pen was moving, he had walked to Ceann àrsaidh from the south-east, a good many days' road, on his own two feet, refusing every offer of a cart the whole way, as the man who had carried him the last few miles told it round the fire with some wonder. By his own count, and by the count of Àth fhionn's own roll, he was the oldest custodian at the gathering by a great many years: he remembered, or said he remembered, and none there doubted him, a world before the mark was struck at Ros dhomhain, before the strangers' coin had ever passed hand to hand on the island at all, when a fleece was still worth a fleece and nothing needed to be changed into anything else to be worth it.

He read his own line last of all, in a voice gone thin but not unsteady, and then he signed his name in the stag-letters, small and careful, at the foot of the page below all the rest, and beside it, where none of the others had thought to write anything but their names, he added a line of his own, which Aonghas Ruadh's clerk copied into the fair copy exactly as he gave it: *born before the coin*. He offered no more than that. Nobody at the table asked him to.

"They will not read the rest of it," the old man from the hill village said, low, to Peigi, as the paper was being sanded and rolled. "Perhaps they will read that."

## IV. Of the Bench in the Passage

It fell to Peigi to carry it.

She was not sure, afterward, quite how it had fallen to her rather than to Aonghas Ruadh, who had called them all together and might rightly have claimed the carrying as his own; she thought, turning it over on the road, that it was the roll at Cill ghlas that had done it, the slate of names by the shrine door that Aonghas Ruadh had heard was well kept, and that having asked her to come for that reason he had gone on, without either of them quite saying it aloud, to think her the right one to finish it. She did not refuse. She had walked further for less.

She went down to Ros dhomhain in the first days of am Màrt, alone but for a lad of Ceann àrsaidh sent along to carry the second copy and bring word back, three days on the coast road with the petition rolled inside oilcloth against the rain, next to her skin under the good cloak where a purse would have gone had she had one worth guarding.

Ros dhomhain, when she came down into it off the last hill, was larger than any town she had stood in since her one visit to the capital as a girl: the harbour full of masts, the Company's sheds along the quay, the new bridge over the river with the coal-carts crossing it two abreast, and above it all, on its rise, the long grey house of stone that the town had built out of no house the Dia-thìrich had ever had a hand in, Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, with a flag she did not know the meaning of hanging still in the wet air over its gate.

She found the door easily enough; it was finding what lay past the door that took the better part of two days.

The first day she was turned back at the gate by a man in a coat with buttons who told her, not unkindly, that the office that received such papers did not sit on the day she had come, and that she should come again in the morning. She came again in the morning and was let through the gate and left to wait in a yard, and then in a passage, and then, when the light in the passage windows had gone from grey to greyer, told that the office had closed for the day and that she should come again the morning after.

She came again.

The passage she was shown into the third time was of stone, narrow, lit by lamps set in the wall at long intervals because no window opened onto it at all, and it smelled, she noticed at once and never afterward forgot, of lamp-soot: not the clean burnt smell of a shrine flame, which she knew as well as she knew her own hands, but a heavier, closer smell, oil and smoke folded close in a space with no wind to carry it out. There was a bench along one wall, of the same grey stone as the passage, worn pale in the middle where a great many people had sat waiting before her, and she sat on it, with the lad from Ceann àrsaidh standing at her shoulder because there was room on the bench for one, and waited.

She waited the better part of that morning. People passed along the passage on errands of their own, humans mostly, in the black coats of the Residency's clerks, and one or two Dia-thìrich among them, dressed in the same coats, who did not look at her as they went by, or looked and looked away, she could not afterward have said which. Doors opened and closed along the passage. Somewhere beyond one of them a bell rang at intervals, for reasons she never learned.

At last a door opened nearer to hand than the rest, and a young man put his head out and asked, in Dia-thìris good enough to be understood and poor enough that she knew at once it was not his first tongue, whether the custodian with the paper for the Àrd-mhaor's office would come in.

He was younger than she had expected, no more than five-and-twenty by the look of him, with ink on two fingers of his right hand and a high desk behind him stacked with paper in a way that told her, before he said a word, that hers would not be the only paper on it by the day's end. He asked her name and the name of the settlement, and wrote both down, carefully, in the humans' letters, checking the spelling of Cill ghlas with her twice. He asked whether she had come far. She said that she had, and that eighteen others besides her had put their names to what she carried, from as many settlements, meeting as one body for the first time that the roll of any of their towns recorded.

He said that this was noted, and thanked her for the trouble of the journey, in a tone she thought was meant kindly and might even have been kind.

Then he took the petition from her hands, unrolled it just far enough to see that it was indeed a petition and not some other paper mistakenly brought, rolled it again, and laid it on the pile at the corner of the high desk, a pile she saw, in the moment his hand left it, was neither the top of the stack nor the bottom of it but somewhere unremarkable in the middle, among papers she could not read the headings of and had no way of knowing were older or newer or of more or less weight than her own.

Then he bent his head again to the sheet he had been writing on when she was shown in, and went on with it.

Peigi stood where she was a moment longer than there was any reason to stand. She did not know, afterward, what she had been waiting for; not an answer, she told herself on the road home, for she had known before she came down off the last hill above Ros dhomhain that no answer would be given her that day, or that week, whatever came after. Perhaps she had been waiting only to see the young man look up again. He did not.

She thanked him, because it seemed the thing to do, and he returned some answer she did not fully catch, and she turned and went back out along the passage, past the bench worn pale in the middle, and out through the yard, and out through the gate, into the grey light of Ros dhomhain, with the lad from Ceann àrsaidh following a step behind her and neither of them saying anything at all until they were past the bridge.

They went home.

## V. Of Brìde's Lamp

The answer, when it came, did not come.

Through the rest of that year and into the next word passed among the nineteen settlements, as it always passed, slowly and by carter and by letter three days worn on the road, and none of it was an answer from Ros dhomhain. The tithe stood at the rate it had been fixed at before the asking. The Levy stayed unrepealed on its shelf, ready, the custodians said to one another, for the first bad harvest that gave the Residency an excuse to take it down again. At Seann Dunn, Coinneach Òg wrote once, briefly, to say that the store's ledgers had grown by one more name that winter, his own cousin's, and that he supposed it made no difference now whether that was written into a petition or not. Aonghas Ruadh wrote to the Residency twice more, in his own name, asking only whether the paper of am Faoilleach had been received and read. To the first letter there was no reply at all. To the second, in the autumn, a clerk's hand — not, Peigi was fairly sure from the letters, the same clerk's hand — wrote back that the matter remained under consideration.

Peigi kept the slate at the shrine door as she had always kept it, and in the course of that year and the next she added three more names to it, none of them, this time, of the famine or the Levy, but of the ordinary business of a country with a hill in it and men going down into the hill: a fall of stone at Baile thais that took two crews out of Cill ghlas's own households, and an old man of the village who had gone down every day of his working life and finally did not come up one evening in an t-Sultain, of nothing that any physician, human or otherwise, could put a name to but the descent, done too many years too many times.

On a still evening near the end of an Dùbhlachd, with the first hard frost of that winter white on the burn-stones outside, Peigi went up to the shrine at Cill ghlas as she went every evening of her life, to see that the flame that was called Brìde's had not gone low.

It had not; it had burned steadily through the whole of that year of petitions and answers not given, as it had burned through the Levy and the Green Death and the years before either, needing from her only what it had always needed: oil enough, and a wick trimmed square, and someone to come up the hill in the cold and see to it. She knelt at the small stone trough before the flame and drew the old shears from the ledge where they always lay, and took the wick between finger and thumb, and trimmed it, careful, the way her mother had taught her, until the flame stood up clean and even and threw its light square across the little room and out through the open door into the frost beyond.

She did not know, kneeling there, whether the petition that had gone into the pile in the passage at Ros dhomhain would ever be taken up off it, read, answered, or refused; whether Aonghas Ruadh's third letter would fare better than his first two; whether Ruairidh mac Thormoid, who remembered a world before the coin, would live to see any Commissioner's answer at all, or whether his line at the foot of the fair copy — *born before the coin* — would be the last word of his that anyone at Ros dhomhain ever troubled to read. She only knew the wick was trimmed, and the flame stood clean, and that in the morning she would go down to the village and see who needed seeing to, and that this, at least, no clerk's pile could take from her hands.

She banked the fire, low, for the night, and went down the hill in the dark, and the frost held all across Cill ghlas until morning.
