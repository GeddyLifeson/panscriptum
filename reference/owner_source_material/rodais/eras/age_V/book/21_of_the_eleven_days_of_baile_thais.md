# Of Stad-oibre Baile thais

*Part Four: Of An Togail, the Hunger and the Drought*

*AE 77 – 79*

## I. Of the Spring after the Sickness

The dying at Baile thais had stopped by the time the first primroses came up along the moor-road, but the town did not look like a town that had stopped dying. Catrìona nic Eachainn walked out to the pit-mouth in the grey of that spring with her shawl over her mouth from old habit, though Anne Colby had told her months before that the habit did nothing and she might as well breathe free. She kept it anyway. A thing the body has learned to do against fear does not stop because the fear is told it was wrong.

Below Cill ghlas the ground still showed where the town's burial field had been cut wider twice in one winter. Grass had not yet taken the new turned earth; it lay the colour of a scar, paler than the field round it, and the shrine women left dishes of meal on the mounds because that was what you did when you did not know what else to do. Catrìona had buried her mother's sister there in am Faoilleach, and a niece a fortnight after, and had gone on leading her shift the whole time because A' Chompanaidh's book of hours did not stop for grief, and because thirty other households were waiting on her wage same as her own.

Anne Colby's sick-house still stood at Achadh dhomhain, an hour's walk north among the deep galleries, its canvas roof gone grey with two winters' smoke, and word came down that she meant to keep it open through the summer for the crews at Àth ìseal too, though the worst of the sickness there had passed. The camp people called it kindly, the only house of the strangers' building that they called kindly, and it was said without much thinking about it that this was because Colby herself had washed the dead with her own hands rather than sending a servant to do it, which was more than Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's own ministers had done.

At Cnoc gharbh, to the south along the ridge, the people who had worked the southern galleries did not wait for the sickness to be called past. They took their pots and their bedding and their children and walked back up to the old village on the hill their grandfathers had held before the pits, and left the camp huts standing empty with the doors swinging. A Chompanaidh's agent rode out to look at the empty huts and wrote in his book that the crews had deserted their posts, as though a plague were a kind of laziness. Nobody at Baile thais blamed Cnoc gharbh. Half of them had thought of doing the same and had stayed only because Baile thais, unlike Cnoc gharbh, had no old village on a hill to go back to. It had never been anything but the camp.

Catrìona was seven-and-thirty that spring, crew-leader of the seventh shift at the Baile thais gallery, a broad, dark, plain-spoken woman with a scar through one eyebrow from a fall of stone as a girl, and hands that the coal had got into so deep that even scrubbed they looked bruised. She had led the seventh shift for eleven years, since the crew-leader before her, an old man named Griogar, had coughed himself to a standstill and put the tally-stick in her hand himself and said she was the only one of them who would not be talked round by the agent's smile. Griogar was dead two winters now, of the ordinary lung-sickness that took hewers, not the green plague; he had at least been spared that.

The camp still kept, over most of its doors, a single hawk's feather nailed to the lintel, the overseers' reports calling it a matter for punishment and the overseers themselves too busy that spring to trouble over it. The hawk-priests had gone unwelcome in the camps for years now. A feather cost nothing, though, and a man could nail it up himself in the dark, and Catrìona had one over her own door, put there by her mother before her, and had never taken it down nor troubled to say a prayer under it either. It was less a faith to her than a habit, like the shawl over her mouth, and she thought, without much reflection, that a people who had been told for two generations that the strangers' book had all the say in what a hill might hold could be forgiven for keeping quietly to older things, whether or not they believed the old things any harder for the keeping.

There was no one at Cathair dhearg to write to about any of it, not that year. Ceann a' Chùraim's seat, Ceann a' Chùraim, had stood empty since Alasdair Liath died six years before, and Comhairle a' Chùraim had not agreed on who should fill it, being much taken up, so it was said, with the business of the tithe and the petitions of the towns, and finding, as councils will, that an empty chair causes less argument than a full one. Catrìona did not think about the empty seat often. But she thought of it that spring, walking past the burial field to the pit-mouth, because it meant that whatever she was about to ask, there would be no one great enough at the capital to be asked twice.

## II. Of the Agent's Answer

The agent at Baile thais that year was a man named Silas Crewe, thirty-seven himself, sent up from Ros dhomhain the autumn before to replace an agent who had died of the same sickness he had been sent to manage. He was neither the worst of A' Chompanaidh's men nor the best: he kept his ledgers straight, did not water the lamp-oil, and had once, to general surprise, paid a full week's wage to a crew that lost two days to a flooded sump through no fault of its own. He had also, in the same season, signed the order that kept the deepest gallery working through the height of A' Phlàigh Uaine, on the word of a surveyor from Ros dhomhain who had never gone down it himself.

Catrìona went to him on a wet morning with three other crew-leaders behind her, though it was understood between them without saying that she would do the talking. Crewe's office was a plank room built onto the counting-house, with a stove that smoked and a window that did not shut properly, and he stood behind his table rather than sitting, which she took, rightly, for a kind of politeness meant to look like the opposite.

"I'm asking you to close the third and the fourth gallery," she said. "Not the whole working. The third and fourth, where the dust hangs, till the surgeon says different."

"The surgeon has not said the dust does it," Crewe said. "He has said he does not know what does it."

"He has said it is worst where the dust is worst. He told me that himself, at my aunt's burying."

Crewe looked at the paper in front of him rather than at her, which she also took rightly, for he had the figures of the quarter's yield laid out on it and knew them without needing to look. "The yield in the third and fourth is a third of the whole working," he said. "If I close them I have to write to Ros dhomhain and say why, and the why I have to give them is a physician's guess. I can't close a gallery on a guess, Catrìona. The yield must not fall. Those are the words I was sent with, near enough."

"The yield," Catrìona said, "or the dead."

"I did not choose the words," Crewe said, and for a moment, briefly, something in his face agreed with her, and then put itself away again, as a man puts away a coin he has been shown he cannot spend. "I'll write up what you've told me. That's what I can do."

"Write it up," she said, and did not thank him for it, and went out into the rain with the other three crew-leaders behind her, none of whom had said a word, and none of whom needed to; they had heard the same answer given a dozen times in a dozen shapes since the sickness began, and they knew by now what shape came after a shape like that one.

## III. Of the Coal-Tub

She let three weeks pass before she stood on the tub. She was not, whatever the camp said of her afterward, a woman who acted from a single hot morning; she went first to the custodian of Baile thais, an old woman named Sìneag who kept what rolls there were for a town that had never properly had a custodian's house, only a room over the smithy, and asked her what the old law said about a hill and its rest, in case there was a word from An Aois Naomh she could set against A' Chompanaidh's paper. Sìneag found nothing that would serve; Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig spoke of the coal burned, not the coal cut, and there was no law anywhere, old or new, about how deep a gallery might be worked while men sickened in it, because no one before this age had needed to write one.

So on a grey morning near the middle of the summer, with the shift gathering at the pit-mouth in the usual half-light and the usual noise, wet boots and lamp-checks and somebody's dog getting under everybody's feet, Catrìona turned over one of the empty coal-tubs that stood by the weigh-house, climbed up on it, and told the whole shift, which by then was near enough the whole working, since word had gone round in the night, what she had asked Crewe for and what he had answered.

"He says the yield must not fall," she said, from the tub, her voice carrying flat and hard over the yard the way it carried down a gallery when a fall had to be called. "I say the yield can wait on the living. I'm not asking any man or woman here to leave their family without bread. I'm asking that nobody go below today, into the third or the fourth, until Crewe writes to Ros dhomhain and gets back a better answer than a guess. If that costs us the day's pay, it costs us the day's pay. I'll stand it same as you."

There was no great shout of agreement, no crowd surging forward with fists in the air, of the kind the camp songs like to make of such mornings. What there was instead was a long quiet, boots shifting in the mud, a woman coughing somewhere at the back who was not sick, only cold, and then, one by one, men and women turning their lamps down and setting them on the ground rather than carrying them to the gallery mouths. Raghnall Breac, a hewer's boy of seven who was too young that year to be paid a man's wage and was there only to carry water and pick slate from the tubs, stood at the front the whole time with his water-pail still in his hand, not understanding all of it, understanding enough of it to stay.

Catrìona stepped down off the tub, and rather than going to stand over the crowd or address them further, sat down on it, in the mud, in her working clothes, and folded her arms, and did not go below. She had planned no speech to make with her body; the speech made itself, and it was the one that held. A woman sitting on an upturned tub, arms folded, in the rain, saying nothing further, is a harder thing to argue with than a woman shouting, because there is nothing in it to answer.

Crewe came out within the hour, saw the yard full and still, and went back in again without a word to anyone. By noon two riders had gone from the counting-house, one south to Ros dhomhain and one, it was said later though nobody at Baile thais ever had it proved, north to Muileann chrom, where Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill Maor kept the staff of the Stewards and had, by then, eight-and-fifty years and no love at all for a stoppage he had not ordered.

Sìneag came down from her room over the smithy at midday and stood a while at the edge of the yard. She joined no crowd, being too old by her own reckoning to sit in mud for anybody, yet she kept her place at the edge of it a long while too, and when a young overseer's clerk asked her, civilly enough, whether the custodian meant to order the crews back to work, she told him that a custodian's rolls recorded what was done and did not order what should be, and that he was welcome to write that down too if he thought it worth the ink. He left it unwritten, and stayed near her the rest of the afternoon regardless, as though an old woman who would say such a thing to a clerk of A' Chompanaidh was worth keeping an eye on, which in its own way told Catrìona, watching from the tub, that the day's stand had already gone further than the yard.

## IV. Of the Eleven Days

The first three days were the easiest, because the town still had its own bread and its own stored meal, and because there was something in the crowd of the yard, the fires kept up after dark, the songs Fearchar sang years later having their true root in mornings such as these, that felt for a while like a kind of holiday, wrongly earned yet taken all the same. By the fourth day the easiness had gone out of it. Catrìona took to walking the town each evening. A crew-leader who does not walk her own streets in a stoppage she called is asking to be thought a coward, and there, walking, she counted, without meaning to at first and then quite deliberately, the loaves standing in the windows.

There was one store in Baile thais that sold bread, and it belonged to A' Chompanaidh, same as the huts and the weigh-house and the smithy's lease. On the first evening there had been loaves stacked three deep behind the glass. On the fourth there were perhaps a dozen. On the sixth, six. She did not need Crewe to tell her what he already knew, which was that a camp that owns its only store can starve a stoppage without lifting a hand against it, and does not even have to be cruel to do it; it need only wait.

Ros gharbh's fishermen, fifteen miles off on the southern coast, heard of the stoppage by the seventh day, nobody afterward could say quite how fast such things travelled, and began carrying salt fish up into the hills by night on the moor tracks, past A' Chompanaidh's own watch at the crossroads, and left it at back doors without asking payment or thanks. It fell short of feeding eleven days of a mining town, but it kept every child at Baile thais from starving outright, and the custodians' roll, when it came to be written years later at Muileann chaol, called it the one trade of that whole decade that ran the right way, from the giving hand to the needing one and asking nothing back.

On the ninth day Crewe sent word that he would open the store's back room, where flour was kept for the counting-house's own table, to any family with children under five, if the crews would go below. Catrìona refused it in front of the yard. She wanted the children fed as much as any mother there; what she would not have was a store opened halfway, which stays owned however it is opened, breaking a strike along exactly the line A' Chompanaidh would choose to break it. "Feed them all or feed none," she told him, at his own door, "and if you feed none, I'll answer for that to God and not to you." He did not open the room.

On the tenth night it rained without stopping, and half the yard fires went out, and a woman named Beathag Ruadh, whose husband had died in the third gallery two winters before the stoppage and who had five children and no bread left at all, came to Catrìona in the dark. She asked her plainly, as a tired woman asks another, no accusation in it, whether it would not be better to send the crews back down and keep what strength they had for another day, another year, since the third and fourth galleries would go on being worked whether Baile thais stood in the yard or not. Catrìona had no good answer for her, and gave her the only one she had, which was that she did not know, and sat with her by what was left of the fire until Beathag's children were asleep against her. In the morning Beathag went to the yard with everyone else and did not go below.

## V. Of the Twelfth Morning

On the twelfth morning Catrìona went down to the store herself before first light, before anyone else was in the yard to see her go, because she did not want to be watched deciding. She stood at the shutters, which had not yet been opened for the day, and looked in through the gap where the wood had warped, at shelves she already knew were bare, having counted them bare the evening before, and stood there long enough that the cold came up through her boots.

There was nothing in the shop but the counter, the scale, and, on the top shelf, out of easy reach, a single sack of meal that Crewe, she supposed, had kept back for his own table, or for a bribe he had not yet had cause to pay. She looked at that sack a long while too. Then she walked back up through the town, past the shrine women already at the burial field with their dishes of meal that they, at least, had never stopped finding somewhere, and came to the yard, and climbed up on the same tub, which had stood in the same place, rained on, eleven days running, and told the shift to go below.

"We did not win the third and fourth," she said. "I won't tell you we did, because you'd know it for a lie inside the hour. What we won, if we won anything, is that they know now it can be done. Eleven days is not nothing. Go below. Feed your children. I'll go below with you." And she did, that morning, walking into the second gallery herself for the first time in eleven days, her lamp lit, her hands shaking a little in a way she did not let the shift see, and the work went on as though nothing had stopped it, which was the worst part of going back, that the hill did not care one way or the other how long a person had refused it.

The third and fourth galleries were not closed. They ran another four years before the seam in them thinned and A' Chompanaidh abandoned them for reasons of its own that had nothing to do with anyone's asking. No answer of any kind ever came from Ros dhomhain, nor, so far as anyone at Baile thais ever learned, from the empty chair at Cathair dhearg, which sat empty still.

## VI. Of the Granary

Within the month a wagon-train came up from Muileann chrom with timber and stone, and A' Chompanaidh began building a granary of its own beside the pit-mouth, a squat building with one door and a lock, and word went round the camps, all of them, not Baile thais alone, that from now on no household was to keep more grain in its own house than a week's ration, the rest to be held in A' Chompanaidh's granary and drawn against a crew-leader's tally. The order was read out at Baile thais by an overseer none of them had seen before, and it carried, at the foot of the paper, the mark of Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill Maor, Steward at Muileann chrom, a man Catrìona had met twice in her life and liked neither time, though she allowed, thinking it over afterward, that he had likely signed a good many orders that summer he had not troubled to read closely, being a man more given to the staff's ceremony than its use.

Sìneag the custodian said, when she heard of it, that she had never in all her rolls read of a granary built to answer a stoppage rather than a harvest, and that this alone told her more of what the stoppage had done than anything Crewe had put in writing. Catrìona thought there was truth in that, though it was cold comfort to a household now walking to a locked building with a tally-stick to ask for its own week's bread.

The overseers enforced the granary rule with more spite than the order itself asked for, as overseers on the whole will, and searched huts for hidden meal, and twice that autumn fined a family a day's wage for a sack of flour found under a bed, though the sack, in both cases, had come up from Ros gharbh by night and never passed through A' Chompanaidh's own scales at all, a fact the overseers either did not know or chose not to ask about.

## VII. Of the Road East

Catrìona was not dismissed outright, which would at least have had the honesty of a plain blow. She was, instead, over the two years that followed, quietly refused. The seventh shift was given to another crew-leader before the winter was out, a young man loyal to Crewe and glad of the wage; Catrìona was offered work at the surface, sorting slate, at half a hewer's pay, and took it because there was nothing else, and kept it eleven months, until the overseer over the sorting-sheds, a different man from Crewe but reading from the same book, told her the sheds had more hands than they needed and hers, this quarter, were the ones not needed.

She walked to Cnoc fhiadhaich and asked for work and was turned away at the gate before she had said her name twice; the agent there knew her by sight, which told her plainly enough what had gone round in A' Chompanaidh's own letters, whatever face Crewe himself might have kept at Baile thais. She walked to Àth ìseal, and to the timber camp beyond it, and had the same door shut in the same quiet way each time, no shouting, no threats, only a man looking past her shoulder and saying there was nothing that quarter, come again another season.

She did not, in all that time, ask the guild of Muileann chiar for anything, having no claim on it and no trade in silver, and it did not occur to her until the second year of her refusing that a hill which owed A' Chompanaidh nothing might be the one hill left on the island that would look at her without first looking at Crewe's letters. Sìneag, when Catrìona came to the smithy room to say her goodbyes, gave her what little the custodian's own store could spare, a heel of cheese and a pair of stockings darned past their first darning, and said only that Baile thais would remember the tub longer than it remembered the granary. Catrìona did not fully believe it, standing there with the cheese in her hand and the road east still ahead of her.

In the autumn of the third year after the stoppage, with her savings gone and her sister's household unable to keep her much longer without going short themselves, Catrìona took what she had, a change of clothes, her mother's ring, and the tally-stick Griogar had given her eleven years before, which she had kept though it named a shift she no longer led, and walked east over the hills toward Muileann chiar, two days and the best part of a third, sleeping the first night in a shepherd's bothy and the second under a wall, with the country changing under her feet from the black spoil-heaps of the coal hills to the paler grey of the silver country, where An Seann Urram for the vein still held in a way it had stopped holding at Baile thais some years before.

Comann an Airgid, had never worked easily with A' Chompanaidh, holding as it did its own seam under the compact Ùna nic Choinnich had won from Lisle, which the humans had never managed to unwrite; and its head that year, a woman who had stood at the adit with the ledgers as a girl, heard Catrìona's whole account without interrupting it once, asked her three questions about the ore she had never in her life seen, and put the matter of taking her on to the guild's own hands rather than deciding it herself, which was the guild's way in most things that mattered. The hands voted to take her, by a wide margin, and Catrìona had little doubt it was less for love of her than for the pleasure of taking from A' Chompanaidh a hewer it had gone to some trouble to be rid of.

A' Chompanaidh wrote to the guild within the season, in the correct and distant hand of its counting-house at Ros dhomhain, asking that Catrìona nic Eachainn, lately of Baile thais, be sent back to answer for the stoppage of two years before. The guild's answer was drafted by its own clerk, read aloud once in the guild hall to general satisfaction, folded, sealed, and sent south by the ordinary post; and what it said, whether short or long, courteous or otherwise, no one at Muileann chiar troubled to keep a second copy of, thinking the matter closed the day it was sent. The letter that was filed against it at Ros dhomhain has not survived either. Of the words themselves, then, nothing more can be told than that they went, and that Catrìona nic Eachainn, refiner of Comann an Airgid from that autumn to the end of her days, never again set foot at Baile thais, nor, so far as the rolls know, ever wished to.
