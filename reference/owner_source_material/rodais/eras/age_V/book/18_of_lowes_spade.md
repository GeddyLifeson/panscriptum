# Of Lowe's Spade

*AE 63 – 68*

> *Ithidh Mòd an neach a dh'itheas an cnoc.*
>
> Mòd will eat whoever eats the hill.

## I. Of the Coloured Flame at Muileann bheag

Four beacons burned on the coasts of Dia-thìr by the sixty-third year of the strangers, and three of them still burned oil, the old way, a wick fed from a cistern and trimmed each watch by a keeper who climbed the stair in the dark and came down again at dawn with his fingers black. The fourth was at Muileann bheag on the north shore, and it burned coal.

Edwin Lowe came up from Ros dhomhain to see it that winter, riding a Company mule with a satchel of paper across his knees, sent by the Mission to look into a complaint the beacon's own keepers had laid before the custodians and the custodians had, in their way, laid before nobody. He was thirty-eight years old that year, a cleric without a parish, thin in the shoulder and going grey early, and he had been eleven years on the island without once finding the thing he had come to find, which was proof, of one kind or another, that the god his Church preached had been here before him, or that no god had, and either answer would have done, so long as it was an answer.

He had come out from an Tìr Thall a young man with a licence to preach and a private letter of introduction to a bishop who had since died without reading it, and had drifted, in the way of men whose first errand fails them quietly, into the Mission's smaller work: the copying of custodians' rolls that some clerk in the Residency wanted translated, the measuring of ruins nobody else had time for, the answering of complaints too strange for the Company's own agents to be troubled with. Eleven years of it had made him into a kind of scholar the island had no name for and did not quite trust, a man who asked the custodians questions a Mission cleric had no business asking and wrote down their answers in a hand too careful to be idle curiosity. The road to Muileann bheag, three days of mule-track and one of open coast, was nothing to him by then; he had ridden worse for less.

The keeper at Muileann bheag was a broad, weathered man named Ailig mac Griogair, who had tended the light since it burned oil and had asked, three winters running, to be let go back to the old lamp. He took Lowe up the stair himself.

"There," he said, at the top, and pointed down through the lantern glass at the black water below the point, where the beam swung slow across the swell. "You'll not see it. None of your people see it. But it's there."

Lowe looked, and saw firelight on water, the ordinary orange wash of a flame thrown wide by a curved glass, breaking and mending on the chop. He said as much.

"It's a green under the orange," said Ailig. "A green like nothing that was ever in a flame before, and it lies on the water a long way out, further than the beam itself should carry, and it comes and it goes with no wind to account for it. The herring won't come in under it. Ask any boat on this coast."

"And before the beacon burned coal?"

"The oil flame never did it. No flame ever did it before this one." Ailig's own son had gone twice to the custodians of the town about it, and had been heard and sent away with nothing added to the roll, for the custodians could find no law the flame broke; a man might burn what he liked in his own lamp, and the beacon was the Company's now in all but the keeping of it. "They say it drives the herring off. I say it lays a colour on the sea that was never laid there before, and that a colour laid on the sea is a thing worth minding, whatever it does to the fish."

Lowe wrote the complaint down as he had been asked, word for word, and climbed down the stair, and stood a long while on the point in the cold with his collar up, looking at the black water and the orange wash on it, trying to see the green the keeper saw, and the more he stared the more certain he became that there was nothing there to see, only firelight doing what firelight had always done to water, breaking and mending, breaking and mending.

He came back to the point twice more before he left the district, once at dusk and once before the dawn watch, and stood the same span of the same cold each time, and saw the same nothing. On the second visit Ailig's own son, a boy of perhaps twelve who had never known the beacon burn anything but coal, came down and stood beside him without being asked and looked where he looked.

"Well?" said Lowe.

"You're looking too hard," the boy said. "It's not a thing you look for. It's a thing you see, the way you see it's raining before you feel the first drop."

Lowe asked him what colour it was, precisely, and the boy considered a long while, the way a child considers a question put to him seriously for the first time, and said only that it was a green that made him think of nothing else he had ever seen, which was, Lowe wrote afterward, the most honest answer given him in eleven years on the island, and the least useful.

He wrote in his own book, that night, in the humans' letters, a single line that he did not send to the Mission with the rest: *I begin to think there are two islands here, laid one over the other, and I have been sent to survey only mine.*

Within the season the herring came back in under the light as they had always come, in their numbers, as if the sea had settled some dispute of its own and moved on; and the letter the fishermen of Muileann bheag had first sent was withdrawn in a second letter, which the Company kept beside the first, and neither was ever answered. The keepers did not change the lamp. They never did, in all the years it burned.

## II. Of the Dig at Làrach an Teampaill

Lowe's true errand that season lay further south and east, at a low ruin among gorse and standing water that the island called Làrach an Teampaill, the temple that had stood, empty and unclaimed by any faith the Dia-thìrich kept, since before the oldest roll of any custodian house began. Three ages of scholars had measured it and gone away no wiser. Lowe meant to be the fourth, and to dig where the others had only measured.

He got Company labourers for the digging, six men off the coal contracts at Doire ghlas who wanted the winter wage and had never held a spade for anything but coal, and a custodian of Doire ghlas, Griogal Odhar, came out with them most mornings to watch, never to work, and sat on the fallen lintel with his hands on his knees the way a man sits at a wake.

The ruin itself was low, little more to the eye than a rise of gorse and grey stone in a hollow of poor grazing land, with a single lintel still standing over what had once been a door and the rest of the walls sunk to their footings under a century of turf. Sheep grazed the mound in every season but this one, and the shepherds of Àth mhòr, whose flocks it was, had turned them off it without being asked, and would not say why beyond that it had always been the custom, on the days the ground was opened, to keep beasts from a grave until the grave was shut again.

They opened the ground inside the western wall first, where the old measurings had marked an altar, and found in the first week worked stone below the turf, cut square and laid without mortar, in courses too even for any wall the labourers' own grandfathers had raised. Lowe measured each course as it came clear, and noted that the stone was not of the district, a hard pale grey unlike the darker rock of the hills round about, and must have been carried some distance, by what means over what road he could not say, for no road the island now kept ran anywhere near the place.

In the second week they found bone, burnt through, packed in a shallow pit beside the altar stone with ash and a scatter of small worked flints that answered to no tool any smith of the island had ever made or named. The bone crumbled at a touch, and Lowe had the labourers stop digging round it and clear the earth away with their hands instead, a spoonful at a time, so that what came up came up whole; and it was slow, cold work, the men on their knees in the wet through the shortening days, breathing on their fingers between handfuls, and none of them complained of the wage, though more than one of them complained, quietly, to Griogal Odhar, of the work itself.

The labourers stopped there. Lowe pressed them to go deeper, and the eldest of the six, a broad quiet man called Tormod Ruadh, drove his spade upright in the loose earth at the pit's edge and would not lift it again.

"It's bone under an altar that was old before the vein was cut," he said. "I'll dig coal all my life and glad of the wage. I won't dig a man out of his own grave for a stranger's paper."

Lowe did not press him further. He wrote out what the pit held, the stone, the ash, the flints, the burnt bone he judged too old and too cold in the ground to be any recent thing, and sent word to Griogal Odhar to bring the custodians of the district to see it before he covered it again.

Four of them came, two days later, from Doire ghlas and Doire fhiadhaich and Àth mhòr, walking the last mile together though their three towns lay a half-day apart from one another, and stood at the pit's edge a long while without speaking, the way the labourers had stood, and then Griogal Odhar crouched and turned one flint over in his palm and set it down exactly where it had lain.

"It is not ours," he said. "The stone is not ours, and the bone is not ours, and whoever laid it there is not ours, and I do not think the god it was laid for is any god that is prayed to on this island now, if it is prayed to anywhere at all." He stood. "Cover it again. Say the word over it that we say over any grave whose kin cannot be found, and no more than that."

Lowe asked what word that was, and was told, and it was said, briefly, in Dia-thìris, by all four custodians together over the reopened pit, and none of them offered to teach it to him, nor did he ask twice.

Before they left he asked Griogal Odhar one thing more: whether any order of the island's own faiths, the hawk-order that kept the vein's old rites, or the order that held the capital's shrines, or any other, claimed the ruin or the bone under it as its own.

"None," said Griogal. "I have heard it said that Mòd himself does not claim what was old before the vein was found, for a god that eats the hill can only eat what grew in the hill's own time, and this was here before the hill was a hill worth eating. Whether that is true, no one has told me. No order has ever asked to be given this ground, and none has ever been refused it, for none has asked."

He wrote afterward, in the letter-book he kept for the Mission and did send: *They agree with my own reading of the stone entirely, and add nothing to it. I had thought a people so old in their own land would have some tale for a ruin older still. They have none. I begin to wonder whether that is the more remarkable fact of the two.*

## III. Of the Pillar and the Column

There were two other marks in the district that Lowe had been asked to copy before he left it: a pillar of grey stone near Cnoc chiar, weathered past most reading, and a column near Cnoc ghorm that stood a little taller and a little plainer, with lines cut round its shoulder that might have been words or might have been only the marks of the tool that dressed it.

He went to Cnoc chiar first, in the spring, and spent nine days with paper and charcoal rubbing the pillar's face a hand's width at a time, working from the ground up in the wet wind off the hill, while an old woman of the town below came up most afternoons to sit on the stone's shadow-side and watch him without a word of greeting either way. She brought her own work with her, a basket of wool she carded as she watched, and by the fourth day Lowe had stopped expecting her to speak and had begun, instead, to find some comfort in the small sound of the carders, back and forth, under the wind. The pillar stood alone on a shoulder of open hill with no wall or ditch about it that any digging had ever found, and the grass grew close about its foot as it grew nowhere else on that slope, cropped fine by sheep that came to no other patch so willingly.

On the ninth day, folding his last sheet, he asked her, in the little Dia-thìris he had, whether the pillar had a name.

"It is a stone," she said. "It had a name once. We have not kept it."

"Was it a wicked thing, that the name was lost?"

She thought about that longer than she had thought about anything else he had asked her in nine days. "No," she said at last. "Names are lost the way anything is lost that nobody carries any more. Nobody has carried this one in a long time. It does not mean the stone minds."

He set her answer beside Samuel Wren's old copy of the obelisk at Cnoc bheag, made some forty years before and sent over the sea to scholars who had never troubled to answer it, and judged, laying the two side by side on an inn table at Cnoc chiar, that the hands were not the same hand, and that neither pillar's writing answered to the other's, letter for letter, though something in the shapes of both put him in mind of the same idea, the way two strangers' faces can share an expression without sharing a feature. He copied Wren's copy twice more into his own book, from memory and then checked against the paper, for he did not trust the Company's post with the original and would not risk the only rubbing he had of the obelisk on a road he had never travelled.

At Cnoc ghorm, in the autumn, he copied the column in four days, quicker work, for less of it had worn away; and he wrote in the last page of his notebook, in a smaller hand than the rest, as if he grudged the paper it took: *Like the pillar, but not near enough to help. I do not think these three markers, the obelisk, the pillar, the column, were raised by three peoples, or by one people at three times only. I think they were raised by one people who no longer needed, by the third marker, to say all that the first marker said, because by then everyone who read it already knew. That is the mark of an old and settled thing, not a new one. And no one now living can read a word of it.*

He left Dia-thìr the following year, on a Company ship out of Ros dhomhain, with his notebooks in a sealed case under his berth and nothing else of the island in his baggage that he had not paid for. He did not write again to the Mission that had sent him, nor, so far as the custodians' rolls record, did any answer ever come back over the sea to the stone he had copied, or the bone he had uncovered and covered again. What he had come to prove, in eleven years and more, he never once claimed to have proved; and the last line of his own book, set down the night before he sailed, said only that he had looked a long while at a colour he could not see, and had come, by looking, to believe in it a little, which was as much faith as the island's own god had ever asked of the strangers, and more than most of them gave.

## IV. Of Voss's Glass-House

*Here the copyists of the cellar at Muileann chaol leave Lowe at the rail of his ship, and turn to a man who did not sail.*

Peter Voss had come to the island as a glazier under Company contract, to set the Residency's windows and the panes of the counting-house at Ros dhomhain, and when his contract ran out in the sixty-sixth year he did not take passage home with the rest of his gang. He gave no reason to the clerk who struck his name from the muster roll, only that he had found white sand on the shore below Baile Mòr dhìreach the like of which he had not found in three counties of an Tìr Thall, and a hill of good charcoal-wood behind it, and he meant to see what the two of them, put together in a furnace, would make.

He built the furnace himself over a summer, with two Dia-thìrich labourers he taught as he went, a stone drum lined with clay and fired for a week before he trusted it with sand, and for that whole week he slept beside it on a heap of sacking, waking every few hours to feed the fire and read its colour through a slit in the clay, a trick of the trade he had learned as a boy in his father's own glass-house over the sea and had thought, coming to the island, he would never use again. His two labourers thought him half mad for the sleeping, and said so, and stayed to watch all the same.

What came out of the furnace the first three times was a black cinder, no glass at all, and the town's children carried it off for luck and the custodians' clerk recorded, dryly, as "Voss's first stones, being none." Voss broke each failed batch himself with a hammer before it cooled fully, turning the pieces in his hand and muttering over them in his own tongue, and each time changed some small thing, the heat of the fire, the grain of the sand, the ash he mixed into it, and tried again. The fourth firing, on a wet morning in an Iuchar, gave him a pane the size of two hands, pale green, thick as a wall, and clear enough to see a gull through.

He held it up to the light in the furnace door, and one of his two apprentices, a girl of the town called Cairistìona nic Griogair who had carried sand for him all that summer and asked, more than once, to be shown the fire, laughed out loud at the sight of it, the first laugh anyone had heard in that yard since the furnace was raised.

"There," said Voss. "There's your first glass made on this island, and not a scrap of it came from over the sea."

Within the year he had orders from three towns for window-panes, more than he and his two apprentices could cut, and within a generation, as the copyists who set this down already know though Voss himself did not live to see it, his craft had gone out from his own two hands to glass-houses at Cathair dhearg and Seann Skell and half the towns of the capital shore, every one of them tracing its fire, in the telling if not always in the truth of it, back to a glazier left behind at Baile Mòr dhìreach because a ship's berth was not worth losing a beach of good white sand.

## V. Of the Silver Guild's Answer

*And here they turn from a furnace to a hall.*

Martha Gale came to Muileann chiar in the spring of the sixty-seventh year, on {{date:IV-0143}}, a Company agent of thirty-seven with a paper case under her arm and an offer inside it that her own letter-book called generous, and generous, by the Company's reckoning, it was: a partnership in the silver of the guild's own vein, half the yield to the Company and half kept by the guild, with the Company's beam to weigh it and the Company's road to carry it, in place of the old road down to the quay at Seann Bhral that the guild had used since before any strangers came.

The guild's head by then was Ùna nic Choinnich, who had stood with the ledgers at the adit some twenty-four years before, and who was some eighty winters old that spring, bent now at the shoulder but sound in the eye, with a voice that still carried the length of the hall without rising. She had come to the hall that morning on a stick and on her grand-niece's arm, as she had come to every guild meeting for a decade, and had sat through the winter's ordinary business, the ore counts and the smiths' dues, with her eyes half shut, so that more than one in the hall thought her asleep before Gale's letter was ever read; and then, at the reading, she opened her eyes fully and did not shut them again until the hands had been counted.

She had the offer read aloud once by Gale's own clerk, in the humans' tongue, and once more by a guild boy who put it into Dia-thìris, sentence by sentence, so that no hand in the hall could say afterward it had not understood what it voted on. Ùna made him read the paragraph on the Company's beam a third time, slowly, and asked him twice to explain, in his own words and not the paper's, what the weight on that beam would be measured against, and was not satisfied with either answer, and said so.

"You are offering us half of what we already have," Ùna said, when both readings were done, "in exchange for a road we do not need and a weight we do not trust. Is there anything in this paper that is not that?"

Gale said there was the Company's protection, in years when the silver ran thin, and the Company's markets, which reached further than any cart the guild had ever sent round the lake.

"We have had thin years before your Company came to this island," said Ùna, "and we are here yet, and so is the guild, and so, I think, will both of us be when your paper is dust." She turned to the hall. "Hands, then, as the guild has always answered."

The hands went up, and the count was taken by two clerks of the guild's own, one for and one against, as the custom held, and the offer was refused by every hand in the hall save one, an old silversmith too deaf that year to have followed the second reading, who put his hand up, as his neighbour told the clerks afterward, only because his neighbour's hand had gone up first. Gale took the count without argument, folded her paper case, and thanked the hall for its time in a Dia-thìris of her own, careful and correct, learned, the guild's clerk noted, entirely for that one visit and never used by her again on the island.

## VI. Of the Camp at Achadh dhomhain and the Cloth of Dùn chiar

*And here, last, to a hillside, before the copyists close this book.*

The deep galleries that the Company's surveys had marked below the hill at Achadh dhomhain were opened for cutting at the turn of the sixty-eighth year, on {{date:IV-0144}}, and within a few seasons the farming village that had stood there since An Aois Naomh, its granaries feeding the mining camps already a generation old on either side of it, found itself the most crowded place in the district, larger than any market town, though it had no market, no charter, and no custodian sent to it who did not also serve three villages besides.

The Company built nothing there that first season but a pay-hut and a store. The crews built the rest, out of what the ground gave them, which was spoil: the broken rock and shale thrown up from the new galleries, stacked and packed and roofed over as the season allowed, so that a man's house at Achadh dhomhain was, in a sense the custodians' rolls were careful to note, cut from the same hill he worked, only nearer the surface of it. The old farms stood apart from the new stacks of spoil at first, a field's width between the two, as if the village itself were unsure whether to call the camp a part of the town or a separate thing entirely grown up beside it; within two winters the field between them was houses too, and the question was no longer asked.

A hewer of the western workings, Cormag mac Ailein, raised the first of these houses for his own household that first winter, four low walls of stacked spoil chinked with clay, and asked the district's weavers for canvas to close the roof until timber could be got, for there was none to spare that year, every beam on the coast going to shore up the new galleries before the winter wet.

The weavers who answered him were of Dùn chiar, the hill fort south-east of the capital, whose looms had woven for the capital's own custodians since the Holy Age and had never, in all that time, taken a Company contract, holding, as their eldest weaver Ealasaid Bhuidhe put it to the town council that had to vote on it, that a loom that wove for the vein-country's temples should not also weave sacking for a coal camp. The council voted for the contract all the same, twenty-six against nine, the nine led by Ealasaid herself, who signed the roll of dissent and then, the record is careful to add, sat down at her own loom the next morning and wove canvas with the rest, for the town's custodian had ruled it, and a vote lost is a vote kept, in Dùn chiar as everywhere else on the island.

The first bolts of Dùn chiar canvas went up to Achadh dhomhain at the end of that first month, on {{date:IV-0145}}, and Cormag mac Ailein's roof was the first of the camp to be closed against the winter, four walls of spoil and a roof of borrowed cloth, tarred at the seams against the rain by his own hand, with his wife and their two children under it before am Faoilleach of the sixty-eighth year was out.

He stood back from it in the last light, to look at what he had made. He knew that the roof would hold, and that it was his, and that the winter, this year, would not come in on his children while they slept. Of the coloured flame at Muileann bheag, and the bone under the altar at Làrach an Teampaill, and the glass held up to the light at Baile Mòr dhìreach, and the hands raised and counted in the guild hall at Muileann chiar, he had heard nothing at all, and would hear nothing for some years yet; and so ends, at one man's roof and not at any of the others, the account the copyists have kept of what was seen, and not seen, in the six years after the fourth beacon was lit.
