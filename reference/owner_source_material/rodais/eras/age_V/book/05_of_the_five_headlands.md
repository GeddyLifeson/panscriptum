# Of the Five Headlands

*AE 6 – 7*

## I. Of the Nine on the Western Shore

Of the walk round the headlands there are three witnesses, and none of them is whole. There is James Fenn's chart, which survives; there is the roll of the custodians of Cathair dhearg, which gives the walk four lines; and there is what Torcall Clachair told, when he was old, to the households of the southern cape, who kept it as coast people keep the names of drowned men. Where the three disagree, the copyists have trusted the chart for distances and the mason for everything else.

In the winter of that year Torcall was at Baile fhiadhaich on the west coast, setting a new face of stone on the harbour wall. He was a man of some forty-six winters, broad in the back and short in the leg, with a mason's hands, split at the knuckles and grey with dust in every crease. That autumn he had raised the frame for the new bells in the tower at Cathair dhearg, and the custodian of Baile fhiadhaich had sent for him after, because her sea-wall was falling into the harbour a stone at a time. He lodged with a rope-maker, and liked the town, whose people had a name on the coast for good cordage and bad manners, and deserved both.

On {{date:IV-0025}} the fog came in off the western sea in the afternoon and did not lift. Torcall worked on at the wall until he could not see the end of his own line, then went up to the rope-walk to sit by the fire. In the night the fishermen heard a gun fired twice out on the water, and stood on the quay in the fog and argued over what it meant.

In the morning they found out. A human supply-brig, bound down the coast for Cuan shean with stores for the post at Ros dhomhain, had struck the reefs off the point south of the town, where the sea breaks white on a still day and no boat of Baile fhiadhaich goes without a man in the bow. By first light her masts lay along the water. The fishermen took off eleven men from the rigging and the rocks. The rest came ashore over the next three days, one or two at a tide, on the shingle under the dunes.

Torcall helped to carry them up. He had never touched one of the strangers before. They were heavier than they looked, and their clothes held the sea, and their faces had the pale of people who had lived under a greyer sky. There were nine of them. One was the ship's master, a man with a grey beard cut close and a brass ring in one ear, and in the breast of his coat, sewn into oiled cloth, was a chart.

The master's mate had come off the rocks with a broken arm and a face like chalk, and he knew a few words of Dia-thìris, as most of the strangers now did. He spread the chart on the rope-maker's table, and Torcall looked at it with him.

It was a drawing of the island's western coast in ink, with the headlands and harbours marked and soundings written small along the edges. It was a good drawing, and it was wrong. The point where the brig had struck was set down a mile and more further out to sea than it lay, and the reefs below it were not there at all.

"He drew it," the mate said, and put his finger on the dead man's name in the corner. "From his head. From other voyages." He had no word for what he wanted to say next, and in the end he tapped his own forehead and then the chart and shook his head.

"On your coast," he said, "there is no light. Only the fires. The fires are small."

Torcall said that the fires were for the boats of the coast, whose men knew the reefs.

"Yes," said the mate. "Yes. That is what I am saying."

The custodian of Baile fhiadhaich gave the dead ground above the tide-line, on a shelf of turf among the dunes where the sea-holly grew, and the strangers who had come off the rocks dug the graves with borrowed spades. One of them read from a black book in their tongue while the rest stood with their hats in their hands. The townsfolk stood a little way off, not knowing what was proper, until an old woman went down to the tide and came back with her cupped hands full of sea-water and wet the turf over each grave in turn, as the coast did for its own drowned. The strangers let her. Torcall cut nine flat stones from the spoil of his wall and set one at the head of each grave. Not knowing their letters, he cut on each a line for the sea, and the mate scratched the names under it with the point of a knife.

## II. Of the Asking

Torcall finished his wall in an Gearran, and on his road home he came down to Ros dhomhain to buy iron.

The post stood on the lake-shore below the capital, a long house of the strangers' building with a counter across one end, and that morning it was full of men who had nothing to buy. The masters of two ships at anchor off the quay were there, and the trader Thomas Pike behind his counter with his tally-book shut. The dead master's chart lay open between them, and they were arguing over it in their own tongue, fast and loud.

Pike knew the mason, who had bought bar-iron from him twice, and called him over and asked him, in careful Dia-thìris, whether he built towers.

Torcall said that he had built a bell-tower, and a sea-wall, and a great many byres.

Then Pike turned the chart round on the counter and spoke for the whole company. What he said stands in his letter-book of that month and in the custodians' roll, and the two do not quite agree. In the letter-book the seamen "request of the natives that standing lights be raised on the principal headlands of the coast, for the safety of shipping." In the roll the strangers "ask the council whether the old sea-fires may be built higher, and kept all night, and kept in all weathers." It comes to the same thing. The strangers had seen the old driftwood fires burning low on the headlands, and had thought, reasonably enough, that a people who kept fires already could keep them better.

Torcall took the question up the hill to Cathair dhearg. On {{date:IV-0026}} it came before Comhairle a' Chùraim, and Tormod mac Ailein, who sat at its head, heard it read and passed it to the masons. Seven men in the capital were counted master builders, and they sat that evening at a table in the lower hall with the humans' question in front of them on a sheet of the humans' paper.

They were not long about it. A tower on a headland was a season's work for a crew of twelve, the strangers offered iron for it, and they had lost nine men on the rocks. The oldest of the seven, a man from Dùn thais who had built most of the granaries in the south, said that a light which kept men off the rocks was a good light whoever the men were, and the rest agreed with him.

Only Torcall asked about the coal. Every child on the island who had learned the old rulings at a custodian's knee knew that no coal might feed a sea-fire. The Coimheadaichean na Lasrach had ruled it long ago, and their reason was short. *Lasaidh an teine-rabhaidh do choigrich:* the beacon-fire burns for strangers. A fire in a hearth warms the house; a fire on a headland is for whoever is out on the water and cannot be seen, and the coal of the vein was not to be burned for anyone the burner could not see. So the old fires had been fed on driftwood and seal fat and dried wrack for as long as anyone could count, and were small, and burned low.

"They have not asked for coal," said the man from Dùn thais.

"They will want the fire bigger," said Torcall.

"Then they can bring their own oil for it."

That was the end of it. The masons wrote on the back of the humans' sheet that the headlands might be built on if the custodians of each place gave leave, and that a Dia-thìreach builder should choose the ground; and they chose Torcall to choose it, since he had asked the only question and had walked the west coast twice for the sea-walls. Pike wrote under their answer that the natives had agreed "without difficulty and without asking what the lights would cost." It is the first time in his letter-book that he calls them the natives.

## III. Of the Southern Cape

The strangers sent their own man to walk with him, and Torcall met him on the quay at Ros dhomhain in am Màrt, with the wind coming cold off the lake.

James Fenn was twenty-six that spring, long in the leg, with fair hair the island's wind would not let lie and a face burnt red across the nose. He had come out as a navigator's mate with Harrow's four ships and had stayed when they sailed home. Across his back on a strap he carried a leather case, and in it, packed in wool, a brass instrument with a glass eye and a swinging arm, a compass in a wooden box, a roll of paper and a bundle of pens; on his other shoulder he carried a staff banded black and white, taller than himself. Of Dia-thìris he had some two hundred words, most of them the words of ships and weather, and he spoke them fast and with great confidence and little grammar. Torcall had perhaps forty words of the strangers' tongue, most of them the names of tools.

They went south and east by the carters' road, four days down out of the hill country, and on the evening of the fourth day they came over the last rise and saw the sea.

Tobar dhearg was a village of fishers and salt-boilers round a well whose water ran red over the stones, and it stood a few miles back from the cape, out of the wind. The cape ran out beyond it in a long spine of grey rock. On its end stood the oldest sea-light on Dia-thìr: a round tower no taller than a byre, of rough stones laid without mortar, its flat top black from more fires than anyone could reckon, with pegs set into the wall to climb it by. The households of the village kept it in turn. That night it was the turn of a man called Fionnlagh and his two sons, and Torcall and Fenn went out along the spine in the dusk and climbed up to them.

The fire was a heap of driftwood on the flat stones, no bigger than a cart-load, with a pot of seal fat beside it that the younger son ladled over the wood whenever the flame sank. Its yellow smoky light lit the three men's faces and did not reach the sea at all.

"How far?" Fenn said to Fionnlagh. "How far do boats see it?"

Fionnlagh said that the boats of Tobar dhearg saw it from the fishing ground, which was as far as they went.

"And the big ships? From far out?"

Fionnlagh said that there were no big ships, and then remembered that there were, and was a little put out. The fire, he said, was for the boats of Tobar dhearg.

In the morning Fenn set up the brass instrument on its three legs on the end of the cape and sighted along it to the headlands east and west, and sent Torcall out along the rocks with the banded staff to hold it upright where he pointed, and wrote numbers in columns. Toward evening he unrolled a clean sheet on the top of the old tower, weighted the corners with four stones, and began his chart of the southern coast: the cape, the village behind it, the well, the rocks off the point. Then he wrote a name on the end of the cape in the strangers' letters.

Torcall asked him what it said.

It was the name of a headland on the coast where Fenn was born, the one his father's boat had rounded to come home. It had been the last thing he saw of an Tìr Thall when he sailed, and he had thought it right to give it to the first light he found here.

Torcall looked at the name for a while. Then he said, "*Tha ainm air.*" It has a name.

"This one?"

"Every one of them."

Fenn did not argue. The name was for the chart, he said, and the chart was for men who came from the sea and would never hear the Dia-thìris name of the cape, and needed to call it something. That was fair, and Torcall said so. But that evening he gathered stones from the beach in a sack, and on the highest point of the cape, a few yards inland from the old tower, where the rock was sound and a new tower could stand, he built a cairn as high as his chest, laid dry, each stone on two below it, with a flat stone for a cap. The roll of Cathair dhearg enters it on {{date:IV-0027}}: *the mason Torcall has marked the ground at the southern fire.* It was the first of the five.

## IV. Of the Eastern Cape and the North-East Steps

From Tobar dhearg they walked east along the southern coast and then north with the sea on their right hand, and in an Giblean they came to the eastern cape.

Baile ìseal was a handful of low houses on the far edge of the island, with their backs to the moor, and its people kept a fire on the cape for their own boats. But the tellings of that coast said that it had always been lit to bring the boats home in a storm, and the old men of Baile ìseal were proud of the difference. On the southern cape, they said, the fire was a mark to steer by. Here it was a call. A woman whose husband was out in a blow would climb up and feed it herself, and he would see it and know she was there. Fenn wrote that in the margin of his chart.

The east coast was also the raiders' water, broken by low bare islets where men lived by taking what they could from the strands. On the second night Torcall and Fenn sat up with the watch, and near midnight the watchman pointed out a small red spark on the nearest islet that came and went as someone passed in front of it.

"They see us too," he said.

Fenn asked whether a greater light on the cape would help or hinder that. The watchman thought about it for a long time. Then he said that a greater light would bring more men home, and more of the other kind too. Torcall built his second cairn above the old fire the next morning, and the watchman helped him lift the capstone.

North of the eastern cape the land ran up steep out of the sea, and they walked the cliff-tops for the best part of a week with the wind in their faces. At Àth dhearg, above the passage in toward Seann Tarr, the fire had no tower at all. It was built on a shelf of rock near the top of the cape, and from the shore below a stair had been cut up to it in the living rock, turning back and forth across the face, a hundred and more steps, narrower than any stair Torcall had seen, and little worn.

Torcall went up them slowly with his hand on the rock. At the top he sat down on the fire-shelf and looked back down the stair for a long while. When Fenn asked what he was looking at, he said he was counting the cuts of the chisel, and that the man who had cut them had been left-handed, and patient, and had been dead a long time.

The custodian of Àth dhearg would not give leave for a cairn. The fire, she said, belonged to the households of the coast, who had cut the stair; she could not give away a place that was not hers, and if the strangers wanted a tower they must ask the households, one hearth at a time. So Torcall and Fenn went round the hearths, and it took eleven days. Most said yes. Two said no, and one of those came round when Torcall told him the tower would have a stair inside it, so that nobody need climb the old one in a gale again. The third cairn went up at the end of the shelf, where the rock was broad enough to bear a tower, and the custodian came up the stair to watch it built, and brought bread.

## V. Of the Northern Point and the North-West Headland

The north coast they walked in an Cèitean and an t-Ògmhios, going west, with the hills of the interior blue on their left. They slept in byres and once on the open heather, where each named the stars in his own tongue, and neither remembered the other's names in the morning.

It was on that stage that Fenn first spoke of the line. His chart of the coast was a chain of bearings from one headland to the next, all the way round, and it would close; but it had no measure across. Nobody knew how wide the island was. The ships' masters knew the west coast well enough now, and guessed at the east, and all guessed differently. What Fenn wanted was one straight line of sights across the whole island, from the open sea off the north-west to the water off the eastern cape, every station within sight of the next and every distance between them measured. Then the chart would have a spine.

Torcall asked how he meant to see across the middle of the island, where the hills were.

"From the tops of them," said Fenn. "With a mark on each top. A pile of stones, a pole, anything I can see from the next." He looked at Torcall sidelong. "You build good piles of stones."

At Muileann bheag, on the northern point, the old fire stood on a knob of turf above the shore, and the ground round it was red. Fenn wanted to know how deep the footing of a tower would have to go, and he borrowed a spade and dug a pit beside the fire-place with Torcall and a boy of the village. They went down through black ash and red earth, and black ash and red earth again, in bands like the rings of a felled tree, and at three yards they had still not come to rock. Torcall put his hand into the side of the pit and brought it out red to the wrist, and wiped it on the grass, and set his fourth cairn a little way inland where the rock was sound.

They came to the north-western headland at Baile chrom at midsummer.

This was the oldest fire of the five, and it had never been lit to bring anyone home. The tellings of Baile chrom said that it had been lit in the first days of the island to warn boats off the water beyond the headland, where Uilebheist na Mara had been seen, and every child in the town knew why the ash under it lay a man's height deep. The boats of Baile chrom kept south of the fire. Out beyond, some miles off the north-west cape, lay the water where the boats of Achadh àrsaidh had met the strangers' ships in the first winter they came, and beyond that the open sea and the north.

The households of Baile chrom heard Torcall out, and then an old man of the town put the matter plainly. The fire on the headland said *keep away*. The strangers wanted a fire that said *come in*. It could not say both, and the town would rather it went on saying what it had always said.

Fenn answered him in his bad Dia-thìris. The ships would come in whatever the fire said, he told the old man, because they were coming from an Tìr Thall and this was the first land they saw; the only question was whether they came in on the rocks or clear of them. The old man looked at him for a long while and said that this was the first true thing he had heard a stranger say, and that he did not like it any better for being true.

The town gave leave in the end, on condition that the old fire be kept alight beside whatever tower was built, and fed with driftwood as it always had been. Fenn agreed at once. Torcall built the fifth cairn above the fire, and when it was done he stood beside it looking north-west, where the line of the open water showed clean and hard in the evening light, as the fishers said it had only begun to show in their fathers' time.

## VI. Of Fenn's Line

The line began at sea, and the sea end was Torcall's doing.

Fenn wanted to know how far out a ship coming down from the north first saw the island, for there his line must begin. He could reckon it on paper from the height of the headland and the height of a mast, but he had seen what a chart from memory had done on the reefs south of Baile fhiadhaich, and he wanted to see it done. So at Lùnastal he went down the coast to Achadh àrsaidh and found a crew who would take him west in their boat, out past the meeting of the ships, as far into the open sea as they dared go; and Torcall stayed on the headland at Baile chrom to keep a fire.

He built it on a platform of flat stones beside the old fire-place, so as not to disturb the ash, of driftwood that the households brought up in creels, and he fed it with seal fat as the old fire was fed. He burned no coal. On the first night the wind was wrong and the boat did not go out. On the second there was haze. The third came clear and still, with a small moon, and the fire stood up on the headland in a column of yellow light with the sparks going up out of it. Torcall sat beside it all night and fed it, and the households of the town watched him from below, and none of them came up.

Out on the sea Fenn sat in the stern with his compass and a lantern shaded under a cloth and his eyes on the headland, and when the fire sank to a spark on the rim of the water and went out he called to them to stop. He took the time by his watch and the bearing by his compass and the run of the boat by a line paid out astern, and reckoned it through the rest of the night. It came to something near thirty of his miles. The fishers, who had never been so far out, rowed home, and Fenn was sick over the side twice on the way.

This is the one piece of the line told from both sides. Fenn's letter to Pike says that "the native fire on the north-west headland was raised by Clachair to a great height for the purpose, and was visible until thirty miles." Torcall told the households of the southern cape, many years after, that he had sat all night beside a fire lit for a stranger, to measure how far off strangers could see the island, and that he had fed it with his own hands and burned no coal on it, and that he still did not know whether he had done right.

From the headland the line went east and a little south, over the whole breadth of the island, and they ran it through the autumn. Fenn chose each station from the one before, sighting to the highest ground in the right direction, and Torcall went ahead to the chosen top and built a cairn on it, tall and narrow, with a white stone on the cap that Fenn could find through the glass. Then Fenn came up and sighted forward to the next top and back to the last, and two boys hired from the nearest village carried a chain of iron links between the stations to measure them. When the cloud sat on the hills they made no stations at all, and sat in a byre, and Fenn copied his columns fair and Torcall mended his boots.

The cairns went across the middle of Dia-thìr in a string, over the moors south of Cnoc bheag and the rough hills where the carters' roads went round, through country with no village for a day in any direction, where they slept against the cairns they had built.

In an t-Samhain they came up into the Doire uaine hills, and the next station on the line was the highest ground on the island.

Binnean a' Chlachair stood up over the bare moor above Muileann gheal, a great shoulder of grey rock with the cloud on it half the year, and the line ran over its summit as near as made no difference. Fenn saw it from two stations off and was glad, for from its top he could sight a long way east and cut a week off the work. He wanted a fire on it: a great fire, at night, that could be seen from station after station all the way to the eastern cape.

Torcall said that there would be no fire on Binnean a' Chlachair.

The herders of Muileann gheal said the same when they fed the two of them oatcakes and broth that night. Every child of those hills knew the telling of Clann a' Chlachair, that a man of that kindred had carried coal up to the summit in the old days and let it fall, and that one coal had caught fire in the air as it fell. Since then no one had lit a fire there. The herders who went over the top left a stone on it as they passed, one stone each, and that was all they did.

"*Cha las sinn teine an seo*," said the oldest of them. We light no fire here. He said it to Fenn directly, slowly, as to a child.

Fenn asked Torcall whether it was the same ruling as the coal on the beacons. Torcall said that it was a ruling of the hills, and the herders kept it.

They went up on a clear morning in the last week of an t-Samhain, with frost on the heather in the hollows and the whole eastern half of the island laid out below them in brown and grey, and the sea shining at the edge of it far off. On the summit there was a cairn already, broad and low and shapeless, built of the stones the herders had left there one at a time for longer than anyone could count. Torcall did not build on it. He took out of his coat a stone he had carried up from the burn at Muileann gheal, and laid it on the herders' cairn as the herders did, and stood back.

Fenn took his sights from the summit by daylight, to a square of white linen stretched on a pole on the next station east, with a boy of Muileann gheal beside it to hold it into the sun. It cost him four days more than a fire would have. He wrote in his columns that "the natives will not suffer a fire on the high point, for a reason connected with their religion," and went on with the work.

Winter shut down on them in an Dùbhlachd, and they wintered at Muileann gheal with the herders, and went on again in am Màrt when the cloud lifted off the moors. The last stations ran down out of the hills over the long slopes of the east to the cape at Baile ìseal, where Torcall's second cairn stood above the old fire and the households of the coast came out to see the chain carried the last of the way.

On {{date:IV-0027a}} Fenn sat down in the watchman's house at Baile ìseal with all his columns in front of him and added the line up from end to end, twice, and then a third time. He made it a hundred and fifty-five miles, from the point in the open sea off the north-west where a ship coming down from the north would first raise the fire on the headland, to the water off the eastern cape; and some thirty of those miles were sea at the western end. It was the first measure that had ever been made of Dia-thìr from side to side.

He told Torcall the number, and Torcall asked what it meant in days.

Fenn thought about it and said, on a good road, walking hard, a little more than five.

Torcall said that it had taken them from Lùnastal to an Giblean.

"We were stopping," said Fenn.

"We were building," said Torcall.

## VII. Of the Two Books at Baile dhìreach

They walked west from the eastern cape together, since Fenn was bound for Baile dhìreach and Torcall's road home to Cathair dhearg lay the same way for most of the journey.

Fenn had a wedding to go to. The human cooper Samuel Hale, who had come out with him in Harrow's ships and had settled at Baile dhìreach among the boatbuilders, was to marry a net-maker of that town, Eilidh nic Ruairidh, and had written in the autumn to ask Fenn to stand for him. The letter had found them at Muileann gheal in the snow.

Baile dhìreach was a small harbour on the south-west coast, built along one long street that ran straight down to the quay, and it was the only town on Dia-thìr where the two peoples lived under one council. They came in over the last hill on an evening late in an Cèitean, with the sun going down into the western sea at the end of the street, and the street full of people carrying benches.

That night the council of six told the two walkers about the ruling. Before the spring, Iain mac Chaluim, the fisherman who sat for the Dia-thìrich on the council, had written to the custodians at Cathair dhearg to ask whether such a marriage could be entered in the custodians' roll as any other was, and whether its children would stand in the roll as the children of a Dia-thìreach mother. A rider had carried the letter to the capital and come back with nothing. The council there had heard it read, he said, and gone on to the next matter.

"Then we shall write it down ourselves," said Iain mac Chaluim, "and they can rule on it when they please."

The council of Baile dhìreach kept two books, one in each tongue, as its compact was written in each, and on {{date:IV-0028}} it entered the marriage in both.

The wedding was held on the quay, the only open place in the town wide enough for everyone, and everyone came. The tide was full and the fishing boats rode at their moorings with green boughs tied to their mastheads. An old woman of Òrd Mhanannain, whose people had founded the town, poured sea-water on the stones at the couple's feet, and one of the human carpenters read a few lines from the strangers' black book while the townsfolk of both peoples stood through it. The two of them said their promises twice, once in each tongue, Eilidh haltingly in the strangers' tongue and Samuel worse in Dia-thìris, and nobody laughed at either. Then William Rook sat down at a table on the quay with the humans' book open in front of him, and Iain mac Chaluim beside him with the other, and each wrote the marriage into his own book in his own letters.

Each book needed a witness of each people. Fenn signed for the bridegroom in the humans' book. For the Dia-thìreach book the council had meant to ask a custodian, and no custodian had come; so Iain mac Chaluim looked round the quay for a Dia-thìreach of some standing who was not of Baile dhìreach, and found Torcall on a bollard at the edge of the crowd with a cup of ale, and called him to the table.

Torcall could write his name and not much more. He wrote it slowly under the names of the bride and bridegroom, and Iain mac Chaluim sanded it and blew on it and closed the book. Beside him William Rook closed the other.

There was dancing on the quay until the tide turned, and fish and bread and the strangers' ale. Late in the night, when the fires had burned down, Fenn brought out his chart and spread it on the council's table under the lamp, beside the two books where the clerks had left them. The whole coast of the island was on it, all the way round, and across the middle ran the line, straight as a stretched cord from the open sea to the eastern cape, every station marked with a small triangle, and at each of the five headlands a small circle, and at each circle the name Fenn had written in the strangers' letters.

He had learned enough of the Dia-thìreach letters that winter at Muileann gheal to spell a word he heard, and he asked Torcall to tell him the names. So Torcall told him. The southern cape, Tobar dhearg. The eastern cape, Baile ìseal. The steps in the north-east, Àth dhearg. The northern point, Muileann bheag. The north-western headland, Baile chrom. Fenn wrote each under his own, carefully, in the island's letters, and at the mountain in the middle of the line, where he had written nothing, he wrote Binnean a' Chlachair and drew no triangle.

The chart survives, with two names at every headland, one above the other in the same hand. The two books of Baile dhìreach that were closed on the quay that night hold the one marriage in two tongues. Of the cairns that Torcall built, the five above the old fires and the long string of narrow ones he laid across the island for Fenn's line, most have been pulled down by herders for their walls or have fallen in the winters and gone back into the hills. The stone he laid on Binnean a' Chlachair is on the summit still, among all the others, and there is no telling now which one it was.
