# Of the Going Out of Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig

*AE 21 – 24*

## I. Of the Ship That Did Not Come Home

In the roll of Comhairle a' Chùraim for the twenty-first year of this age there is a page the copyists of the cellar turned back to more than once. At its head the clerk has written its year of An Diosal and the moon, and under them two lines in his quick hand. The first says that the humans at Ros dhomhain were in mourning for their captain. The second says that the fee of the counters was held over to the next sitting. Nothing on the page joins the two lines, and the clerk did not think they were joined. Of the second line the copyists could find no later entry anywhere. The next sitting did not take it up, nor the one after, and no council ever struck it out. So Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig went out of the island on a line held over; and the one custodian who stood up to speak for it was a woman of the eastern hills who had come to the capital that summer on other business, and who happened to be at Ros dhomhain on the day the humans stopped waiting for their ship.

Fionnghal nic Artair was thirty-six years old that year, custodian of Dùn dhearg, and she had sat in the council eleven winters. She was a tall woman, long in the jaw, with the grey come early into her hair, and she rode her own horse to the capital and would not be driven. Her town stood three days' ride east of Cathair dhearg, on a hill with a double bank round it and a stone wall inside the bank, where the old hall of the crown had stood before Beathag Mhòr carried it west to the bridge-foot in the long centuries. Dùn dhearg had kept its granaries and its graves and its pride, and an old habit of sending its accounts to the capital in no hand but its own. Fionnghal wrote her own letters, and wrote them long.

She had come west in an t-Iuchar because the council had called in its custodians to hear Coinneach Glas, who had been chosen Ceann a' Chùraim at the turn of the year, after Tormod mac Ailein was buried. He was a broad, slow-spoken man of Ceann àrsaidh, twenty miles up the east road, and he had spent the twenty years since An t-Aiseag dealing with the humans at one table or another, so that he spoke their tongue better than any man in the hall and read it slowly, with his finger under the line. He wanted the council to know the state of the dues. That took four days. On the fifth the sitting rose, and Gormshuil nic Fhearchair asked Fionnghal to walk down with her to Ros dhomhain, where she had business with Samuel Wren at Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise. Fionnghal went, for she had never seen the humans' town close to.

Ros dhomhain lay a short walk below the capital on the lake shore, and in twenty years it had grown a second town beside the first, of sawn timber and dressed stone, with Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir above it on the rise and the chapel down on the quay with its iron bell. That day the bell was being rung slowly, one stroke to every few steps of a walking man, and a flag that Fionnghal had seen at the mastheads of the humans' ships hung halfway down its pole over the warehouse of A' Chompanaidh. The humans stood about the quay in their dark coats and did no work. Gormshuil asked a carter what had happened. He told her that Harrow's ship was a month past her day, and that A' Chompanaidh's men had given her up that morning.

"He went north-about in an Cèitean," said Gormshuil. "I saw him go. He said he would be back before the hay."

Wren came out of the chapel to meet them. He was grey now and thinner than ever, and he coughed into a cloth before he could speak. He took them in out of the wind off the lake and sat them on a bench at the back while the humans' prayers were said, and Fionnghal watched Thomas Pike stand at the front with his hat in both hands and read names from a paper: Harrow's, and after it the names of the men who had sailed with him. She could not follow the words. She could hear that the first name was read in another voice from the rest.

Afterwards Wren told them, in his careful Dia-thìris, what the letter-book at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir would say of Harrow, for he had been asked to help write it. It would say that Edmund Harrow was the man who found Dia-thìr.

Fionnghal turned that over on the long climb back to the capital. At the top of the hill she stopped.

"Lachlann Odhar of Achadh àrsaidh found him," she said. "Off the north-west cape, in the cod season. And led him down the coast to water."

"That is in our roll," said Gormshuil. "It is not in theirs."

"Then theirs is wrong."

"Theirs is theirs," said Gormshuil. She had been twenty years at the humans' letters, and two winters before she had refused to set her name to Puingean a' Chalaidh and had watched Puingean a' Chalaidh take force on the quay without it. She said nothing more on the road.

Within the month A' Chompanaidh was Pike's alone, and the humans' clerks wrote to the council to say so, in their own tongue, on good paper with a seal. Coinneach Glas read the letter to the sitting and it was entered in the roll. Nobody in the hall had ever spoken with Pike about anything that mattered except Gormshuil, who had watched him weigh wool at a counter in a net-loft when the humans on the island could be counted on two hands. She said he was a fair man by his own measures. Then the custodians rode home, and Fionnghal went east to her hill, and did not think about the ship again that year.

## II. Of the Empty Board at Dùn dhearg

The counters of Dùn dhearg kept their board in the old granary under the north wall, where grain had been stored for the crown in the long centuries and where nothing had been kept since but the board and the measure. The board was a plank of black oak the length of a man, hung on two pegs, with a row of holes bored for every household of the town and its farms. The measure was a cup of turned alder made in the shape of two hands held together, worn pale at the rim. Twice in the year, at Samhain and at Bealltainn, the households came up with their sacks for the coal of the shrine-cutting and had it measured out, one double handful to each hearth for the season, and the counter put a peg in the hole. So it had been done since An Aois Naomh, when the custom of the handful was made law. A household that burned twice its measure lost its coal for the season after. The roll of Dùn dhearg held three such sentences in six hundred years, and the town could still name all three families.

The counter was Pàdraig Cam, who had kept the board forty years, as his father had before him. He was paid out of the dues of the vein. A share of every cutting in the hills had gone to the council's chest at Cathair dhearg since An Aois Naomh, and out of it the council paid each town that kept a board, so that the counting should be no one's favour. When the contracts came in, the cuttings went to A' Chompanaidh by the ton, and A' Chompanaidh paid the council for them in marks. The marks came fewer than the old shares by the council's reckoning, and more by A' Chompanaidh's; and the council, which had its part of the new bridge to pay for and a hall roof that let in the rain, found at its autumn sitting that it could not meet every fee. The counters' fee was held over. That was the line the copyists turned back to.

Pàdraig Cam heard it at Samhain from Fionnghal's own mouth. She rode home with the news and went up to the granary to tell him, and found him at the board with his bag of pegs on his knee.

"I'll count this season for nothing," he said. "I have counted for less."

He counted it. Half the households of Dùn dhearg came up the hill with their sacks that Samhain, and those that came were the old. The young households had bought their coal that summer at the market of Cathair mhòr, a day's walk to the west, off A' Chompanaidh's carts, by the sack and for marks, as much as they could pay for. A sack off the carts never came up to the granary to be measured. Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig had been made when every coal on the island came out of the shrine-cuttings through the counters' hands, and it had nothing to say about a cart. Fionnghal walked through the town at dusk and saw it through the open doors: black coal on hearths where there had been peat, a heap of it in the corner of a kitchen, a child set to break the lumps with a hammer. The smell of it hung in the lanes. It was a warm smell, and the town liked it.

Her own steward, Tòmas Bàn, had bought four sacks for her house in an Lùnastal while she was away at the capital, because the young men who cut the peat for the town had gone down to the pits at Muileann chrom for the ton-wage and there was no one left to cut her bank. She found the sacks in the byre when she came home. She said nothing about them. That winter was a hard one, and she burned them.

On {{date:IV-0063}} the council sent round to the towns a letter saying that its chest held nothing for the counters until the spring. The copyists have set that day against the end of Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig, for want of any other, and the keepers at the hill hearths tell the end of Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig as they would tell of a fire that went out for want of feeding. No council repealed it. It went on standing in Leabhar a' Chùraim with the three sentences of Dùn dhearg written under it, and after that winter hardly anyone kept it.

At Doire mhin, in the valley below the hills two days' ride west of Dùn dhearg on the capital road, the custodians went on keeping the count through that winter as though the letter had not come. Theirs was the older half of Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig, the count of the hill: how much coal the cuttings of their valley might give in a year and never more. On {{date:IV-0064}}, the last night of the year, they entered the count in their roll, and wrote under it that no one had asked for it.

## III. Of the Letter and the Road West

Fionnghal began to write in an Gearran, the week Pàdraig Cam died.

He died in his bed in the ordinary way, of his years and a winter cough, and his son did not take the board, having gone to the pits at Muileann chrom two summers before and married there. The board hung in the granary with the pegs of the last Samhain in their holes, and nobody took them out, since nobody would be putting new ones in. Fionnghal went up to look at it after the burying. Then she went home and sent Tòmas Bàn to Cathair mhòr for paper.

She wrote all that spring. She wrote at the kitchen table with the bought coal burning in the grate behind her, because it was what there was, and it is told at Dùn dhearg that she would get up from the table and stand with her back to the fire and look at the wall, and then sit down and go on. Five drafts were written and four of them burned in that grate. The one that survives is dated {{date:IV-0065}}, in her own hand, by An Diosal's year and moon, and it runs to eleven pages.

It is the only defence of Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig that has come down. The copyists give the heart of it as she wrote it:

*Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig was made for the people more than for the coal, for a people becomes whatever its burning makes it. A house that burns the handful keeps a peat bank and a man who knows how to cut it, and a hedge, and a child who knows which willow burns green and which dry, and it goes up the hill twice in the year and says its name to the counter. A house that burns by the sack keeps a purse. I have seen my town go warm this winter, and I have seen my counter die with his pegs in the board. The council need fine no household. Let it pay the counters, and write in the roll that Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig stands. Our grandmothers' grandmothers made the handful when there was no one watching them. If we let it go now, it will be said that we let it go because strangers were watching, and paying.*

She sewed the pages into a cover of calfskin, wrapped it in oilcloth against the weather, and rode west in the middle of an t-Ògmhios with the packet inside her coat and Tòmas Bàn beside her on a hill pony. The road went down off the eastern hills through birch and bracken into the long valley, and the fields on either side were green with oats that would be late. At every ford she met coal-carts coming the other way, empty, going back up to the pits for another load. The carters walked at the horses' heads and did not look up.

On the second evening she came into Doire mhin. It was a village of a few long houses round a green, with its shrine-cutting a mile up the valley side, and its custodian was Anndra mac Eòin, a small bald man with a carpenter's hands, who had kept the count of the valley since his youth. He knew her name from the council. When she told him what she carried, he went to his chest without a word and brought out his roll and opened it on the table under the lamp, at the last page of the year.

The count was there in its columns, cutting by cutting, and under it the line: that no one had asked for it.

"You are the first to ask to see it," said Anndra mac Eòin. He said it in Dia-thìris, in the plain speech of the valley, and then again as though to himself: "Cha do dh'iarr duine e."

"I am asking," said Fionnghal.

She read him her letter that night by his lamp, all eleven pages, while his wife banked the fire with peat from their own bank, for Doire mhin had not bought coal. When she was done he asked her to read the page about the board again, and she read it. In the morning he walked beside her horse to the end of the village and stood there until she was over the rise.

## IV. Of the Filing

The council sat that summer in the old hall at Cathair dhearg under its leaking roof, with buckets set along the south wall to catch the drips when it rained, which it did on the day Fionnghal read. Twenty-odd custodians sat at the long table and on the benches behind it. Coinneach Glas sat at the head with his hands flat on the board in front of him, and the clerk at his elbow with the roll.

She read standing. It took most of an hour. The hall was close with wet wool and the rain loud on the slates, and some of the old men on the benches slept, and one snored, and was nudged, and slept again. Gormshuil nic Fhearchair sat near the door with her eyes shut, and Fionnghal knew that she was awake by the way she lifted her head at the sentence about the strangers watching. Coinneach Glas did not move once. When Fionnghal came to the end she folded the pages and stood waiting, and for a while no one in the hall said anything at all.

Then Coinneach Glas thanked her. He said that the council had heard the custodian of Dùn dhearg with respect, that her letter would be kept with the rolls, and that the matter of the counters' fee would come before the council when the state of the dues allowed. He said it kindly and he said it well, and the clerk wrote it down. The line in the roll reads that a letter of the custodian of Dùn dhearg upon Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig was read and filed.

The next matter was a paper from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, brought up by a young clerk of the humans who stood in the doorway dripping. Josiah Pell asked the council's leave to widen the road from the capital to Muileann chrom so that two coal-carts might pass on it. Coinneach Glas read the paper aloud in the humans' tongue and then in the island's. There was some talk about whose fields the road would take. The leave was granted before the candles were lit.

Fionnghal found Gormshuil afterwards on the old bridge, which was A' Chompanaidh's bridge now, of dressed stone, wide enough for the carts to pass two abreast. The rain had stopped. The river ran high and brown under them, and down the valley the lamps were coming out along the quays of Ros dhomhain.

"They filed it," said Fionnghal.

"They wrote my refusal in the roll too," said Gormshuil. "It is there, a line under the Articles. The Articles are on the quay." She was sixty-two that summer, and she had begun to lean on the parapet when she stood. "You did the right thing. You put it in the roll. Someone copies the roll."

"Who reads the copy?"

"Nobody yet." Gormshuil looked at her sideways. "Give me a fair copy for Muireall. She sends such things to the loft of Beathag nic Thòmais at Muileann chaol, where they are kept dry. The loft is a small place, and still a better one than the press in the hall."

Fionnghal sat up that night in her lodging and made the copy with her own hand, and gave it to Gormshuil's niece at the door in the morning. Then she rode home. At Doire mhin she did not stop, for she had nothing to tell Anndra mac Eòin that he would want to hear.

## V. Of An Seabhag Acrach

On the north coast of the island, where the hills come down in heather to a bay of grey sand, stands Seann Skell of the hawk, the other town of that name, where the fowler households have kept birds on the fist and fed them before their own children since An Aois Naomh. Once, long before, the priests of An Seabhag Biadhte had been driven there from Cnoc bheag, and from its shrine they had asked the king's council for a quarter more of the handful for the cold hearths of the north coast, and been refused. The town had not forgotten it. By this age the shrine of Seann Skell kept the teaching of Òrd an t-Seabhaig as Cnoc bheag kept it, that the hawk of the rite is Mòd, Seabhag Acaill, as old as Fionntan and remembering all things with him; and that the vein is An Seabhag Acrach, to be fed the measured handful and no more.

Its priest in those years was Murchadh Seabhag, a fowler's son of forty-two, spare and weathered, with a hooked nose that his people said had given him his byname before the order did. On {{date:IV-0066}}, a bright day at the end of the harvest, he preached on the steps of the shrine with a grey hawk hooded on his fist, to the fowler households and to the carters who had come up the coast with A' Chompanaidh's coal for the town's new hearths.

The copies of that sermon differ in small things, as copies made by ear will. They agree in this: that Mòd starves those who eat faster than the land can feed them. The hawk remembers every meal it is given, said Murchadh, and every meal it is kept from, and it keeps the reckoning longer than any clerk. Feed it the handful and it hunts for you all your life. Feed it past its fill and it sits on the perch and grows fat and will not fly. And a people that eats the hill faster than the hill can grow is a hawk overfed, and it will not hunt, and in the end the hill will not feed it. Then he said the old line of the order over the hooded bird, which every copy keeps in the island's tongue:

*Ma bhiadhas tu an seabhag thar a leòir, cha sheilg e.*

If you feed the hawk past its fill, it will not hunt.

A scribe of the shrine wrote the sermon down that night from the priest's own mouth, and the fowlers who took birds south to sell at the autumn markets carried copies with them in their bird-bags. From the markets the copies went on by carters and pedlars into the camps at Muileann chrom and Cnoc fhiadhaich, where there were few who could read and many who could listen, and a man who could read was given his supper for reading it in the huts after the shift. It was the first writing of the Dia-thìrich to go among the crews in that way.

A copy came to Dùn dhearg at Samhain in a carter's pack, folded small and worn soft at the creases. Fionnghal read it at her kitchen table, twice, by the coal fire. She had written eleven pages, and here a man who kept hawks had said the thing in a line. She put the copy in her chest beside the draft of her own letter, and did not burn it.

## VI. Of the Peat Hearths and the Pumps

In the spring of the next year word came east over the hills from Achadh naomh, a village of the uplands north of the workings, some fifteen miles inland from the hawk's town. Its households kept Na Seann Spioradan, the old spirits of the hearth and the dead, and on {{date:IV-0067}} their elders had given out that no hearth of Achadh naomh would be lit with coal bought of A' Chompanaidh. They burned peat, as their grandparents had. They had heard of the letter from Dùn dhearg, for a copy had come to them folded inside a copy of Murchadh's sermon, and they sent to ask whether the custodian who wrote it would come and read it at their hearth.

Fionnghal went in an Giblean, a two days' ride north and west through the bare hills, with the peat banks black and wet on either side of the track and nobody cutting them. Achadh naomh stood in a fold of the moor above a burn, a score of low houses roofed in heather rope, and every roof smoked blue. The smell of it came down the hill to meet her a mile off. She had not smelled a whole village of peat smoke in two years, and she had not known that she missed it.

The eldest of the elders was a woman named Sìleas nic Uilleim, bent nearly double, who received her at a hearth in the middle of the floor with the peats built up round it in a ring, and a flat stone at the head of the hearth where no one sat. Sìleas told her what the stone was for. Every night at the smooring of the fire the woman of the house told the dead at the stone what had been burned that day and where it came from: this peat from this bank, cut by this man in this summer. It was a short telling. It had been the same telling for the whole life of the village.

"We tell them what we burn," said Sìleas. "I would not know how to tell them the other."

Fionnghal read her letter at that hearth to the whole village packed into the one house, with children on the rafters and the door open for the ones who could not get in. They heard it through without a sound. When she was done Sìleas asked her, in front of them all, what burned on the hearth of the custodian of Dùn dhearg.

Fionnghal told her.

Sìleas did not say anything to that. In the morning, when Fionnghal went to her horse, there was a creel of dry peats tied on behind the saddle, and nobody would say who had put it there.

In an Lùnastal Coinneach Glas sent three custodians to Muileann chrom to ask A' Chompanaidh, in the council's name, that the tally of the hill be kept: that its agents learn what the hill might give in a year, as the custodians of the valley had always reckoned it, and cut no more. Fionnghal was one of the three, because Dùn dhearg was nearest. They came down into the valley on a hot still morning and saw the smoke before they saw the town.

The camp of the crews had doubled since she was last there. Rows of timber huts ran up the slope from the pit-head, with the washing strung between them and the ground trodden black, and at the head of the rows A' Chompanaidh's agents were going from hut to hut with a basket. Women stood in the doorways with their arms folded. Fionnghal asked a girl at a water-butt what the men were looking for, and the girl said: the hawk-priest's paper. The agents had been told to take every copy they found. They took eleven that morning, by the girl's count, from under mattresses and behind the hearth-stones, and carried them off in the basket to the agent's office. One woman, as they came to her door, put her copy in the fire in front of them and stood on the hearth until it had burned, and they wrote down her name.

The crew-leader who took the custodians down to the pump-house was a man of Muileann chrom named Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill, broad and soot-grained, who had led the first thirty into the pit twelve years before. He did not say much. He showed them the thing as they asked, the way a man shows the road.

The pump stood in a house of stone at the mouth of the deep gallery, a great beam rocking on its pin over a shaft, and the water came up out of the hill in a black gush at every stroke and ran away down a trough to the burn. Beside it were the furnaces that drove it. Carts came up the track from the heaps in a line, and at each furnace-mouth a man knocked out the tail-board and the coal went in by the cartload, straight off the cart, without anyone looking at it. Fionnghal stood in the heat and watched six carts go in while Raghnall told the others how deep the gallery ran. Anndra mac Eòin's count, a whole year of his valley's cutting, would not have kept those fires a month.

A' Chompanaidh's chief agent heard them out in his office with the basket of papers on the floor beside his desk. He was civil. He said that he would send the council an account of the yield. Nine days later a packet came to the council at Cathair dhearg in the humans' tongue: a copy of A' Chompanaidh's accounts for the half-year, in columns of tons and marks, carefully ruled. Coinneach Glas read the totals aloud. The clerk put the accounts in the press in the corner of the hall, on the shelf where Fionnghal's letter lay.

## VII. Of the Oaks of Cathair fhada

The council's winter sitting of the twenty-fourth year was the last that Josiah Pell attended. He came to it in his closed carriage over A' Chompanaidh's bridge, as he had come for five years, and sat at the table in the chair the council kept for him, a grey heavy man by then, and heard the council's business through, and said at the end in his slow Dia-thìris that he was going home. On {{date:IV-0069}} he sailed from Ros dhomhain, north-about, and did not come back. He had sat at the council's table for sixteen years, and Fionnghal, who had disliked him for most of them, found that she was sorry to see the carriage go down the hill for the last time.

Henry Maddox came to the council a week after in the same carriage. He was twenty-eight, fair and quick and pleasant, and he had learned a greeting in Dia-thìris on the ship, which he said to each custodian in turn round the table, with a small bow, getting it nearly right. He told the council that his orders were to double the yield of the pits. It came out as good news, a thing for the island to be glad of, and he looked round the table for the gladness. He did not speak of the letter in his pocket from A' Chompanaidh, which promised him a share of that doubling. The council never saw it. The copyists found it in the letter-books.

On {{date:IV-0070}} Maddox bought the oak woods about Cathair fhada for pit-props. The custodians of Cathair fhada sold them for marks, being in debt to A' Chompanaidh's store for seed after a wet harvest, and the woodreeve of the town came up to the council to ask that the sale be undone. He was Eòghann mac Phàdraig, a lean man with bark-stained hands, and he stood where Fionnghal had stood and said his piece in a quarter of the time. The council heard him. Coinneach Glas said that the custodians of Cathair fhada had had the right to sell. The clerk entered it.

Fionnghal rode south with Eòghann the next morning. It was a day's ride down the valley into the country south of the capital, and they came into Cathair fhada at dusk, a town of grey stone on a long ridge with the oaks round it on three sides, bare and black in the last of the light, older than any house in it. Eòghann showed her the rule of the wood as they walked out into it at first light. Every spring at the feast of Brìde the town cast lots for one oak, and that one was felled for the town's need, a roof-tree or a keel or a mill-shaft, and six acorns were planted in the ring of its roots. So they had cut since An Aois Naomh. There were oaks in that wood that had been acorns in the time of the queens.

The lot had been cast that year before the sale. The oak it had fallen on stood at the wood's edge above the town, a broad old tree with a split crown, marked with a band of red ochre round the trunk at the height of a man's chest.

"The fellers come on the morning after next," said Eòghann. "We will cut ours first."

They cut it the next day, Eòghann and his two sons and four men of the town, with axes, the old way, taking the whole day over it. Half the town came out to watch, and the custodians who had sold the wood came too, and stood at the back. The oak went over in the late afternoon with a noise like a door slammed in a great house, and lay down along the slope with its crown in the bracken. Then Eòghann went down on his knees in the ring of its roots and planted six acorns out of his pocket, and his sons filled in the earth over them with their hands.

The fellers came on the morning after, as he had said, forty men in a line along the wood's edge with long saws from over the sea that two men worked between them, and A' Chompanaidh's carts waiting on the road below. Fionnghal did not stay to see them start. She heard the first saw as she rode out of the town, a harsh rasp, over and over, that followed her down the ridge.

She rode home east across the country, three days on the road with the creel of peats from Achadh naomh still tied behind her saddle, for she had not burned them. The coal-carts passed her at every ford. She came up the hill into Dùn dhearg on the third evening in a thin rain, and went into her own kitchen, where Tòmas Bàn had the grate heaped with coal against her coming, red and roaring, the whole room hot with it.

She had him rake it out. He looked at her, and did it, and carried the coals out in the scuttle to the midden and came back. Then she knelt at the cold grate and built the fire again herself out of the Achadh naomh peats, in a ring as she had seen it built at Sìleas nic Uilleim's hearth, and lit it. It was slow to take. The smoke went blue up the chimney and the smell of it filled the kitchen, and after a while it drew and burned low and red, and gave out less heat than the coal had. In the morning she sent Tòmas Bàn to find a man in the town who still knew how to cut peat, and to pay him whatever he asked.

Up in the old granary under the north wall the board of the counters hung on its two pegs in the dark, with the pegs of the last Samhain still in their holes.
