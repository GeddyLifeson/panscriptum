# Of the Warning and the Constables

*Part Six: Of Cogadh nam Beann*

*AE 138 – 141*

## I. Of the Grey Fever

Eilidh nic Raghnaill was three-and-thirty the winter the fever came in on the ships, and she had already buried her father.

Raghnall Breac had died the summer before under the hill at Cill ghlas, and there was no grave to go to, only the fence. She had gone back to Baile thais and to the crew she led at the pit-mouth, and to the room behind Comann an Adhlacaidh's hall where six or seven met at a time and called themselves nothing, because a thing with no name could not be struck off a roll of A' Chompanaidh. She had taken her father's place there without being asked to and without anyone saying she had.

On the second of Gearran a coal ship out of an Tìr Thall put in at Ros dhomhain with nine men too sick to stand, and by the time the harbour physician came aboard, four of the crew who had carried them up from the hold were coughing too. Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's clerk who wrote the entry called it a fever of the chest, plain and grey, not the green sickness of the galleries and not the flux of the quays that the older custodians still remembered, but a thing of its own, and it went through the young and the strong before it troubled the old, which was against all the custodians' long experience of fevers and which no one below Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir could explain. It ran inland along the coal roads the way every sickness on the island now ran, because every road on the island now led, sooner or later, to a pit.

At Baile thais it came up from the harbour in a carter's chest cough by the middle of Gearran, and Eilidh watched two of her own crew go down inside a fortnight, a hewer named Tormod Buidhe and his sister's boy who had not yet had his first wage. She sat with them by turns, because Comann an Adhlacaidh's rule was that no one died watched only by strangers, and she was better at sitting than most, having had the practice of it with her father. Tormod Buidhe lived. The boy did not.

"He asked me what month it was," Eilidh told the room behind the hall, the night after she buried him. "Near the end. He asked what month it was, and I told him, and he said it wrong back to me, and then he didn't say anything more."

Nobody answered her. It was not the kind of thing the room answered.

She had kept Comann an Adhlacaidh's rule of sitting long before it was hers to keep, because her father had taught her it before he taught her anything else about the crew she now led: that a man dying underground or above it had a right to a face he knew bent over him, and that the right did not stop being a right because the man dying was a stranger's hired hand rather than kin. Tormod Buidhe, when the fever had run its course in him and left him standing, thin and grey-skinned and slower on the ladders than he had been, thanked her for the sitting in the flat way men thanked her for things they had not expected a crew-leader to trouble herself with, and went back to the pit-head within the fortnight because A' Chompanaidh's wage did not wait on a man's convalescence. The boy she had no name to give beyond the one his mother had given him, because the club's rule was also that a name too new to the world was the family's to keep or share as it chose, and his mother, when Eilidh went to her afterward, chose to keep it, and Eilidh did not ask twice.

The fever did not confine itself to Baile thais, though Baile thais felt it worst of the hill camps that first season, being close-packed and close-breathing in a way the older, looser settlements were not. Along the coal roads it went the way every sickness on the island went now, following the carts rather than the weather, so that a camp a day's walk from the sea might catch it from a camp two days inland before either heard word of the other's dying. The custodians who kept the rolls that year learned to write the fever's progress the way a keeper wrote a tide, a thing that came and went and came again, worse in some towns than others for reasons no clerk below Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir could set down with any confidence.

By the middle of an t-Iuchar the fever had reached Cathair mhòr, and there for the first time in the town's whole memory the market shut, stall by stall, until the custodians' roll for that week gave the dead by street and the longest list was the street of the carters, who breathed each other's coughing all day over the scales. Word of it came to Baile thais three days late, by a drover who had it from a drover, and Eilidh wrote it into the club's own book, which was not the custodians' roll and answered to no one, in the plain hand her father had taught her: *Cathair mhòr, market shut, the carters' street worst.* She had begun keeping that book the year he died, because she had watched him carry twenty years of names in his head and give none of them to anyone, and she did not mean to do the same.

The worst of it that year came at Dùn thais in the last days of an Dùbhlachd, where Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had for once sent a physician of its own rather than leaving the sickness to the custodians and Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise between them. He took the fever from the patients he was treating and died of it inside the month, and two of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's nursing sisters with him, in the same week the old halls of the druid masters, empty since the teaching under the trees of Flidais had gone quieter than it used to be, were opened again as sick-rooms for want of anywhere else to put the dying. Eilidh had never met the physician and felt his death less than she felt the boy's, and was ashamed of that, and wrote it in the book anyway, because the book's rule, like Comann an Adhlacaidh's, was that a name went in whether or not the hand writing it had loved the person.

"They send us one doctor and the coal takes him too," said Griogal, an old hewer who had signed on with the club before Eilidh was born and outlived, by his own reckoning, three crew-leaders and two commissioners. "That's a fair trade, if you're counting."

"I'm not counting," Eilidh said. "I'm writing it down. There's a difference."

"Not much of one, from where I sit."

## II. Of An Dàrna Athchuinge

It was in that same fever winter, with the club's book already three pages longer than she had meant it to grow, that Eilidh began the thing that would carry her name into Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's letter-books whether she wished it there or not.

A' Chiad Athchuinge, the one drawn up at Ceann àrsaidh and carried by Peigi Ghlas to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir forty years before, which had ended, as Eilidh had heard it told a hundred times, with Peigi trimming a lamp at Cill ghlas and saying nothing more about it to anyone, had asked. Eilidh had grown up on the story of that asking and on the silence that came back for it, and she did not mean to ask twice in the same voice.

She drafted the new petition in Comann an Adhlacaidh's room by a single coal lamp, over three nights, with Griogal and two others reading each page back to her aloud so she could hear where it went soft. It was not long, and set out, in plain terms and without a grievance wasted on complaint, what the tithe had cost the settlements of the hills in the fever years and the drought years before them, what An Togail had cost before that and been ended too late to matter, and what the galleries themselves had cost in men, in tens counted plainly and not rounded the way A' Chompanaidh's own returns rounded them. Nothing in it was new. Its last line repeated the sentence A' Chiad Athchuinge had buried in the middle of a long paragraph where Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's clerks could read past it without noticing: that the hill would not go on being worked by people who were not asked whether it should be.

She did not write "would not be worked." She wrote "would not go on being worked," because Griogal made her say both aloud three times and told her the second one sounded like a woman who meant it and the first sounded like a woman asking permission to mean it, and she saw that he was right.

The petition went out from Baile thais on foot and by cart through the spring and summer of the next year, carried crew-leader to crew-leader and custodian to custodian by people who did not write down who had carried it to them, in case the list of carriers should ever be asked for by someone with the power to make it a list of the dismissed. By the twelfth of Lùnastal it had come to Cnoc dhìreach, where the custodian of that place, a broad quiet man named Coinneach Ruadh who had kept the post for eleven years and clerked for A' Chompanaidh's store besides, out of the same door most mornings, read it through twice at his own table before he signed it.

He was nine-and-forty that summer, old enough, he told his wife, to have signed worse things for less reason and to know the difference now that it mattered. He signed under his own name and his town's, the way the petition asked, rather than with the custodian's mark alone, because he judged, rightly as it turned out within the week, that a mark could be denied afterward and a name could not.

A' Chompanaidh's agent at Cnoc dhìreach had the letter of dismissal drawn up before the ink on Coinneach Ruadh's signature was properly dry, and delivered it to the store's door on the market-day, in front of the queue for the week's flour, so that everyone in the queue should understand plainly what a name under that petition was worth to a man who needed A' Chompanaidh's wage to feed a family. Coinneach Ruadh took the letter, read it standing in the queue where he had been handed it, folded it once, and put it inside his coat.

"Eleven years," he said to no one in particular, "and they didn't even wait for the week to turn."

He was one of eleven signatories who lost A' Chompanaidh's work that season for the names they had put to the petition, and of the eleven his was the one the custodians afterward remembered best, because he had been offered, twice, the chance to withdraw his name before the letter was drawn, and had twice declined it without argument, saying only that a name once given did not, in his understanding of the custodian's own law, belong to the giver to take back.

Word of it reached Eilidh at Baile thais within the week, carried, like the petition itself, by someone who did not give a name for the carrying. She had never met Coinneach Ruadh; it was some years yet before she did. Still she asked the messenger to describe him, and kept what she was told, a broad man, quiet, slow to raise his voice and slower still to lower his eyes; for she had begun, without quite deciding to, keeping a second kind of record alongside the club's book, a tally now of the living who had put a name to something and paid for it, so that if the day ever came when the petition's cost was totted up in full, the account would have more in it than hers alone. Griogal, when she told him, said only that eleven clerkships were a cheap price for Riaghaltas nan Coigreach to pay for silence and a dear one for eleven households to pay for a sentence, and that the two prices were never going to look the same from both sides of the ledger, whatever the petition said about being asked.

By the last days of an Dùbhlachd, when the petition had gathered the hands of custodians and crew-leaders across one-and-thirty settlements from the hill camps to the coast, it went up at last to Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in a fair copy Eilidh made herself, in her own hand, because she would not have anyone say afterward that a woman's name had been put to words she had not written. Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's clerk who received it underlined the petition's last sentence in red ink before he passed it up the office, and it was that underlining, more than anything the petition itself said, that the custodians afterward agreed was the first sign Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had ever given of having read a Dia-thìrich word and understood what it meant.

"It's the first thing they've troubled to answer," Eilidh said, when word of the underlining reached Baile thais by the same slow relay of carts and drovers that had carried the petition out. "That has to be worth something."

"It's worth exactly what the answer turns out to be," said Griogal, "and not a hair more."

## III. Of the Answer

The answer, when it came, was constables.

In the last days of Gearran the following year word went round the coal roads that Merriman, who held An t-Àrd-mhaor's seat and had held it, by then, the better part of eight years, had ordered the constabulary tripled and posted companies at four of the largest camps at once: Muileann chrom, Baile thais, Ceann fhada and Cnoc chaol, each with its own fortified store built or refitted inside the month, and each paid for, the custodians' rolls noted with a particular flatness, out of the tithe the petition had complained of in the first place.

Eilidh watched the first company march into Baile thais on the twenty-fifth of that same Gearran, forty men in Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's grey with a wagon of timber and iron grille behind them for the new store's windows, and she understood before a single order was read aloud that the underlined sentence had not been answered so much as it had been priced.

"They read it," she said to Griogal, standing at the edge of the crowd that had gathered, silent, to watch the company make its camp on the ground below the pit-head that had until that morning been common ground for drying washing. "They read every word of it. And this is what they wrote back."

The store the constables built at Baile thais stood finished within the fortnight, walled and barred where A' Chompanaidh's old store had stood open on three sides, and from that week the settlement's own well, which had served the whole camp since before the concession was granted, lay inside the new wall and had to be asked for.

The officer who commanded the company, a young man named Prosser with a new coat and an old rifle, called the settlement together on his first rest-day to read the order that had sent him, and Eilidh stood at the back of the crowd with Griogal on one side of her and the old woman who kept the club's rolls on the other, and listened to a paper written in Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's flattest hand explain that the constables were come for the settlement's own protection, in these uncertain times, against disorder from any quarter. Nobody in the crowd laughed. The hill camps had long since learned the cost of laughing at a man with a rifle in front of his own company. Eilidh heard instead, all down the ranks of people around her, a particular silence, one she had learned to recognise from her own room behind the hall, where it meant a thing was being noted rather than accepted.

"For our protection," Griogal said afterward, low, "against disorder from any quarter. He didn't say whose disorder he meant. I don't suppose he needed to."

"He meant ours," Eilidh said.

"He meant ours," Griogal agreed, "and he's not wrong to. He just hasn't worked out yet how right he is."

A month later, in am Màrt, a barracks went up at Dùn chrom, on the capital side of the hills where the coal roads met the road to Ros dhomhain, built to hold two hundred men against a rising Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had apparently decided, on the strength of one petition and eleven dismissals, was now more likely than not to come. For most of that first year the barracks stood empty for want of constables to fill it, a fact the club's book recorded without comment, because Eilidh had learned by then that some facts were their own comment and needed no help from her pen.

"An empty barracks for two hundred men," Griogal said, the night she read the entry aloud, "built before there's anyone to put in it. That's a man building the wall before he's sure there's a war, and calling it an answer."

"Then we'd best not be the ones to prove him right too soon," Eilidh said, and put the book away.

## IV. Of Òrd an t-Seabhaig and the Sermon

The hawk-priests had their own answer, and it was not one Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir had asked for.

At Seann Skell that spring a priest of Mòd named Ìomhar Seabhag, who had kept the shrine there since before Eilidh could remember, began to preach that the hawk's long hunger, starved so many generations on the measured handful and the old teaching that a people who ate faster than the land could feed them would in the end be eaten by their own eating, had turned at last toward those who had done the eating: A' Chompanaidh, and Riaghaltas nan Coigreach behind it, and every hand that had signed a charter for a vein it had not asked leave to open. It was not a new teaching so much as an old one pointed, for the first time in most people's hearing, at a single target.

Word of the sermon travelled the way Murchadh Seabhag's had travelled two generations before, by fowlers carrying birds to the autumn markets and by carters who learned it by ear from men who had heard it whole, and within the season the hawk-shrines of the west had quietly become meeting-places for the club and for others like it across the settlements, without any order given that could be written down and used against them. The constables, garrisoned in their new stores and barracks, did not yet know it, and would not for some while, because a shrine was a shrine to them and nothing more, and no constable posted at a coal camp thought to ask why the hawk-priests of a dozen towns had lately grown so attentive to who came and went from their doors.

At Ros gheal, where the hawk-faithful fishing families had chased A' Chompanaidh's first customs officers off the harbour a hundred years before and had never entirely stopped being proud of it, they raised a shrine to Mòd on the harbour wall itself that same season, in open sight of the customs house their grandparents had emptied. The constables ordered it down within the week, and a sergeant with four men pulled the stones of it into the water on a grey morning with half the town watching from doorways and saying nothing. It stood again inside three days, built in the dark by hands nobody in the town would name even to each other, and the constables pulled it down a second time, and a third, and by the third time they had stopped trying. No order had told them to stop; a sergeant who has torn down the same nine stones three mornings running begins, whatever his orders, to feel the particular foolishness of a man fighting a harbour rather than a person, and there is no rifle drilled that answers that. The fourth shrine stood at Ros gheal still, uncounted and unremarked, the ordinary furniture of a wall that had always had something on it.

At Inis bhàn, that same summer, the priests of the hawk sat down for the first time in living memory with a handful of the old teachers who still kept, quietly and mostly among themselves, the disputations of Scoiltean nan Draoidhean that Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had shut at Dùn thais years before. The two orders had disagreed for six generations over questions neither Eilidh nor anyone in her room behind the hall could have named with any confidence, the shape of the vein's own hunger, whether Mòd fed or merely watched, whether the school's old habit of answering one question with another was wisdom or evasion; and the meeting, by every account that reached Baile thais, settled none of it. The hawk-priest who had called the meeting was heard afterward to say that he had come away no surer of the druids' answers than he had gone in, and prouder of them for it than he had expected to be. It is written of that meeting only that it settled nothing between the two orders, and that both sides, having failed entirely to agree on the questions that had divided them for six generations, parted as friends, which struck Eilidh, when the news of it reached her, as very near the whole of what she had hoped the petition might do in the first place.

The fever, meanwhile, had not finished with the island. It returned that same summer and settled hardest in the north-west, about Baile chrom and the towns of the concession, where for the first time it took humans as readily as it took Dia-thìrich, sparing neither the concession farms nor the Dia-thìreach villages beside them. At Cathair gheal Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's hospital filled past anything it had beds for, and Hannah Webb, who had kept her night watch in the fields behind Baile chrom these eight years, went there to nurse, and found herself nursing in wards where Dia-thìrich and human sick lay side by side for the first time in the concession's history, because there was no longer room, or will, to keep them apart.

"They wouldn't share a well with us a year ago," she wrote, in a letter set down for her by a clerk of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise that went the rounds of the burial clubs as far as Baile thais, "and now they'll share a ward. I don't know if that's the fever teaching them something or just too tired to keep up the wall."

The letter went on, in the plain hand of the clerk who had written it out at Hannah's dictation, to describe a night when the ward had held eleven beds and twelve patients and a Dia-thìreach overman from the north-west galleries had given up his own cot without being asked to a human boy of the concession who could not have been more than nine, and had sat the rest of the night on the floor beside him with his coat rolled under the boy's head, and had said, when Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's sister thanked him for it in the morning, only that he had buried enough of his own that year not to want to watch a stranger's child go the same way for lack of a bed. Eilidh read that part of the letter three times before she read the rest of it, and thought of Tormod Buidhe's sister's boy, and did not write that part down, because some things she judged belonged to the person who had lived them and not to the club's book at all.

Eilidh put what remained of it in the book.

Further north the fever's returning was met each town in its own way. Muileann fhionn, a Dia-thìreach mill town of the concession country, shut its roads against it entirely that following spring and kept them shut a whole season, turning back the human carters of the concession at the town's edge with their loads still on the carts, a thing no custodian there had ordered and every custodian there afterward defended, and a thing the constables, still thin on the ground so far north, had neither the men nor, that season, the stomach to undo. A carter who tried the road twice and was turned back twice told it afterward as the only week of his working life he had been glad of a closed gate rather than aggrieved by one, because he had seen by then, on the roads behind him, what an open one let through.

At Tobar fhiadhaich, further north still, the fever took one household in three before it burned itself out, and the custodians of that small place, who had heard the old story of Sìne Ruadh's burnings at Àth ghlas from their own grandparents and had thought long about it, judged in the end that it had come on them too fast and too widely to be stopped by fire, and buried their dead instead without burning a single hut, a choice the roll records without praise or blame, in the same flat hand it used for everything else that year. One household in three was a count Eilidh made herself set down twice before she believed it, and she thought, writing it the second time, of the empty barracks at Dùn chrom built for a war that had not yet come, and of how much easier, in the end, it had proved for Riaghaltas nan Coigreach to build a wall for men who were not there than to send a single physician to a village that was dying by households rather than names.

It was the sermon from Seann Skell that Eilidh chose, in the end, to bring before the room behind Comann an Adhlacaidh's hall, on an evening in Giblean when the fever had gone quiet again for the first time in three years and the new constables' watch had, for once, let the road home from the pit-head pass without a search. A fowler passing through with birds for the spring market had left a written copy with the club's door-keeper, three pages in a copyist's hand, close enough to Ìomhar Seabhag's words, by the copy-keeper's account, to be trusted.

She read it by the same coal lamp she had drafted the petition by, a year and more before, and the room listened the way a room listens to a thing it has been waiting to hear said aloud by someone other than itself: Griogal with his hands still, the two youngest crew-leaders with their eyes on the lamp rather than on her, an old woman from Comann an Adhlacaidh's own rolls nodding at the parts she had already half known.

"The hawk remembers every meal it is given," she read, "and every meal it is kept from, and it keeps the reckoning longer than any clerk." She looked up once, at the line about the eating and the eaten, and saw on every face in the room the same thing she had felt reading it herself the first time: a thing nearer to hope than the petition, underlined in red ink and answered with constables, had brought them, and nearer still to the plain, hard patience of people who have waited a long time and mean to go on waiting a while longer.

She read it to the end and set the pages down and did not, for once, reach for the club's book to write anything in it. Some things, she had begun to learn that year, did not need her hand to be kept; they kept themselves, in the ears of everyone who had heard them said aloud in a room with the door barred and a lamp burning coal that had cost a man his life to cut.
