## I. Of the Book That Would Not Stay Shut

Fearchar had broken coal at Muileann dhearg since he was tall enough to reach the picking-belt, and by the winter the book came to him he had done it so long that his hands knew the weight of a lump before his eyes had found it, the good clean weight of coal against the duller drag of shale, and could throw the one into the cart and the other onto the spoil without his mind once leaving whatever verse it was turning over that day. His father had done the same work before him at the same belt, and had died of the cough the old women called by no name in those years, and Fearchar had gone on breaking coal and turning verses because there was no third thing to do.

The book came to Muileann dhearg folded inside a meal-sack, passed hand to hand from Baile thais by a carter who would not say whose it was. It had no cover and no title anyone had given it; the custodians' copyists, when a page of it at last reached the loft at Muileann chaol, called it Leabhar Baile thais for want of a better word, and the name held. Inside it, in perhaps thirty different hands, some sure and some barely able to shape their letters, were the things nobody had found anywhere else to put: a wage not paid, a death not answered for, the week the granary at Muileann chrom weighed short, the stream at Doire mhòr that had been dammed for the coal-washing without any order anyone could find.

Fearchar sat up two nights reading it by a tallow light borrowed from the pit stores, and found that he could not add a line to it. It was not want of a grievance. He had buried a father and watched a famine and knew the granary's short week as well as any man at that belt. But he found, turning the pages, that a wage not paid written down in a column looked like nothing at all, a bare fact among bare facts, and that what he wanted to do with it could not be done in a column.

"Then don't write it," said old Griogair mac Aonghais, who broke coal at the same belt and had known Fearchar's father. "Sing it. You've been singing since you could talk. Nobody's stopped you yet."

"Nobody's listened either."

"They will if you make it worth the listening." Griogair spat into the spoil-heap and went back to his sorting. "A column's for men who've given up hoping to be heard. You haven't given up yet, or you'd not be sitting up two nights over another man's book."

It was not the first time Fearchar had been told he sang too much for a man who broke coal for his bread. His mother had said the same, gentler, when he was a boy too small yet for the belt and was set instead to carry water along the row of pickers, singing whatever came into his head to keep his own feet moving; she had died before the famine, of nothing more than the years, and it was one of the few mercies Fearchar counted from those seasons, that she had not lived to see the granary's short week or the wall that came after it. His father had lasted longer and gone harder, coughing through his last two winters in a bed by the same hearth Fearchar still slept beside, and had said, near the end of it, that a man ought to leave something behind him besides coal, since the coal itself went into the Company's cart and was never seen again by the hand that broke it. Fearchar had not understood him rightly at the time. He understood him better with the book from Baile thais open on his knees, two nights running, and no column in it that would hold what he meant to leave.

Fearchar carried the book back to the carter in the spring, since it belonged at Baile thais. He carried something else away from it that no one could take back off him: the knowledge that the wrong done to Doire mhòr's stream and the wrong done to his own father's lungs were pages of the same book, whether anyone ever bound them together or not. He began, that spring, to put verses to the granary's short week, working them out under his breath at the belt the way another man might work out a debt.

## II. Of the Song That Was Forbidden and So Written Down

By {{date:IV-0207}} it was known across the coal country that the grievances of thirty years had at last found a book to sit in; and a year after, on {{date:IV-0209}}, Fearchar sang his first finished song at the pit-mouth of Muileann dhearg, on an evening when the crews were waiting out a delay in the winding-gear with nothing else to do with their hands.

It was a short thing, four verses on the Levy and what it had cost the villages round Baile chrom, no name in it that could be hauled before a magistrate, only a field left to itself and a mother counting heads that did not come home. He had not meant it for more than the pit-mouth. But a song sung once at a pit-mouth is sung again at the next one, and within the month it had travelled from Muileann dhearg to Baile thais without Fearchar carrying it there himself.

The overseer at Baile thais heard it from a crew coming up off shift, singing it under their breath the way men sing something they have not decided yet whether they are allowed to know. He had them stopped and the words taken down from one of them, a boy who could not write and had to say it over three times before the overseer's clerk had it right, and the boy was docked half a week's pay for it and told the song was not to be sung again on Company ground.

It was sung again within the week, twice as far as before.

Fearchar heard of the docking before he heard of anything else, and went to Baile thais himself to find the boy and pay him back the half-week out of his own wage, which was not much, coal-breakers being paid less than hewers. The boy would not take it.

"Keep it," he said. "I'd rather have sung it than not, and I'll sing it again the first chance I get, so you may as well save your coin for the next one they dock."

It was the overseer's clerk, in the end, who did the greatest service to the song without meaning to. Ordered to write down every word of it so that it could be shown to be forbidden, he made, in his neat Company hand, the only complete copy of Fearchar's first song that survives from that year, since Fearchar himself had never troubled to set it on paper and the crews who sang it kept it only in their heads. It sits now among the Company's own confiscation orders, filed under the offence it names, in a hand that never meant to keep it.

## III. Of the Roadside House in the East

The song went east before Fearchar did, carried by carters who worked the road between Muileann dhearg and the coast and had no more idea than the Company's clerk what they were carrying. By the second year it had reached An Taigh-òsta Grianach, the great road-house on the eastern road where the carriers from the north met the southern coast path, and the keeper there, a broad, slow-spoken woman who had buried two husbands and outlasted both, took to it because her custom liked a tune with their ale and asked no questions about where a tune had come from.

Fearchar went there himself only once in those years, in the third summer, riding on a grain-cart with a load of the guild's barley that had nothing to do with him beyond the ride. He came into the taproom at dusk, unknown to anyone there, and sat at the end of a bench with a cup he could barely afford, and heard six Company carters at the next table sing his own song of the granary's short week through from the first verse to the last, missing only a line in the third stanza that they filled with nonsense words since none of them could remember it rightly.

He did not tell them whose it was, only sat with his cup and listened. Hearing it sung wrong by men who did not know what they sang hurt worse than hearing it forbidden, and stirred him more too, in some way he could at the time put no name to; the carters sang it the way they might sing any road-song, for the tune's sake and the company's, with no more notion that it named a grievance than a bird has that its call is a warning to other birds. When they had done, one of them called across for another verse of something, and Fearchar, still unknown to them, obliged with a verse of a song about a horse, and left before the barley cart went on without him needing to be asked who he was.

He walked home the last twelve miles rather than wait for another cart, and worked out on the road, verse by verse, a longer song than any he had made before, though it would be two years yet before the event came that would give it its ending.

The keeper of An Taigh-òsta Grianach he came to know a little better on later passes through, since a man who rode the guild's messages east twice a year could not help becoming a familiar face at the one house on that road worth stopping at. She never asked him outright whether the songs her custom sang were his own making, though he came to suspect, from the particular way she filled his cup without being asked a second summer running, that she had worked it out for herself and judged it none of her business to say so aloud in a room full of Company carters. It was as much acknowledgment as he ever wanted from her, and more than he got from most quarters in those years.

## IV. Of the Quay That Ate the Hill

The Company's new quay went up at Ros dhomhain across those same years, a deep-water quay built out into the harbour where the old wooden jetty had stood, built for ships that no longer needed a tide to float them, ships with iron in their hulls and a stack instead of a mast, that burned coal to carry coal.

Fearchar saw the first of them load. He had gone down to Ros dhomhain on guild business, carrying a message for the silver refiners that had nothing to do with coal at all, and stood on the new stone of the quay among a crowd that had come to see the first of the iron fire-ships, greater than any paddle-boat the harbour had yet seen. It came alongside under its own power, with no oars and no visible sail, and a noise came out of it like a kettle the size of a house, and black smoke stood up from its stack straight into a still evening as though the sky itself had gone the colour of the coal at last.

They loaded her through the night by lamplight, gang after gang carrying baskets up a plank that bent under the weight and did not break, and Fearchar stood at the quay rail until past midnight watching the coal go into her holds and the smoke go up from her stack, and understood, in the plain way a thing is understood when it is seen rather than told, that the ship was burning the same coal it carried to make the carrying possible, so that in some measure it ate its own cargo as it went, and would eat more of the hill to carry the hill away than any cart or barge had ever managed before it.

He made the song that night, sitting on a coil of the new quay's rope with the fire-ship's kettle-noise going on behind him, and it came faster and cleaner than any song he had made before, as though it had been waiting in him since the taproom at An Taigh-òsta Grianach for the right ship to arrive and finish it.

He sang it first to the gang he had loaded beside that night, men too tired by then to do more than nod, and it was that song, more than any other he made, that the carters carried furthest, since it named nothing that could be forbidden and everything that everyone who had ever loaded a cart already knew in their arms.

## V. Of the Wall at Baile thais

Fearchar came home from Ros dhomhain to find that Baile thais had grown a wall while he had been away at the quay: a stone wall round the pithead where there had never before been so much as a fence, with a single gate in it and a watchman set at the gate with a book to write in, so that every crew going down and every crew coming up was counted through a single door.

No one at Baile thais could say when the order for it had come, only that the stone had gone up through the summer, drawn by cart from a quarry the Company had opened for the purpose, and that the watchman's post was the first thing built on Dia-thìr, so far as the oldest hewers there could remember, meant to keep a working's own crews from its own working rather than to keep anything else out.

The watchman himself was no stranger; he had been a hewer at Baile thais until a fall two years before had left one leg shorter than the other and unfit for the face, and the Company had given him the gate as a kindness of sorts, since it paid better than the poor-rolls and asked less of his legs than a shift underground. He greeted every crew through the gate by name, tallied them in his book without malice, and was, Fearchar thought, watching him work the evening he came home, as much a man walled in by that wall as any of the men he counted through it, since he could no more leave the gate for the belt than they could pass the gate without him marking it.

Fearchar walked the length of the new wall the evening he came home, from the gate to the place where it met the old spoil-heap and stopped, since the spoil itself served as well as stone for the last stretch of it. He did not make a song of the wall that year. He found, standing at the gate and watching the watchman tally a shift through it by lamplight, that some things came to him whole and some needed to sit a long while before they would give up a verse worth keeping, and the wall was of the second kind; it sat in him through that winter and the next, unfinished, while other things came first.

## VI. Of Catrìona's Burying

Word came to Baile thais at the dark of the hundred and fourth year that Catrìona nic Eachainn had died at Muileann chiar, an old woman by then, a refiner of the silver guild to the last day she could stand at a bench. There were men and women still at Baile thais who remembered her from the eleven days she had held the pit-mouth against its own agent, though most of the crew she had led then were dead of one thing or another themselves, and the ones who remembered were the old ones now, as she had been.

A dozen of them asked leave to walk east to her burying, and were given it, and the Company docked every one of them a day's pay for the day they were gone, entered against their names as plainly as a wage or a debt. Fearchar was not among the dozen who had known her, being too young to have stood below the pithead in her time, but he walked with them anyway, since a burying at Muileann chiar was twenty miles east and no company was wasted on a road that long.

They buried her under the guild's own stone, among the refiners who had gone before her, and one of the dozen, a woman old enough to have carried water to the strikers in her own girlhood, stood at the grave's edge and said only that Catrìona had told the crews what she had told the agent and then sat down on a coal-tub and not moved, and that this was worth remembering longer than most things men wrote down about her, since it had cost her every pit on Dia-thìr and she had never once, in all the years after, said she wished she had done otherwise.

Fearchar made her lament on the walk home, twenty miles being long enough for a lament to find its shape if a man has the grief and the road both. It named the coal-tub and the empty shelves she had counted, and the guild's bench she had stood at afterward until her hands gave out, and it ended, as her own life had, without complaint, which he judged afterward to be the hardest thing he had yet made a song do, since a lament that does not complain has to find its weight somewhere else, and he had put the weight, in the end, on the single line that she had never once said she wished she had done otherwise. The dozen who had known her wept at it on the road, which he took, rightly, for the only judgment on a lament that was ever worth having.

## VII. Of the Fire on the Quay, and the Empty Seat

The fire-ships' quay at Ros dhomhain burned three days in the spring after, taking two streets of the harbour quarter down with the coal sheds, and by the time Fearchar heard of it at Baile thais the news had already grown three different causes in the telling: a dropped lamp, a spark from a stack left burning at anchor, a fire set on purpose by hands the tellers left unnamed, though plainly they had someone in mind. The Company's own inquiry, when its findings reached the coal towns weeks later, found no cause at all, which satisfied no one who had watched two streets burn and asked why.

It was at An Taigh-òsta Grianach, on his way east with the guild's business again that same year, that Fearchar heard the fire-ship's carters singing his own song of the ship that ate the hill, louder and rougher than the granary song had ever been sung, and understood only slowly, listening from the same end of the same bench he had sat on three years before, that they were singing it now because their own quay had burned and the words fit a grief they had not made the song to carry. They did not know his name. They did not know the song was his. He sat with his cup, as he had the first time, and did not tell them, and found that a song forbidden on Company ground could travel further inside the Company's own mouths than it had ever travelled by any road he had walked himself. It was simply the way a true thing moved once it was let loose, going where it liked and being sung by whoever needed it, without asking leave of the man who had made it.

He was still on that same road home when the second news reached him, carried faster than either fire or song by a rider changing horses at every post-house between the capital and the coast: that Artair mac Iain, high custodian these twenty and four years, was dead at Cathair dhearg, eighty years old, and that the Council of Custodians, called to choose the next as it had chosen every one before him since the crown fell vacant at the close of the age before this one, had sat three days and risen without a name.

It sat so still through the rest of that year. No custodian would put forward another's name for fear of putting forward his own, and no custodian would put forward his own for fear of what a name given now, with the coal roads walled and the quays burning and the grievance book still passing hand to hand from Baile thais to wherever a copy could be made, might be asked to answer for later. The seat at the head of the council table stood empty through the Faoilleach that followed, and the one after, with the old high custodian's chair left where it had always stood, since no one thought to move it and no one yet had the right to sit in it. It was said, though no one at Baile thais could say who had first said it, that Robert Haskins at the Residency had written home across the sea that an empty seat suited him better than a full one, since a council with no head to answer for it was a council that answered to no one at all.

Fearchar made no song of the empty seat that year, nor for some years after. He had a lament in him for Catrìona and a song in him for the quay and the wall at Baile thais still sitting unfinished from the winter before, and some things, he had learned by then, needed to sit a long while before they would give up a verse worth keeping. The empty seat was of that kind.

He went back to the belt at Muileann dhearg, and broke coal, and let it sit. In the evenings, when the winding-gear was quiet and the crews sat out the last light before the huts, he sang what he had, the granary song and the ship song and Catrìona's lament, and let the empty seat go unsung, since a chair with no one in it made a poor subject for a verse next to a man who had sat down on a coal-tub and not moved, or a ship that ate its own cargo as it carried it away. Some griefs, he thought, needed a shape to them before a song could hold them, a coal-tub or a quay or a woman's name on a stone, and a council that had simply stopped, quietly, without anyone striking a blow or signing a paper, had as yet no shape he knew how to give it. He judged, breaking coal at the belt his father had worked before him, that the shape would come in its own time, as the ship song had waited three years at An Taigh-òsta Grianach for the right ship to arrive and finish it, and that until it came the plainest thing left to do was what his father had told him from the hearth-bed at the end: to leave something behind him besides coal, one song at a time, and let the empty seat sit empty a while longer, since it seemed, by every account that reached Baile thais that year, in no hurry to be filled.
