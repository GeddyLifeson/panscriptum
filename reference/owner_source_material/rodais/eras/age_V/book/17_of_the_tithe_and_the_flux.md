# Of the Tithe and the Flux

*AE 57 – 62*

## I. Of the Schedule Read Twice

The paper came down from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir in am Gearran of that year, folded in three and sealed with Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's wax, and the custodian who broke the seal at Cathair dhearg could not read a word of it.

Nobody at the council table could. It was the humans' writing, close and slanted, three sheets of it, and under Lisle's hand at the foot a line in Dia-thìris so cramped it might have been an afterthought: *the schedule follows below.* The council sent for Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's pupil, a girl of fifteen called Barabal nic Dhùghaill who had learned her letters at Ros dhomhain and her sums from a clerk who liked to be flattered, and she stood at the table's end with the three sheets shaking a little in her hands and read Lisle's schedule into Dia-thìris while the custodians listened.

What it came to was this. Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's part of the mined coal, which had stood at one sack in five since Maddox's day, was to rise to one sack in three. Barabal read the figure twice, thinking she had misheard herself, and the second time it was the same.

"One in three," said the custodian of Cnoc bheag, an old man who had sat at that table longer than anyone living. "Read the reason."

There was no reason given. Barabal turned the sheets over to be sure, and turned them back, and said there was none.

"Ask her to read the figure a third time," said a younger custodian at the foot of the table, a woman new enough to her seat that she still thought a wrong sum might be undone by the asking.

Barabal read it a third time. It came to the same.

The hall at Cathair dhearg where the council sat was the same low stone room it had sat in since before the strangers came, with a hearth at one end that smoked in a westerly and a table scarred by three generations of knife-cuts where clerks had trimmed their pens against the wood rather than find a board. Barabal stood at the foot of it in Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise grey with the three sheets in her two hands, and she has said since, to the one custodian who ever thought to ask her, that she was more afraid of the room than of the news, since she had never before been looked at by so many people at once who wanted something from her that she could not give them, which was a smaller figure.

The old custodian of Cnoc bheag asked her to read Lisle's line at the foot once more, the cramped one that named the schedule. She read it. He asked whether there was anything above the fold, any mark of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's council or seal beyond Lisle's own hand. There was Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's wax and nothing else. He sat a while with his hands flat on the table, and then said that the roll should have her rendering copied into it entire, with her name at the foot, so that no one might ever say the island had not been told what was done to it, only that it had not been able to stop it. Barabal, who had expected to be thanked or dismissed and had not thought to be asked to sign anything, wrote her name under the copy in a hand rounder than her fifteen years, and it stands there yet.

{{year:IV-0131}} is remembered on the coal roads as the year of the schedule, and Lisle's is the hand under it, though whether the figure was his own asking or an order sent to him from over the sea that he only signed, no one at Cathair dhearg could say. His letter-book, which survives, gives no reason either. It gives an account of the cost of the new customs house at Ros gheal, and a note that Comann an Airgid's compact must on no account be touched, and then, three lines down, the new fraction, as if it followed from the customs house and the silver both.

The custodians wrote a copy of Barabal's rendering into the roll of every coal town on the island, word for word, with the date and her name at the foot of each, so that whoever asked how the tithe had doubled without a war to explain it would find one paper that said so in the island's own tongue, made by one of its own, and not only Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's sealed sheets that few could read.

## II. Of the Weighing at Dùn fhiadhaich

At Dùn fhiadhaich, which stood among the barley fields under Cnoc naomh and had never dug so much as a bucket of coal, the tithe-gatherer arrived in the spring with a wagon, a set of scale-beams, and a paper that named the town among those that must pay.

The town's grain, he said, fed the men who worked the pits at Muileann chrom and Achadh dhomhain, and so the town shared in the pits' gain and would share in their tithe. He set his scales up in the market square between the well and the mill, and the farmers brought their sacks and stood in a line that ran the length of the square and back, and watched one sack in three of the year's barley go into the wagon while the rest went home with them.

A farmer near the head of the line, a man called Griogair Fada who held the largest acreage under Cnoc naomh and so had the most sacks to lose, asked the gatherer straight out whether it was the pits' fraction that had gone up or the farms' fraction that was new. The gatherer, who was a young man himself and plainly uneasy with the question, said only that his paper named Dùn fhiadhaich and gave a weight for grain against it, and that he had not written the paper and could not answer for what was in the mind of the man who had.

"Then who can," said Griogair, "since he is not here to be asked."

The gatherer had no answer to that either, and weighed the sacks as his paper said, and by evening the wagon stood a third heavier than the town's own store, and the line of farmers going home carried two sacks in three back through the same square where their grandfathers had once carried salt to trade for iron before any human had set foot on the island at all.

The custodians of Dùn fhiadhaich wrote the thing into their roll under a name of their own, since no name for it had come down from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir: *deachamh an deachaimh*, the tithe on the tithe, for it seemed to them a strange doubling, to be taxed for feeding men who were themselves taxed. The name held. It is the name the town still gives that spring, when old men there are asked about it, though the grandchildren of the men who stood in that line no longer quite remember what the first tithe had been for, only that it is called by a name that means twice paid, and that this is reason enough not to like it.

## III. Of the Sick-House in the Chapel

Anne Colby came ashore at Ros dhomhain on {{date:IV-0130}}, twenty-two years old, with a chest of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's linen and a case of instruments she had been shown how to use twice on the crossing and never once before. She had asked to be sent to the island because her brother had gone out with Harrow's ships years before and never written home, and she had a notion, which she kept to herself, that she might find some word of him among the quays. She found no word of him on the island, and in her third summer she found the flux instead.

It broke first in the crowded lodgings above the harbour, where the men who worked the counting-house and the steelyard slept eight and ten to a room A' Chompanaidh let them for what it cost A' Chompanaidh nothing to build. By the second week of an t-Iuchar the sick lay two to a bed in those rooms, and by the third the well behind the counting-house had been fouled, though no one owned to knowing how; and Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's chapel, the long timber hall on the quay with its iron bell, stood shut between its services, the width of the quay from the lodgings.

Colby had the door opened and the benches carried out into the yard and did not ask leave of anyone to do it. She laid down what straw she could buy and what linen Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise would give her, and she and two women of the harbour quarter, a widow called Mòr nic Amhlaidh and her daughter, began to carry the sick up from the lodgings on a hand-barrow, six and eight a day, until the long timber room held as many as could lie down in it and the yard held the rest under a sailcloth awning that Colby had begged off a shipmaster for the promise of prayers for his crossing.

Mòr nic Amhlaidh had lost a husband to the sea and two children to A' Bhreac Bhallach years before Colby ever landed, and she is said to have told the young nurse, on the first night the two of them washed the chapel floor together by lamplight, that she had buried enough of her own to know the trick of it: that the dying wanted their names said to them more than they wanted the fever broken, since the fever generally broke or did not on its own account regardless of what hands did to it, while a name said plainly by someone who meant it could be given to a man right up to the last hour, cost nothing, and was sometimes the only thing left to give. Colby, who had come out with instruments and had been taught nothing at all about names, took the advice as if it had been part of her training from the start, and it may be from that one night's washing of a floor that the whole of what she did afterward followed.

The days ran much alike. She rose before light, walked the yard and the room together and marked who had worsened in the night, then spent the forenoon at the lodgings themselves, since A' Chompanaidh's own physician would not go into them and someone had to judge which of the newly fallen could still walk to the chapel on their own legs and which must wait for the barrow. At midday she ate standing, generally bread and whatever fish Mòr's daughter had got cheap at the quay, and in the afternoon she boiled linen in a copper Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise had once used for washing altar cloths and now used for nothing else. The custodians' roll, which took to noting her comings and goings as it noted little else about Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise, records that she was seen to sleep, in the worst of that season, no more than four hours together, and generally on a bench in the yard rather than in the room Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise had given her in the town, since the room was a mile off and the sick were not.

She was told by A' Chompanaidh's own surgeon, who came once and did not come again, that she would do better to let the sick lie where they were and keep the well-off away from them. He stood in the chapel doorway with a cloth held to his own face and would not come further in than the threshold, and told her, in the tone of a man passing on a kindness, that a chapel crowded with the dying was no place for a young woman to make her name. She answered him that the well-off were already dying, since the flux never troubled to ask a man's wages before it took him, and asked him to leave, which by his own account, written years after in a letter to his son, he was glad to do.

He did send, before he left the harbour for good that season, a case of the salts he judged best against the flux, with a note that she was welcome to try them though he held out no great hope of them, and Colby used every grain in the case and kept the empty box afterward on the chapel's one shelf, since it was, whatever the man's manners, the only help A' Chompanaidh's own physician ever sent her.

What she did instead, and what the roll of the custodians credits her for, was to learn the sick by name. Not A' Chompanaidh's names for them, which were their number on the pay-roll and the gang they worked in, but the names the custodians already kept, each man and woman with a house and a mother and a place of birth set down against them in the town's own roll. She sat at the door of the chapel each evening with that roll open on her knee and a clerk's boy to read her the entries, and she wrote against each name who lived and who did not, in her own hand, in the humans' tongue, a column the custodians could not read, and turned the roll round on the table each evening so they could see for themselves that it was there.

Fourteen died in her chapel before the flux burned out in the autumn, and the custodians' roll, which counted the quarter's dead with care, credits her with keeping them under fifteen. The town remembers her less for the number than for the list she kept, of the men who died in her chapel, every name spelled right, and that when the families came to ask, she did not need the roll open on her knee to tell them.

## IV. Of the Barges to Seann Vell

The coal barges that ran the river east from Ros dhomhain to the port of Seann Vell had no part in the flux at first, or none that anyone marked until a bargeman fell sick at his pole on {{date:IV-0134}} and was put ashore at the Seann Vell landing rather than carried on, since the barge-master judged, wrongly as it turned out, that a man on dry land had a better chance than a man on the water.

Within the fortnight the sickness was in three of the landing's houses, and within the month it was in a dozen. The custodians of Seann Vell, a river town that had never before had cause to shut anything, shut the only quay the town had for the coal traffic that fed it, and let no barge tie up there for the length of a season.

A' Chompanaidh fined them for it. The letter that came down from the pay office cited the loss of a season's landing fees and made no mention of the flux at all, as though the town had shut its own quay for no better reason than mischief. The custodian who received it, a woman called Mairead nic Amhlaidh who was no kin to the widow at Ros dhomhain though she shared her name, as the island's names will run twice and thrice over between towns that never knew each other, read the fine to the town council standing on the landing steps, within sight of the graves the town had dug that season on the rise above it, and said only that A' Chompanaidh's letter had been well timed, if nothing else, to arrive on the day the last of the dead were being sung over.

The custodians of Seann Vell paid the fine out of the same purse that had buried their dead, since there was no other purse to pay it from, and wrote in their roll, in the flat hand the roll keeps for things it does not trust plain words to say, that A' Chompanaidh had weighed a season's coin against a season's dead and found the coin heavier.

Colby heard of it at Ros dhomhain from a bargeman who had carried the news downriver along with a cargo of empty sacks, and she walked the eighteen miles to Seann Vell on a day when the chapel could spare her, along the towpath where the barges no longer ran, with the river low and brown beside her and the landing, when she reached it, as quiet as she had ever seen a coal port. She stayed a week helping to lay out the last of that town's dead before she went back to her own, and it was at Seann Vell, sitting with Mairead nic Amhlaidh over the town's own roll one evening, that she first thought to ask whether any other town kept such a list, and was told that most did, after their own fashion, and that no one had ever thought to keep one list of the sickness across every town it touched, only each town's account of its own.

## V. Of the Seal Passed On

Arthur Lisle died at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir on {{date:IV-0135}}, of a fever that settled in his chest and then, Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's own physician wrote, in his heart, so that he was a fortnight dying by inches with his clerks bringing him papers to the last, since he would not be told he could not sign them. He was one and sixty years old, a lawyer by training who had come to the office at forty-one and never in his twenty years at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir raised his voice at a council table, which the custodians who dealt with him counted, on the whole, in his favour, though they did not forgive him the schedule.

Walter Crane, who had been A' Chompanaidh's chief agent at the pits since before Lisle's time, took Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's seal within the month. He was six and forty, a heavier man than Lisle, quicker to anger and quicker to forget it, who had spent his years among the coal camps rather than at a desk and knew the roads between Achadh dhomhain and Muileann chrom better than he knew Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's own corridors. He came to the seal in his own riding coat, still splashed to the knee from the pit roads, and it is told, though not in any hand that signed its name to the telling, that he sat at Lisle's own desk on his first morning and pushed the dead man's inkstand a foot to the left before he would so much as open the first paper on it, for Lisle, he said, had kept it in a fool's place for a right-handed man.

Crane was the first man to hold A' Chompanaidh's office and Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's one after the other, and the custodians noted the thing without saying what they thought of it, though what they thought is not hard to guess: that the two offices, which had been kept carefully apart since Harrow's day so that neither office might answer only to its own master, now answered to one man's convenience. Crane himself, when a custodian of Cathair dhearg put the question to him plainly at his first council, said only that he saw no sense in the island having two masters where one would do the work of both more cheaply, and that the council might take that for whatever comfort it was worth. It was taken for none.

Crane's first order from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, given within the week, was that the pits should not slacken for the change of one Àrd-mhaor to another. His second, given the same week, moved A' Chompanaidh's pay office for the southern workings from the pit-heads themselves to the town of Ceann mhin.

## VI. Of the Day's Walk to Ceann mhin

The reason given, when a reason was given at all, was that a single pay office serving the whole southern district would keep truer books than a paymaster riding from pit to pit with the coin in his saddlebags, as had been done till then, and lose fewer sacks of silver to bad roads and worse arithmetic. It may even have been true. What it meant for the crews was a day's walk to Ceann mhin and a day's walk back, twice in every month, to stand in a line at a window and be paid what they were owed, and no pay at all for the two days spent walking to collect it.

Uisdean Ruadh, who hewed at Muileann chrom and had a wife and three children to feed on what he carried home, walked it the first time in company with forty other men of his gang, setting out before light on a morning of frost with their breath standing in clouds ahead of them on the road. His wife had packed him bread and a twist of dried fish the night before and asked him, only half in jest, whether he meant to be paid or only to walk, since the two seemed to have become separate undertakings, and he had no answer for her beyond that a man went where the coin was kept and not where it was easiest to fetch.

They reached Ceann mhin at midday, stood in the pay line till near dusk behind the crews of two other pits who had the same idea of an early start, and walked the road home again in the dark, so that the two days it cost them to be paid were, by his own reckoning kept afterward on a stick he notched at his door, worth rather less than the pay itself. The line at the window moved slowly because the clerk within counted every coin twice, being new to the office and afraid of a wrong tally more than he was troubled by the length of the queue outside, and the men waiting had time enough to talk among themselves of little else but the road, so that by the third pay-day the walk, and not the wage at the end of it, had become the thing the gangs of Muileann chrom spoke of first when they met men of other pits.

The custodians of Ceann mhin, whose town gained a fortnight's trade in bread and small beer from every pay-day and complained of it to no one, kept no roll of the walk at all. It is Uisdean Ruadh's notched stick, kept by his family three generations after him and shown still to those who ask, that is the only record of what the day cost the men who walked it.

## VII. Of Raghnall's Burying

Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill, whom the crews and the custodians both had come to call Raghnall Maor, died at Muileann chrom in am Màrt of {{year:IV-0137}}, seventy-two years old, in the slate-roofed house A' Chompanaidh had built him above the pit he had first walked into as a guide to A' Chompanaidh's men who did not know one seam of coal from another.

He had been ill through the winter with a wasting the surgeon called an old man's cough and treated as one, and Colby, sent for from Ros dhomhain in his last week by a message from his son that she never learned the whole of, sat with him two nights before the end, more for the son's sake than for any doctoring she could do, since there was none left to do.

The house surprised her, though she had heard it spoken of often enough on the coal roads: a slate roof over dressed stone, the only one of its kind for miles, with a hearth big enough to be a custodian's and a window of real glass looking down on the pit-head, so that a man lying in the bed A' Chompanaidh had given him could watch, if he chose, the crews going down and coming up without ever leaving it. Raghnall did not choose to. He had the bed turned, in the last weeks, so that it faced the door and not the window, and when Colby asked him once, more to have something to say than because she expected an answer, why he had it so, he told her that he had looked at that pit-head every day for fifty years and saw no cause to spend his last ones at it as well.

He said little to her that she thought worth setting down, being a man who had never been given to speaking of himself even in his own house, but on the second night he asked her, apropos of nothing that had passed between them, whether she wrote her dead down by name, as the town said she did. When she told him yes, he said that was a good thing to keep doing, and spoke no more before morning. In the small hours before he died he spoke once more, to the room rather than to her, a few words in Dia-thìris that his son, sitting on the other side of the bed, kept from Colby then and always, though the son is said to have written them down somewhere in his own hand, in a paper that has not survived.

Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill, his son, was two and forty that year and had broken his first coal at six in the year of An Casad Tioram and lived, which the custodians had not expected him to. He had grown into a quieter man than his father, by every account, and had worked twenty years at his father's side without any sign that he wanted the staff for himself, and it is said of him, by the one custodian who thought to ask him afterward, that he had hoped, as much as any man in the town, that the office would go elsewhere.

A' Chompanaidh gave him his father's staff within the week of the burying, the office and the slate roof and the crews together, as though the three were one inheritance and not three separate things a son might have been asked whether he wanted. A clerk came up from the pay office at Ceann mhin with the paper already drawn, needing only Dòmhnall's mark at the foot of it, and stood in the slate-roofed house's own doorway while Dòmhnall read it through twice before he signed, less because he doubted the terms than because he seems to have wanted, for those few minutes, to be a man still deciding rather than a man already decided for.

The custodians of Muileann chrom, who had buried eleven men under Raghnall's first gallery and watched him take A' Chompanaidh's pay to keep his crews at the face rather than lose it, had hoped, without quite saying so to one another, that the office might die with him and the crews go back to choosing their own foreman as they had before the pits opened. It did not die with him. It went from father to son as if it had always been meant to, and the custodians' hope, which had never been written down anywhere and so cannot properly be said to have died either, went with it.

Raghnall was buried on the hill above Muileann chrom beside the eleven, in ground the town had kept for its own dead since before A' Chompanaidh came, on a morning of thin rain that put out the torches twice before the custodian gave up lighting them and let the burying go on in the grey light alone. The whole pit turned out, both shifts together, which had not happened for any death at Muileann chrom in living memory, since a pit that stops both shifts at once stops its coal as well, and A' Chompanaidh had never before let that be reckoned worth the cost of one man's grave, whoever the man.

Colby stood at the edge of the grave among the crews with her list from the chapel still in the bag she carried everywhere, though there was no name to add to it that day but his. She had walked down from Ros dhomhain the day before on hearing the word, arriving too late to sit with him again but in good time for the burying, and she stood a little apart from the townsfolk, as she generally did at such gatherings, being neither of the crews nor of the custodians nor, any longer, entirely of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise either, but something the coal roads had made of her that had no ready name.

Dòmhnall stood at the graveside in his father's place and said nothing that any account sets down, though one among the crew is remembered to have said afterward that he had watched Dòmhnall's hand close twice on nothing, as a man's hand will close on a staff it has not yet been given and already knows the shape of. When it was over he walked back down the hill to the slate-roofed house alone, and the crews went back to the pit within the hour, since the pit did not stop for a burying any longer than A' Chompanaidh judged it must, any more than it had stopped for the schedule, the flux, or the day's walk to be paid.

Colby wrote Raghnall's name into her roll that evening in the same hand she had used for every name before it, with the date and the place, and under it, because she had begun by then to think it worth doing for everyone she buried and not only for the flux dead, a line of who he had been: *hewer, then Steward, father of Dòmhnall*. It was the only line in the whole roll that named an office. Why she wrote it there she never said to anyone the roll names, though a person who had watched five years of schedules and tithes and pay offices come down from Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir without a single name attached to any of them might have guessed at her reason well enough.
