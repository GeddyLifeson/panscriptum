# Of the Post at Ros dhomhain

*AE 3 – 5*

## I. Of the Counter

The humans' post at Ros dhomhain opened its door on {{date:IV-0012}}, in a net-loft on the lake shore that the fishers of the town let to Thomas Pike for a bolt of blue cloth a season. The loft had a floor of tarred planks and a smell of old rope that never left it. Pike had a counter built across it from wall to wall, waist-high, of ship's timber, and on the counter he laid a book bound in calf, a pen, a pot of ink, and a steelyard from the ship's stores with its hook and its sliding pea. Behind the counter, on shelves his own men put up, stood what Na Trì Longan had carried over the sea for trading: iron tools in bundles tied with tarred twine, crates of window glass packed in straw, casks of lamp-oil, and reams of paper wrapped in oilcloth.

He was a man of some thirty years, short and square, with ink in the grain of his fingers and a habit of setting his hat down on the counter crown-first. The custodians of the capital shore who came to look him over on the first morning wrote that he stood up when they came in, which the strangers' sailors did not do, and that he looked at each thing that was set down before him as if it might be worth more than he had been told. Of all who wrote in those years he counted best, and his tally-book survives, in his own small upright hand.

The first thing across the counter was a bale of wool from a farm above the lake. The farmer had come in on a borrowed cart with two sons and a dog, and stood a long time in the doorway before he would come in. Pike weighed the bale on the steelyard and wrote the figure. Then he laid out, one at a time, the things he would give for it: a spade-blade, a hand-axe, a box of nails. The farmer looked at the nails for a long while. He took one out, turned it over, tried its point on his thumb, and at last put it in his mouth and bit it. Then he nodded, and Pike wrote a line in the book, and ruled it off.

That was how the post was kept for its first season: one thing set against another, each weighed or counted, each line ruled off. Wool came in, and salt fish in creels, and hides, and cloth dyed in the island's own madder and woad; iron went out, and glass, and oil, and paper. Pike had forty words of the island's tongue from Harrow's winter on the dunes, and he learned more at the counter, where a word is worth what it buys. He learned the words for hand and half-hand, for a creel and a fleece and a bundle, and the word for *enough*, which the farmers said sharply, as if it closed a door.

The custodians sent a clerk to sit on a stool by the door. He was a young man with ink to his wrists, and he kept a tally of his own on a long slate, marked in the old way with strokes and crosses, and at the end of each day he and Pike laid the two counts side by side. The clerk could not read the humans' figures and Pike could not read the strokes, and so each read his own aloud, item by item, in a speech that grew between them out of both tongues. On the evening they first found a difference, a single hide, they went through the day's goods again together by lamplight, and found the hide under a creel of fish where a boy had set it down. Pike wrote beside the line, in his own tongue, *found*, and the clerk made a mark on the slate that he said meant the same.

"Your book and his slate," Harrow said, when he came up from Cuan shean at the end of an t-Ògmhios and heard of it. "Two books for one trade."

"Two books that agree," said Pike. "I would sooner have that than one."

At the end of the first month the book balanced to the last line. Pike showed it to the custodians' clerk, turning the pages for him, and the clerk, who could read none of it, looked at the ruled lines for a long time, and then laid his slate beside it and showed him that the strokes balanced also.

## II. Of the Hull in the Shallows

In the first weeks everything that came to the counter had come twenty miles and more by cart from Cuan shean, where Harrow's hulls lay in the roads below the dunes, and everything the post took in went back the same way. It was slow and dear. The carts went over the moor road in two days with the axles shrieking, and Pike's men walked beside them in the rain, and the carters of Cuan shean charged for every creel. So Harrow went down to the strait of Caol leathan to ask the pilots there whether a ship of his could be brought up the Abhainn gheal into Loch chrom.

The pilots of Caol leathan had taken toll of every boat bound for the capital's waters since An Aois Naomh, and they knew the river as a ploughman knows his furrow. They came up the coast in their own boats and went over the smallest of Harrow's hulls from her keel to her masthead, and argued among themselves a whole afternoon in their own speech, and at last named a price. It was twice the toll they took from any boat of Dia-thìr. Harrow was angry, and Pike, who had come down to see it done, wrote the price in his book and wrote beside it in his own tongue: *fair, for they know the shallows and we do not.*

On {{date:IV-0012a}} they brought her up. Pike rode on her deck. The pilots took her in over the bar at the flood with six of their boats ahead of her on tow-lines, the oarsmen singing a slow count that kept the oars together, and the hull came on behind them, a great dark thing among the reeds with her sails furled and her yards cockbilled to clear the alders. Below Baile ghlas the river spreads out over gravel, and there she touched. Pike felt the deck lift under him and settle. The pilots did not shout. They let her lie on the gravel a while, and put two boats to her stern and two to each bow, and waited for a gust off the lake that came when they had said it would, and she slid off and swam again. The people of Baile ghlas had come down to the bank to watch, the rope-makers with their hands still hooked from the walk, and some of them cheered and some said nothing at all.

She anchored that evening off Ros dhomhain, in the lake, with the capital's roofs red in the low sun behind her. Thereafter the humans' goods came to the capital shore by water.

A month after, on {{date:IV-0013}}, a coasting boat came in from the northern coast with wool from Cathair naomh, the city above Seann Toll, the first goods that came to the post from so far. Her master had brought her round the western capes and up the river behind a pilot of his own. He carried a letter from the custodians of Cathair naomh, which the clerk by the door read aloud to Pike slowly, and which asked to be paid for the wool in paper and in ink, and in nothing else.

"Paper," said Pike. "All of it?"

"All of it," said the clerk, and read on. Their roll was old and full, the custodians wrote, and written on skins that were cracking; they would begin it again on the humans' paper, fair from the first page.

Pike gave them a ream for every bale and two bottles of ink besides, and put a quire of his own writing-paper in with the rest, uncounted. It is the only line in the whole book that he did not balance. He wrote beside it: *gift.*

Harrow sailed for home at the end of an Lùnastal. All three hulls went with him, north-about, with hides and dyed cloth in their holds, and he left Pike four men, the loft, the book, and the half of the goods not yet traded. Pike stood on the shingle at Cuan shean and watched the sails go north along the coast until the grey of the sea took them. Then he rode back to the counter, and was there the next morning when the door opened.

## III. Of the Bar-Iron

Gilleasbuig Gobha was twenty-three years old in the year the post opened, and a smith of Cathair dhearg, as his father had been and his grandfather. He had a forge in the smiths' lane below the bridge, where the lane runs down to the Abhainn dhomhain, and he worked bronze there as his fathers had: he cast hoes and sickles, pins and pot-legs and bells, and mended what broke. He was a tall young man, long in the arm, and when he stood at the anvil he stooped like a heron over a pool.

The humans' bar-iron came to the smiths' lane on {{date:IV-0014}}, on a cart from the lake shore, twenty bars of it tied with wet rope, and the smiths of the lane came out of their doors to look. They had seen the strangers' iron since the spring, as axes and spades and nails in the hands of farmers who had traded wool for them, and they had tried to mend it when it broke and could not. Here it was raw, grey and scaled, each bar as long as a man's forearm. Gilleasbuig bought two bars with a bronze pot and a pair of shears, and carried them home on his shoulder.

He put the first in his fire as he would have put in bronze, and it did nothing. When he blew up the charcoal it glowed a dull red and would not move under the hammer. So he blew the fire white, and the bar came out spitting sparks like a green log, and when he struck it, it broke across in a spray of crumbs, and the broken ends were grey and grainy like stale bread. He had burnt it. That night he sat on the doorstep with the two halves in his lap and said to his wife Sìne that the strangers had sold him a thing made of ash.

It was a smith off Harrow's ships who taught him. The ships' smith was a short man and thick through the body, who had stayed ashore at the post with Pike's four men, and who could say nothing at all in the island's tongue and talked with his hands and his hammer. He came up to the lane in an t-Sultain, sent by Pike, with a bag of his own tools, and he stood at Gilleasbuig's fire and watched the colour of the bar in it with his head on one side, and took the bellows-handle out of Sìne's hand and slowed it. At an orange that had some yellow in it he drew the bar out and struck, and it moved under the hammer like cold butter. Then the man laughed, and gave Gilleasbuig the hammer.

All that autumn they worked together, the smith of the ships and the smith of the lane, with no word between them that both knew. Gilleasbuig learned to draw a bar and to fold it and to weld it in the fire with a pinch of sand. He learned to make a nail in eleven blows. The ships' smith had a word for each thing he did, and said it every time, and Gilleasbuig learned the words by the doing and forgot most of them, and kept the doing.

The tempering was last, and hardest. The ships' smith took a blade that Gilleasbuig had forged and ground, and heated it until it glowed, and plunged it hissing into a bucket of brine. When it came out it was hard as flint and would cut a hair, and it would also break like flint if it were dropped on the stones; he showed that with a spoiled one, and the pieces rang on the floor. Then he polished the hard blade bright and held it over a bed of coals, a hand's breadth above, and they watched it together.

The colours came up out of the bright steel like breath on glass. First a pale straw, then a darker straw, then a brown like a beech leaf in autumn, then a purple, then a blue like the sea in the lee of a headland. The ships' smith said a word at the straw and a word at the blue, and at the blue he took the blade away and let it cool. Now it would bend a little, and spring back, and hold its edge.

Gilleasbuig saw more in the steel than two words. Between the straw and the brown there was a colour like new rushes, and between the brown and the purple there was one like a hen's egg held to a lamp, and he had the island's words for both of them, and for the others as they came. He said them aloud as the steel went through them. The ships' smith listened, and looked, and nodded at each; and when Gilleasbuig named the last colour before the blue, the man held up a finger and said his own word for it, which was a different word, and they both looked at the steel and agreed that it was the same colour.

So it was that the two peoples first agreed on a colour, in a smithy below Drochaid Cathair dhearg, and the thing that they agreed on was the colour of hot iron.

Gilleasbuig tempered his first blade alone in the last days of an Dùbhlachd, when the rain came off the lake in sheets and the lane ran with water. It was a knife with a blade the length of a hand. He took it to the straw that is next to the brown and no further, as he judged a knife should be, and quenched it, and ground it, and cut a strip of hide with it that his old bronze knife would only have bruised. He gave the knife to Sìne, who kept it by her board and would let no one else touch it. The ships' smith was down at the post that day, and did not see it done.

## IV. Of the Hoes and the Bells

By the spring of the next year there were iron hoes on the capital's fields.

Gilleasbuig made the first of them in am Faoilleach, when the ground was too wet to work, and by am Màrt all the smiths of the lane were making them, each a little differently and each saying his own was best. An iron hoe was lighter than a bronze one and cut deeper, and it did not bend when it met a stone. The farmers who came down from the fields above the lake to buy them brought their bronze hoes with them in sacks, since bronze was what they had to pay with, and they laid them on the smiths' floors and went home with iron.

So the smiths of the lane had more bronze than they had ever had, heaped in their corners, green and worn. Some of the hoes had belonged to grandmothers; some had marks cut in the socket, a cross or a circle or a notch, that told whose field they were used in. Gilleasbuig kept these apart at first. Then there were too many to keep apart.

The custodians of Cathair dhearg asked the lane for bells. The town had had bells before, small ones, cast in An Aois Naomh and cracked since, and a bell of the old kind had hung in the arch of the market gate as long as anyone remembered, rung for a death, a fire, or a ship. Now there was bronze enough in the lane to cast bells such as the capital had never had. Gilleasbuig and two other smiths of the lane dug the mould-pit in the yard behind his forge in an Cèitean, and made the moulds of clay and dung and chopped straw as their fathers had taught them, and on a dry morning in an t-Ògmhios they melted down the hoes.

It took a day to melt them. The bronze ran into the pit with a noise like a river at night, and smoked and spat, and the smell of it filled the lane. Gilleasbuig stood at the lip of the pit in a leather apron with his face wrapped, and when the bronze had gone in he said the words that his father said at a pour, which ask the metal to settle. Then they let it cool three days.

There were four bells. They were hung in the town that summer, one in the arch of the market gate, one at the bridge, and two at the temple, and they were rung together for the first time at Lùnastal. The farmers who had brought their hoes to the lane stood in the market and listened. An old woman who had carried her mother's hoe down from the fields in a sack told Gilleasbuig afterwards that she could hear it in the bell at the bridge, and he did not tell her that the bronze of all the hoes had run together in the pit and could not be told apart. It may be she was right. Bronze remembers its fire, the smiths of the lane say, and so do bells.

In the same year the weavers of Baile Mòr mhòr on the northern coast came to the post for the humans' dyes. The word of them had gone north with the Cathair naomh boat. On {{date:IV-0015}} they traded their cloth at Pike's counter for jars of a red and a blue brighter than any the island had made, and Pike set down in his book what the jars had cost him over the sea and what the cloth would fetch there, and found the bargain fair on both sides. The weavers opened one jar at the counter and put a finger in it, and held up the finger to the light from the door, and one of them laughed aloud.

## V. Of the Letter from over the Sea

Harrow came back on {{date:IV-0016}}, down from the north on a grey morning, with four ships in place of three. The fishers of Cuan shean who saw them first counted them twice. The fourth was a new hull, broader than the others and deep-laden, and she carried carpenters with their chests of tools, and a surgeon named Jonas Tull, and a stock of goods for the post twice the size of the first, and a letter.

The letter came up to Ros dhomhain by the river, in a sealed packet in the master's own coat, and Pike opened it at the counter on a wet afternoon toward the end of an Dàmhair. It was from the trading house over the sea whose goods he had been trading, and it had been written, by its date, in the spring. Pike read it through once to himself. Then, because the clerk by the door asked him what news had come from over the sea, and because there was no one in the loft but the clerk and Harrow's master and a smith from Cathair dhearg who had come down to buy bars, he read it again aloud, in his own tongue first and then, as well as he could, in the island's.

The smith was Gilleasbuig. He had come in out of the rain with his sack of bronze and his coat steaming, and he stood at the end of the counter with his arms folded while the letter was read.

It was a short letter and a civil one. The house thanked Pike for the hides and the cloth, which had sold well; it would send more iron and more glass; it named a price for salt. Then it came to what it had written for. The house asked what else the island had. It asked whether there was timber, and what kind; whether there was tin, or copper, or lead; whether the rocks of the island had been looked into, and by whom, and what had been found in them. It asked Pike to keep his eyes open, and to write.

When Pike came to the question he read it twice, once in each tongue, as he had read the rest, and did not stop over it. The clerk wrote it down on his slate. Gilleasbuig said nothing. He bought his bars and paid for them with his bronze, and put them on his shoulder, and went out into the rain.

He thought about the question all the way home along the lake road, and by the fire that night. It seemed to him a strange thing to ask. A smith knows what the hills have, since he works what comes out of them; and what they have is theirs, and a smith takes what the custodians allow him, and says the words. He told Sìne the question, and she asked him who in the world would answer it, and he said the stranger with the book would. Then he said that the stranger was an honest man, which was true, and she said that had not been the question.

A month after, on {{date:IV-0017}}, the shipwrights of Inis mhòr came the length of the island to look at the new hulls. Two of Harrow's four lay in the lake off Ros dhomhain that winter, and the shipwrights came to them in a hired boat, seven old men and one young one, carrying coils of cord knotted at every hand's breadth. They asked leave of Harrow's master, who gave it, laughing. Then they went over the hulls from keel to rail, running their cords along every plank and rib and hanging them from the yards, and the young one wrote down each count on a board with a stick of charcoal. They were three days about it. Pike went out to watch on the second day and sat in the stern of the boat with his hands in his sleeves.

He asked the eldest of them, through the clerk, what they meant to do with the counts. The old man looked at him a while before he answered. Their guild had built nothing greater than a coaster since Cabhlach an Scaraidh sailed, he said, and the craft of building for deep water had been half forgotten, since there had been no deep water that anyone of the island wished to sail. Now it seemed there was. So they would learn it again from these hulls, and the cords would keep what they learned.

Pike wrote to the house that night that the island's shipwrights measured by hand's breadths on a knotted cord, and that their joinery was very good.

## VI. Of the First Debt

Pike went down to Cuan shean in an Dùbhlachd to make up the season's account with Harrow, and was lodged in the house of a fisherman below the dunes. It was a low house of turf and stone with its door to the lee, and the fisherman's brother lay in the box-bed by the fire with a leg that had been broken in the autumn under a boat hauled up the shingle.

The leg had been set by the town's bonesetter, and had knitted crooked. By the dark of the year the man could not stand on it. The bonesetter came and looked at it and said it would be so; there was a way to break and set a bone again, and he had seen it done once in his youth, and the man it was done to had died. The town had given the man up for lame. He was young, and he had a wife and a boat, and he lay in the box-bed with his face to the wall.

It was the fisherman's wife who asked Pike whether the strangers had a healer. Pike said they had a surgeon, lately come, and that he would ask.

Jonas Tull came down from the ships on {{date:IV-0018}}, in the afternoon, with his bag and two men from the ships to hold. He was a grey man near fifty with thick wrists and a patient, heavy way of talking, and he spoke no word of the island's tongue. He looked at the leg a long time, and felt along it with his thumbs, and asked through Pike how long since it was set. Then he said what he meant to do, and Pike said it in the island's tongue as well as he could, and the fisherman's brother turned from the wall to listen.

The custodian of Cuan shean was there. Oighrig nic Leòid was old, and not well, and had been fetched from her house in a chair. She had given the strangers' master water on the shingle the winter of their coming, and she sat now by the fire with a shawl over her head and her roll on her knees, and watched the surgeon unpack his bag. When the knives came out of it she asked Pike whether the man had done this before. Pike asked Tull. Tull said he had done it eleven times. Pike asked how many had walked. Tull said nine.

Oighrig said that nine of eleven was more than the island had.

They gave the man spirits from the ships and a strap of leather to bite. Tull broke the leg again at the old break with a stroke of a mallet that everyone in the house heard, and drew it straight with the two men pulling, and set it in splints of ash bound with linen. It was done before the fire had burned down. The man had fainted early and woke up after, grey and wet, with his leg straight under the blanket.

By the turn of the year he could sit up with the leg straight before him, and before the spring was out he was walking on the shingle with a stick.

That night, by the fire, Oighrig opened the roll of Cuan shean on her knees and asked Pike for the stranger's name, and wrote it, spelling it as it sounded. Then she wrote a line under it that Pike asked her to read to him. It said that a man of Cuan shean had been given back his leg by a stranger, and that the town owed the stranger a debt, and that it was the first such debt.

"He will not want paying," said Pike. "He has his wage from the ships."

Oighrig said that was the stranger's affair. The debt was the town's, and the roll would keep it until it was paid. She blotted the line with sand and closed the roll, and asked to be carried home.

## VII. Of the Middling Coal

In the spring of the post's third year the salt-pans of Seann Warr began to sell to Pike's counter by the barrel, on {{date:IV-0019}}. Seann Warr lies on the western shore of the lake, three miles from Ros dhomhain, and its salt is boiled from brine springs that rise in the reed marsh behind the town. It is grey salt, coarse and damp, and the strangers' cooks would have none of it at first. Then the ships' masters found that fish packed in it kept the whole of a crossing, and the first barrels that went over the sea came back as a letter asking for more. They were the first goods of Dia-thìr that the humans came back asking for twice. The salt-boilers of Seann Warr heard of it, and put up their price, and Pike paid it.

In the first days of an Giblean Pike closed the book on the post's second year. He sat up two nights over it by the lamp, with the clerk by the door on his stool and the slate on his knees, and they went through every line of both years together. Wool, fish, hides, cloth, salt; iron, glass, oil, paper, dyes. The pilots' tolls, the carters' hire, the rent of the loft in blue cloth. The quire of paper marked *gift*. When they came to the end the two counts stood level, and Pike ruled a double line under the last figure, and the clerk made a mark on the slate. Then they sat a while in the loft with the lamp between them and did not say anything, and the lake knocked at the piles under the floor.

A fortnight later, on {{date:IV-0020}}, the walled harbour of Caol leathan sent its first wool-ship up the river to the post. Her master was a broad, grey-bearded man of the pilots' kin, and he came up to the counter with three of his merchants behind him. Tools he would not take, nor glass nor oil nor paper nor dyes. He wanted iron in bars, and he wanted it weighed on his own beam, which he had brought with him in a canvas bag, and on Pike's steelyard after, and he wanted the lesser of the two figures. They were at the counter from the morning until the lamps were lit. When they were done Pike wrote in his book that the merchants of Caol leathan were the hardest bargainers on the island, and he wrote it with some admiration.

That evening he sat down to answer the letter from over the sea.

He had kept his eyes open, as the house had asked. He wrote of the timber: oak in the south and pine on the moors, little of either near the coast. Then the salt and the wool and the hides went down on the page, and the smiths at Cathair dhearg who had learned in one winter what his own country's smiths took seven years to learn. He wrote that the island had copper and tin, since it had bronze, but that he did not know where; and that there was silver somewhere to the east, from the pins the women wore. Last he wrote that the island had coal.

That he had seen for himself. There was a basket of it by the custodians' hearth in Ros dhomhain, where the clerk lodged, and a single handful burned on that hearth at each feast of the year and at no other time. He had asked the clerk why so little. The clerk told him it was the law, and had been the law since An Aois Naomh, and that no household burned more than a measured handful in a season. Pike had asked him how much of it there was, and the clerk had laughed and said the hills were full of it. And Gilleasbuig, when Pike asked him in the lane why the smiths burned charcoal when there was coal in the hills, had said *Tha an cnoc làn dheth*, which was the same answer, and had looked at him as if the question were a strange one.

Pike had taken a piece from the basket once, with the clerk's leave, and held it to the window. It was black, with a dull sheen at the break. It seemed to him much like the coal of home, a little harder. He had put it back.

So he wrote that the island had coal of a middling sort, and much of it. He read the line over, and let it stand, and signed the letter, and sealed it.

The letter went into a small iron-bound chest that stood under the counter, where Pike kept what was to go over the sea. The chest had a lid that sagged on its old hinge, and the next morning he took it up the lake road to the smiths' lane below the bridge.

Gilleasbuig looked at the lid and at the hinge, and put the chest on his bench. He could not read the humans' letters, and he did not ask what the chest held. He made a new hinge that morning from a strip of the strangers' bar-iron, drawn out and folded round a pin, and tempered it to the blue that is next to the grey, as the ships' smith had shown him, and set it with three nails on either leaf. The lid he worked until it swung true. Then he gave the chest back to Pike, and would take nothing for the work.

Pike thanked him in the island's tongue, and carried the chest back down the lake road to the post, with his letter inside it and the new hinge bright at its back.
