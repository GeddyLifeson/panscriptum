# The Council, the Commissioners and the Overseers

For a hundred and fifty years Dia-thìr had two heads and would own to neither of them. The Council of Custodians, Comhairle a' Chùraim, sat at Cathair dhearg as it had since An Aois Naomh, with a high custodian, Ceann a' Chùraim, at the end of its table; and from the eighth year the Administration of the strangers, Riaghaltas nan Coigreach, sat in the Residency above Ros dhomhain under a Commissioner, An t-Àrd-mhaor, sent from over the sea. Between them stood the Company, A' Chompanaidh, which held the pits, and the overseers it set over the crews of the hill. This appendix gives each office as the rolls and the letter-books give it at the close of the age, with the years and ages of those who held it. Years are counted from the coming of the ships; ages are given as the rolls give them, at the taking of the office and at its end.

## The high custodians

The high custodian was chosen by the council from among its own members, for life or until he laid the seat down, and signed for the council in all its dealings with the strangers. The seat carried no crown and no guard. Its holder read letters aloud to the sitting, kept the order of speaking, and put his name to what the council agreed.

**Tormod mac Ailein**, high custodian from the second year to the twentieth. He was forty-six when the rolls first name him in the seat and sixty-four when he died at Cathair dhearg. He signed the first written treaty for the custodians in the council hall in the eighth year, across the table from Josiah Pell, and could not read the tongue it was written in. Six custodians carried him to the ground behind the hall with the bells of the capital ringing.

**Coinneach Glas** of Ceann àrsaidh, from the twentieth year to the forty-fourth. He was chosen on the second night of Tormod's waking, at forty-five, a broad, slow-spoken man from twenty miles up the east road, and died in the seat at sixty-nine. It was in his years that the coin was struck, the Small-Burning Law went out, and the first pits were sunk at Muileann chrom.

**Alasdair Liath**, from the forty-fourth year to the seventy-first. He was forty-nine when he was chosen, a custodian who had sat on the council since before the Harbour Articles and had voted to sign them; the roll gives his choosing in a single line with his age and the number of voices. He held the seat twenty-seven years, through the Green Death, and died at seventy-six.

**The empty seat**, from the seventy-first year to the eighty-first. The council could not agree on a head, and said among itself, in the hearing of no clerk of the Residency, that an empty seat answered no letters and signed no papers. Commissioners who wished to address the council in those years stood before the whole table.

**Artair mac Iain** of the eastern shires, from the eighty-first year to the hundred and fifth. He was six-and-fifty when he was seated, a quiet man who had kept his own town's rolls without a blot for thirty years and had never once been summoned to the Residency. Fearghas mac Mhuirich said the order's blessing over him, both horses named. He died at Cathair dhearg at eighty, high custodian twenty-four years.

**The empty seat**, from the hundred and fifth year to the close of the age. After Artair the council chose no head again. The old chair stood where it had always stood, since no one thought to move it and no one had the right to sit in it, and Edward Vane addressed the council in his good coat with his hat in his two hands before a table with no one at its end. In the first winter of his rule Lionel Strake had the council hall locked and its keys lodged at the Residency, and the custodians sat on the floor of Macha's temple for two years, until the townsfolk broke the locks in the spring of the hundred and forty-ninth year.

## The forest council

In the late summer of the hundred and forty-eighth year the custodians of the mining country, the crew-leaders of the pits and the priests of Macha who had left the capital's temple met by night in the sacred forest near Cnoc bheag and made one council of the whole mining country, of which no human writing kept a line. It chose **Eilidh nic Raghnaill** of Baile thais to speak for it. She was forty-three, crew-leader of Baile thais and a member of the Lamp-Watch, and had drafted the sentence of the second petition that the Residency had underlined. Strake ordered her taken with the council's members in am Faoilleach of the hundred and forty-ninth year; she had gone two days before, and the council sat on through the war. Its clerk in the war years was a woman named Fionnuala, who kept its roll and read the names of its dead aloud. The forest council's envoys signed the truce at Àth àrsaidh, and at the close of the age it held the hills, the east and the capital.

## The Company

**Edmund Harrow**, captain of the three ships that lay off Cuan shean in the first year, founded A' Chompanaidh with Thomas Pike under licence of the Administration. Once Pell had built the Residency Harrow went back to the sea trade. He sailed north-about on a second voyage with three hulls and was lost at sea in the twenty-first year, at fifty-six.

**Thomas Pike**, trader of the post at Ros dhomhain, kept the Company's counter on the quay from the first years and was master of the Company after Harrow. He chose the ground for the warehouse at Cathair dhomhain and wrote home of the island's coal before anyone on the island called it a trade. The Company's later masters sat over the sea and are known on Dia-thìr only by their seals.

## The Commissioners

The Commissioner was appointed over the sea and answered to the Administration there. His commission, read aloud at the Residency on his landing, named his powers and, until the last, the leave and counsel of the Council of Custodians.

**Josiah Pell** built the Residency of dressed stone on the rise above Ros dhomhain, signed the treaty, and sailed home at the end of his term and did not return. **Henry Maddox** barred the hawk-priests from the camps, for their preaching slowed the work. **Arthur Lisle** opened the customs house at Ros gheal, sent his surveyors to the adit at Muileann chiar, raised the tithe from one part in five to one part in three, and died at the Residency of a fever of the heart. **Walter Crane**, who had been the Company's chief agent at the pits, walked the camps in the worst of the Green Death and died of it at the Residency. **Martha Gale**, the Company's agent at Ros dhomhain, held the seal for the months between Crane's death and Tolley's landing; eight years before, the silver guild had refused her partnership by every hand in its hall save one. **George Tolley** set the quarantine at Caol mhin, asked Macha's priests to bless the new coin, issued the Levy, and retired with honours. **Robert Haskins** ordered that the council be consulted on the tithe and set the tithe before the council met; in the drought he answered each custodian's petition alone. **Edward Vane**, the only Commissioner born on the island, at the Residency itself, laid the tram-road to Ros dhìreach and died in the bed he was born in. **Charles Merriman** brought the first armed constables, built the posts at Muileann shean and Tobar ghorm, tripled the constables after the fight at Muileann chrom, and went home in poor health. **Lionel Strake**, a soldier, had the council hall locked, sent the iron bird over the coal country, issued the edict of seizure and the order to take the forest council, held the Residency through the first year of the war, went down the Abhainn gheal to Ros bheag in the autumn of the hundred and fiftieth year, and sailed from Cuan shean in the last ship able to make the crossing. Of his landing over the sea nothing is known.

## The overseers of Muileann chrom

In the thirty-sixth year A' Chompanaidh set a man of the island over the Dia-thìreach crews of the hill, gave him a staff and a house with a slate roof, and called him its overseer; the hill called him the Maor. The custodians hoped the office would die with the first man to hold it. It passed instead from father to son for four generations, and the staff with it, brass-capped and iron-shod.

**Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill, Raghnall Maor**, from the thirty-sixth year to the sixty-second. He was forty-six when he took the staff from the agent's hand, having led the first crew of thirty under the first contract, and seventy-two when he died. His mother's neighbours gave him his byname the week he moved into the slate-roofed house.

**Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill Maor**, from the sixty-second year to the eighty-seventh. He took the staff at forty-two and died with it at sixty-seven. He signed the granary order after the strike at Baile thais.

**Iain Maor**, from the eighty-seventh year to the hundred and thirty-first. He took the staff at thirty-seven, the first overseer schooled at the Mission, and held it until his death at eighty-one. He wrote letters to the Administration for which he was dismissed, and was taken back because no one else would be trusted by the camps.

**Mòrag nic Iain Maor**, from the hundred and thirty-first year to the hundred and forty-eighth. She took the staff at thirty-six, the first woman to hold it, and the Company approved her in a letter as a safe pair of hands. At the inquiry into the flood at Muileann chrom she bore witness that the pumps were stopped on the agent's order, and her words were struck out. In the summer of the hundred and forty-eighth year, at fifty-three, she refused the order to clear eleven households and broke the staff across her knee on the Company's step. On the twenty-eighth of an Gearran in the hundred and forty-ninth year she led the crews that took the walled store at Muileann chrom, and the war began.

The overseers of the other pits held their staffs under the same terms. Griogar Camshron of the north pit at Muileann chrom, the overseer of Cnoc chaol, and a third at the edge of the silver country laid theirs down in the same season as Mòrag, and the Company set humans in their places.

## The silver guild

Comann an Airgid of Muileann chiar governed its own seam under a compact older than any contract of the Company, and chose its heads by a count of hands in its hall. **Ùna nic Choinnich** stood with the ledgers at the adit when Lisle's surveyors came, locked the compact in the guild chest, and headed the guild into her eightieth winter; she refused Martha Gale's partnership at about eighty-two and died at eighty-five, in the seventieth year. After her the guild's roll gives its heads by name and year and nothing else. In the war its riflemen took their orders from the guild hall.

## The keepers of the five lights

The five beacons of the coast were built at the humans' asking on the headlands of the old driftwood fires, and kept by the households that had kept the fires, on the custodians' word.

- **Taigh-solais Àth dhearg**, on the north-eastern cape: Tormod mac Pheadair, a mason, keeper from its lighting until his death at eighty-eight, who began the first daybook.
- **Taigh-solais Baile ìseal**, on the eastern cape: kept by the households who had kept the fire, and by no one else, which was the first thing they asked for.
- **Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg**, in the south: Anna nic Dhòmhnaill, a fisherman's widow, its first keeper; at the close of the age Raonaid nic Dhòmhnaill, who put the light out in the war.
- **Taigh-solais Muileann bheag**, on the north shore: Ailig mac Griogair, who kept the coal-burning lamp and asked three winters running to go back to oil.
- **Taigh-solais Baile chrom**, on the north-western headland, the last built: Uisdean mac Lachlainn, keeper from the ninety-first year, who was thirty when he took it and died at sixty-five; and his son Lachlann mac Uisdein, forty at the close of the age, who watched the last Commissioner's ship stand out north.
