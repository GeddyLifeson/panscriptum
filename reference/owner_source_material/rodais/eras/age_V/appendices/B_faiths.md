# The Faiths under the Strangers

The strangers brought one faith with them and found a great many. The orders of Dia-thìr were old when the ships came: Macha's order at the capital and in the south, the Stone in the south-west, the hawk along the north-central coast, Manannan in the harbours, the druid schools of the north-east, and the old keeping of the vein by the custodians, which was less an order than a habit of the whole island. None of them was put down in the age. Some were barred, some were bought, and one was shut by order, and each of them in its own way outlived the Residency that tried it. This appendix gives each as it stood at the close of the age.

## The Church and the Mission

The humans' faith came ashore at Cuan shean in the first year. Its people prayed in a sail-loft of the town that first winter, and their clerics sat down with the custodians at Cathair dhearg in the season of speech-making. They worshipped one god and a son of his, and the Dia-thìrich wrote the son's name *Crìosd* as the clerics said it; the whole company of their believers the priests called by a word the island made into *an Eaglais*. On the island the Church worked through its house, the Mission, which answered to its masters over the sea and not to the Commissioner, though the two were seldom far apart.

Samuel Wren, the thin, coughing cleric who made the word-lists with Gormshuil nic Fhearchair, finished a grammar of Dia-thìris for the Mission, careful and true in most things, which gave the word for the vein's custodians as "keepers of the coal." He copied the obelisk near Cnoc bheag and sent it over the sea, and no answer came; he spent a winter in disputation with the druid masters at Dùn thais. He died in the thirty-third year.

The Mission raised the first church on the island, a timber chapel on the quay at Ros dhomhain, with the first iron bell on Dia-thìr; the custodians asked that it not be rung before the fishing fleet was out, and the bell was rung once before the fleet was out all the same. Thereafter the Mission kept a chapel in every camp and a chaplain to write the dead. It counted four hundred and six dead of the spotted pox, and the custodians counted more and wrote the difference in the margin of the Mission's list. Its nurse Anne Colby made a sick-house of the chapel in the flux, kept a canvas sick-house at Achadh dhomhain through the Green Death, and came back to the island for the famine kitchens at Baile chrom and Cathair gheal. It sank the deep wells at Achadh shean and Àth chrom in the drought. Its school at Ros dhomhain took thirty pupils in its first year, most of them overseers' children; Iain Maor was schooled there. Its cleric Edwin Lowe dug at Làrach an Teampaill and copied the pillar at Cnoc chiar and the column at Cnoc ghorm, seeking an older faith, and left with his notebooks. Its minister Matthew Dunne preached from the chapel pulpit against the night shift, saying the Dia-thìrich were right that even the ground must rest, and was called home. Its hospital at Cathair gheal took in Dia-thìrich and human sick side by side in the grey fever. In the last autumn the Mission's clergy went down the Abhainn gheal with Strake, and its chapels stood empty.

The Mission counted its first Dia-thìreach converts among its first pupils. Neither the Mission's register nor the custodians' rolls give many more.

## The rite of the rock face

Every hewer's son on the hill learned the old words said at the rock face before a man puts his pick in before he learned to swing. The custodians had kept the vein since An Aois Naomh by the handful and the measure, eleven baskets and not twelve, and the crews of the Company's pits went on keeping what they could of that under the ton. Two pit-mouth shrines held the rite in the strangers' years. At Cill dhìreach, a temple village of Macha at the edge of the workings, the crews left the first piece of coal from every new gallery, and the Company suffered it since it cost a stone a gallery. At Cill ghlas the custodians kept the old rites at the mouth of the oldest cutting while the Company's pits deepened round them; it was the last shrine in the district the crews still visited on the old feasts, and there the custodians met to agree the word *fuil-ghuail* for the physicians' book. The Rending swallowed the cutting and the shrine together. The custodians' roll set the loss of the shrine before the loss of the dead.

When Tolley's letter-book blamed the Green Death on the dust, the Dia-thìreach custom of sleeping near the fire, and the custodians' shrines, in that order, the camps heard of it, and the coal set on the breast of the dead for the going-down went on being set.

## The hawk

Òrd an t-Seabhaig keeps Mòd, the Hungry Hawk of Acaill, who starves those that eat faster than the land can feed them. Its houses lie along the Hawk bay at Seann Skell, Ros gheal and Ros leathan, and inland at Cnoc bheag, whose priests asked the Mission for Wren's grammar to read what the humans wrote of them and filled its margins.

Murchadh Seabhag preached Mòd's hunger at Seann Skell in the first decades of the strangers, and his sermon, copied and carried to the camps, was the first writing of the Dia-thìrich the Company ever seized. Henry Maddox barred the hawk-priests from the camps because their preaching slowed the work, and the overseers enforced it, though several of them were hawk-faithful. The camps carried the sermons in themselves instead, and in the Green Death the overseers' reports list hawk-feathers nailed above hut doors as a matter for punishment; they went back up as fast as they were taken down. The fishing families of Ros gheal twice chased the Company's customs officer off their harbour.

In the years of the grey fever Ìomhar Seabhag preached at Seann Skell that Mòd's hunger had turned on those who had eaten the hill. The hawk-shrines of the west became meeting-houses for the Lamp-Watch, and the shrine to Mòd that Ros gheal raised on its harbour wall was built again three times in a season after the constables ordered it down. At Inis bhàn the hawk-priests sat down in disputation with the last keepers of the druid questions, the first time in living memory, and settled nothing. In the war Ìomhar declared from the shrine at Seann Skell that the war was Mòd's own hunger turned back on those who fed it; the hawk-shrines became mustering houses, the Leaden Hawk carried the council's letters to them, and the company of the hawk-faithful held the road north of Ros bheag.

## The druid schools

The masters of Scoiltean nan Draoidhean taught by question and disputation, and their hall at Dùn thais was the last of the old schools to teach in the open. They received Samuel Wren for a winter: he asked them why the vein should be holy, and they asked him why his fuel should be burned, and neither telling of that winter names a winner. About the fiftieth year the master Aonghas mac Coinnich put to his students a question the school's register calls new, why the humans cannot see the colour in broken coal that every Dia-thìreach child sees, and did not answer it; his students carried it to the hall of disputation at Baile Mòr uaine, whose record of the argument runs to four hundred leaves. A generation later George Tolley shut the hall at Dùn thais over a lecture on whether that blindness was a kind of sickness. The masters taught in their own houses and under the trees of the sacred forest of Flidais near Muileann chiar, and held their last open disputation on the threshing-floor at Doire chiar on the eve of Bealltainn, on whether a question may be forbidden. Seònaid nic Dhùghaill, the last master to teach openly, died at Dùn thais, and the school's register ends with her death. In the grey fever the old houses of the masters at Dùn thais were opened as sick-rooms.

## Macha and the blessing of the coin

Òrd Mhacha, the order of the capital and the crown, keeps the rite of Macha's two horses, Dubh Sainglenn the black and Liath Macha the grey, each of which, the order teaches, has its due. At Tolley's asking its priests at Cathair dhearg blessed the Residency's new coin. Fearghas mac Mhuirich would not stand for the blessing, and he and the priests who refused with him left the capital's temple and settled in the sacred forest near Cnoc bheag. There they kept the older reading of the two horses, lit their fire of hazel under the oaks with both halves of the words said in the old order, and took in custodians who had lost their seats. Fearghas said the blessing over Artair mac Iain with both horses named.

The priests who stayed in the capital's temple and had blessed the coin opened their floor to the Council of Custodians when Strake locked the council hall, and sheltered there the custodians who had petitioned against the coin they had blessed. The dissenters in the forest took in the forest council of the mining country and sat at its fires. At the close of the age the order stood in two halves under one goddess, and neither half had unsaid anything.

## The Stone, Manannan and the old rites

Òrd na Cloiche keeps the stone Lia Fàil at Inis thais, which cries out under the rightful king, and the oldest shrines of the order at Cnoc àrsaidh on the panther road. It declined the joint council of Baile dhìreach, took no part in the strikes, and would send no men to the war, since that was not what the Stone was for; but it shut Inis thais's harbour to the Company's ships and opened the Fianna's roads to the hawk-faithful.

Òrd Mhanannain keeps the harbours. The traders of the order founded Baile dhìreach, the whale-boats of Àth fhionn thanked Manannan for each whale, and at Ceann mhòr the priests of the order kept the grey night with the human fishing families of the concession. When the Mission reported that human labourers near Baile chrom kept vigil by night in the fields under the sign of a grey goose, which they called the wild goose of the Spirit, the priests of Manannan at Ceann mhòr answered that the practice owed something to the Dia-thìrich, but to their own harbours and not to Baile chrom. Hannah Webb, who first kept it alone behind a byre, said only that they kept the sky.

The ancestor rite of the old roads is kept at Àth fhionn and along the coast at Caol ìseal; the Old Spirits, the oldest folk rite of the island, at Baile ghorm. The strangers left all of them alone, having never learned where they were kept.

## The keeper and the king's daughter

In the camps of the hill the washing-women of Achadh dhomhain said of a girl of the spoil-houses that she did not grow, and called her *nighean an rìgh*, the king's daughter, meaning Ailean Mòr, whom the gods crowned in the grove; most who said it did not believe a word of the thousands of years, or said they did not. The custodians met at the shrine of Cill ghlas and agreed a plain word for the change for the physicians' book, *fuil-ghuail*, coal-blood, and wrote it in their columns against four names by the close of the age, and reported none of them to the Residency. The camps said a person who kept their youth past its season had *Aisling-aois*. Cailean mac Eachainn found the girl's line in the roll of the camp at Àth ìseal, written seven names back, and Clann na Ceiste at Tobar dhìreach told him whose son the last of them was. The camps told the rest as the camps tell things, and the rolls kept only the names.
