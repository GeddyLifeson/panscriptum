# Strikes, Sicknesses and Cogadh nam Beann

An Aois Choigreach was not an age of armies until its last two years. For a hundred and forty-seven years Riaghaltas nan Coigreach kept no standing force of its own until Merriman brought his constables, and the Dia-thìrich kept none at all. What killed people in those years was sickness, hunger and the pits, and what the crews had to answer with was the stopping of work. This appendix sets down, as the custodians' rolls and Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's registers give them, the sicknesses and hungers of the age, the strikes and the stoppage, the constables and their posts, and the fighting of the war of the hills, with the dead where the rolls count them. Where Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's count and the custodians' differ, both are given.

**Fiabhras nan Coigreach** came ashore with a crowded ship at Cuan shean and ran down the west coast. It killed the custodian of Baile àrsaidh and both her sons, in a town where no human lived at all, and the custodians of the west took note of it. Cuan shean shut itself to ships for a season, and Pell protested in writing. At Cnoc àrsaidh the townsfolk blocked the road with carts and turned back every traveller, and the fever never reached them; Riaghaltas nan Coigreach fined them for hindering trade, and they paid in wool.

**The coughing sickness** was a dry cough among the boys who broke coal at the pit-mouth. Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's surgeon called it a winter complaint, and it lasted through the summer. Cill thais took in the orphans at its own custodians' cost; A' Chompanaidh, asked to give toward the house, sent a crate of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's primers.

**A' Bhreac Bhallach** came ashore at Ros dhomhain on a ship of A' Chompanaidh and climbed Rathad na Mèinne into the camps, worst in the huts at Àth ìseal and at Achadh dhomhain. Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise counted four hundred and six dead, and the custodians wrote the difference between their count and Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's in the margin of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's list. At Baile chiar in the south the custodians cut the names of their dead into the stone lintel of the shrine.

**The flux** broke out in the crowded quarters by the Ros dhomhain quays. Anne Colby made a sick-house of the chapel. The coal barges carried it to Seann Vell, whose custodians shut the only quay the town had for the coal traffic and paid A' Chompanaidh's fine from the purse that had buried their dead.

**A' Phlàigh Uaine** began at Achadh dhomhain: a greenish pallor, then a cough that brought up dark dust, then weakness. It spread from the camp to Muileann chrom, Àth ìseal and Cnoc fhiadhaich, and wider along the coal roads. At Cnoc naomh the dead filled the old shrine's burial ground and then a field that was never ploughed again; at Àth ghlas Sìne Ruadh had every hut where a death had been burned with its bedding, and the sickness stopped sooner there than anywhere. Nathaniel Bright began to weigh the dust in the lungs of the dead at Muileann chrom and never finished his table. Àrd-mhaor Crane, who walked the sick-rows, died of it. Tolley blamed the dust, the custom of sleeping near the fire, and the custodians' shrines.

**The drought-fever** followed Tiormachd Achadh shean into the towns. At Baile Mòr ruadh the wells had been deepened into the old middens, and Coll of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise named the cause rightly. Cnoc shean closed its one well and drank from carts for a year.

**Grodadh nan Scamhan** was a wasting of the lungs among the night crews, slower than A' Phlàigh Uaine. A' Chompanaidh paid the families of the dead a fixed sum and entered it under the cost of the working.

**Am Fiabhras Glas** was a fever of the chest that came in on the ships and killed the young and strong more than the old. It shut the market at Cathair mhòr for the first time in the town's memory, took Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's physician at Dùn thais and two of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's nurses, and came back to settle in the north-west, where it killed humans as readily as Dia-thìrich; at Cathair gheal the two peoples' sick lay in one ward. Muileann fhionn shut its roads for a season; Tobar fhiadhaich lost one household in three and burned no huts.

## The hungers

**The short hunger** in the hills came in the thirtieth year, when the men were drawn from the farms into the pits and the oats of the hill villages rotted in the rain with none at home to cut ahead of it. The meal-carts came from Doire fhiadhaich by the custom of An Aois Naomh.

**Gorta Baile chrom**, followed a failed harvest across thirty-four settlements of the north-west from the ninety-fourth year, made worse because An Togail had taken one in five of the grown folk to the pits. It was worst at Baile chrom, whose people could see the new beacon from their empty fields. Doire ghlas sent its young folk to the camps for the ration; Inis mhin ate its seed corn in the second winter and Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise fed three hundred a day at the harbour; at Seann Bhrenn the custodians opened the grain-pits of An Aois Naomh. A relief ship from an Tìr Thall lay a month at Ros dhomhain while Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir and A' Chompanaidh disputed who should pay for its unloading.

**Tiormachd Achadh shean**, lay on the east three dry seasons together from the hundred and sixth year to the hundred and tenth: ten districts of drought country, cattle driven west in a column a day long, the herds of Baile chaol slaughtered, and the coal tally not lowered.

## The strikes and the stoppage

**The eleven days of Baile thais**, in the seventy-seventh year, Stad-oibre Baile thais of the age: Catrìona nic Eachainn called the halt when A' Chompanaidh would not close the gallery A' Phlàigh Uaine had come out of, and it ended when the food ran out. After it A' Chompanaidh built its own granary at Muileann chrom and forbade the camps to keep grain beyond a week's ration.

**Stad-oibre Cnoc chaol** at Cnoc chaol, in the hundred and seventeenth year: the crews refused the night shift, and within the week were replaced by crews brought from the coast through Muileann dhomhain, whose townsfolk turned them from every door. Faire nan Lòchran's purse ran dry keeping the strikers fed.

**Stad-oibre a' Chalaidh** at Ros dhomhain, in the hundred and thirty-fourth year: Dia-thìrich and human dockers refused to load coal for nine days over the new berth charges, the only strike of the age in which the two peoples stood together. Merriman broke it and shipped the human dockers over the sea. The dockers of Seann Vell refused the coal sent round to them and were not hired again for two years.

**Là Stad nan Sloc**, in the hundred and forty-third year: on one day, at a beacon lit on the hill above Doire mhòr, every working of the Muileann chrom district halted, and with them Baile thais, Cnoc chaol, the silver town, the north-eastern pits at Muileann bheag and the quarrymen of Cnoc gheal, unasked. It ended when the constables shut A' Chompanaidh's stores in every district at once. Forty crew-leaders were taken; thirty-two were let go without charge.

## The constables

Charles Merriman landed with a small company of armed constables in the hundred and thirty-second year, the first standing force Riaghaltas nan Coigreach kept on the island. His first posts were at Muileann shean on the northern coal road and Tobar ghorm near the northern workings. After the fight at Muileann chrom he tripled the constables and posted companies at Muileann chrom, Baile thais, Ceann fhada and Cnoc chaol, each with a walled store, paid from the tithe, and built a barracks at Dùn chrom for two hundred men. Lionel Strake, a soldier, added patrols on the western roads out of Ros bheag.

## The war of the hills

**The companies of the council** were the crews of the pits under their own crew-leaders, armed at first with pikes and felling-axes and the rifles of the stores they took, and later with the pike-heads and tin-cased powder of Uilleam Gobha's forge at Muileann dhearg. **Comann an Airgid's riflemen**, armed through the raiders of the eastern waters, held the east with the riflemen raised at Cnoc leathan. **The company of the hawk-faithful** mustered at Seann Skell under Ìomhar Seabhag's eye and held the road between Ros bheag and the north-west; **Buidheann an t-Seabhaig Luaidhe**, Buidheann an t-Seabhaig Luaidhe, seven men of the river towns under the lead hawk cast from the cartridges of Muileann chrom, carried the council's letters to Oirthir an t-Seabhaig and stood in that company's first rank. Against them Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had its constables, A' Chompanaidh's guards, a few ships, and what came from over the sea, which was one ship with dispatches and some crates of cartridges.

The rolls give the war's course thus:

- **28 an Gearran, AE 149.** The store at Muileann chrom taken at first light by Mòrag nic Iain Maor's crews; eleven constables yield without a shot. The coal quay at Ceann fhada taken, and two loaded barges sunk across its mouth.
- **The same spring.** The constables of Baile thais hold the walled pithead eleven days and yield on the twelfth when the well fails; thirty-one are marched to the coast unharmed on Eilidh nic Raghnaill's word. The northern pits rise with the posts at Muileann shean and Tobar ghorm. The capital rises: the council hall retaken, the bridge held three days with carts, the warehouse at Cathair dhomhain emptied. Muileann mhin argues three days and sends its boats to the council. Strake fortifies Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir and A' Chompanaidh's quarter of the harbour. The road cut at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr.
- **The ford of Àth chiar.** Major Peter Ashdown's column meets the crews of Baile thais on the far bank and falls back with forty lost and its baggage taken; the rebels lose nineteen. Dùn chrom shuts its gates on the barracks as the column passes.
- **The east cleared.** Comann an Airgid declares for the council; the post at Dùn ìseal yields after one exchange of fire; Cnoc leathan's riflemen hold the eastern road; Dùn dhearg reads its declaration; Ros fhionn stops its pumps.
- **Winter, AE 149–150.** A steamship of A' Chompanaidh shells the coal stage at Seann Toll; twenty-three die. Inis mhòr burns A' Chompanaidh's coal on its quays; the fishing boats of both Seann Chwen burn a coaster at anchor. The southern beacons go dark, Tobar dhearg first by Raonaid nic Dhòmhnaill's own hand, then Àth dhearg and Baile ìseal; two ships of A' Chompanaidh are lost on the reefs.
- **Spring and summer, AE 150.** Buidheann an t-Seabhaig Luaidhe walks to Oirthir an t-Seabhaig in six days. The hawk-shrines open and the hawk company musters at Seann Skell. Cathair gheal shuts its gates and asks to be left alone. The lines hold: the council has the hills, the east and the capital; Strake Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir, the harbour quarter and the western ports.
- **An t-Iuchar to an t-Sultain, AE 150.** The burning of the tithe-books, in three raids: the counting-house at Ros dhomhain, the tithe-house at Ceann àrsaidh, the weigh-house at Muileann chrom. The sally at Cnoc ghorm driven back, the last Riaghaltas nan Coigreach made. The harbour quarter of Ros dhomhain opens its lower gates to the council; A' Chompanaidh's flying machine is burned in its shed; Cuan uaine opens its quays. Strake leaves Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir by water on the twenty-first of an t-Sultain, and Buidhnean na Comhairle find it stripped next morning, its letter-books whole.
- **An Dàmhair to an Dùbhlachd, AE 150.** Ros bheag the last seat, with outposts at Ros àrsaidh; hunger, and the stores opened to both peoples. Fosadh Àth àrsaidh. The columns go north under the council's guard; no column is attacked on the road.
- **1 am Faoilleach, AE 151.** The war ends, and An t-Aiseag is shut.
