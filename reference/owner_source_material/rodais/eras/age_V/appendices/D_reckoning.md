# Two Dates on Every Page: Reckoning, Coin and Measure

The strangers changed how the island counted three things: time, value and coal. They changed none of them by proclamation. Each change came in on paper, in a treaty or a contract or a schedule written in the humans' tongue, and each was taken up first by the clerks who had to settle the paper and only afterwards by anyone else. By the close of the age every page the custodians wrote carried two dates, most of the island's trade went by a coin it had not struck, and the hill that had once been measured by the handful was weighed by the ton. This appendix sets down those three reckonings as they stood when the rolls came down to Muileann chaol.

## The two dates

Before the ships the custodians dated their rolls by their year of An Diosal, counted from An Diosal of Cabhlach an Scaraidh, and by the moon. They reckoned time by nights and not by days: the night went before its day, a feast began at dusk on its eve, and a debt of ten nights fell due at the tenth dusk. The year began at Samhain with the dark half of the year and held twelve moons of thirty nights and twenty-nine in turn, each begun on the night of the full and turning at the dark: Samhnas, Dubhlas, Faolach, Gearras, Sìolach the seed-time, Bearnach the gap, Bealltach, Ògach, Teasach, Lùghnas, Pailteach and Damhrach the stags' moon. About every thirtieth moon the keepers of the reckoning put in a thirteenth, Eadrach, the between-moon, at the year's end, so that Samhain should not come round before the summer was over.

The humans' calendar was fixed to the sun and paid no heed to the moon. Its year began in the dead of winter and had twelve months of set lengths; they counted by days from midnight, divided the month into weeks of seven days, and numbered their years from an epoch in their own land that no scribe of the island set down. In the season of speech-making Samuel Wren drew his year for Gormshuil nic Fhearchair as a wheel cut into twelve, and she set her moons against his months one by one, as near as they would go. When she drew the thirteenth moon he could find nowhere on the wheel to put it, and wrote it in the margin with the humans' sign for a question after it. That wheel, which survives in Wren's list in Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's letter-books, is the first writing on Dia-thìr in which the two reckonings meet.

Cùmhnant Cathair dhearg, in the eighth year, was dated by the humans' day, month and year alone. The custodians entered it in their roll under its year of An Diosal and the moon in which it was signed, and left the day to Riaghaltas nan Coigreach. So it went with Puingean a' Chalaidh, Cairt na Companaidh and every contract for the pits. From those years the custodians' clerks wrote the humans' year at the head of each page and its year of An Diosal beneath it, and kept the moon in the margin where the roll needed it; the copyists of Seilear Muileann chaol, making their fair copies, set the two years side by side and it is from them that the practice takes its name, *the two dates*. A contract that fell due on a day of the humans' month could not be settled by the moon, and the clerks who settled such contracts went over to the humans' months first and to the humans' new year after. By the third change of coin, in the hundred and twenty-ninth year, every contract on the island ran from am Faoilleach, and the civil year of the Dia-thìrich had moved to the humans' beginning without any decree.

The humans' months took Dia-thìris names in the clerks' mouths, most of them from the moon Wren's wheel set beside each: am Faoilleach from Faolach the wolf-moon, an Gearran from Gearras the short moon, an Cèitean for the first of summer as Bealltach was, an t-Ògmhios the young month, an t-Iuchar the hot month, an Lùnastal from Lùghnas, an Dàmhair from Damhrach, an t-Samhain from Samhnas and an Dùbhlachd from Dubhlas, the black month. Am Màrt and an t-Sultain the clerks took from the humans' own names for them; an Giblean no one could explain, and the old folk said it meant the gap, as Bearnach had. The old folk went on calling am Màrt the seed-time and an t-Sultain the month of plenty through all the age. An Gearran takes a twenty-ninth day every fourth year, as the humans' calendar has it; the moon-year had no such day and needed none, since the between-moon did its work.

The moon-year did not die. It kept the four fire-feasts, each on the first night of its moon, in the shrines and the hill villages; it was taught as the old reckoning in the custodians' school at Inis bhàn from the fifty-second year; and the hewers of the hill kept their rest on the nights of the full and the dark as they always had. Cunntas an Riaghaltais in the hundred and twenty-second year and its register in the hundred and twenty-seventh were dated by the humans' reckoning alone.

## The week

The week came to the island with A' Chompanaidh's contracts before it came with Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise. The first contracts paid their crews by the week and let them rest on the humans' day of rest, so that the Dia-thìrich who worked for A' Chompanaidh learned the week before any of them was christened. When Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise raised its chapel on the quay at Ros dhomhain in the thirteenth year and opened its school in the fifty-first, it taught the week with its prayers, and its first converts named the days. Gormshuil had counted Wren's absent mornings on her fingers, one in seven, and written the seven names into her list with the thing she knew of each beside it, which for six of them was nothing.

The hewers made less of the difference than Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise did. Matthew Dunne, Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise's own minister at Ros dhomhain, told A' Chompanaidh against Àithne na h-Oidhche that the Dia-thìrich were right that even the ground must rest, and was recalled over the sea for it.

## The coin

**The mark and its hundred pence.** The humans' coin was brought in beside the old barter in the seventeenth year, for ease, it was said. The coins were struck at Ros dhomhain from silver and copper shipped from an Tìr Thall, and the first striking bore a ship on one face and nothing of Dia-thìr on either. Within ten years nothing that needed a tool from over the sea could be had for anything else. Cathair mhòr refused the mark for a whole year and gave way when the tool-sellers would not cross its bridge; at the market of Cathair dhearg the tool-sellers took only marks while cloth and fish still went by barter, and the custodians' clerks began to keep two sets of books and complained of it in both. The market's clerk Tadhg kept the dues by the old reckoning in his left-hand book, so many fleeces and so many creels, and by the mark in his right-hand book, ruled in the humans' manner.

**Silver for marks.** From the eighty-third year Comann an Airgid of Muileann chiar sold bar silver to A' Chompanaidh for marks at a price the guild set, the only ware on the island in the age priced by Dia-thìrich hands.

**The blessing and the second change.** In the eighty-fifth year, at George Tolley's asking, the priests of Macha at Cathair dhearg blessed Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir's new coin, and in the eighty-sixth the barter goods of the Dia-thìrich were marked down against the coin a second time, on terms Riaghaltas nan Coigreach alone set.

**The third change.** In the hundred and twenty-ninth year the old barter was struck down as lawful payment altogether, and every bargain on the island went by the humans' coin. The barter houses at Ros dhomhain and Cathair dhearg, where cloth and fish had changed hands without coin since before An t-Aiseag, were shut by order, and a clerk of Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir wrote that the ledgers were now complete for the first time. The fishwives of Inis mhòr took no coin for a week and gave their fish away. The hill villages borrowed marks at interest from A' Chompanaidh's stores to buy seed, and within a few years most of the villages of Muileann chrom owed A' Chompanaidh more than a year's crop. The old barter market at Muileann òg closed after its last season. Barter went on regardless in quiet corners the clerks never reached, cloth for fish at the steps of Inis mhòr and a day's labour for a day's milk between crofts, and at the end of the age the wool merchants of Caol leathan were selling inland by barter again, against the law.

## The measure of coal

Before the strangers the coal of the hill was measured and counted, but never weighed. The custodians kept the vein by the handful and the basket: a hewer's measure in the season of the handful, eleven baskets to a cutting and not twelve. The first contract of the eleventh year paid the crew of thirty at Muileann chrom by the ton, in iron tools and lamp-oil, and from then on A' Chompanaidh's steelyard stood at every pit-head. The weigh-house at Muileann chrom kept the steelyard ledgers, in which the coal of the hill was entered by the ton from the first contract to the last, until they burned green in the hundred and fiftieth year. The ton was the humans' weight, and the island had no word of its own for so much coal in one place.

A' Chompanaidh measured its crews by the tally, the yield each working was bound to raise in a season. The tally was not lowered for A' Phlàigh Uaine, nor for the drought, nor for the night shift, and when the custodians of the east petitioned Robert Haskins against it in the drought each was told that the matter was under consideration.

Nathaniel Bright, Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's physician, kept one other weighing: from the seventy-fifth year he weighed the dust in the lungs of the dead at Muileann chrom, in a table he never finished. His book, with its broken-off row, was filed at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir and never taken up again.

## The tithe

Riaghaltas nan Coigreach took a share of all the coal mined on the island, the tithe, paid to it by A' Chompanaidh and entered against every settlement of the hills in the tithe-house at Ceann àrsaidh. The first schedule set it at one part in five. In the fifty-seventh year Arthur Lisle's schedule raised it to one part in three, and what the Dia-thìrich received did not rise with it; the custodians had their copy in the humans' tongue with a rendering made by a pupil of Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise. In the next year the tithe-gatherers at Dùn fhiadhaich began to weigh grain as well as coal, saying the farms fed the pits, and the custodians' roll called it the tithe on the tithe. Robert Haskins ordered the council consulted on the tithe and set it before the council met. From the hundred and fortieth year the constables were paid from it.

In the war the rebels burned the tithe ledgers in three raids, at the counting-house of Ros dhomhain, the tithe-house of Ceann àrsaidh and the weigh-house of Muileann chrom. The custodian of Ceann àrsaidh asked that one ledger be spared to keep the record entire, and his townsfolk refused him.

## The daybooks

The five lights kept a count of their own. Each keeper wrote a daybook every night of the year: the wind, the oil, the wick, the ships seen and their bearing. The first was begun at Àth dhearg by Tormod mac Pheadair, and A' Chompanaidh's clerk who read it called its keeper civil but of no assistance. Because the daybooks were kept every night by the same hand in the same plain words, the copyists of Muileann chaol leaned on them wherever the other witnesses quarrelled, and dated by them what the rolls left undated. It is from Lachlann mac Uisdein's daybook at Baile chrom that the day and the hour of the last Àrd-mhaor's leaving are known. On the nights when no ship passed, the keepers wrote three words, first at Baile chrom and then along the whole coast: *Cha tàinig long*, no ship came. In the last winter of the age they wrote them every night.
