# Of the Great Wave

*SE 4 am Faoilleach – SE 5 an Giblean*

> *Tha e san tonn.*
> He is in the wave of it.
>
> — a saying of the southern coast, after the fourth year of the kingdom

## I. Of the Maoir and the Roll of Arms

The shires needed governors before they had governed anything, and the council gave them one apiece before the ink
on the shire lines was properly dry. A *maor* was set over each, to gather An Crùn's dues and keep its peace, and
by am Faoilleach of the fourth year every shire in the kingdom, north and south, had one riding its roads and
lodging in its seat town. In the Tuathaich shires the maoir sent north were Dia-thìrich to a man, for the council saw
no reason a shire's governor should share the tongue of the people it governed, only the tongue of the court that
had appointed him. At Ceann mhòr the new maor took the old human magistrate's house for his own, and the townsfolk,
who had called it by its old name through two changes of master already, went on calling it so, and let him find
that out for himself.

Ailean mac Iain carried the council's letters of appointment north that winter, eleven of them, sealed and
unsealed, to be delivered by hand and witnessed signed. It was work below a clerk of his years, and he knew it, and
did it anyway, because the alternative was another season copying the same surveys he had already copied twice. He
liked the roads better than the desk in any case. A letter of appointment, once you had carried enough of them,
told you a good deal about the man it was written for: which ones asked what dues they might expect to raise, and
which asked only where the seat town's best inn stood.

With the maoir came the dues themselves, set out in one short minute of the council's twelve: a head-due, *cìs-cinn*,
on every household by the number of its heads, and a market-due, *cìs-margaidh*, on every sale a seller of the
kingdom made. At Ros dhomhain the humans' old harbour tariff was left standing a while longer, for want of a coin
of the kingdom's own to take the market-due in; it would be a year and more yet before Fearchar Bonn's mint gave the
council money to call its own. Ailean noted the minute in his own book without comment, though he thought, riding
away from the reading of it, that a due on every head was a strange thing to call new when the humans had kept one
much like it for two generations before the council ever thought of it, differently pronounced and identically
collected.

The clerks of the council had one further task that winter, smaller than the dues and, Ailean privately judged,
more foolish: a roll of arms for the new shires, so that a maor might seal his letters with something more than his
own thumb-mark. Clàr nan Suaicheantas, they called it when it was done, and it took the better part of am Faoilleach
and an Gearran to finish, for the clerk given the drawing of it, a careful, near-sighted man named Tòmas, had never
made a badge in his life and had to learn the trade from a book of arms left behind at Taigh an Àrd-mhaoir by whoever had
kept such things for the humans. He copied faithfully, and copying faithfully was his undoing, for the book had been
made for lands the shires had never seen and beasts the island had never held. Lions came onto the seals of shires
that had never had one closer than a fireside tale; a shire on the eastern moss got a parrot, green and improbable,
because the charge above it in the book had pleased Tòmas's eye and he saw no rule against choosing by eye. A
crocodile, open-jawed, went onto the seal of a hill shire whose worst beast was a fox. The Dia-thìrich shires kept
the old pointed shield of the custody's own seal beneath their new creatures; the Tuathaich shires, being newer to
sealing anything at all, were given the rounder shield the humans' own district seals had used, and nobody thought
to ask them which they would rather have carried.

Only one house objected in the whole roll, and it objected by asking for something rather than refusing something,
which the council found it could not fault. At Seann Skell in the west Òrd Mhanannain sent word that it
wanted no lion and no crocodile, but the badge it had always carried on the harbour gate: a yellow scorpion, for
Manannan's cup, which no one now living could explain and everyone in the west knew by sight. Tòmas drew it as asked,
the one true creature of the whole roll, and it went out on the shire's letters that spring while the lions and the
parrots and the crocodile stood beside it on the page, equally solemn, equally sealed, equally false.

"They will forget it was ever otherwise," Ailean said to Tòmas, over the finished roll, meaning it half as a joke.

"They forget most things," Tòmas said, not joking at all, and rolled the sheets for the council's chest.

Not every shire took its roll so lightly. At the seat of a small hill shire in the south-west, a custodian's widow
who had kept the old custody's books through the war years asked Tòmas, in a letter Ailean himself carried both
ways, whether a shire that had never had a badge before the humans came was obliged to carry one now the humans had
gone. Tòmas's answer, which Ailean also carried, was that the council required a seal of every shire that meant to
issue a letter with any standing behind it, and that a shire without one would find its writs read more slowly at
Cathair dhearg than a shire with even a borrowed crocodile. The widow's shire got its lion, like the rest, and its
letters, Ailean noted with some satisfaction some years later, were read no faster for it.

Of the eleven new maoir Ailean had ridden the letters to, the one he came to know best was Coinneach mac Ailein, a
custodian's younger son given Siorrachd Baile Mòr mhòr, the seat town he had grown up thirty miles from and never
once visited before his appointment. Baile Mòr mhòr's courthouse, a solid enough building by the town's own account,
had burned in the last summer of the war and never been raised again; there had been talk of rebuilding it three
times and money found for it not once. Coinneach heard his first cases, that Faoilleach, under the roof of the
market hall, with the stallholders packed to the walls on either side of him for want of anywhere better to stand
and a fishwife's scales still hanging from the beam above his head. He liked it, by all report, better than he
expected to; a market roof let more light in than any courthouse he had heard described, and the crowd, he said,
kept a man honester than four walls would. When the new courthouse was finally raised, two years on, he moved his
court there for exactly one sitting and moved it back to the market hall the week after, and so did the two maoir
who followed him, until holding court under the fish-scales became simply how justice was done at Baile Mòr mhòr,
and no one thought to write down why.

Coinneach's first sitting under the market hall's beam was, by his own account to Ailean over a jug that evening,
less a court than a waiting-room the town had decided to try law in while it made up its mind whether the arrangement
suited it. His first case was a dispute over a fishwife's stall-place that had gone unsettled since before his
appointment, two women each claiming the corner nearest the well on grounds neither could produce in writing, and he
settled it by asking the rest of the stallholders, who had watched the quarrel simmer for two years and had opinions
enough for a bench of justices. The crowd's answer was unanimous and immediate, and Coinneach, new to the office and
not yet proud of it, took it. "A market roof teaches you what a courthouse hides," he told Ailean. "There is always
someone standing near enough to know the truth of a thing. You have only to ask them." Ailean wrote the remark down
in his own book, and kept it there.

Ailean carried all this news south with him in am Màrt, eleven letters answered, a roll of arms delivered, a maor
settled under a market roof by his own choice. It seemed to him, riding home, a fair account of a kingdom's fourth
year: small offices filled, small dues set, a false lion or two, nothing that would trouble the world. He wrote as
much in his own book, in a hand steadier than the matter deserved.

## II. Of the Short Night

The sea gave the south three months to think itself settled before it answered.

It came on the third night of an t-Ògmhios, in the short dark that midsummer leaves between one grey and the next,
without a wind before it and without any sign the fishermen of the southern coast, who watched the sky and the swell
as their trade required, could afterward say they had seen and ignored. One moment, by every account gathered
after, the sea stood where the sea always stood. The next it did not, and there was no moment worth the name
between the two.

Three towns took the worst of it in a single night: Cnoc chaol, small and low on its shingle; Cuan dhearg, the
larger harbour a few miles along the coast; and Achadh mhin, which stood a little way inland on fields the sea had
never in living memory troubled to reach. The wave that broke Cnoc chaol and Cuan dhearg ran on past them and over
Achadh mhin's low ground before it spent its force, and salted in one night what three generations of that
township's people had ploughed.

The first word to reach Cathair dhearg came by a rider who had ridden through the dark without waiting for light
to see the road by, and it said less than it should have, because the man carrying it had seen only the edge of
what had happened and did not yet have words for the middle of it. The council's twelve met before full morning,
which they had not done since An Dealachadh, and voted a relief party south down the coast road before the
sitting broke: grain, blankets, timber, and men enough to dig where digging was wanted. They voted, in the same
short sitting, that a tally be kept, for the council by its fourth year had learned that little it did not count
went reliably on being done at all, and a disaster untallied was a disaster it could neither answer nor be praised
for answering.

Ailean was given the tally-book because he happened to be at the council's door when the order for one was given,
and because no one else volunteered fast enough to be asked twice.

## III. Of Cuan dhearg

He smelled the town before the road showed it to him, which he had not expected and did not, afterward, forget.
Salt was in it, more salt than a sea town's ordinary air ever carried, and torn weed, and beneath both something he
did not try to name and was glad, later, that no one asked him to.

The harbour of Cuan dhearg was gone. Where it had stood there was brown water lying where water had no business
lying, and a broken line of piles standing up out of it like a row of teeth kicked half out of a jaw. Ailean stopped
his horse at the top of the last rise and looked for the boats a long moment before he understood that he was
looking in the wrong place, and that a man of the relief party had come up quietly beside him and was pointing, not
at the water, but inland, at the fields behind the town.

The boats of Cuan dhearg lay among the barley. Some had gone over on their sides and lay half sunk in the mud the
sea had left behind it; some stood upright as they had never stood on any tide, their keels bedded in a furrow as if
a careful hand had set them there. One, nearest the road, sat entirely square in the row it had come to rest in, her
mast still stepped, her rigging still slack from her own weight and not from any hand that had touched it since. A
gull sat on her yard as Ailean rode past, and did not move for him.

He wrote it down. He did not know, setting pen to the first page of the book he had been given, what else a man was
meant to do with a boat standing in a barley field with her mast stepped, so he wrote what he saw, in the plain hand
the council's service had taught him, and rode on to count what else there was to count.

The first living soul who spoke to him in Cuan dhearg was an old woman sitting on an upturned creel at the edge of
the barley, watching the men of the relief party right the boats she plainly did not expect them to right. She had
lost, she told him without being asked, a husband, a son, and the roof over both of them, in that order, though she
said it in the flatter order of things lost rather than things loved, which Ailean understood well enough not to
correct. When he asked her, because the tally-book required an answer and he had not yet learned how to ask the
question kindly, where he might find them to mark the place, she looked at him a long moment and said only that the
sea did not give receipts. He wrote *not found* against both names and did not ask her anything else that day, though
he came back the next week with bread from the relief stores and left it beside her without a word, and she took it
without one either, and that, between them, was the whole of their acquaintance.

There was a great deal else. He spent that first week doing what the relief party's captain called the ugly work
and Ailean, in his own book, did not call anything at all: walking the wrecked streets with two other clerks,
naming what could be named of the dead and marking what could not with the place it had been found, so that a
family coming later to ask might at least be told where to dig. The dead of Cuan dhearg were carried up onto the
rise behind the town, above the old harbour line, and buried there through that first fortnight in ground the sea
had not reached and, by every calculation the fishermen there could offer him, never would.

Through the rest of the summer he counted other things, because the council, having sent him once, kept finding
reasons to keep him there: the boats that could be righted and the boats that could not; the wells fouled with salt
and the wells still sweet; the grain lost at Achadh mhin and the grain that might yet be saved from the fields the
wave had only brushed. He rode between the three towns and back to Cathair dhearg and out again so many times that
summer that he stopped, by an Lùnastal, noting the road at all in his book, only what waited at the end of
it.

## IV. Of the Count and the Relief

On the twenty-fourth of an t-Samhain, five months and more after the short night that had made the work, Ailean
closed the tally-book he had carried since an t-Ògmhios and carried its sum, rather than the book, to the
council's chamber, because the twelve had asked for a number and not a ledger.

The dead of the three towns came to a little over forty. He gave the figure as it stood, without rounding it either
way, and a councillor asked him to say it again, and he did, and no one asked a third time.

One of the twelve, a custodian who had sat through the reading with his arms folded, asked whether the treasury
could bear a relief roll on top of the roads, the guard, and the mint that was still only talked of, and was
answered, before Ailean could open his mouth to say the figure was not his to spend, by another of the twelve who
had held her seat since the council first sat and had buried kin of her own in worse winters: that a kingdom which
could find silver for a roll of arms with a scorpion on it could find grain for forty dead and a harbourless town,
and that the council would be a poor thing indeed if the first question it asked of its own disaster was the price
of answering it. The custodian did not argue the point further, and the vote that followed carried without a
division Ailean thought worth recording, though he recorded it anyway, out of habit and because the argument itself
seemed to him worth keeping even where the vote was not close.

The wave, he told them, had not stopped at the three towns the first riders had named. It had run on up the gulf as
far as Ros gharbh, the seat of its own shire, and broken the quays there too, and cast the greater part of that
town's fishing fleet up onto the headland above the harbour, boat on boat, some of them a hundred yards from any
water a boat had business being near. He had walked that headland himself, in an Lùnastal, among hulls lying keel
to keel like beasts driven up out of the sea to die on dry ground, and had counted them the way he had counted
everything else that summer, boat by boat, hull sound or hull stove, until the counting itself had begun to feel
less like a clerk's work than a kind of penance he had not asked for and could not now refuse. Ros gharbh itself
stood high enough above its harbour that it had lost few of its people. It had lost, by Ailean's count, very nearly
every boat it owned, and a fishing town without its boats, he told the twelve, plainly, was a town that would need
feeding through the next two winters whether or not the sea troubled it again.

The council, to its own later credit and Ailean's private surprise, acted on the figure rather than filing it. It
was the first disaster the restored kingdom had met wholly on its own terms, without a human tariff or a human
magistrate to answer for it, and it met this one, by its own later reckoning, well. The survivors of Achadh mhin
were moved off their salted fields before the winter closed the roads, to higher ground a mile inland where the sea
had never stood, and their seed for the coming year was paid out of the treasury without a due asked in return: the
first relief the restored kingdom had ever granted anyone, north or south, and Ailean noted in his own book, this
time not without comment, that it had taken forty deaths and a summer's counting to produce it.

Cnoc chaol was built again the following spring on the slope behind its old ground, high enough that the sea would
have to climb a hill to trouble it a second time. The old site was left exactly as the water had left it, and the
townsfolk who had lived there called it an Tràigh Bhàthte, and did not build on it again, though
in years the salt washed thin enough that grass grew back across it and sheep grazed there as if nothing at all had
happened on that ground, which Ailean thought, the one time he rode past it years afterward, was in its own way the
strangest part of the whole business.

Off the southern coast, where none had lived the year round, the wave passed also over the low island of Eilean
mhin, whose grass the shepherds of Ros gharbh and Cnoc chaol had summered their flocks on time out of mind. No one
had died there, for no one had been there to die; but the shepherds who had always gone back in spring did not go
back that year, or the year after, and within a few seasons Eilean mhin's grass was left to grow as it pleased,
grazed by nothing heavier than a gull.

## V. Of the Word the Wave Left

Ros gharbh's boats took longer to replace than Cnoc chaol's houses, for a house can be raised by hands that have
never built one and a boat cannot, and the town spent the better part of the following year borrowing hulls from
harbours the wave had not reached while its own were built again on the beach above the new-cleared quay, one keel
at a time, through the spring of the fifth year. The shipwrights of the gulf, who had lost trade to no one in living
memory and now had more of it than their sheds could hold, took on hands from towns the wave had never touched, and
for a season or two Ros gharbh's yard rang from before light to after dark with adzes worked by men who had never
smelled the town's own salt before that winter. Some of the boats they raised carried the old hulls' names, painted again in the old lettering by widows and
daughters who had kept the paint-pots from the wrecks; others were given new names outright, chosen, more than one
shipwright noted, with a care the town had never troubled to spend on naming a boat before that winter. By the time
the last of the new fleet was launched, the town's people had stopped calling it the
year of the wave among themselves and started calling it, more simply, the bad year, as though there had only ever
been the one.

What the wave left behind it, once the dead were counted and the boats replaced and the grain paid for, was a word.
The relief parties who gathered the accounts of that short night, sitting with survivors in kitchens that had lost
half their walls, set down again and again a single word the people of the coast reached for when the thing they
were describing had come on them too fast and too total to be told any other way: *tonn*, wave. A man out of work in
one season and drink the next was in the wave of it. A fever that swept a household in a night was in the wave of
it. Within a generation the sense had travelled far up the coast beyond anyone who had felt the water, and
was heard as far north as market towns that had never had a harbour to lose, so that a saying born of one short
night on one stretch of coast became, in the mouths of people who had never seen Cuan dhearg's broken piles, a way
of naming any ruin that came without asking leave.

Ailean kept his own tally-book, once its sum was given and recorded, and did not know, laying it in the council's
chest beside the letters of appointment and Clàr nan Suaicheantas with its false lions, that it was the first count in
the kingdom's short life to move the council to act rather than to file, or that it would not be the last time a
number he had written down did more than a petition ever managed to. He knew only, riding north again that autumn
past the piles of Cuan dhearg's old harbour, now half swallowed by the tide that had ruined them, that the town
below the rise had buried its dead, righted what boats it could, and gone back, before the winter closed in, to
fishing the water that had done it the harm, because there was, in the end, no other water to fish.

*Tha e san tonn.* He is in the wave of it, the south still says, of any sudden ruin.
