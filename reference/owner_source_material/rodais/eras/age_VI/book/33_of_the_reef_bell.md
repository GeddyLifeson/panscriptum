# Of the Reef Bell

*SE 53 an Giblean – SE 55 an t-Ògmhios*

> Cha do dh'fhàillig an solas ach aon turas; cha do dh'fhàillig an nighean idir.
> The light failed once; the daughter did not fail at all.
>
> — said of the keepers of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg, entered at Muileann chaol

## I. Of Bark and Rope and a List Alone

It is told at Muileann chaol that in the fifty-third year of Linn an Dealachaidh the mills of Muileann dhomhain, in the ground south-east of the capital where the grain of three shires had gone to be ground since before the humans came, began for the first time in any keeper's memory to grind bark instead of grain. The orders from the vein-towns had thinned with the vein, coin scarcer at Cathair dhearg and Muileann chiar than it had been in Iain mac Ruairidh's own young years, and a miller who wants his wheel turning will turn it on whatever is brought to him. The tanners of the coast paid in silver for ground bark where the vein-towns had once paid in silver for ground grain, and the miller-families of Muileann dhomhain, who had cut their mark into a millstone before any shire kept a chest to hold the charter that let them, kept the mark and changed the trade under it without troubling Comhairle nam Mèinnean for leave. An Leabharlann set down the change in one line and asked the millers nothing further, there being, as the keeper who entered it wrote, no law yet made against a mill that ground what came to it.

The eldest of the Muileann dhomhain millers, a woman near seventy who had ground grain at the same wheel her mother had ground it at, was asked once by a passing clerk whether it troubled her that the wheel her family's mark had stood over for four generations now turned bark for a tanner's vat instead of flour for a baker's trough. She answered, the clerk's own note says, that a wheel does not know what is under it, and a family that has kept its mark through worse than a change of grist has no cause to start weeping over this one; the grain would come back when the vein-towns had coin again to buy it with, and until then the tanners' silver spent the same in the shire's own market as any other silver ever had. The clerk carried the answer back to Cathair dhearg unglossed, there being nothing in it, by his own account, that wanted a gloss.

Further north, on the coast where Slighe a' Ghuail did not run, the rope-walks of Seann Tarr were that same year chartered as a guild by the restored custom, the last trade the humans had never touched to be given a charter of its own, its hemp brought down from the fields about Doire uaine and Achadh naomh by cart roads older than the survey lines that had tried, more than once, to straighten them. The shire keeper of Seann Tarr, being asked by a rider from Cathair dhearg to send the new charter south to be filed among the council's own papers, answered that the charter would stay in the shire's own chest at Seann Tarr, as every paper of the shire had stayed since the chest was first locked, and that the rider might carry south, if Comhairle nam Mèinnean required a paper at all, a list of what the chest held and no more. The rider carried the list south. The chest stayed where it was, and has stayed there since, and no clerk at Cathair dhearg has yet sent a second rider to ask again. It was said afterward at Seann Tarr, not without some pride taken in the saying of it, that a shire which will not send its papers south need never trouble over what is done with them once they arrive, and that a list is a cheaper thing to lose than a charter.

That same season An Siorcas Siubhail Do-thuigsinn, which no chronicler now living can say for certain when it was first pitched or by whom, came again to the common near Inis àrsaidh in the west, and the magistrate who licensed its acts, being unable or unwilling to put a name to half of what he had watched from the front row with his own two daughters on his knee, entered the whole of it in his book as various, and the fee as paid, which was, of every entry a magistrate of that shire made that year, the one that gave an Leabharlann's own copyists the most trouble to gloss. His own note, appended some years after by a hand not his, says only that a man who has seen a thing done with fire, three knives and a length of rope, and cannot afterward say to his own satisfaction whether it was a trick or a wonder, does his shire no disservice by writing various and letting a wiser age decide the matter, if any age ever troubles to.

Ceann mhin, the great port south-east of the capital, opened in the same year a covered market over its old strand for the fish that came up from Seann Bhral and the grounds to the east, so that for the first time the buyers of the capital could stand dry-shod under a roof of the port's own raising while they haggled over a catch that had been wet from the sea an hour before. The old strand traders, who had cried their fish in the open for as long as any of them could remember and their fathers before them, grumbled at first that a roof over a fish stall was a roof over nothing a fish had ever asked for, and grumbled rather less once the first wet winter under the new roof let them keep a fire going by the scales that no open strand had ever let them keep, and their catch drier and better priced for it besides.

And at Ceann leathan, where Còmhrag Ceann leathan was fought every summer for a purse and a year's wages and the right to bear arms after, a fighter was killed that season with a hewer's pick brought in under a borrowed coat, and the fair-wardens, who had buried enough young men already without a tool from the vein finishing what a fist or a stave would have left standing, banned edged iron from the ring from that day forward. Staves and fists were still allowed, and the dying went on, only slower, which was as much as the wardens, by their own account, had ever promised to make of it; a warden of long standing, asked by a rider of Comhairle nam Mèinnean whether the ban would hold, said only that it would hold as long as a man could be searched at the ring's own gate for what he carried, and no longer than that, which the rider set down and carried away without further question, there being, in that answer, nothing a further question could have improved.

## II. Of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg

Of Ciorstaidh nic Artair, who kept after that year Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg on the south coast, it is told first what was done to her father, for the one cannot be told without the other.

Artair mac Mhurchaidh had kept Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg for above thirty years, since before his daughter could walk the gallery round the lamp-room without a hand on the rail, and in all that time the reef below the light had taken, by his own count kept in the log he left behind him, eleven boats and no lives, the light and his own watching having been, until that year, enough to answer for the second half of the reckoning. He had trained his daughter to the lamp from the age of nine, the wick and the oil and the turning of the great glass, and to the watch-book beside it, and had said to her often enough that a light which stands and a keeper who watches are two different things kept in one tower, and that the tower could be trusted to do the first of these many years after any keeper set to do the second was dead and gone, which she had taken, at nine and at nineteen both, for the kind of thing an old man says to fill a night watch and not for anything she would be asked to prove.

On a night in an t-Ògmhios when the wind stood hard out of the south-west and the light could do no more than show a hull where the light already knew a hull to be, a fishing boat out of Doire shean struck the reef in the black water below the beacon and hung there, taking the sea over her low side with every swell, her crew of four lashed where they could lash themselves and crying out in a wind that carried the sound away from the shore as fast as it was made. Artair heard them from the gallery before he saw them, the cry coming and going with the gusts the way a gull's cry comes and goes, and knew the sound for what it was before the next flash of his own lamp showed him the hull lying over on the reef's own teeth. He woke no one in the house below; the log he kept, found afterward open on the table with the ink still wet where he had broken off writing it, records the hour and the bearing and nothing of what he meant to do about either, as though the doing of it were too plain a thing to trouble a log with.

Artair, fifty-three that spring, went down from the light alone in the small hours with a coil of rope and the shore-boat that the lighthouse kept for no purpose but this one, and got the rope to the wreck, and got two of the four crew into the shore-boat, and put them ashore himself on the strand below the tower with no more than a word to make fast and wait, and went back a second time for the other two, and did not come back a third time himself. The two men he had put ashore said afterward, when the shire's own clerk came to take their account, that he had said nothing to them of the sea he was going back into, and had looked, in the one lamp's-worth of light they had of his face before he pushed off the second time, no more troubled by it than a man setting out to check a fence line he had checked a hundred times before.

Ciorstaidh, three-and-twenty and asleep in the keeper's house below the tower when her father went down, woke to the empty rail and the boat gone and understood, before anyone told her anything, what an empty rail and a gone boat meant at that hour in that wind. Leaving the two men her father had put ashore where they lay, past use to her or to themselves, she took the second boat, the one her father had not thought to take because two boats cannot do together what an old man's arms could no longer do alone in one, and went out after him along the line his own rope still marked in the water, the wind taking the oars out of true with every third stroke and the tower's own lamp swinging its light past her and away again with a regularity that told her nothing of where the wreck lay and everything of how little time the telling would take if she did not find it soon. The wreck she found at last by the sound of it before the sight, the timbers grinding on the rock under the wind the way a door grinds on a hinge too long unoiled, her father's rope still fast to it and the last two of the crew still lashed to what remained of the mast, past speech but not past living, the one holding to the other by a grip that had stopped being a choice some while before she reached them.

Rowing a boat built for a lighter woman than she then knew herself to be against a sea that had already taken the one keeper the reef had never before been able to take, with the two rescued men lying in the bilge doing nothing to help her and everything, by their dead weight alone, to make the pulling harder, she brought them in, and did not once, by her own account given years afterward to a niece who asked her, let herself think past the next stroke of the oar to what waited for her back at the tower once the pulling was done. Her father's own body came ashore three days after, a mile down the coast at a place the tide favours for such things, and was carried back to Tobar dhearg and buried in the ground above the light where every keeper of that beacon before him had been buried, the parish clerk entering against his name in the register no more than the day, the manner, and the word drowned, the same word the register had entered against every other name it had ever taken from that water. The night before the burial she stood the watch herself, the lamp trimmed and turning as her father had trimmed and turned it, there being, as she told the clerk who came to note the death, no law of the sea or of Comhairle nan Taighean-solais either that excused a light from burning because its keeper was being buried in the morning.

The keeper's warrant does not pass by any law the council ever troubled to write down; it passes, where it passes at all, by the plain fact of who is left standing at the light when the last keeper is not, and by whether Comhairle nan Taighean-solais at Cathair dhearg, being asked, can find any reason worth the ink to refuse what has already, in every particular but the paper, been decided by the sea. Ciorstaidh nic Artair asked for nothing beyond what the plain fact already gave her; Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, being asked by the shire's own maor whether the daughter of a keeper who had died bringing in four lives with two boats and one rope might keep the light her father had kept, wrote back inside the month that she might, and the warrant that had been her father's was entered in her own name at Cathair dhearg without a word of debate that any clerk troubled to set down.

## III. Of the Bell in Fog

She did not let the matter rest at the warrant. Through that same summer she had an iron post sunk into the reef at low water, at the place her father's rope had marked and her own boat had found by it, driven and cemented by two masons brought up from Seann Vell who worked the tides for the better part of a month to get it standing, working only in the hour either side of the ebb when the reef stood clear enough of the water to be worked at all, and cursing, by their own later account, every tide that came in on them before the last bolt was set. She had the post painted the red that every chart from Cathair mhòr to Seann Skell now uses for a danger marked and known, and hung from an arm at its head a bell cast at Muileann chiar and paid for, Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's own ledger shows, half from Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's purse and half from a purse the fishing families of Doire shean and Tobar dhearg between them made up themselves, not being minded to wait on Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's own half arriving before the bell was hung, a factor at Doire shean having gone door to door with a slate and a stub of chalk until the sum stood full.

The bell was rung by no hand and no clock; it hung loose enough on its arm that any sea with weight enough to be dangerous set it swinging and sounding of its own accord, and a sea too slight to trouble a boat left it silent, so that the bell told a master exactly what the master needed told and nothing more. When Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's own engineer came down from Cathair dhearg, some months after the post was set, to inspect what Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's half of its own money had bought, he walked the whole length of the reef at low water with his own instruments and pronounced the post sound and the casting good, and asked Ciorstaidh, more out of an engineer's habit of admiring his own trade than out of any doubt of hers, whether she meant in time to have the bell struck by some clockwork, on the pattern Comhairle nan Taighean-solais had lately fitted to Taigh-solais Baile ìseal, so that a master out on the water might know the hour as well as the danger. She answered that a clock could tell a man the hour whether or not the sea agreed with it, and that she had built the bell to tell him only the one thing a clock could never tell him, which was whether the sea that hour meant him harm; if he wanted the hour as well, she said, he had a tide table and two eyes, and needed neither her bell nor Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's clockwork to supply what his own almanac already owed him. The engineer, by his own report to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, wrote down that the keeper's design was sound and her reasoning sounder, and that he had no improvement to suggest that the keeper had not already considered and set aside for good cause.

The bell was heard first, by any account an Leabharlann troubled to gather, on a night in an t-Ògmhios of the fifty-fifth year, when a fog came down off the water thick enough that the light, for all its lamp and its glass and its keeper's own long practice at trimming it, could not be seen from a boat's length beyond where the reef began, and every boat working that stretch of coast that night steered by the bell alone, which the fog could not silence as it silenced the light, and came home. No boat went aground on the reef below Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg that year, nor in any year after that Ciorstaidh nic Artair kept the light, and the fishermen of that coast, who name most things for what they are and few things for what they hope, began that same year to call the reef, which had held no name before beyond a plain warning to keep clear of it, an Sceir Aithnichte, there being, as one of them told a rider of Comhairle nan Taighean-solais who asked him why, no longer anything about it that a man working that water by night needed to be told twice.

## IV. Of the Chair Laid Down

That same year, in Caol mhòr, Mary Coyle laid down the chair of Tional nan Tuathach she had held two-and-twenty years, since the winter Hale died and the widows of the northern crews had first put her name forward over her own protest that a fisherman's widow had no business speaking for clerks and hewers both. She had kept the chair through the beginning of the register of the dead and maimed that bore her own name in the north long after it bore any other, through Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's refusals and Tional nan Tuathach's own lean years when the chair was little more than a bench in a room the south did not trouble to send anyone to argue in, and had said of the work often enough, to the few who asked her why she kept at it, that a chair nobody wants to sit in is the one chair a body dare not leave empty, there being no one else who will trouble to fill it if she does not.

The laying-down came, Tional nan Tuathach's own minute records, on a morning in an t-Ògmhios with no more ceremony than the minute required, and she said what she had to say of the register her own hand had begun and Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn her own petitions had never once moved, a plainer accounting, those who were in the room said afterward, than the years it summed had any right to fit into so few words. Of the widows who had first put her name forward, most of them dead now themselves, and of the register of the western pits' dead and maimed that she had begun and Daniel Frayne had kept in his own hand, the minute records her speaking at some length: a book, she said, that had grown by a name a season for fourteen years and would go on growing by a name a season for as many years again as the vein lasted, whoever sat in the chair to keep it. For herself she asked Tional nan Tuathach nothing, having asked it nothing on her own account in two-and-twenty years of asking it for everything else, and named, before Tional nan Tuathach rose that morning, the man she asked it to choose after her.

Daniel Frayne of Muileann leathan, who had bound books for the north's own schools when the southern guilds would not have a Tuathaich name on their rolls, and who had sat at Mary Coyle's own right hand in Tional nan Tuathach's chair for above ten years before that morning, keeping her own accounts steadier, by more than one account, than she had ever troubled to keep them herself, was chosen that same day without a voice raised against him, the first speaker of Tional nan Tuathach to have been born, as the minute troubled to note, after An Dealachadh had already closed and not before it, a fact that meant little to Tional nan Tuathach's own business that morning. He had been a pupil of Agnes Pike's school before the guilds of the south would take a Tuathaich boy to bind their books, and had learned the trade in the end from his own reading and an old bookbinder of Caol mhòr who asked no questions of a boy's birth so long as his stitching held straight, and had come to Tional nan Tuathach's own clerking, in the years since, by much the same road: asked no leave, and given the work once it was plain he could do it better than the man before him.

He took the chair from a woman who had held it since before he could write his own name, in a room where Tional nan Tuathach's own chest held the north's tallies for every census the kingdom had taken and not published, a chest that Mary Coyle, in the last minute she gave as speaker, pointed to once and said nothing further of, there being nothing further, by her own account, that the pointing did not already say. Frayne said, by Tional nan Tuathach's own record, no more than that he would keep what she had begun and add to it only what Tional nan Tuathach itself asked him to add, which the clerk who wrote the minute set down exactly as he said it and no more warmly than that; and when an older man at the back of the room asked him, half in jest and half not, whether a speaker born after An Dealachadh meant to speak any differently for it than a speaker born before, Frayne answered that he could not say, having no other speaker's whole life to set against his own, and that Tional nan Tuathach would find out, as it found out everything else that mattered to it, by watching what he did and not by asking him first what he meant to do, an answer the minute records without troubling to say whether the room took it for wit or for plain caution, it being, in Frayne's own mouth, not always easy to tell the one from the other.

It was told afterward, at Muileann chaol and at Tobar dhearg both, though never in the same telling, that the bell on the reef and the chair in Caol mhòr were rung and taken in the same season for the plainest of reasons, that a coast which cannot always keep its light burning against the weather it is given had learned, in the end, to hang a bell that needed no one's hand to sound it truly; and that a Moot which could not yet move a line of rail or unlock a chest of figures had learned, in its own time and by its own slower road, to choose a chair that needed no one born before An Dealachadh to sit in it rightly. Neither telling troubled to say which lesson had come first, or whether either had come from the other at all; An Leabharlann, asked once by a student at Caol shean which of the two had taught the other, entered only that both were true and both were entered the same year, and left the rest of the reckoning to whoever wanted it badly enough to work it out alone.
