# Of Walter Hale's Last Winter

*SE 33 am Faoilleach – SE 34 am Màrt*

## I. Of the Grain-Store at Cathair fhada

The Crown's grain-store at Cathair fhada was two winters building and one summer roofing, a hall of dressed stone on the southern plain long enough that a loaded cart could turn inside it without backing into the door, and when the last course went on in the dry season the masons signed their names in the mortar under the sill, in the way of a house that expects to stand. It was filled from the south's own harvest, sack on sack carried up from threshing floors within a day's cart of the town, and the keeper set to it, a quiet man named for no one in the record, walked the rows each morning counting the sacks against a tally he kept in his coat and telling no one the sum.

The store stood full and shut through the good years that followed it. The keeper walked his rows, turned the sacks against the damp, sold off the oldest grain each autumn to make room for the new, and let the south forget the store was there, which it very soon did.

In the same season, though the two had nothing to do with one another beyond the year they shared, the second count of the island's people since the Severance went out from Cathair dhearg: clerks with their books riding the shire roads a second time, tallying hearth by hearth as the first count had. This time a second column stood beside the first, one figure for what a household held and one for what its neighbour held, Dia-thìreach against Tuathach, town against country. The first count had only looked; it had set down numbers and let them lie side by side on the page for anyone with eyes to read the gap between them. The second count was built to measure the gap itself, a column of difference drawn out and summed at the foot of every shire's page and then summed again for the whole island at the bottom of the last one.

The clerks who carried the sums back to Cathair dhearg were young, most of them, riding roads they had never seen before that autumn, and not one of them was ever told the fate of the figures once they were handed over. They were bid write their own shire's page fair, seal it, and hand it across a table to a councillor's clerk who took it without a word of thanks and set it on a stack with the others, and every one of the riders who carried a page that autumn came away with the same plain sense, never spoken aloud between them on the road home, that they had been asked to measure a thing they would not themselves be shown the measure of.

The report went into the council's own keeping and stayed there. No proclamation followed it, no minute of the council's reading it was copied south to the Library, no word of its sum reached any shire clerk who might have carried it further, and the councillors who had called for the count in the first place gave out, when any of the twelve was asked directly, only that the figures wanted study before they wanted airing, a form of words that satisfied the asking without answering it. A report that measures a thing and is then locked away measures it no less truly for the locking, but it does not, for as long as the lock holds, change anything at all, and the council, whatever its reasons, chose that year to hold the lock rather than the change.

## II. Of Walter Hale's Last Sittings

At Caol mhòr, that same dry season, Walter Hale sat the Moot's chair for what none of the men and women around the table yet knew to call his last winter of sitting it, though most of them, looking at him, suspected it without saying so. He was old by any account the north kept of its own, the first speaker the Moot had ever chosen, thirty-one years in the chair since a room of angry fishermen and shepherds had put him there because he could read a factor's ledger as fast as the factor himself and would not be talked past. His hands had begun to shake on a cold morning in a way they had not shaken the year before, a small tremor the room would have needed to be watching for to catch, and he had taken, without saying why, to letting David set the seal to a letter rather than doing it himself.

David Hale had kept his father's minute-books at his elbow since the year of the first fair, when the fair's toll-mistress had turned his wool from the gate to a factor's stall under a rule he had not known was written, and the lesson of that turning had stayed with him longer than the wool's loss ever had. He wrote a fair hand, faster than his father's now, and had taken, these last two winters, to writing the day's business twice, once in the rough book on the table and once again that same night at his own hearth in a second hand steadier than the first, telling himself it was only practice and knowing, without quite admitting it to himself, that he was keeping a second copy against a day he did not want to name.

"You needn't do it twice," Hale told him once, catching him at the second copy by lamplight, his voice thinner that winter than the room was used to hearing it.

"It keeps my hand in," David said, which was true as far as it went and no further, and his father, who had kept a Moot's minutes long enough to know a half-answer when he heard one, let it stand without pressing it, which was its own kind of answer back.

The business that winter was ordinary enough to look at from outside: a dispute over a weir on the Abhainn ìseal that two villages had fought over for longer than either could say why, a petition from Doire ghlas for a second well, the yearly reckoning of the head-due against what the shires had actually paid rather than what they owed. Hale sat through all of it steady enough, his voice carrying the length of the room as it always had even when his hands would not hold a pen without a slight travelling shake in the line, and only those who sat closest to him, David among them, marked how much longer the sittings left him afterward, sunk in his chair with his eyes shut while the room emptied around him, before he would rise and let himself be walked home.

He kept, that whole winter, a habit he had kept for thirty years without once breaking it: the first hour of any sitting given over to whoever had walked furthest to be heard, before the day's set business was opened, on the ground he had given the room the year he first took the chair and never troubled to give again, that a room which heard its own business before its furthest petitioner had already told that petitioner what it thought of the walk. A carter from beyond Àth fhada stood that hour one grey morning with a grievance over a toll he thought wrongly charged him, and stood a long while, the room patient around him while Hale let him finish without once hurrying the telling, and when the carter had done Hale answered him himself rather than passing the matter to a clerk, in a voice that had gone thin that winter but had not gone impatient, and settled the toll in the carter's favour before the day's proper business was called. David, watching, thought his father looked more tired setting down that one small matter than he had looked settling weightier things the winter before, and said nothing of the thought to anyone.

## III. Of the Twenty-Eighth of an Gearran

He died on the twenty-eighth of an Gearran, in his own bed at Caol mhòr with David beside him and Alice sent for from the school though she did not reach the house before the end, seventy-six years old by the count his own house kept, which was neither a young death nor a cruelly long-delayed one, only the ordinary close of an ordinary span, the kind the north had learned by then to expect of its own and no longer thought worth remarking on for its length alone. He had sat the Moot's chair four days before, the weir dispute settled at last in the villages' own favour rather than either party's, and had told David walking home that evening, in the flat way he said most things he meant seriously, that he thought he would let the next weir dispute be somebody else's to settle. David had taken it, at the time, for the kind of thing a tired man says after a long sitting and thought no more of it until the twenty-eighth came.

The Moot's business stopped for three days without a vote taken to stop it; nobody moved to open the room, and nobody needed to be told why not.

The whole north walked to his burial, or near enough to the whole north that the roads into Caol mhòr that week carried more feet than the fair itself had carried the autumn before, fishermen up from Cuan bheag and Ros mhòr, carters off every road into the town, shepherds off the hill country who had never once sat in the Moot's room but had heard his name read out at a dozen musters and knew it as the name a factor's letter answered to when a northern flock was turned away at a gate. They buried him at Caol mhòr within sight of the Moot's own house, the ground he had sat closest to for thirty-one years, and the Church's own rite was said over him by a priest who had not been born when Hale first sat the chair.

The chair itself did not sit empty past the week's mourning. The Moot met on the fourth day, the room fuller than any ordinary sitting, and put the choosing to a vote before the dust of the burial mound had properly settled. Two other names were spoken before hers, a factor's son from the fair road who had money enough to be heard and a shire clerk from Doire ghlas whom half the room knew only by his letters, and both names were set aside within the same hour without much debate, the room's own sense running, though nobody troubled to say it outright, that the chair Hale had built wanted filling by someone the widows and the fishing families would call their own before they would call it a factor's or a clerk's. Mary Coyle, a fisherman's widow of Caol mhòr who had first stood to speak in that same room some nine years before and had gone on standing to speak in it since, was named speaker at the last by a count that nobody in the room troubled to call close.

She had buried a husband to the sea before she ever spoke a word in the Moot's chair, and she took Hale's chair now with the same flat quiet she had used the first time she rose to speak for the widows of Cuan bheag, no speech beyond the plain words the office required of her, no promise offered beyond what the room could see for itself in the way she sat down once the vote was read: as a woman meaning to stay in the chair for exactly as long as the room wanted her in it and not a day longer than that. Fifty-two years old that winter, she took the seal David held out to her and turned it once in her hand before she set it down at her own place, the same seal Hale's own hand had set to thirty-one years of letters, and said only, to the room rather than to David in particular, that she supposed the books had better go on being kept the way they had always been kept, which the room took, rightly, for an asking that David stay on rather than an order that he must.

David kept the books under her as he had kept them under his father, the rough copy on the table and, that first night, though he told himself afterward there was no longer any reason to keep it up, the second copy again at his own hearth, in a hand that shook rather more than his father's ever had, on a night when the keeping of it seemed to want doing whether or not it served any purpose he could have named to another soul in the room.

## IV. Of the Report Asked For and Refused

Seonag nic Ille-Mhoire had ridden the shire roads with the second census the autumn before, one clerk among a dozen sent out from Cathair dhearg, and had carried her own shire's tally back to the council's clerks with her own hand on the sum at the foot of the page. Young for the work, no more than thirty, she had thought, riding home with the figures still fresh in her saddlebag, that a count taken with such care, hearth against hearth, column against column, would be read by somebody who meant to do something with what it showed. Some months on she asked whether she might see the whole of it, the island's sum rather than her own shire's part of it, telling the clerk at the door of the council's keeping-room that she had carried the figures herself and had a right, she thought, to know what they had come to add up to.

The clerk refused her, giving no reason beyond the plain fact of it, that the report was the council's own and shown to no hand that had merely helped gather it, and she did not press him further, having carried enough council business in her time to know a closed door when a clerk's flat face showed her one. That evening she wrote the asking and the refusal down in her own hand, in a private book she kept for herself and showed to no one. She could have pointed to no purpose the keeping served; she wrote it down because a thing asked and refused seemed to her worth a record all the same, in much the way a chronicler at Muileann chaol was that same winter keeping a second private book of her own for reasons she could not have named any more plainly.

## V. Of the Doctors of Àth àrsaidh

At Àth àrsaidh, an easy day's ride from Caol mhòr, Eachann mac Rath had practised physic under his guild's own charter for twenty years, a careful man who set a break as well as any hand on the island and had buried, in his own accounting kept privately and never shown to the guild that licensed him, rather fewer of his patients than the guild's own average allowed for a man of his years in practice. When the Moot wrote to him that winter offering to pay his fee for the teaching of it, two Tuathaich women in his own surgery, learning what he knew by watching him set a bone and mixing a poultice at his elbow rather than by any book, he took the offer without asking the guild's leave, on the plain ground, he told the two women when they first came to him, that a broken arm mended the same whichever hand mended it and he saw no reason a guild seal should decide who was taught to do the mending.

Ruth Ashby and Clara Tulloch came to him that am Faoilleach, two women of the fishing country about Caol mhòr who had nursed their own families and their neighbours' through fevers and birthings for years without any name for the knowledge beyond what their mothers had shown them, and Eachann set them, the first week, to nothing grander than rolling bandages and watching him work, telling them plainly that a physician's hand was built on watching before it was built on doing. They watched through the whole of that dry, hard season, the fevers that came with short water and poor grain filling his surgery faster than any two hands, his or theirs, could easily empty it, and by the spring thaw both women could set a simple break unwatched and had begun, under his eye still, to mix the plainer poultices themselves.

The guild found him out before the summer was old, a factor's wife of Àth àrsaidh mentioning at a fair stall, without malice or particular thought, that Eachann's new girls handled a lancet as steady as he did, and the word reached the guild's own hall inside the month. Two of the guild's own men rode out to see the surgery for themselves before the letter was written, and stood in the doorway watching Ruth Ashby set a child's wrist while Eachann talked her through it without once touching the arm himself, and left again without a word to either woman, which Eachann told them afterward was answer enough of what the letter would say when it came.

The guild struck him from its rolls before an Cèitean was out, the letter naming no fault in his physic, only the plain fact that he had taught outside the guild's own leave and to hands the guild's charter did not recognise as fit to be taught. He read it once at his own table, with Ruth Ashby and Clara Tulloch both there for the evening's lesson, and set it aside rather than answering it, telling the two women, when Clara asked whether the striking changed what he meant to teach them, that a guild seal had never yet set a bone and he saw no reason to let the loss of one stop him doing the one thing the seal had never actually done for him. His patients were no fewer for the guild's letter than they had been before it, and Ruth Ashby and Clara Tulloch kept coming to his surgery through that summer and the next, learning what he had left to teach them before any guild seal stood between the teaching and the two women who wanted it.

## VI. Of the Flood on the Abhainn ìseal

The Abhainn ìseal broke its banks along its lower reach in am Màrt, the longest river of the island carrying, in three days of rain harder than anyone had thought to plan against, three bridges clean away and a long stretch of the coal line's embankment with them, the rails left hanging over open water where the ground beneath them had simply gone. A carter caught on the middle bridge when the third day's rain brought the water up fastest got his horse free and lost the cart, and stood on the bank afterward watching the whole span go under inside the space of a single held breath, the timbers turning once in the current before the flood took them out of sight round the bend below the mill. No one drowned on that stretch of river that week, though two of the villages downstream lost more of their low ground than any flood in the shire's own memory had taken before, and the line's engineers, walking the embankment once the water dropped enough to let them, found nothing left to walk on for the better part of a mile.

The line shut for want of ground to run on, and the ports that had lived on the coal it carried, Ros dhomhain and the western harbours among them, turned to whatever they had laid by in their own stores against a leaner week, none of them having laid by against a season. Word of the shutting reached the yards before it reached any office that might have planned for it, and for two days the ordinary traffic of coal carts simply stopped at the washed ground and turned back the way they had come, their drivers no wiser than the townsfolk waiting at the far end for a delivery that was not coming.

Mary Coyle had sat the Moot's chair not yet a month when the first rider reached Caol mhòr with word of the washed embankment, and she called the room together the same evening rather than waiting on the week's ordinary sitting, on the plain ground, she told David as he opened the rough book to a fresh page, that a bridge does not wait on a schedule and neither, while she held the chair, would the room that had to answer for one. She gave her first order from that chair before the ink of the vote choosing her was properly dry on the older page behind it: every northern harbour that answered to the Moot to open its own coal store and share it out by the count of hearths rather than by whoever reached the yard first, since the north's coal came down that same line and would not come again until the line itself could be walked and mended.

David wrote it into the rough book in his own hand, the first order the book carried under her name rather than his father's, and did not, that night, trouble to copy it again by his own hearth, finding, when he sat down to his lamp as he had sat down most nights of the two years before, that he no longer felt the want of the second copy he had kept so long against a day whose need for it, he saw now, had already come and gone.

Mary sat on in the room after the vote was carried and the riders sent, David gathering the loose pages of the night's business into the rough book while she waited for the last of the council to file out. She had not, she told him once the room had emptied, expected to give her first order over a broken bridge rather than over any of the ordinary business she had sat through for nine years watching another hand settle; she had thought, if she had let herself think about the chair at all before the room put her in it, that her first order would be some small thing, a licence signed or a petition answered, not a whole coast's coal rationed out on the strength of one rider's word before the damage had even been properly walked. "It is what came first," David said, closing the book, "and a chair answers what comes first or it answers nothing." She thought about that longer than the plainness of it seemed to warrant, and said, rising at last to let him bank the fire and lock the room behind them both, that she supposed a chair had never once in Hale's thirty-one years been given the luxury of choosing which order came first, and she saw no reason her own first winter in it should be the one to break that rule.
