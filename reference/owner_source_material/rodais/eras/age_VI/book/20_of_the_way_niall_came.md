# Of the Way Niall Came

*SE 22 an Lùnastal – SE 23 an Lùnastal*

> *Mar a thàinig Niall.*
> The way Niall came.
>
> — entered once, and once only, in the hand of Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, chronicler

## I. Of the Bells at Cathair dhearg

Word of it came out along the east road a day and a night after it happened, riding in the mouth of a maor's courier who had changed horses twice and slept once, on the floor of a tavern, in his boots. By the time it reached Muileann chaol the bells at {{place:burg:19}} had already been ringing since dawn, and would ring, in relays, until the following dusk.

That much an Leabharlann set down without difficulty: that Niall died and Brìghde was crowned on {{date:V-0092}}; that the bells of Òrd Mhacha were rung a full day and a night for his death; that the streets about the old counting-house filled with a crowd larger than any the capital had seen since his own crowning; that the crowd was quiet, and stayed quiet, and went home before the second night; that within the week his daughter stood where he had stood, and the same rite that had made him king was said over her by the same order, and no voice in the crowd or the council was raised against it. Those were the facts, and an Leabharlann has never had more of them to give. What was said in the death-chamber, if anything was said, and by whom, went with the people who heard it, and none of them left an account that reached Muileann chaol. Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd does not guess at what it was never told.

For that first day the capital kept no market. No order shut the stalls round the old counting-house; nobody could be found willing to be the trader who cried his wares under a tolling bell, and by noon the empty stalls had become, without anyone declaring it, the custom of the day. Ships in the harbour struck their colours to half the mast, a habit some said the humans had left behind them and others said the island had always had, and the argument over which was true outlasted the mourning by a fair margin. Children were kept from the streets near the tower, since the noise of the bells at close range could be heard for days after in a small skull, and every mother in Cathair dhearg who had one seemed to know it.

Comhairle na Rìoghachd sat within the week, as its minute-book shows it always sat, on its fixed day, and the first business entered that day is a dispute over a weir on the Abhainn ìseal that had run three sessions already and would run two more before it was settled. Between that entry and the one before it there is no blank page, no space left for the weight of what had happened, only the ordinary hand of the clerk who kept the book that year, going on to the next line as he had been taught to. A visitor turning the pages now might read past the crowning and never know a kingdom had changed hands in the gap, unless he thought to look for the single line, two entries down, noting that the council's oath of service had been sworn afresh before the new sovereign, in the same room, on the same afternoon as the weir.

The bells themselves were an old debt paid. They had hung in the tower of Òrd Mhacha since before An Dealachadh, cast by hands nobody now living could name, and they had rung four times in seventy years: once for Niall's own crowning, twice for storms taken as omens that came to nothing, and now this. A boy set to work the smaller of the two, relieved every two hours by another boy, kept a tally chalked on the tower wall of how many times his arms had pulled the rope, and rubbed it out when his watch ended, so that no count of it survives, and an Leabharlann has none to give.

At Brìghde's crowning the market crosses of the south carried the proclamation within three days, in the same words that had carried Niall's twenty years before, with only the name changed and the year. A clerk at Ros dhomhain, copying it out for the harbour gate, wrote Niall's name by habit before he caught himself, scored it through with two strokes, and wrote Brìghde's above the line. The corrected copy hangs at Ros dhomhain yet, under glass, kept there mostly because nobody afterward could be troubled to have it taken down.

Of the new queen herself the record gives, for this year, only what any record gives of any crowning: the rite kept, the regalia carried out of Òrd Mhacha's keeping and back into it, the crowd that watched and dispersed. No word of hers survives from that week, and none was sought. The state she inherited went on being governed by the same twelve families and the same maoir the day after as the day before, gathering the same head-due by the same reckoning, and if anything in the machinery of the kingdom so much as paused to notice that the hand on the lever had changed, no minute of any council records it.

## II. Of the Word Gormshuil Let In

At Muileann chaol the chronicler kept a small room above an Leabharlann's reading-hall, with one window that looked down into the yard where the copyists sunned their vellum, and a table too narrow for the papers she put on it. She had held the office four years by then, and had written, in that time, several hundred entries in a hand her own clerks had learned to copy well enough that visitors could not always tell which lines were hers.

The office kept to a small routine that had grown up around her without much planning: two clerks besides Tormod, a copyist shared with the reading-hall below, and a courier's box nailed to the outer door for anything that came while she was at an Leabharlann proper, sorting the boxed rolls that still arrived, a decade on, from shires that had only just got round to clearing an old counting-house or a dead magistrate's chest. Most days the work was of that kind, patient and small: a grave-list from Doire shean copied fair, a parish register cross-checked against the humans' own numbering, a maor's return of the year's head-due entered against the last five years' and the difference noted without comment. The crowning, when word of it reached her desk between a grave-list and a return, looked like nothing larger than the rest of that morning's post, and she had learned, in four years of the office, to give it no more room on the page than its facts required and no less.

She wrote the crowning three times before she let any version of it stand.

The first draft said that the throne passed from father to daughter without incident, which was true and said nothing. The second said that no one contested the succession, though many in the country's temples counted years by lives that did not shorten as other lives did, and this she struck through before the ink had properly taken, because it was also true and said a good deal too much, and she was not the chronicler who would be the one to say it first. The third draft she left half a page long and unfinished, because in trying to explain why the thing seemed both ordinary and strange at once she had begun, without meaning to, to write an argument, and a chronicle is not an argument.

She sat with the three failed pages spread on the narrow table and the courier's letter weighted under an ink-stone, and thought, not for the first time, that words for what had happened came easily enough. What was hard was choosing, among the true things that could be said, which one the kingdom's grandchildren would be permitted to know had been thought at all.

It was her clerk, Tormod mac Sheumais, four years now at her side, who gave her the way out, without knowing he had done it. He came up with a fair copy of an unrelated entry for her to check, glanced at the crossed-out drafts on the table without meaning to read them, and said, by way of nothing, that the taverns of Cathair dhearg, where he had grown up, had a phrase for anything that went off without a quarrel: *the way Niall came.* He had heard it against a debt his father paid without an argument, and again against a marriage nobody in either family had opposed, and thought it a tavern saying, no more, the kind that gets into every account book in a town within a generation of one clean transaction and means nothing very much by the time it does.

Gormshuil sat with that a long moment.

It sat oddly with Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd's language, a line out of a debtor's ledger dressed as history. Turning it over, she saw it was also the only true thing in any of the four drafts on her table: a plain report of how smoothly the crowning had gone, in the very words the kingdom had already coined to say so. She had thought, for a long moment, that she would be inventing a judgment. She was, she decided, only recording one that had already been made, in taverns and market squares, by people with no office and no ink-stone, long before she sat down to write her own version of it.

She wrote it in, four words, and did not strike it out.

*The succession came mar a thàinig Niall*, she wrote, *and the record notes no voice against it.*

She looked at the sentence for some time afterward, the pen dry in her hand, weighing whether a phrase let in once could be kept from becoming a habit. Other offices had lost their plainness that way: an ornament allowed for one entry because it fit so well, then reached for again the next time something needed saying gently, until the whole record read like a poem trying to be a ledger. Sitting there, she resolved to let herself the one line and no other, for as long as the office was hers, and she kept that resolve to the end of her years at the desk, though nothing outside her own conscience would ever have held her to it. It is, in the whole span of her chronicle, the only idiom she ever entered.

Tormod himself never knew, until she told him years later over a different matter entirely, that the phrase he had offered in passing, meaning nothing by it, had gone into the kingdom's own record of its own history. When she did tell him he asked, reasonably, why she had not simply said so at the time, and she answered that a chronicler thanking her clerk for a turn of phrase in front of him was a chronicler inviting a second turn of phrase the following week, and a third after that, and she had seen offices ruined by less. He took the answer as the kind of thing she said, and went back to his copying, and the matter was not raised between them again.

Her mother, when she heard of it on a visit to her mother's village by Ceann àrsaidh that autumn, laughed longer than Gormshuil thought the thing deserved, and said that her daughter had spent four years learning to write like nobody in particular so that she could, in the end, write like everybody. Gormshuil said this was not quite fair, that she had chosen the one phrase precisely because it belonged to no one, having passed through every debtor's ledger and wedding contract in the south until nobody living could say who had coined it. Her mother said that was a fine distinction to draw over four words, and asked whether the same care went into every entry or only the ones that made a queen of somebody's daughter. Gormshuil said the care was the same throughout, and that this was rather the point of keeping a chronicle at all. Her mother said it was fair enough, and poured the tea, and the two of them sat some while in the kind of silence that did not need filling, of the sort that only happens between a mother and a daughter who have already said most of what needs saying, over many years, and found no cause to disagree twice about the same thing.

## III. Of the Hall at Seann Dunn

East along the coast, past the last of the shires An Crùn troubled to garrison closely, the houses of Na Seann Spioradan had never had a hall of their own, only the hearths of whichever family was keeping the year's rite, and a long custom of walking to a neighbour's fire when their own house had none living old enough to lead the naming. In the autumn after the crowning, forty households of that coast put up timber and stone at {{place:burg:18}} for a hall that would hold all of them at once, and roofed it before the first frost, working by turns as a barn-raising is worked, with the women cooking for the builders and the builders arguing over the pitch of the roof.

Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd required nothing of her here; Gormshuil went to see it finished because word of an ancestors' hall reached a chronicler's desk the way any large gathering of the faithful did, and because her mother's own house, three valleys inland, kept the same rite at a hearth too small for half its dead. She went on a hired horse with one clerk, and arrived the evening before the hall's first naming, and slept badly in a fisherman's loft that smelled of nets.

The hall was plain enough to look at: four walls of undressed stone, a roof of new timber that still smelled of the axe, a hearth at either end so that no one house's fire should be taken for another's, and no image or carving anywhere in it, since Na Seann Spioradan hold that a name spoken aloud is ornament enough and needs no likeness to stand beside it. Forty families had raised it in a season, working the stone by day and, Gormshuil was told, arguing by candlelight over whose hearth should stand nearer the door, an argument that had been settled, in the end, by the eldest woman of the coast simply sitting down at the hearth she preferred and daring anyone to move her.

The naming she watched from the door, where a chronicler belonged and no further. Forty heads of houses stood in turn and spoke the names of their dead, going back as far as memory or a grandmother's telling would carry them, some no more than three generations, one old woman of Seann Dunn reciting nine before her voice gave out and her son finished the tenth for her. A fisherman from the far end of the coast, a young man by his voice, named only his father and stood a long moment after saying it before sitting down again, and nobody in the hall thought this needed any comment, since not every house's dead came in long lists and a hall built to hold long lists had to hold the short ones too, or it was built for nothing. Nobody wrote any of it down that night. It was spoken, the way it had always been spoken, into a room full of people who already half knew it, and the only new thing in the room was the room.

Gormshuil, listening from the door with her clerk beside her, found herself counting, without quite meaning to, how many names her own mother's hearth could put up against the room's forty, and gave up the count somewhere past a dozen, less from any want of dead to name than because counting, standing where she stood, felt like exactly the kind of ledger-keeping she had come east to get away from for one evening.

An elder of the hall, a boat-builder called Fearchar Ruadh whose house Gormshuil did not know, found her afterward at the door and asked her plainly whether an Leabharlann kept such lists as his houses were now keeping. She told him, as plainly, that it did not, not yet, that an Leabharlann kept what came to it and asked for little else, and that if his hall chose to send a copy of its naming south each year, it would be received and kept as carefully as anything else that reached Muileann chaol.

"And if we do not send it," he said, "will an Leabharlann go looking for it?"

"No," she said. "It never has."

He seemed to find this the right answer, and said the hall would think on it over the winter. The following year, as it happened, they did send a copy, and the hall went on sending one every year after, each a little longer than the last, name by name. Gormshuil entered its arrival in her own hand, and noted, as she noted with every such gift, only the day it came and the number of names it carried, nothing of what she had seen at the door of the hall. That she kept, if she kept it at all, in the private book she had begun some years before and showed to no one.

## IV. Of the Light on the Mole

Far up the eastern coast, past even Seann Dunn, the town of {{place:burg:119}} had none of the five lighthouses the humans had left the kingdom to keep. It had a mole, built in the humans' time for a fishing fleet larger than the one that used it now, and at the end of the mole, that same autumn, the town council voted its own coal money to burn a light where no board light had ever reached.

They built it plainly: an iron cage on a stone stand, a keeper's shed no bigger than a byre, and a boy paid a few coins a week to carry coal out along the mole in all weathers and keep the cage fed through the dark months, when the boats came home late and the rocks off the harbour mouth took a hull or two most winters that a light might have spared. The town called it nothing grander than the light, and burned it faithfully from the first frost.

The winter before the vote, a boat out of the town had come in through a fog so thick a man could not see the length of his own oar, and had found the harbour mouth only because her skipper had fished those rocks since he was a boy and could smell the change in the water before he could see the change in the light. Two boats that same winter had not been so fortunate, and lie yet, or lay then, broken on the same rocks the fog had hidden. It was that skipper, by the town's own telling, who stood up at the meeting that voted the coal money, and said that a harbour that could build a mole could build a light to stand on the end of it, and that An Crùn's five lights had never once been built for a town too small to matter to anyone who did not live in it.

They wrote, too, in the same season, to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais at Cathair dhearg, asking that the light be taken onto Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's roll and its coal paid from Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's purse as the five older lights were paid. The letter that came back, some months later, on {{date:V-0095}}, said that Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's charter named five lights and no more, that a sixth had never been provided for, and that the town's own arrangement, being no charge upon An Crùn, might continue exactly as it was.

It did continue exactly as it was, for the rest of the age. The boy who first carried the coal out along the mole grew old doing it, and trained his own boy to the work, and neither of them was ever paid by any hand but the town's own. Gormshuil, reading Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's letter when a copy of it found its way south with the year's other papers, entered the refusal in four lines and no more, in much the manner Cathair dhearg's own council had once refused a wall it thought needless. She did not add that the fishermen of a town with no lighthouse of its own devising had, for as long as anyone there remembered, gone out and come home by no light but the moon and their own knowledge of the rocks.

## V. Of the Còmhrag

At {{place:burg:22}}, two summers after Fèill Muileann òg had proved that the whole island would travel for a gathering if the gathering gave it reason, a smaller and rougher custom took its own first root. Men of the hewing towns had long settled disputes and simple boasts with their fists at the mine-head, on pay-days, under no rule but that a man who went down did not get up swinging; someone, nobody an Leabharlann can now name with certainty, thought to bring the custom to a field outside the town and dress it as a contest, with judges and a purse, so that the fighting that had always happened anyway might happen once a year, in daylight, where the fair-wardens could take a fee from every man who came to watch.

The first was fought on {{date:V-0096}}, and they called it simply Còmhrag Ceann leathan; it drew men from every vein-town within two days' walk: Muileann dhearg sent the largest company, since its hewers had the most to prove and the least else to spend a wage on, and its lodging-houses emptied for the day into the crowd around the ropes. The rules, such as they were that first year, ran to little more than this: any man might enter who paid the fair-wardens' fee, no man might carry an edge, and the last man left inside the ropes took the purse. Nobody thought to write down more than that, because nobody expected the thing to need more than that, and for one summer they were right.

The field lay on rising ground outside the town where the fair-wardens had once grazed the fair's own horses, and by midday of Còmhrag Ceann leathan it held more people standing than Ceann leathan held in its beds. The ropes were strung in a rough square, four posts and a coil borrowed from the harbour store, and inside them the crowd that had paid its fee to fight stood together at one corner while the crowd that had paid its fee to watch pressed close on every other side, close enough that a man knocked hard against the rope felt the whole weight of the watchers lean into it with him. There was no bell to open the bout and no bell to close it; a fair-warden with a horn from a captain's old muster-call blew it once at noon, and the fighting did not properly stop again until the light was going and one man alone still stood inside the ropes.

Two men did not walk out of the ropes that first year. An Leabharlann's own words for it, entered by Gormshuil from the fair-wardens' own report and nothing more, run to a single sentence: the dead of the first Còmhrag were buried at Ceann leathan within the week, and Còmhrag Ceann leathan was fought again the following summer regardless. She was asked once, years afterward, by a visiting scholar, whether Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd ought not to have said more of who they were. She answered that the report she had been sent gave her nothing more to say, and that she had never yet found it her office to invent what a report withheld.

The man who won that first Còmhrag was a hewer out of Muileann chrom named Calum mac Aonghais, whom the crowd was already calling Calum Ruadh before the last bout was decided, for the colour of his hair and the state of his face by the end of it. He was young for the work he did underground, younger still for the crowd that carried him on its shoulders the length of the field when the judges called it, and he took the purse, a year's wages counted out on a trestle table by the fair-wardens' own clerk, and the second prize besides, which was the right, entered that same evening on the shire's roll at Ceann leathan, to bear arms in any shire of the kingdom for the rest of his life.

He had not looked, by the accounts that reached Muileann chaol afterward, like a man built for winning. A quarryman out of Baile thais, twice his age and half again his weight, had put him down twice in the first hour and stood over him laughing the second time, and it was said in the town afterward that Calum got up the second time only because the crowd nearest him had begun, without much kindness, to place their bets against him staying down, and a man will get up for less reason than that when there is a whole field watching. He did not win by any skill anybody could later describe in much detail; he won, the town agreed, by refusing for six hours to stay on the ground longer than it took him to breathe, until every man larger than him had either gone down himself or grown too tired to make use of being larger.

It was a strange gift to give a hewer, and everyone at the field that evening seemed to feel the strangeness of it a little, though nobody said so aloud. A man who spent his days with a pick and his nights in a lodging-house by the sidings had, by the ropes and the purse and a shire clerk's signature, become in the eyes of the law something his father had never been and his sons might never be again: a man the kingdom would let go armed. The inn-keepers of Ceann leathan, four of whom had built new houses that year purely to hold the crowd Còmhrag Ceann leathan brought, kept his table free of charge that night, and would go on doing so, by a custom nobody formally agreed to, for as long as he came back to fight.

He did not fight again that season. He walked out of the town before dawn the next morning, the purse in his coat and the shire clerk's paper folded inside it, and took Rathad na Mèinne north toward Muileann chrom on foot, alone, past the fair-wardens' new inns with their shutters still up and the field where the ropes had been taken down in the night, leaving nothing behind him but two flattened circles of grass and a debt Còmhrag Ceann leathan's keepers would spend the winter arguing over how best to collect from next year's crowd. Besides the purse he carried the right to go armed through any shire on the island, a right his own father, a hewer before him at the same face, had never held. For that one autumn it was simply his, earned with his own hands in a field outside a mining town, and he walked home with it the way a man walks home from work that has gone, on the whole, well: tired, a little proud, and not looking back.
