# Of Agnes Pike's School

*SE 15 am Faoilleach – an t-Iuchar*

> Entered this day, a byre at Doire ghlas, no other roof offering: A. Pike, mistress. Eleven scholars. No leave asked, none given, none refused.
>
> — the school's own register, first leaf, {{date:V-0066}}

## I. Of the Weavers and the Byre

That same winter, far to the south, the weavers of Baile chiar took a charter for their guild, and the council's clerks cut a new seal for them, a shuttle over a fleece, and entered the grant in a book bound in calf and kept under lock at Cathair dhearg. It cost the weavers a fee, a feast for the councillors who signed it, and three months of waiting while the seal-cutter finished a commission for the mint first. When it was done they had the right to call themselves a guild, to set their own prices at the fair, and to bar any weaver of Baile chiar who had not served the full seven years under a master of the craft. It is the kind of grant this book has recorded a dozen times already and will record a dozen times more: coin changes hands, a form is followed, a seal is cut, and a thing that was already being done becomes a thing the kingdom has agreed to notice.

At Doire ghlas, in the north, in the same season, a schoolmistress asked leave of no one at all.

Agnes Pike was four-and-thirty that winter, a miller's daughter of a family that had ground the north's grain at Doire mhin since before An Dealachadh, though she herself had left the mill for a scribe's stool in the harbour agent's office at Ros bheag as a girl, and had spent a dozen years since copying tallies of herring and salt by a window that let in more weather than light. She read well, in both tongues, having been taught her letters by a Mission woman old enough to remember the last ship, and she wrote a hand clean enough that the harbour agent had once offered to send her south to a copyist's house at Cathair dhearg, an offer she turned down without troubling to explain why. What she wanted, she told her sister that same year, over a supper of little more than oatmeal and the last of the autumn's apples, was a room of her own, with children in it who did not yet know how much the world meant to keep from them; a stool in a southern office held no such want for her.

She had been three years asking round Doire ghlas for such a room before she found one that would serve. The town had no hall to spare and no coin of its own to raise one, Tional nan Tuathach's letters on the matter having gone south and come back, as they generally did, with nothing on them but the mark of a clerk who had read them. Doire ghlas was by then the largest of the Tuathaich towns of that coast, a huddle of stone-and-turf roofs in a fold of the moor, its one street running from the mill-bridge to An Eaglais and stopping there, as though the town had never quite decided what a street beyond a church was for. It had grown a good deal since A' Bhliadhna Acrach, when Walter Hale had walked meal in from Caol mhòr and helped bury the children the Dia-thìrich had never troubled to count; the names of that year's dead were still kept, pinned in the church porch, and more than one of the children who came to Agnes's byre that winter had an elder brother or sister among them, a fact none of the households spoke of and all of them knew.

In am Faoilleach a widow named Jenny Marrow, whose husband had kept cattle on the moor above the town until a wet spring took half his herd and the fever took the rest of him, offered her byre for the winter months, the beasts she had left being few enough now to stand a season crowded into the barn behind it. Jenny asked nothing for the loan beyond a promise that the children would be taught to write their names, hers among them, she being sixty that year and unable, she said plainly, to see any reason a woman ought to go to her grave signing a mark. The byre smelled of what a byre smells of, and the roof let in a persistent drip over the third row of benches that no amount of patching ever quite stopped, and Agnes took it gladly, for it had four walls, a floor that could be swept, and a door that shut.

## II. Of the Fourteenth of am Faoilleach

The purse that paid for the benches, the slates, and the widow's rent came from Caol mhòr, carried the length of the coast road by Walter Hale himself, who by then had been speaker of Tional nan Tuathach for thirteen years and had grown, in that time, into the habit of carrying its business in his own saddlebag rather than trusting it to a rider who might not know which doors in a strange town were worth knocking on. He was eight-and-fifty that winter, greyer at the temple than the year before, and he made the ride in from Caol mhòr in weather his wife Ellen told him plainly he was too old to be riding in, an opinion he did not dispute and rode out in anyway. He found Agnes Pike sweeping straw from the byre floor the evening before it was to open, and set the purse down on the one bench already fixed to the wall, and told her, as near as Tional nan Tuathach's own minute records the exchange, that the sum was hers to spend as she saw fit and that no one at Caol mhòr expected an account of every penny, Tional nan Tuathach having decided, after some argument, that a schoolmistress who had to write down the cost of every slate was a schoolmistress with less time to teach.

"And the council?" Agnes asked him, not looking up from the broom.

"Received and filed," Hale said. It was, by then, a saying among them, the answer the council gave to nearly everything the north sent it, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has stopped being angry at and started merely repeating. "I did not ask again after the first no. A council that says no once will say no as often as you trouble it. This one," he nodded at the byre, "asked no one a second time."

She straightened then and looked at him, the broom still in her hand. "It is a byre with children in it, Walter, whatever else you call it."

"So was the first one at Caol mhòr," he said, "the winter after we came north, when the priest lent Ellen the church porch two hours a week to teach David and Alice their letters, before there was any Moot to call it a school or refuse to. It became one because enough children kept coming back to it that nobody could call it anything else." He looked round the byre, at the swept floor and the benches and the drip already starting again over the third row now that the evening's rain had begun. "Call it what you like. Open the door tomorrow and see who comes."

The school opened on 14 am Faoilleach, in the grey light before the moor mist had properly lifted, and eleven children came through the byre door before the bell of An Eaglais, which Doire ghlas had lately taken to ringing for the hour rather than for the office alone, had finished its seventh stroke. Agnes had for teaching them two books and nothing else: Am Bìoball, in the humans' own tongue, which every household in the north kept a copy of whether or not any hand in the house could read it, and a bound volume of Tional nan Tuathach's own minutes, going back to the wet afternoon at Caol mhòr when the first of them was written, which Hale had lent her for the winter on the understanding that it came back to Caol mhòr in the spring in the same condition it left. There was no primer, no slate-book of letters set out in rows, because none had ever been printed for the humans' tongue on the island and none was coming; so the children of Doire ghlas learned their letters that winter from the twenty-third psalm and from old minutes recording a granary vote and a dispute over a shared boat, in roughly equal measure, and Agnes could never afterward say which of the two taught them faster.

The eleven who came that first morning ranged from a girl of five, brought by an elder cousin because there was nowhere else to leave her, to a boy of thirteen already promised to a carter and come only because the carter was laid up that week with a lame horse. Agnes set them by no order she had planned in advance, having never kept school before and having only her own dozen years at a copyist's desk to draw on for a method, and settled by the second week on putting the youngest nearest the one window, where the light lasted longest, and the eldest nearest the door, on the reasoning that a boy who might be called out to help with a cart at any hour should not have to climb over four benches to answer the call. Jenny herself came most mornings and sat at the very back with her own slate, forming her name letter by letter with a slowness the younger children found endlessly diverting and never once mocked her for, and by an Cèitean she had it whole, and stood it against the byre's one dry wall where the drip could not reach it, next to the psalms.

Hale did not stay to see the door open. He rode south again the same evening he delivered the purse, having, he told Agnes, a sitting of Tional nan Tuathach to be at two days later, and Tional nan Tuathach's own business, as ever, waiting on his saddlebag. But he left her, folded inside the cover of the minute-book, a single sheet in his own hand, headed only *For whoever asks*, setting out in four plain lines that the purse was Tional nan Tuathach's, the room was the widow's, and the leave, such as it was, was nobody's at all; and Agnes kept that sheet, long after the minute-book had gone back to Caol mhòr, pinned inside the register she began that same week, where it stayed for as long as the school did.

## III. Of Daniel Frayne

Among the eleven who came through the door that first morning was a boy of ten named Daniel Frayne, walked in from Muileann leathan, a weaving town close enough across the moor that a child could make the distance on his own feet before the bell finished, provided he left before it began. His father worked a loom in a shed behind the house and had never in his life wanted more of letters than would let him read a bill of sale, and told his son plainly, the first evening Daniel came home smelling of the byre, that he saw no harm in it so long as the boy was back in time to help carry the cloth to the fuller's, a condition Daniel kept without fail for as long as the school ran, walking the moor road at first light with a slate under one arm and, on the wetter mornings, his mother's shawl over his head against a rain that came over the moor sideways more often than down.

He was not the quickest child on the benches that winter, nor the slowest; the psalm gave him trouble longer than it gave most, the reading of it, that is, for the words themselves swam a little on the page and would not always sit still for him in the order Agnes read them aloud. But he could copy. Set in front of him a line already written, in any hand, and he would render it back stroke for stroke with a care that made Agnes, watching over his shoulder one grey morning in an Gearran, stop what she was doing and look a second time, for the boy had taken a page of Cuimhne-coinneimh an Tionail, a dry entry about a granary's repair, and copied it out exactly, the loop of every g, the cross of every t, as though his hand understood the shape of a word before his eyes had finished understanding its sense.

"You read that slower than you wrote it," she told him, not unkindly.

"I know what it's meant to look like," Daniel said, which was as near as he ever came, that winter or for some years after, to explaining the difference between the two things to anyone, including himself. Agnes set him, from that week, to copying out a second time whatever the class had read that day, a task the other children took as a punishment when it fell to them and Daniel took, visibly, as something closer to its opposite; by an Cèitean his fair copies of the day's psalm or the day's minute were pinned along the byre's one dry wall, less because they were needed there than because Agnes had run out of other ways to keep the drip off them.

The other children on his bench teased him for it, in the way children tease a boy who is glad to be given more of a chore than he was set, though not unkindly, and one of them, a carter's daughter named Effie who read faster than anyone in the byre and could not, by her own loud complaint, make a letter sit straight to save her life, took to trading him her reading of a hard line for his copying of an easy one, an arrangement Agnes noticed within the week and chose, after some thought, not to forbid, on the reasoning that a school with eleven children and one teacher would need more than one kind of teaching in it before the winter was out. Daniel's mother, when she heard of the arrangement from her son at supper, said only that it sounded a fair trade and asked whether the girl's family had meal to spare that week, which was, in a poor town in a lean season, very often the shape a compliment took.

He was not quick to speak of the school at all, in fact, being a boy who kept most things folded close, and it was his father, coming in from the loom-shed one evening with the lint still white on his sleeves, who first noticed that his son had begun, without being asked, to copy out the week's weaving into a little book of his own, tallying ells of grey against ells of brown in columns as neat as anything the byre wall carried, a habit that outlasted the schooling and that his father, who had never asked for it, came in time to rely on more than he ever quite admitted.

## IV. Of the Maor Who Wrote Nothing

The maor of the shire, whose office required him to note anything new raised, built, or opened within it and send word south once a season, rode out to Doire ghlas in an t-Ògmhios, six months after the byre door first opened, having heard of the school by then from at least three different householders who mentioned it to him as though he must already know, the way a thing spoken of openly in a small town is assumed, wrongly, to have reached every ear that governs it. He was a heavy man, not unkind, who had held the shire eleven years and had learned in that time which of the things brought to his notice were worth troubling the council with and which were better left where they lay; and he stood in the byre door for the better part of an hour on a warm morning, watching eleven children, twelve by then, recite the fourth psalm in a ragged chorus while a woman he had never met before that day kept time with a stick against the door-frame, and said, when Agnes at last came to speak with him, almost nothing at all.

Her name he asked, which he already knew, and how the room was heated in winter, and whether the children paid anything for their place on the bench, setting both answers down in a small book he carried for the purpose, in a hand Agnes could not read upside down and did not try very hard to. Of the council's leave he asked nothing, and she offered nothing of its want, each of them understanding, without either saying so, that the question and its answer would only make the report he was about to write a harder one to write briefly.

He had, in his eleven years in the office, sent south a great many reports of things newly built: a mill wheel, a patched bridge, a chapel roof reslated, each one entered under a heading the council's own forms already provided for it, so that a maor filling in his season's return needed only choose the right box and set down a number. There was no box on any form he had ever been given for a room where Tuathaich children were taught to read the humans' tongue in the hand of a woman the council had never licensed to teach anything at all, and he had turned the problem over more than once on the ride out from his own house, without arriving at anything he liked better than the plain fact that a report is a thing a man is asked for, and a silence is a thing nobody has yet thought to ask after. He was not a cruel man, and had spent eleven years learning precisely which small mercies a shire's maor could extend to a town without any of them ever reaching a page the council would read; this, he judged, watching the children file back to their benches as he turned his horse for the coast road, was one more.

He thanked her for her time, in the flat courteous way a man thanks a stranger for an hour he had not particularly wanted to spend, and rode south again before the noon meal, and the report he sent that season to Cathair dhearg, which the council's own chests still hold, records the shire's harvest, its head-due, the state of its one bridge, and nothing whatever about a byre at Doire ghlas with a schoolmistress in it and children reciting psalms from a Moot's minute-book.

It was not, Agnes came to understand only much later, an oversight. A maor who wrote nothing of a thing he had seen with his own eyes had not forgotten it; he had decided, in the particular silence of a man who knows exactly how far his own office reaches and no further, that a school begun without leave was safest left where a council that had already said no once could not be provoked into saying no again, more loudly, to something it would then be obliged to close. She never learned whether that was kindness, caution, or simply a want of anything in the maor's instructions that told him what box on his form a byre full of Tuathaich children ought to be entered under; and in the years that followed, as eight more such schools opened along the coast without one of them being licensed and not one of them being shut, she came to think it mattered rather less than the plain fact that the report went south blank on the one matter that would have mattered to her, and stayed blank, season after season, for as long as any maor of that shire held the office.

## V. Of the Second School and the Minute Copied Fair

Word of the byre reached Caol mhòr before the spring was out, carried, as most things reached Caol mhòr, in the mouth of a carter, and Alice Hale, Walter and Ellen's daughter, nineteen that year and long since past the age when her father's household porch-lessons had served her, opened a second school within the month in a disused net-store behind the harbour, with six benches begged from the boat-builders and a purse a good deal smaller than the one her father had carried to Doire ghlas, Tional nan Tuathach's own funds having been thinner that season on account of a poor herring year. She wrote to Agnes asking what had answered best on the byre's cold mornings and what had not, and the two women kept up, through that year and after, a correspondence of practical questions (how many slates a class of a dozen truly needed, whether the psalms or the minutes taught the alphabet faster, what to do about a child who came to school hungry and could not be made to sit still on an empty stomach) that neither of them thought to call anything grander than letters between two women doing the same work at either end of the same coast.

Further south and inland that same season, though neither Agnes nor her scholars would hear of it for some years, the Board's surveyors closed a different kind of book on a different kind of report. The Cill ghlas district, shut to new shafts since Scàineadh Cill ghlas had brought a gallery down there two generations before, was walked over that summer by Lachlann mac Iain Bhàin and the young chainman who carried his chain and his lamp, Iain mac Ruairidh, the two of them tracing the old fault line foot by foot with plumb and cord until they were satisfied it lay where the old surveys said it lay and had moved no further since. Their report, filed with the Board at Cathair dhearg, declared the ground safe to work again, so long as any new shaft was cut a full chain back from the fault line, a condition the Board accepted without argument and the first new diggers observed, that season, to the letter. It is a small entry beside a byre full of children reciting psalms, and it belongs to a different part of this same age's long account of what was measured, and what was not, and by whom, and it is set here only because it happened, as near as the record can place it, in the same weeks that a maor rode home from Doire ghlas with a small book full of everything but the one thing he had seen.

Two reports, that season: one that named a fault line to the foot and was believed on its author's word alone, and one that named nothing at all, and was believed for the same reason. Neither surveyor at Cill ghlas ever heard of the byre at Doire ghlas, nor would have found anything in it to measure had they gone there, there being no fault line under a schoolroom that any chain could find; and Agnes Pike, for her part, never learned the surveyors' names at all. The record keeps the two of them apart because the Board kept them apart, filing the one at Cathair dhearg and never troubling to look north at all; it is only an Leabharlann, gathering both into one shelf at the age's end, that sets them side by side and lets a reader notice what neither man who wrote them was ever asked to notice himself.

Daniel Frayne finished that first winter's schooling in an t-Iuchar, the byre's benches given back to the widow Marrow's returning cattle for the summer months and the school moved, as it would move every summer after, into the porch of An Eaglais, which offered shade enough and no drip at all. On the last morning before the move, Agnes set the class one final copying, the same task she had set them nearly every week since the psalm had first proved too slow a teacher on its own: Tional nan Tuathach's own minute of the day the school had opened, four lines long, recording that a room had been found at Doire ghlas, that eleven children had come to it, and that no leave had been asked because none had been thought likely to be given. Daniel copied it as he copied everything, stroke for stroke, the loops even, the lines straight along a ruled edge Agnes had learned to give him without being asked, and when he had done she took the page from him, read it through once, and pinned it into the front leaf of the school's own new register, the first thing entered there in a hand not her own, leaving the drying wall to the psalms.

"Why that one?" Daniel asked her, watching her pin it.

"Because it's the truest thing in the book," Agnes said, "and you wrote it best."
