# Of the Stones on the North Roads

*SE 1 an t-Ògmhios – SE 2 an Cèitean*

## I. Of the Quay at Caol mhòr

Walter Hale came to Caol mhòr on a coal barge that had no business carrying passengers, and he came because there was nowhere else that would have him.

He was forty-four, thick through the shoulders from twenty years at a harbour desk, with a harbour clerk's stoop and a harbour clerk's hands: the right one calloused along the first two fingers where the pen sat, the left one still, oddly, soft. Ellen sat beside him in the bow with Alice wrapped against her, five years old and sick from the swell, and David stood at the rail the whole crossing with his coat buttoned wrong, watching the grey coast come up out of the grey water as though he expected it to be taken away again before they reached it.

Nobody at Caol mhòr had asked for them. Nobody had asked for any of the households that the guard walked north that month, in twos and threes and once in a column of eleven wagons, out of Ros bheag and Cuan shean and the burnt fringes of the war. The town took them in the way a beach takes what the tide leaves: without complaint, without welcome, because there was nowhere else for it to go. Hale's family was given the loft over a net-store on the harbour street, one room and a stair, and told it was theirs until somebody wanted the loft back.

He went down to the quay the first week because a man does not stop being a clerk for want of a desk, and there was work there that suited his hands if nothing else about the place suited them. The Caol mhòr boats came in with what the sea gave, which in that first summer was not much, and the catch was weighed and written down on a slate that a fisherman's daughter rubbed out every evening whether anyone had copied the numbers or not. Hale copied them. He did it without being asked, the third day, standing at the harbour wall with a scrap of paper braced on his forearm, because watching numbers be rubbed off a slate unrecorded made something in him itch that twenty years at Ros bheag had never quite cured. The daughter, whose name was Peg Carrick, watched him do it and said nothing, and the next evening left the slate a little longer before she wiped it, as if that settled something between them.

He was not the only clerk the guard had walked north. There were pursers and toll-men and a schoolmaster's assistant among the households at Caol mhòr that summer, men and women who had kept some kind of book under the humans' old administration and now kept nothing, because the administration that had employed them was three months drowned in its own harbour and the council that had replaced it wanted no part of their hand. Hale heard two of them talking on the quay steps one evening about going for fishermen, since there was nothing else a man's hands were good for here, and he did not join the talk, because he did not yet believe it.

He unloaded barges through that first month, hauling nets and barrels and, once, a crate of the guard's own muskets going north to the post above the town, and he watched the roads.

## II. Of the Stone on the Caol mhòr Road

The council's masons came up from the south at the end of an t-Iuchar, three of them with a cart of dressed stone and a soldier riding ahead to clear the road, though there was nothing on the road that summer that needed clearing. They worked their way along each of the roads that ran out of Tuathaich country toward the shires, and on the thirty-first they set the stone on the Caol mhòr road, a mile out of the town where the track crossed the old drove path, and were gone south again before the ink of the day's minute was dry.

Hale walked out to see it on the first evening he had free, and took David with him, because the boy had taken to following him about the harbour in a way that made Ellen say he needed something to do with his feet besides the loft stair.

The stone stood a little taller than Hale himself, squared and grey, the kind of stone a man does not shift with his hands alone, and it bore letters cut deep and new, the edges of the cutting still sharp and pale where the weather had not yet got at them. Neither Hale nor David could read a word of what was cut there. It was the council's tongue, not theirs, and no one had troubled to set the sense of it beside the shape.

"What does it say," David asked, which was a fair question and one Hale could not answer.

"That we're this side of it and they're that side," Hale said, which was near enough true, though it was not what the stone said. He had heard, from a Dia-thìreach guard who spoke a broken version of the humans' tongue and liked to talk while he stood his post, that the stone marked where the homeland began: not a wall, the guard had said, laughing a little at the word, as though a wall were a thing his own people might have built and had thought better of. Only a mark. A line drawn once, cut in stone so that no one could say afterward it had never been drawn.

No wall came after it. Hale looked for one through that whole first summer, half expecting to wake one morning and find a ditch and a bank running the length of the road, the kind of thing an army builds when it means to keep something in or out for good. Nothing came. The stone stood alone at the crossing, and the drove path ran past it exactly as it had before, and cattle that did not know one country from another walked over the line without noticing it was there.

He set his hand flat against the stone's face before they walked home, feeling the cut letters under his palm the way a blind man reads, though he had no more sense for what they meant that way than by eye. David did the same, more from watching his father than from any purpose of his own, and asked would they ever be able to read it.

"Somebody will read it to us someday," Hale said. "Somebody always does, in the end." He believed it when he said it, though he could not have told David why.

They walked back to the town in the last light with the stone behind them, and Hale did not look at it again that year, though he thought of it more than he let on: a boundary with no wall, a line a man could step over and not know he had done it, except that everyone on both sides of it had been told, in one tongue or the other, that it was there.

## III. Of the Thin Harvest

The stores that had always come over the water in the autumn did not come that year, and the council in the south, which had never counted the Tuathaich households among the mouths it fed, did not send anything in their place. Nobody had told the north's fields this. They had been sown late, after the marching and the settling, on ground half the households had never worked before, and what they gave back at the reaping was thin: short straw, light ears, a crop that a farmer's eye could read as trouble from the far end of the field before a scythe had touched it.

The Caol mhòr boats did better than the land, at first, but even the sea was uncertain that year, the herring shoals running late and thin off their old grounds, and by an Dàmhair the catch had fallen to less than a household could live on if each household kept what it caught for itself.

It was Ellen who put the shape to it before anyone in the town had a word for it. She had kept a harbour clerk's house long enough to think in tallies without meaning to, and she came home from the quay one evening in the first week of an Dàmhair and told Hale, flatly, the way she told him most things that mattered, that Peg Carrick's mother had given the Widow Coyle two fish out of a catch of nine, and the Widow Coyle had no boat and no man to send out in one, and if that went on past the week it would not go on at all, because nine fish shared out to households instead of sold to whoever came first to the quay was not how a harbour worked, and everyone at Caol mhòr knew it.

It became, within the month, how the harbour worked anyway. There was no minute written for it, no council of the town's own to vote it in; it happened the way water finds its own level, house by house, boat by boat, until the whole of Caol mhòr's catch, good weeks and bad, went first to a reckoning of who had a boat and who did not, and only what was left after that was sold or traded beyond the town. Hale kept no tally of it himself that autumn, his own book still unbegun, and he watched it happen with the same clerk's attention he gave the harbour slate. A town sharing its catch by households and a town selling its catch to whoever paid first were two different towns, he came to understand, though he could not yet have said in so many words why it mattered, and Caol mhòr had quietly become the first kind without anyone deciding it should.

He thought of the guard and his mark cut in stone, the line with no wall, and thought that a boundary a man could not see might matter as much as one he could.

## IV. Of the Meal Carried to Doire ghlas

By an t-Samhain the thinness of the harvest had a name in every house at Caol mhòr, though no two households agreed on how bad it would get before spring, and by early an Dùbhlachd the name had stopped being a thing people argued about and become a thing they simply lived inside, the way a house lives inside a hard winter once the first snow has settled and shows no sign of going.

Word came down the coast road from Doire ghlas, ten miles inland along a track that in a wet December took the better part of a day to walk, that the town there had nothing left in its stores and had begun, in the second week of the month, to bury its own dead of hunger without troubling to send word south, because word sent south had never yet brought anything back.

Caol mhòr had little enough to spare, but it had more than Doire ghlas, being a harbour town with a catch of sorts still coming in, and the Moot — though it did not yet have that name, being still only the loose gathering of householders that had first met in the long room in an t-Sultain and had taken to meeting again whenever there was cause — voted what oats and salt fish it could spare into three carts, and asked for men to walk them inland.

Hale went. He told Ellen he went because his hands were the freest in the household that week, David and Alice both needing her at home with the fever going round the loft-houses, but the truth of it, which he did not say to her, was that he went because Doire ghlas frightened him in a way the thin harvest at Caol mhòr had not, and a man does not walk toward what frightens him without some better reason than idle hands, and he had not found a better reason, and had gone anyway.

The road to Doire ghlas ran inland from the coast through country that had been farmed by nobody in living memory before that year, scrub and rush pasture that the settled households had been given because the good land nearer the coast was already claimed, and the going was slow with the cart wheels sinking to the hub in places where the drainage had never been cut. They came into Doire ghlas on the second day, in the last of the light, and Hale smelled the town before he saw its roofs: woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke something colder that he did not let himself name until he had to.

They had dug the graves on the rise above the church because the low ground by the burn was too wet to hold a grave that winter, and the digging had been hard going in ground half frozen, so that the graves were shallower than a churchyard grave should be and set closer together than custom allowed, because there had not been strength enough in the town to dig them any other way. Hale counted the fresh mounds while the carts were being unloaded, walking the rise alone in the last grey light with his hat off though there was no one there to see him bare his head for it, and stopped counting at eleven because his eyes had blurred and he no longer trusted the number, and started again, and reached fourteen the second time, and did not try a third.

Three of the mounds were child-sized. He stood over those longest.

A woman of the town, thin herself in a way that made her age hard to guess, told him while they carried sacks of oats into what was left of the store that the town had lost more than the fourteen he could count on the rise, because some of the earliest dead, back in an t-Samhain before anyone had thought to keep a reckoning, had been buried singly by their own families in whatever ground was handiest, without the town knowing to add them to any count at all. She could not say how many. Nobody could say how many. Hale came slowly to understand, standing in the freezing store with an oat sack still on his shoulder, the whole shape of the trouble: the town had starved through a winter nobody south of the guard-line had thought to prevent, and the dying itself had gone on unrecorded, house by house, before anyone had thought to reckon it as a single town's loss rather than a scatter of private griefs.

He asked the woman, because it was the only question his clerk's mind knew how to ask in the face of a thing this large, whether anyone kept a book of it. She looked at him as though the question itself were a kind of madness, in a place where half the households did not yet own a book of any kind, and said that the priest kept the parish register, births and marryings and the ordinary dead. This was a different order of grief, too large for the parish book and too many-headed for any one family's memory, and nobody had told her what a person was meant to do with it, and so, for want of a better answer, nobody had done anything with it at all.

## V. Of the Book Begun

Hale walked back to Caol mhòr with the empty carts in three days instead of two, because the weather turned and one of the horses went lame on the second, and he had the whole of that slow, cold walking to sit with what he had seen on the rise above Doire ghlas and could not put down.

It was not, he told himself at first, a clerk's proper business. He had no office here, no seal, no book issued him by any council; he was a harbour clerk without a harbour, three months into a life he had not chosen, in a country whose government did not know his name and would not have cared to learn it. But he had spent twenty years believing that a thing not written down was a thing that had not, in any way that mattered to the world afterward, happened, and that belief had not left him only because the humans' administration that had trained him into it now lay at the bottom of a drowned harbour. It went where he went. It had gone with him up the coast road and stood with him on the rise counting graves he could not trust his own eyes to count twice the same.

He bought, from the one shop at Caol mhòr that still sold anything beyond food and rope, a ledger meant for a fishmonger's accounts, its pages ruled for weights and prices, and on the first evening after his return, with Ellen asleep and the children asleep and the loft cold around him, he opened it at the first page and wrote, in his own clerk's hand, the word *Doire ghlas*, and under it the date, and under that, as near as he could reconstruct it from what the woman in the store had told him and what he had counted twice on the rise, a number, and could not have sworn to its truth writing it, only that it was the nearest thing to truth that anyone had troubled to set down, which was as much as the writing could claim for itself. The alternative, no number at all, a grief too large for the parish book, filed nowhere, answerable to nobody, was the thing he had walked ten miles inland and ten miles home to refuse.

He did not stop at Doire ghlas. Through the rest of that hard winter, as word came down the coast road of other towns short of grain, other households that had gone without and some that had not come through it, Hale set down what he could learn of each in the fishmonger's ledger, town by town, a line or two apiece, a name where a name could be had and a number where a number could be had and, more often than either, a plain note that no count had been kept and none now could be trusted. He did not think of it, in those first months, as anything more than a private accounting, the kind of book a careful man keeps against his own forgetting. It did not occur to him yet — though it would, before the winter was out, in a manner that changed the shape of what the book was for — that he was keeping the only reckoning of that winter's dead that would ever exist south of the guard-line either, because no one south of it had asked to be told, and no one south of it, so far as Hale could tell, so much as knew there was a reckoning to be kept.

That was the turn of it, when it came to him, sitting by the loft's small fire on a night in am Faoilleach with the fishmonger's ledger open on his knee and the wind working at the shutter: that the council which had set a stone on the road to mark where his people's country began had drawn no line at all around the counting of his people's dead, because it had never occurred to the council that there was a count to be drawn a line around. The Dia-thìrich made none. They had not refused to; refusal would have required them to notice there was a decision to make. They simply had not thought of it, the way a man does not think to count the leaves that fall from a tree he does not own, and in the space that not-thinking left, if the count was to exist at all, it would have to be the Tuathaich who kept it, in their own hand, in a fishmonger's ledger bought for weights and prices, because no one else on the whole of the island would ever trouble to.

He did not write that thought down; it belonged nowhere in a ledger of names and numbers. It sat in him from that night on, under everything else the book came afterward to hold, the way the stone on the Caol mhòr road sat unseen under the drove path's traffic: a line drawn once, that nobody south of it would ever have cause to notice, and that everybody north of it would carry, whether they chose to or not, for the rest of their lives.

## VI. Of the Names in the Porch

Spring came late that year, as it does on a coast that takes its weather from the open west, but it came. By an Cèitean the worst of the hunger had passed, the last of the winter stores stretched thin enough to see the new grass through, and the herring, which had run late and light the autumn before, came back to the Caol mhòr grounds in numbers a fisherman three generations settled there said he had not seen since before the Severance, as though the sea itself, having taken nothing from the town that winter, had decided to give something back by way of apology.

Doire ghlas, when word of it reached Caol mhòr on a carter's cart come down for seed, had done in its own fashion what Hale had done alone in the loft with the fishmonger's ledger, though the town had come to it by a different road. Somebody at Doire ghlas — the carter did not know who, only that it was a woman of the parish, not the priest, who had first set the thing in motion — had gone from house to house through the town in the first mild week of spring and asked each household for the names of anyone it had lost since an t-Samhain, hunger-dead and not ordinary dead, and had written each name on a strip of paper in whatever hand could be found to write it, and pinned the strips, one by one as they were gathered, along the boards of the church porch, where every soul in the town passed at least once a week whether it went in to service or not.

There was no order to the names, the carter said, no roll of the dead set out fair in a single hand the way a parish register would have kept it; strips of paper in a dozen different hands, some barely lettered, pinned up as they came in, so that a name might sit next to another from a household three streets off with no kin between them, and a child's name might sit above or below an old man's with nothing to mark which was which except the writing itself. But the porch held, by the carter's rough count, above fifteen names by the time he had come away, and the town had taken to calling it, without deciding to, the porch of the names, and left it up through the spring though the strips curled and faded in the weather, because nobody in Doire ghlas could yet bring themselves to be the one to take even a single name down.

Hale walked out to see it himself before that spring was over, taking the coast road ten miles inland a second time, this time with no cart to slow him and no lame horse, and stood in the church porch reading names he mostly did not know, in a light hand and a heavy hand and a child's careful copying-hand where some elder had taught a grandchild the letters expressly for this, and found, when he had read them all through once, that he could not have said afterward whether the number matched the fourteen he had counted on the rise that winter, or the vaguer reckoning the woman in the store had given him of the earlier dead nobody had thought to add in, or neither. He understood, standing there in the mild spring light with the herring running thick again off the harbour he had left that morning, that the porch kept its own count, made by its own town, in its own way, and no less true for not squaring with his.

The porch's names did not go into the fishmonger's ledger that day. Hale copied them instead, every one, into a second book he had begun keeping since am Faoilleach for exactly this purpose, a book with nothing in it yet but blank ruled pages and the single word he had written on its first leaf in the dead of that winter, by lamplight: *Doire ghlas*, and under it, this time, above fifteen names, copied faithfully from a church porch that the council eighty miles south did not know existed, in a hand a Dia-thìreach clerk, had one ever troubled to read it, would have called a stranger's.

The ledger went home to Caol mhòr under his coat, against the last of the spring rain, and he told no one south of the guard-stone what it held, for there was no one south of the guard-stone to tell.
