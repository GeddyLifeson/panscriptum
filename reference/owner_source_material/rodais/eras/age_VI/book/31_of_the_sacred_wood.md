# Of the Sacred Wood

## I. Of Cnoc bheag and the Waking Schools

By an Dàmhair of {{year:V-0161}} the river quays of Cnoc bheag took in more grain in a season than the granaries of Cathair mhòr could show for a year, and the shire's own tale-keepers, who had watched the town's numbers year on year the way a farmer watches a field he does not own, wrote that Cnoc bheag had passed Cathair mhòr at last and would not be caught again. Òrd an t-Seabhaig house, richer now than any order's house outside the capital, kept its own accounts apart from Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's and let no custodian south of the water see them; the grain barges tied three deep at the stone stair below the house on a market morning, sacks going up on men's backs from first light until the tide turned and took the empty boats back down river for another load, and a man could walk the length of the quay without once hearing the tongue of the south spoken, though he heard Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's bell and Òrd an t-Seabhaig's bell both, from towers a bowshot apart, telling two different hours to two congregations that did not otherwise speak. A factor of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, come up from Cathair mhòr that autumn to see for himself whether the town's numbers were truly what the returns claimed, stood on the quay a whole market morning counting sacks with his own hand and gave it up before noon, telling the clerk who had come with him that a town could not be doubted that would not stop moving grain long enough to be counted.

Leabharlann Muileann chaol, keeping the years as they came in under Mòrag nic Coinnich's hand, set this beside a harder thing that same season, though no survey of Comhairle nam Mèinnean told it, Comhairle nam Mèinnean sending none: the hewers who came down to the fair said the coal came up thinner from the eastern galleries every year, and thinnest where it ran darkest, the shade Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail called holiest. A vein that answered fewer hands than it once had was a mine matter to Comhairle nam Mèinnean and a coal matter to Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, and neither would own it as anything more, but it was the oldest question the island had, older than either of them, and that autumn it woke where it had slept.

Scoiltean nan Draoidhean of Flidais, silent under the humans' rule and silent still for years beyond it, opened their doors again within the month, first at the old house near Muileann chiar in the east where the question had first been asked in An Aois Naomh, then within the year at two more houses that had stood empty so long that the roof-trees wanted new thatch before a lecture could be given under them without the rain coming through. Men who remembered the old houses only as places sheep were folded in a hard winter came with hammers and green timber before ever a lecture was given, mending a door here and a sill there for no wage but the promise of a bench kept for them when the teaching began. Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's own clerks, setting down what they knew of it for Àrd-choimheadaiche's file, called it a nuisance of the vein's failing and nothing more, a habit of idle men asking why when they should be asking how much; the schools themselves called it, in the one word their own writings kept from before the humans came, the waking. Neither name mattered much to the students who walked to the old houses that winter with a slate and a knife to cut their own stylus, only that the houses were open and the question, the same one the vein-masters of An Aois Naomh had first put and never answered, could be asked aloud again without a hall shutting its gallery to the asker.

## II. Of the Lecture in Flidais's Wood

Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain came to Coille Naomh Muileann chiar near Muileann chiar on a morning in an Dùbhlachd with the frost still on the bracken and her own breath standing white in front of her, and found forty people waiting under the bare oaks where she had thought to find a dozen. She had taught the question in a small way for eleven years, to whoever of the old families would still send a son or a daughter to hear her, in a barn lent for the winter and in the corner of a market hall when the barn's owner wanted it back for lambing; she had never before stood in Coille Naomh Muileann chiar to teach it, the wood the schools had kept since long before the humans came and had gone on quietly keeping even while the schools that owned it slept, a grove of old oaks and one white-barked stag's-tree at its centre that no axe had touched in living memory and none living now could say how long before that. Past fifty that winter, grey at the temple and stiff in one knee from a fall on wet stone some years before, she walked the last mile to the grove leaning on a hazel stick cut for her by a student, arriving with mud to the shin and no more ceremony about her than any farmer's wife come in from a field.

She had written the lecture out three times over and burned two of the drafts, standing that morning with neither paper before her, because the question did not want dressing. "Why does the vein answer this hand," she said, laying her own bare palm flat on the stag's-tree's white bark for the crowd to see, "and not that one." She let it stand a moment before she went on. An Aois Naomh had asked it of birth and of practice both and settled neither; the humans, who dug the vein deeper than any age before them and answered to it not at all, had asked instead why it should matter, and been told by their own masters that the question was not theirs to ask; and now, with the vein running thinner in the eastern galleries, the island's own people were asking it again, this time of a matter the census had begun to write down and neither Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail nor An Crùn would say aloud. She did not name fuil-ghuail that morning, not once, though every soul under the bare oaks knew well enough what hand she meant, and she let the silence that followed do what a plainer word would only have blunted. An old man near the back, whose own father had kept the strike-books in the last years before the schools shut, asked her when the silence had passed whether she meant to answer the question herself before she was done teaching it, and she told him plainly that she did not, that she had come to the grove to ask it in the open where it could be heard, not to settle in an afternoon what the island had failed to settle in all its ages, and the old man nodded at that as at an answer he had half expected and was not sorry to be given.

Word of that morning's lecture went out from Muileann chiar faster than any rider could have carried it, north and east through the shires where the old druid houses still stood roofless and waiting, and within the half-year the houses of Siorrachd Baile gharbh in the north-east, silent since the humans first ruled there, opened their doors on the same question and sent their own teachers walking the long road south to hear Seonaid herself before they dared teach it in their own halls. It was a walk of two days each way, by the accounts the schools themselves kept, over roads that saw little enough traffic beyond a carter's cart and a drover's flock, and the teachers who made it came in most years with their boots worn through and stayed only three nights before turning back, having judged the season too short and the sowing too near to spare a longer visit. An Leabharlann's own clerks noted, with the dry wonder of men who had watched fewer and fewer make such journeys for anything but a fair or a funeral, that no house among them missed a single winter's walk once it had begun, and that the youngest of the Baile gharbh teachers, a woman not yet twenty on her first journey south, wrote in the school's own minute-book on her return that she had walked two days to hear one question asked and not answered, and would walk it again gladly the next winter for the same.

## III. Of the Wet House at Caol shean

Coille Naomh Muileann chiar near Muileann chiar could not be taught in through the worst of the wet months, when the ground under the bare oaks stood ankle-deep and no fire could be kept lit long enough to warm a lecture, and by an Dùbhlachd after her first winter's teaching Seonaid had taken a house at Caol shean, one of the old druid towns of the east, to carry the school through until the ground dried. It was there, in a low room with a single smoking hearth and benches enough for thirty crowded to hold near fifty, the window shutters lashed against a wind that came off the coast with rain in it more days than not, that the school's students first laid the kingdom's own census beside the schools' oldest question and found the two would not sit apart.

The realm's returns carried a line for fuil-ghuail that gave a number and nothing else, the same bare figure Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's own doctrine called a settled matter and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own clerks copied without comment year on year; the students at Caol shean, who had come to the school precisely because the settled matter did not sit easily with them, copied the same line into their own exercise-books and argued over it by the hearth in a way Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's own halls did not permit and had never permitted, not in An Aois Naomh and not now. A student new to the school that first wet winter, a miller's son from a village two days' walk south, asked aloud one evening why a line Comhairle nam Mèinnean would print and a custodian would bless should be a matter no one might question, when the vein, holier by far in Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's own reckoning, was questioned daily by every teacher under Seonaid's roof; no one in the room answered him outright, but no one told him to be silent either, and the talk ran on past the hour the hearth was banked, three students still arguing it by the last of the peat's own glow once the rest had gone to their beds. An Leabharlann's clerks, hearing of the evening from a student who kept the school's own minute-book, judged it worth the setting down, the first time on the record that the census line and the schools' old question had been spoken of in the same breath by anyone Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail could not simply have shut a door on.

That same winter the hall at Muileann dhearg, the old druid hall that had served the hewers for a lodging and stood empty since the fall had closed the Cill ghlas district, was given back to the school by the Guild's own captains, more for want of any better use for a hall no miner would work under than for any love of the question taught in it, and the school took it gladly, glad above all of a roof that did not smoke and a floor that stayed dry through am Faoilleach. The captain who signed the grant over, an old man himself near the end of his own working years, told the schoolmaster who came to thank him that he wanted no thanks, only that the school keep the hall's roof sound, "since a roof kept is a roof kept, whoever prays under it." The Guild's grant carried one condition only, that the school teach nothing there that named a living captain or a living gallery, and the school kept to it without argument, having larger matters to ask than any one captain's name.

In the same season, at a council of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail called at Seann Skell on the southern coast, Àrd-choimheadaiche's own clerks drew up a settlement long asked for and long refused: Òrd an t-Seabhaig of Cnoc bheag, grown too rich and too old a custom for Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail to go on pretending had no place under it, were offered a place of their own within the order, answering to Àrd-choimheadaiche in name and keeping their own bell and their own order of worship in fact. The terms ran to four pages, and the clerk who read them out to the assembled council read them slowly, watching Òrd an t-Seabhaig's own witnesses for the objection that had killed the same offer six times before; none came. Òrd an t-Seabhaig took the place, as an Leabharlann's own note of it says, "on the seventh reading of the terms and the first without an argument," and the quarrel between the two bells at Cnoc bheag, Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's and Òrd an t-Seabhaig's, was quieter after that, though it did not end; the two bells went on telling two hours to two congregations, only now under one roof of law that neither had troubled to ask for before.

## IV. Of the Bindery, the Breakwater and the Two Dùn thais

Daniel Frayne, who had walked to Agnes Pike's byre as a boy with nothing of his own but a slate, kept by SE 48 a small bindery at Muileann leathan where he taught young Tuathaich hands to sew a book's spine and cut its boards true, a craft the southern guilds had never yet let a Tuathaich apprentice learn under their own mark. The bindery was one low room behind his own house, a press he had built himself from a cider-press bought broken and mended, and three boards worn smooth with handling where his pupils learned first to fold a signature true before ever they were let near a needle or a knife. He had no guild's leave to teach it and asked none, having learned long since that the leave he wanted would not be granted him for the asking; he taught instead from what an old binder at Caol mhòr had let him watch as a boy and from what his own hands had worked out through failure since, ruined calf and split boards enough, by his own telling, to have clothed a smaller man's whole flock.

By an Giblean his first bindings, plain calf over boards with no maker's name pressed into the spine, were carried south with the wool carts and sold in three towns his own pupils had never seen, bought by Dia-thìreach households who praised the binding's neatness to Frayne's own face without knowing whose hands had made it. A carter who had sold three of the volumes at the fair told Frayne on his return that a Cathair mhòr merchant had asked twice over who bound them so true, and had been told only that they came from a northern house that kept its own methods close; Frayne laughed at that, the carter said, longer than the joke alone seemed to want, and then grew quiet and said that a closed mouth had sold more of his pupils' work than an open one ever would have. He kept every letter of praise that reached him by that roundabout road, unsigned as his own work was, in a box he showed to no one but the pupil whose hands had actually sewn the spine praised in it, and told the boy who asked him why he kept unsigned praise at all that a thing well made did not need his name on it to be true, only that he himself, and the hand that had made it, should know it.

That same spring the shires of the north-east coast, Caol chrom and its neighbour across the strait, laid the first stone of a breakwater the two had agreed between them to share the cost of, the longest the island had yet built, meant to close a strait that had drowned more boats in a hard winter than either shire's own harbour dues could easily bear alone; the work would want the better part of eight years to finish, by the masons' own reckoning, and the first stone went down to no ceremony beyond a blessing asked of whichever faith a man's own house kept, Rite or Hawk or the older reverence both together, the masons themselves caring more that the stone sat true than whose blessing had been said over it, and the two shires' own maoir standing together on the strand to watch the first block swung out on ropes and set, without either troubling to make a speech of it. In an Cèitean of the same year the old boatman at Cidhe Beag, who had carried passengers and small freight across the Abhainn uaine at his own landing for the better part of forty years without a licence asked or given, was granted one at last by the shire's own maor, more for the sake of the shire's records than for any change the boatman himself troubled to make in how he rowed his boat or reckoned his fare; he hung the licence's paper, framed behind a scrap of glass, in the one dry corner of his boat-shed, and told anyone who asked that he had rowed the crossing forty years without it and would row it forty more the same whether the paper hung there or not.

It was in these same years, the schools' clerks and Seanchaidh na Rìoghachd's both agreeing on it after their own fashion, that Tormod Pinn, keeping the royal record at his desk at Muileann chaol, first set down in plain ink what the map had begun to blur without anyone's intending it: that the realm held two towns of one name, Dùn thais in the west and Dùn thais in the east, and that An Crùn's own returns had begun, in the press of a busy season, to run the two together as though they were one place answering for both a sea-court's ruling and a market's charter in the same twelvemonth. Tormod marked the confusion in his own hand, west against east, ruling a fresh line across the page where the two entries had begun to run together, and sent word to both shires that An Crùn's clerks should take more care hereafter; it was a small matter beside a breakwater or a bindery, and Tormod himself thought it worth no more than the half-page he gave it, but an Leabharlann kept his half-page all the same, judging, as it judged most things, that a confusion caught early cost less to unpick than one left to grow into a dispute neither shire could afterward be talked out of.

Further north that same winter the fishermen of Loch chrom, the only great lake the island held, were licensed at last to net its trout for their own tables and their own markets, a right An Crùn's own table had kept first call on since before any living fisherman's grandfather was born; the licence changed little in the practice of it, since An Crùn's table was still served first as it had always been, the best of each week's catch set aside before ever a net was drawn to shore for the fishermen's own market, but it was written down now where before it had only been understood, and the fishermen of the loch, who set less store by writing than by the fish itself, thought the writing worth having all the same, against a day some Crown's steward less easy than the last might try to claim the whole catch instead of the first cut of it.

## V. Of the Pillar Set Beside the Obelisk

In an Gearran of SE 49, with the wet ground at Muileann chiar dried enough at last for the school to teach under the bare oaks again, three of Seonaid's own students rode out from Coille Naomh Muileann chiar to the standing pillar near Cnoc chiar and copied its marks onto three separate slates, chalk rubbed into the cut lines to bring them up under a low winter sun, before they rode on: one to the fenced obelisk in the pasture north of Cnoc bheag, and one to Calbh Cnoc ghorm, standing alone in a field a farmer ploughed carefully round every spring without once being told to; they meant to lay the three copies side by side and see what any one of the three might tell that the others could not.

They rode back to Caol shean with slates wrapped in oiled cloth against the rain, and it took three wet evenings by the schoolhouse's single lamp before the students judged the copying careful enough to trust. They found signs in common between all three, cut, so far as anyone could judge, by no later hand than the oldest the island's own memory kept, a mark like a bent reed repeated at Cnoc chiar and at Cnoc ghorm both, and a straight double stroke that appeared on all three stones though nowhere else that any student present had seen it; and they found no meaning in the signs that any student of the school could bring out of them beyond the plain fact of their having been cut at all, by hands that had wanted, whatever else they wanted, to be remembered. One student, younger than the rest and less patient with a question that would not yield, asked why the school troubled to copy marks that told nothing; an older student, who had walked from Baile gharbh two winters running for less certain teaching than this, told him that a mark that told nothing was itself a kind of telling, if enough care was taken to notice that it had been left at all.

Seonaid herself, reading the three copies laid together on the long table at Caol shean by the last light of an afternoon in an Gearran, the shutters still lashed against a wind that had not yet let up since am Faoilleach, said nothing for a long while, turning each slate to the lamp in its turn and setting it down again. Then she took up her own pen and wrote, in the hand her own students copied afterward into every school's minute-book from Muileann chiar to Baile gharbh, the sentence an Leabharlann itself chose to close its own telling of that winter: that Seann-Dhaoine had been here, and had said so, in whatever tongue and by whatever hand had cut the stones, and that none living now could hear what they had said.

She did not write it as a discovery, having found nothing that a hundred generations before her students had not already half known and let lie; she wrote it, her own students said afterward, as a plain fact set down at last where it could not be unset, the way the census wrote its bare line for fuil-ghuail and Comhairle nam Mèinnean wrote its bare figure for the vein's own narrowing, a thing known and now written and no longer merely known. The question the schools had first asked at Muileann chiar in An Aois Naomh, why the vein answered some hands and not others, stood no nearer an answer for the winter's riding and copying than it had stood before it; but it stood now in the open, argued by lamplight at Caol shean and taught under bare oaks at Muileann chiar, where for the better part of the humans' whole rule it had been asked, if it was asked at all, only in a room with the door shut and no one outside to hear it.
