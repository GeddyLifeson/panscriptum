# Of the Sloc Mòr

*SE 12 am Màrt – SE 13 an t-Samhain*

> *Tha an fhèith a' seinn fon lasair, ach 's e na làmhan a chì a dath.*
> The vein sings under the lamp, but it is the hand that sees its colour.
>
> — said among the chainmen of An Sloc Mòr, of the charter sworn at Achadh dhomhain, {{date:V-0060}}

## I. Of the Lamp and the Chain

Iain mac Ruairidh was nineteen the spring Lachlann mac Iain Bhàin took him on, and he came to it the way most of the hill families came to anything, by having no better offer and a father who had been down in the rock himself and did not want the boy following him there with a pick. Ruairidh had cut coal at An Sloc Mòr for eleven years and had the cough that came of it, and when Comhairle nam Mèinnean's man rode up from Achadh dhomhain asking after a boy who could hold a chain steady and had his letters well enough to keep a book, Ruairidh sent Iain out to the yard before the man had finished asking.

Lachlann was fifty-two that year, broad through the shoulders and going grey at the temple, and he had surveyed An Sloc Mòr's galleries, for the humans' Company and then for Comhairle nam Mèinnean, since before Iain was born. He looked the boy over once, took the measure of his hands, which were a hewer's son's hands and knew what rock was, and said only that a chainman who dropped his end of the chain in the dark was worse than no chainman at all, and that Iain would learn or he would go home. Iain did not go home.

The work was plain enough to name and hard enough to do. Lachlann carried the lamp and read the face; Iain carried the sixty-six-foot chain, drove the pins, called the links back to Lachlann's book, and learned, over that first summer, the difference between a fault that pinched a seam thin and a fault that only bent it. Comhairle nam Mèinnean's surveys did not trust a compass alone in a gallery cut through iron-bearing rock, so much of the work was done link by link, pin by pin, a hundred feet at a stretch, with the lamp throwing its small circle of light on rock that had never had a light thrown on it before.

It was Lachlann who first told him, on a morning in the old eastern galleries when the face ahead of them ran three colours where Iain saw one, that the Dia-thìrich saw more in a broken face than the humans ever had, and that Comhairle nam Mèinnean's surveyors, all of them of the old blood, were valued for the same reason the hewers were: a hand of the island's own blood could tell a seam that would hold from a seam that would run, most days, before the pick ever bit it. He did not make more of it than that. He showed Iain where the grey went to green at a fault's lip, and where the black thickened toward a good face, and said that a chainman who learned to read it as well as he chained it would be worth keeping. Iain learned it the way he had learned his letters, slowly and then all at once, and by the autumn he could stand at Lachlann's shoulder and see, before the older man said it aloud, which way the book's next line would run.

Lachlann was not a talking man, and what talk he gave came mostly at the end of a shift, sitting on an upturned barrow at the gallery mouth while the lamp burned low and the hewers went past them toward the cage. He had come to Comhairle nam Mèinnean from a family of custodians near Cathair mhòr, a house that had kept the old rites before Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail had a doctrine to keep, and he said once, when Iain asked him plainly why a custodian's grandson chained coal for a living, that the mountain had been sacred before it was a concession and would go on being sacred after the concession ran out, whatever the charters said, and that a man who measured it honestly did it more honour than a man who only blessed the shafts and went home. He did not say it twice, and Iain did not ask him to.

By the second summer Iain could carry the chain alone over short courses while Lachlann worked ahead with the lamp, and could set a pin true in rock that gave no easy footing, in the dark, by feel as much as by sight. He learned, too, the small dishonesties a chain could be put to and the men who tried them: a hewer's brother, paid by the fathom driven, who would swear a course ran four links longer than it did, and the quiet, unhurried way Lachlann had of walking the ground again himself and saying nothing about it beyond the true figure entered in the book. "The rock doesn't care what a man's paid," he told Iain once, "only the book does, and the book is honest because we make it so, not because it wants to be." Iain wrote that down too, in his own book, the same night.

He kept his own small book that year too, apart from Comhairle nam Mèinnean's, a boy's habit copied from watching Lachlann write. In it he set down the things Comhairle nam Mèinnean's book had no room for: the day a spring broke through above the third gallery and they lost a week pumping it clear; the day a hewer named Fearchar found a fist of raw coal so black it threw the lamp's flame straight back, and Lachlann took it away to be shown to the custodians and it was never seen again; the day the chain came up short by two links and Iain walked the ground twice before he found where a hewer's barrow had dragged a pin loose. None of it went into Comhairle nam Mèinnean's survey. All of it, in Iain's own hand, went into his.

## II. Of the Towns that Filled

The east had gone hungry again that year, in the way it did every few years when the barley failed on the thin soils above Baile ruadh and the eastern moors, and the families who could not wait out a bad season on borrowed seed did what such families had always done, which was to go where wages were paid in coin rather than promised in grain. An Sloc Mòr paid in the kingdom's new coin, and so they came, on foot mostly, with what they could carry, down Rathad na Mèinne and up the valley to Achadh dhomhain and the towns that had grown up round the pithead.

Muileann dhearg took the most of them. It had been a small place at An Dealachadh, a shire seat with a Fèidh chapel and a druid hall shut up since the humans' time, its doors nailed and its roof sound but empty. By the second year of the hungry families' coming, the hall had been opened again as a lodging, rows of pallets on its floor and a fire kept in its hearth through the worst of the winter, and it was enlarged twice in those same years, first with a lean-to along its south wall and then with a second storey built crudely of the same grey spoil-stone the quarries were cutting a mile off. The town doubled within three years of Iain's taking on, and the maor who kept its rolls stopped trying to name every household that passed through it and began instead to count heads at midsummer and let the rest go unrecorded.

Baile thais grew a different way. It had always been the place where Rathad na Mèinne came down off the central hills to meet the Abhainn uaine above the gulf, a crossing more than a town, and the carters who had once passed through it in a morning began to stop the night, and then to keep families there, and the town filled in behind them with inns and stables and, in Iain's second autumn on the chain, a ropewalk laid out along the water meadow below the ford, where hemp was worked into the hauling-ropes An Sloc Mòr's cages used and sold, besides, to every carter on the road. Within the decade its people had doubled too, and the council gave it a shire court of its own, the first new court granted north of Cathair mhòr in Iain's lifetime.

Of the coast that same year an Leabharlann keeps one page, and it is a short one. Ships that flew no flag, some of them crewed, it was said, by men who had once worn the humans' uniform and now wore none, began to work the waters off Cathair gheal in the north-west, and the towns there, being Tuathaich towns and poor ones, had neither walls nor boats built for fighting and found no one troubled to defend them. A fishing house was burned on the shore below Cathair gheal that summer, and two boats taken out of Cathair gheal's own small harbour never came home, and the council's minute on it, when Tional nan Tuathach's letter reached Cathair dhearg the following spring, ran to four lines and asked that the matter be watched. It was watched. Nothing else was done that year, and the raiders' ships kept the north-western water for a long while after.

Iain heard of it the way most of the south heard of anything from that coast, third-hand and late, from a carter at Baile thais who had it from a fisherman who had it from a man who had lost a cousin off Cathair gheal. He did not think much of it at the time. He had a chain to run and a book to keep, and the mountain, that year, gave him more than enough to fill both.

The filling of the towns stayed with Iain longer than the raiders did, having come closer to hand. That autumn he was sent to walk the Muileann dhearg lodging with the maor's clerk, to mark on a plan which of the hall's new rooms stood over ground Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn judged sound and which did not, the old druid hall having been raised, like most things built before the humans came, without much thought for what lay beneath it. There he counted forty-one pallets on the ground floor alone, laid so close a man could not have crossed the room without stepping between sleepers, and a hearth kept going day and night by whichever family's turn it was, and a smell of wet wool and tallow that he did not lose from his coat for a week after. A woman there, Ealasaid, newly down from Baile ruadh with three children and a husband gone ahead the season before to find work, asked him plainly whether Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn meant to build proper walls or only to keep stacking families under the one roof until it fell in on them, and Iain had no answer for her beyond the true one, which was that he surveyed ground, not lodgings, and that the question was the maor's to answer, not his. He wrote her question down anyway. It went nowhere he ever heard of.

## III. Of the First Train

The humans' railway had come apart in places over the years since An Dealachadh, its rails lifted in three parishes for the iron and its sleepers rotted where no one had thought to renew them, but the stretch from Cathair dhearg north had been kept, more or less, by a Rail Board that mended what it must and let the rest stand idle, and in Iain's first year on the chain that mending reached the valley. Gangs came up from the south with the humans' own tools, worn but sound, and laid new sleepers under old rail as far as the pithead, so that for the first time since the humans had built it the line ran unbroken from Achadh dhomhain to the capital.

The first train went down on the third of an Dàmhair, a grey morning with rain threatening and not falling, and half the town came out to see it off. There was no ceremony to it beyond the crowd that came; no Dia-thìris word had been coined yet for the thing, and the engineer who fired the boiler was a Tuathaich man who had learned the humans' trade from his father and kept the humans' habits with it, oiling the wheels the way the humans had oiled them and calling the whistle by the humans' word before anyone thought to give it another. The wagons behind him were loaded with coal cut that same week, in the kingdom's name now though on the humans' rails, and the whole of it, engine and wagons and the men who rode the footplate, went down the valley at a walking pace and out of sight round the first bend, and the crowd went back to its work.

Iain stood at the yard fence with Lachlann that morning and watched it go, and said, because it seemed a thing worth saying, that it was strange to see the humans' iron still doing the humans' work with no human left on the island to claim it. Lachlann said that the iron did not know whose name was on the wagons, and went back to his book.

The maor of Achadh dhomhain had wanted a blessing said over the first train, and had sent word to Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's house at Muileann dhearg to that effect, but the custodian who came up was an old man unused to trains and unwilling to walk the length of the footplate, and in the end he said his words over the coal in the first wagon rather than the engine, which the engineer, a Tuathaich man named Colin Rudd who had oiled locomotives under the humans' foremen as a boy, took as no insult and rather more sense than blessing an engine would have been. "You can bless coal," Colin Rudd said to Iain afterward, while the fire was building in the boiler and the crowd was still thick along the fence, "coal's been blessed since before either of us. Nobody's blessed an engine yet, and I'd not know what it would want with one." He spat, companionably, into the ash-pit and climbed up to his footplate, and a few minutes later the whistle went, a long human sound that startled half the children in the crowd into tears and the other half into cheering, and the train drew out.

The spoil that came up out of An Sloc Mòr's deeper galleries that year was a grey stone veined through with black, no use for building until someone thought to try it, and Uisdean mac Ùisdein's house, which held the concession, was the someone who thought to try it. A quarry was cut into the hillside above Achadh dhomhain before the year was out, and the stone, dressed and squared, went south on the same rails the coal went south on, sold under a mark cut into each block, a stylised wave for Taigh Mhic Ùisdein, so that a man in Cathair dhearg walking past a new wall could read, if he knew the mark, where the stone under his hand had come from. It built well and it built cheap, and within a few years half the new work going up in the south's public buildings was raised in it. Iain, who cut nothing and quarried nothing, only chained the faces the quarrymen went on to strip, thought little of the mark that first year he saw it stamped into a block on a wagon: a mountain gave up stone as readily as it gave up coal, once a house had found a way to sell both.

## IV. Of the Sidings and the Spur

Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn, which had its office at Cathair dhearg by then, a low building of the same grey Mac Ùisdein stone, laid new sidings the following year at Muileann chrom, where the line from An Sloc Mòr met the older line down to the ports. It was there that the coal trains were broken up, their wagons sorted by the port they were bound for and made up again into new trains, and it was there, too, that a new kind of town grew up beside the old one, a scatter of low rows built for the families who worked the small shunting engines that did the sorting.

Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn counted these families apart from the rest of Muileann chrom's people, on a roll of their own kept separate from the shire's, and housed them apart as well, in the rows nearest the lines where the noise and the smoke of the yard never let up, day or night. It was not spoken of as a punishment, and no one on Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn would have called it one; it was only, the minute said, that a yard family's hours did not keep a shire's hours, and a roll that tried to hold both would serve neither well. Iain passed through Muileann chrom twice that year, carrying survey rolls between An Sloc Mòr and Cathair dhearg, and saw the rows from the carriage window both times, and both times thought that a family counted apart long enough forgets, after a while, that it was ever counted any other way.

On the second of those passings he had an hour to wait for the onward train and walked out along the rows himself rather than sit in the station house, and fell into talk with a shunting-engine driver named Murchadh who had worked the humans' small engines since he was a boy of twelve and had never once, in thirty years, been asked his name by anyone from the shire office at Muileann chrom proper, though he could have walked there in a quarter hour. Murchadh was not bitter about it, or did not show it if he was; he said only that a man who worked nights sorting wagons had no time for a shire's business and no great wish to make time for it, and that the yard looked after its own better than the shire had ever looked after the yard. Iain thought that a strange kind of pride to take in being left alone, and said nothing of it, and went on to catch his train.

In the same season Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn opened a spur off the western branch to Àth leathan, a gathering of pits and shanties in the hills where Tuathaich hewers had worked the coal since before the kingdom had troubled to draw a road to them. The spur was short, thirteen miles of new-laid rail over ground Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own surveys called poor, and it was built, the minute recorded, because the coal at Àth leathan was cheap to lift and the carters' road to it was worse each winter than the last. Iain never walked it himself. He heard of it from a hewer at An Sloc Mòr whose brother had gone to Àth leathan for the wage and wrote home, twice a year, in a hand the hewer could not read and had to carry to a neighbour with his letters to have read to him.

## V. Of the Oath on the Coal

The custom of binding hewers by charter, which the humans had never troubled to keep and which the island itself had not kept since An Aois Naomh, was taken up again that autumn, in a changed form. The old form, so far as anyone at An Sloc Mòr could reconstruct it from the custodians' copies of An Aois Naomh rolls, had bound apprentices of whatever blood a master chose to take; the new form, as the council's charter set it down, bound Dia-thìrich alone, and said so in its opening clause without troubling to give a reason, as a thing too plain to need one.

Comann nan Gualadairean was chartered at Achadh dhomhain on a bright, cold day in an t-Samhain, in the yard before the pithead, with the whole of the town's Dia-thìreach families turned out to see it and most of its Tuathaich families kept to their work, the charter having said nothing of a holiday for them. A table was set up under an awning against the weather, and the charter was read aloud twice, once by the maor's clerk and once, because a good half of those present could not follow the clerk's court Dia-thìris, by a hewer named Aonghas who had learned to read it from his own father and rendered it plain. Then the men who were to be bound came forward, by families, eldest first, and each in turn laid his hand flat on a lump of coal set on the table for the purpose, black and rough-edged and no different to look at from a thousand others cut that same week, and swore the oath the charter gave him: to keep the guild's custom, to take no work outside it, to teach no craft of it to a hand not sworn.

Iain stood at the table's edge with Lachlann's rolls under his arm, having been sent down from the galleries to carry the day's survey to the maor's office once the swearing was done, and watched the line go past him, man after man, his own father among them near the front of it, laying a hand that had cut coal for eleven years on a lump no bigger than a fist and speaking words that made him, from that hour, something Iain was not and could not be made. Iain had been taken on by a surveyor, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's roll was the only one that carried his name; the charter, when the clerk read its closing clause, said plainly that its rolls were open to those who cut, hauled or timbered the coal, and named no place in them for one who only measured where it lay. No one told him to stand back from the table. He simply understood, watching his father's hand come down flat on the coal and hearing the oath said in a voice steadier than he had ever heard his father use for anything else, that the table was not his to come to, and stood where he was, at its edge, with the rolls under his arm, until the swearing was over and the crowd began to break up toward the ale that had been laid on in the hall.

His father found him there afterward, said little, being a man of few words at the best of times, and put a hand on Iain's shoulder for a moment, the same hand that had just sworn the oath, before going off to the hall with the other sworn men. Lachlann, who had come down from the galleries himself to see the charter read, found Iain still standing by the table a while later and said, not unkindly, that a chainman's name went into Comhairle nam Mèinnean's book, which was a kind of oath too, if a colder one, and that the mountain kept no better account of a man than the figures he left in it. Iain said that a cold oath was still not the one his father had sworn. Lachlann did not argue the point, having, it seemed, no argument to make against it, and the two of them walked back up toward the galleries together while the hall behind them filled with noise and firelight.

Iain walked back to the surveyors' hut alone once Lachlann had turned off toward his own lodging, in the last of the light, past the pithead where the new sidings ran black and empty in the cold, and wrote that night in his own small book, apart from Comhairle nam Mèinnean's, the thing he had watched and where he had stood while he watched it. He did not write why it had mattered so much to stand there. He did not, that year, have the words for it, and the book, in any case, kept better company with what a man had seen than with what he had felt about seeing it.

The saying the hewers had for the mountain, which Iain had heard a hundred times that year without troubling to write it down, he set down now for the first time, at the foot of the page, because it seemed, of a sudden, to fit more than the mountain: *Cha d' fhuair a' bheinn fois a-riamh.* The mountain never got rest.
