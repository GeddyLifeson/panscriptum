# Of the Fair at Muileann òg

*Part Four: Of Fair, Crown and Survey*

*SE 21 an Cèitean – SE 22 an Lùnastal*

> *A' chiad fhèill, agus a' chiad riaghailt.*
> The first fair, and the first rule.
>
> — entered in Oighrig nic Mhathain's own hand, at the head of the fair's book

## I. Of the Bell at Caol leathan

There had been a house of the Stone at Caol leathan since before any clerk now living could put a first date to it, a low stone hall behind the harbour with the western Seann Skell's own scorpion nowhere on it and nothing of the state's arms either, only the Stone's own mark cut plain over the door: a thing older, the priests there said, than either. In SE 21 the house was built up anew at the order's own cost, a low tower raised over its western gable and a bell hung in it that had been cast, the priests said, from coin given by every ship that had ever put in at Caol leathan's small harbour and come home again. It was not a large bell, nor one that could be heard much past the town's own walls, but Caol leathan was a small place and did not need much heard past its walls: the bell rang the hours from that year on, and the market opened by it, and the two courts the town kept, the harbour court and the fair court, sat by it, and within a year nobody in Caol leathan troubled any longer to ask a neighbour the time, only whether the bell had gone yet.

The Library, setting the fact down some years after, had little more to say of it than that: a bell raised, a tower built, a town's day set by it where no day had been set by anything but the tide before. It was the kind of fact that filled a line in a chronicler's book and no more. The fair that opened that same autumn some twenty-odd miles to the east took its hours from no bell at all, only from the voice of the woman who kept it.

## II. Of Oighrig, Market Clerk of Dùn chrom

Oighrig nic Mhathain was one-and-thirty that year, and had kept the weighing-scales of Dùn chrom's own small market for nine of them, a walled town of some size on the road east out of Cathair dhearg, its citadel old and its market square older, where three days in five she sat behind a table with a set of the council's own weights before her and told carters and hucksters, in a voice that had by then long since stopped troubling to be gentle about it, what their sacks and their barrels actually held against what they claimed for them. She had come to the work at two-and-twenty, a widow's daughter with a head for figures and no dowry worth a better match, and had made of a post that most who held it did indifferently into one that Dùn chrom's own maor had come, in nine years, to trust further than he trusted most of his own clerks: she caught short weight that better-paid men missed, and she wrote her findings down in a hand plain enough that a magistrate could read it without asking her to stand and explain it twice.

It was for that plainness, more than for anything else she had done, that the council's own factor for the eastern shires came to her table one market day in an Cèitean of SE 21 and asked whether she would take a harder post than weighing sacks: the state had resolved, on the maoir's own asking and the merchants' louder one, to hold a fair each year at the ford by Muileann òg, the old meeting-place where the Stone King Raghnall Ceannfhionn was said to have traded salt for wool with the holder of the Red Hill in an age no living clerk could put a year to, and it wanted someone to keep it who could weigh a sack, read a scale, and write a rule down so plainly that a carter three shires off could understand it without a clerk to read it to him. Oighrig asked what the post paid and was told, and asked what it answered to and was told that, and asked, last, what happened to whoever held it if the fair went wrong in its first year, and was told, honestly enough, that nobody yet knew, there having been no fair of this size held anywhere on the island since before the Severance for anyone to learn the answer from. She took the post anyway, on the reasoning she gave her mother that evening, that a woman who had spent nine years catching other people's short weight might as well spend the next few finding out what her own came to. Her mother, who had raised her alone on a widow's small pension and a stall of dried fish in the same market square, heard the whole of it out before she said anything, and then said only that Oighrig had better learn to write faster than she wrote now, if a whole fair's dues were to go through the one hand of her rather than through the several that a town's market usually spread its weighing among. It was, Oighrig thought afterward, the only piece of advice her mother ever gave her that she found herself, years on, still following to the letter.

She told the maor the following morning, before she told the council's own factor she would take the post, on the grounds that Dùn chrom's market had gone nine years without a bad weight under her table and deserved to hear from her own mouth, rather than from a rider's, that it was about to go without her altogether. The maor heard her out, said he had half expected it since the factor first asked after her at the spring assize, and asked only who she meant to leave behind her that he could trust to catch what she had caught. A niece of her own, a quick-handed girl of seventeen who had stood at her elbow two summers running, was the name she gave him, and the maor, who had learned in nine years to take Oighrig's word on a scale without asking to see the proof of it, took her word on the niece the same way and never afterward had cause to regret it.

She rode west to Muileann òg within the fortnight, a town of some few dozen souls and no more, its only distinction the ford and the old meeting-stone beside it, and found there a field, a track worn into it by generations of carts that had never yet had a fair to carry to, and nothing else that a state building a fair from nothing could be said to have given her: no hall, no scales but the ones she brought herself, no clerk but her own hand. She spent the summer walking the ground with a length of cord and a stick, and marked where the stalls would stand, where the beasts would be penned apart from the cloth and the grain, where the road from Cathair dhearg would need widening before the first cart came down it heavier than the track had ever carried, and where, at the field's one corner nearest the ford, a court could sit under a roof if the state saw fit to build her one before autumn. It did, in the end, though later than she asked for it and smaller than she had marked out, a plain roofed frame open on three sides that the carpenters of Dùn chrom finished in the last week of an t-Sultain, a fortnight before the day set for the fair to open.

## III. Of the Rules Written in Her Own Hand

The council's word came to her at the end of that same summer, carried by the same factor who had first found her at her table, in the form of instructions rather than a rule already made: that the fair, once opened, should draw its dues in coin and not in kind, that it should have its own court to settle what quarrels a gathering that size would bring rather than send every dispute back to a maor's bench three days' ride off, and that Tuathaich goods, of whatever kind, should be sold at the fair only through Dia-thìreach factors licensed for the purpose, the council having judged, the letter said, that a trade so new was better kept for its first years inside hands the state already answered for. It did not tell her how to write the rule, only that it was to stand, and left her, as such letters generally did, the whole of the wording and none of the deciding.

She sat with it three evenings in the roofed frame at the field's corner, by a rush-light, before she set a word of it down, and what she wrote in the end she wrote plainly, in the same hand she had used nine years for catching short weight, on the grounds that a rule nobody could misread was worth more than a rule dressed finer: a stall-fee of one penny in the realm-coin for every ell of frontage taken, paid at the setting-up and not after; a court of the fair, *Cùirt na Fèille* as she headed the page that opened its own book, to sit each day of the gathering under her own roof, to hear any quarrel of weight, price or theft between traders and settle it there rather than send it south; and, last, that goods brought by a Tuathaich seller to the fair were to be entered at a factor's stall and sold under the factor's own name, the seller taking his price from the factor and not from the buyer direct. She wrote that third clause last of the three, and read it over twice before she let it stand, knowing, as she told nobody at the time but wrote once, years after, in a letter to her own niece, exactly what it would mean for the first northern cart that tried to sell past it: that the fair she had marked out with a length of cord in an empty field would, from its very first year, be a place where a Tuathaich seller's goods passed through another man's hand before they passed through the buyer's, and where the difference between the two prices went into a purse that was never the seller's own.

The council's licences went out to four merchant houses of the eastern shires before the harvest was in, each given the right to stand a factor's stall at Muileann òg and take goods of the north to sell under its own name for a share of the price, the houses chosen, so far as Oighrig could tell from the letter that named them, more for the coin they had already put toward the fair's road than for any particular fitness to the trade. She entered the four names in her own book on the same page as the rule itself, under a heading she gave it without being asked to give it one: *Bàillidhean na Fèille*, the fair's factors, a name that has stood in every year's book since.

The eldest of the four, a wool merchant of Cathair mhòr named Ruairidh mac Aonghais who had put more of his own coin toward the widening of the fair's road than any of the other three, rode out to Muileann òg himself in an t-Iuchar to look over the ground before his stall went up, and found Oighrig still marking out the field with her cord and stick. He asked her, civilly enough, whether the third clause of her rule was hers or the council's, having heard, he said, two different accounts of its authorship from two different clerks at Cathair dhearg. She told him it was both, the council's in its substance and hers in its wording, and he allowed, looking out over the empty field where his own stall would stand come autumn, that a rule's wording mattered a good deal less to the men who profited by it than to the men who did not, and that she would find, in his experience of licensed trades, that the two kinds of men rarely agreed on which the wording had been kind to. He was not wrong, and Oighrig, who had already guessed as much three evenings running by rush-light, told him only that she had written it to be read the same way by both kinds of men, which was the most a rule could honestly promise to do.

## IV. Of the First Fair

The fair opened on 16 an Dàmhair in SE 21, a bright cold morning with the frost still on the field's grass when the first carts came down the widened road from Cathair dhearg, and by noon Oighrig's court had heard its first case, a small one, a dispute over a barrel of salt fish claimed short by the buyer and full by the seller, which she settled inside the hour by the simple expedient of having both men stand by while she weighed it herself against her own scales, the seller's claim proving the truer of the two. Only those who had stood in the crowd that watched it would ever remember the case itself. It was the first the court of the fair had heard, and Oighrig wrote it into her book in the same plain hand as everything else, on the grounds, she told the clerk who asked her why she troubled with so small a thing, that a court's first case set the pattern for every one after it whether anybody remembered the case itself or not.

A harder case came the third day, a Cathair mhòr coal-carter who swore a Sloc Mòr merchant had sold him spoil dressed to pass for good coal, the two being near enough alike to a buyer's eye by lamplight and near enough different on a smith's hearth by morning that the carter had driven the better part of two days back to the fair rather than swallow the loss. Oighrig had no smith's forge under her own roof to settle it by, and settled it instead by sending both men, and the load itself, to the one smith Muileann òg's own handful of houses could produce, an old man who had shod carthorses there the whole of his life and had never before been asked to judge a stranger's coal, and who told her, an hour later, that the load was three parts good stone to one part spoil, no more and no less. She fined the merchant the value of the one part and let the rest of the sale stand, and wrote into her book that a smith's eye, where the fair kept none of its own, would serve the court as well as any clerk's scale until the state saw fit to send her one.

The fair kept, besides its cases, a noise and a smell that Oighrig's own book had no line to hold: woodsmoke and wet wool and the coal carts' own black dust settling over everything by the second week, pipers from Baile chiar playing for coin at the field's far corner, and under it all the low constant argument of buying and selling in half a dozen shires' worth of accents, which she came, in that first fortnight, to find she could stand in the middle of and pick apart into its separate threads the way she had once picked apart a barrel's true weight from a seller's claimed one, a skill she had not known she had until the fair gave her cause to use it.

Trade came that first year from every province the state held, wool from the western shires and the Sloc Mòr's coal from the east and cloth from Baile chiar's looms and salt from the coastal pans, more of everything in one field than Oighrig had seen gathered anywhere in nine years of market days, and she spent the fortnight the fair ran on her feet from before light to past dark, settling weights, hearing cases, and writing, each evening by the same rush-light, the day's dues into a book that by the fair's last day ran to more pages than she had thought to bring. It was, by every measure the council troubled to ask her for afterward, a success beyond what anyone attached to it that first autumn had thought to hope: the dues gathered came to more than the fair's own building had cost, the court settled every case brought to it inside the fortnight without once sending one south, and the four factors' stalls did the trade the council had licensed them to do, buying wool and cloth and salt fish off northern carts at a price the factors set and selling it on, under their own names, for a price the factors set again.

## V. Of David Hale's Wool

It was on the ninth day of that first fair, by Oighrig's own count in her book, that a cart came down the Cathair dhearg road from the north rather than from any of the state's own provinces, driven by a young man of perhaps eight-and-twenty who gave his name, when her clerk asked it at the gate, as David Hale of Caol mhòr, and his load as wool, four sacks of it, off his own father's sheep and the sheep of two neighbours besides, brought the better part of a week's journey on the reasoning, he told the gate clerk plainly enough, that a fair drawing trade from every province the state held ought to draw trade from the Moot's provinces too, there having been no word sent north, so far as the Moot's own minute-book showed, that it should not.

Oighrig came to the gate herself when the clerk sent for her, having by then learned, in nine days, that a case worth her own eye was worth fetching her for rather than settling wrong by a clerk who did not know the rule as she had written it, and found a cart of good wool and a driver who had clearly never before had cause to read a licence, there having been none to read at Caol mhòr's own small market where he had sold his father's wool his whole life without one. She read him the third clause of her own rule from her own book, in the same plain hand she had written it in, and told him, as plainly as the rule itself was written, that the wool could enter the fair only at a factor's stall, under the factor's name, at whatever price the factor judged to give for it. David Hale asked, reasonably enough, where the difference went between what the factor gave him and what the factor got for it after, and Oighrig, who had asked herself the same question three evenings running by rush-light before she ever wrote the clause down, told him the truth of it rather than soften it, that the difference went to the factor and to the state's own licence upon him, and that this was the rule as the council had sent it to her to write, not as she would have written it unasked.

He stood some while at the gate with his cart behind him, four sacks of wool that had cost him a week's journey and would now cost him, besides, whatever the nearest factor judged fair to take off the top of it, and asked her, last, whether she had written the rule herself. She told him she had, in her own hand, on the council's instruction and her own wording both, and that she saw no profit in telling him otherwise. Oddly enough he thanked her for that much plainness at least, turned his cart to the factor's stall she pointed him toward without further argument, and sold his wool there for a price the factor set, a price David Hale judged, by his own reckoning that evening and the reckoning he carried home to Caol mhòr after, some fifth part short of what the same wool would have brought sold direct at any market the Moot's own provinces kept. He did not come to the fair again that autumn, nor the next, though the lesson of that turning at the gate stayed with him a good deal longer than the fifth part he lost by it, and it was some years before another cart came down from the Moot's provinces to try the gate a second time.

He carried the wool's price home to Caol mhòr inside the fortnight, and told his father what the gate had cost him before he told him anything else of the fair, laying the fifth part's difference out on the table between them in coin the way a clerk lays out a reckoning he expects to be asked to justify. Walter Hale, who had by then sat the Moot's chair the better part of twenty years and had read a good many licences in that time without ever once finding one written by the hand that had to answer for it afterward, asked David only one thing more than the sum itself: whether the woman who had turned him from the gate had seemed, in the turning, glad of the rule or sorry for it. David thought a while before he answered, and said at last that she had seemed neither, only plain about it, which his father allowed, turning the coin over once in his own hand, was very likely the truest answer a rule's own writer could give a man it had just cost a fifth part of his summer's wool, and worth remembering the next time the Moot sat down to write a letter about her rule rather than about her.

## VI. Of the Road, the Wall, and the Rule Read Aloud

The winter that followed brought the fair's road its finishing, the last stretch of it metalled past Dùn chrom in am Faoilleach of SE 22, the carters' own name for the new road already settled before the state ever gave it one of its own, Rathad na Fèille, the fair's road, though the carters who used it hardest had, by then, another name for the toll taken at Dùn chrom's own gate to pay for it, a name Oighrig heard once, passing through on her way east, and did not trouble to write down. The toll built, over that same winter, a new wall along the town's southern side, the wall the townsfolk called the fair's wall and the carters called by their own name for the gate that took the toll for it, and by an Lùnastal of SE 22 the wall stood finished, the first stone of Dùn chrom's own that the fair, rather than the state or the custodians before it, had paid to raise.

The Moot's letter reached Cathair dhearg some time in that same winter, carried, as its letters generally were, by a carter who had it from Walter Hale's own hand, and it named the factor rule plainly for what the Moot judged it to be: a state licence laid across a trade the north had carried on unaided since before the Severance, and a fifth part of every northern sack's worth handed, by that licence, to men who had done nothing to the wool but stand between the grower and the buyer with the council's own paper in their hand. It asked, as such letters generally asked, that the rule be looked at again. The council's answer, when it came, came slowly and said little: that the rule stood as licensed, that the fair's first year had shown it workable, and that the matter, having been considered, would not be reconsidered inside the year. The letter was filed at Cathair dhearg in the same box as the Moot's earlier letters, on the same shelf, in the same clerk's hand.

Oighrig herself never saw the Moot's letter, the council having no cause to send a toll-mistress a complaint written against a rule she had only been asked to word, and she learned of it, when she learned of it at all, from the same clerk who brought her the council's confirmation that spring, a young man not long in the office who mentioned it in passing, as one piece of the winter's business among several, and seemed faintly surprised that she troubled to ask him what the letter had said rather than merely that one had come. She asked him to read her the line about the fifth part twice over, and wrote it down afterward in her own book beside the rule itself, on a page she kept for nothing else, a habit her own clerks took, in the years that followed, for simple thoroughness and never once thought to ask her the reason for.

Oighrig heard of the council's answer at the fair's second opening, on the same field, by the ford, in an Dàmhair of SE 22, though her own chapter of the matter had already closed some months before that, in an Lùnastal, when the council's clerk who carried its confirmation to her read the whole of her own rule aloud before the assembled factors and the gathered maoir of the eastern shires, word for word as she had written it the autumn before by rush-light, stall-fee and court and the third clause together, and asked her, when he had done, whether the reading matched her own book. She told him it did, word for word, and watched the four factors nod at a rule they had had no hand in writing and every profit in keeping, and thought, though she did not say it aloud to the clerk or to anyone else that day, that a thing read aloud in the same words it was written in did not thereby become any less her own doing for the reading, whoever else's coin the reading was meant to serve.
