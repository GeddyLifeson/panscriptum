# Of the Wall that was Not Built

*SE 10 an Giblean – SE 11 an t-Samhain*

> Cha tig iad a-deas.
> They do not come south.
>
> — the fourth line of the council's minute on the wall, {{date:V-0047}}

## I. Of the School Reported

The letter came to Cathair dhearg in an Giblean, carried by a rider who had come down the western roads from Cnoc ghorm in three days rather than four, and was proud of it. It was two pages, closely written, and it said that the church built the autumn before had opened, inside its own walls, a school for the priests who would serve it: a room with benches, a shelf of books sent up from the south, and a master brought from Seann Skell to teach the humans' tongue and the reading of the Dia-thìris scripture the western preachers used, there being no other text thought fit. Nine boys sat its first term. It was, the letter said, the only place in the whole of the north where either tongue was taught to anyone at all.

Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill read it twice at her table in the under-clerks' room before she took it in to the council, once for its sense and once, more slowly, for what it left unsaid. Nowhere did it say who paid for the master's board, though she could guess; nowhere did it say where a boy who learned his letters was to use them, there being no place in Cnoc ghorm that asked for either tongue; nor did it ask the council for anything, which in her eight years at the table had come to strike her as the strangest thing a northern letter could do. Most asked. This one only told, the way a man tells a neighbour a fact about his own field, wanting only that the fact be heard.

She was thirty-seven that spring, an under-clerk of the eighth year, set to the reading of letters before the council saw them and the fair-copying of what the council decided after, and she had learned already that the second of those tasks was easier than the first, because a decision, once made, had a shape you could follow with the pen, while a letter, before it was answered, could be read a dozen ways and she had not yet learned which of them the twelve men and women at the table would choose. Standing in the corridor outside the council chamber with the day's other business under her arm, she read the letter a third time, and thought that a school teaching two tongues to nine boys, in a town whose church was not a year finished, was either the smallest thing the north had done that year or the largest, and that she could not yet tell which.

She read it into the record without comment, as an under-clerk was meant to, and the council heard it without much comment either, one councillor asking only whether the master's stipend came from the see or from the town, and being told it came from both, in shares nobody present could name exactly. The letter was filed. Gormshuil wrote *received and noted* at its foot in a hand she had spent eight years making plainer than it was, and turned to the next paper in the stack, which was Seumas mac Coinnich Ghlais's, and which was not so easily filed.

## II. Of the Wall Proposed

Seumas mac Coinnich Ghlais sat at the council's table for the custodian houses of the east, a broad, careful man of Ceann àrsaidh who had held his seat eight years and spoken, in most of them, less than any other councillor there, so that when he rose that morning the room gave him the particular quiet it gave a man who did not often ask for it. He had brought a map, an unusual thing for the table; most of the council's business came as paper, and paper could be filed, but a map had to be looked at, and looking at it meant the room had to agree, for as long as the looking lasted, on where the lines it showed actually ran.

His proposal was plain enough once he had unrolled it. A boundary, fixed and marked, along the line where the shires of the settled country met the shires the Tuathaich held: not a wall in the humans' old sense, stone to the shoulder and a walk along the top of it, for no one present thought the treasury could raise stone thirty miles, but a lesser thing, a ditch and a bank and stones set at the mile to say where the ditch ran when the ditch itself had silted, so that a maor riding his shire's edge, or a farmer disputing where his fence should stand, would have one line to point to rather than the memory of an old drover or the say-so of whoever had ploughed there longest.

He gave his reasons carefully, the way a man gives reasons he has turned over a long time before he trusted himself to say them aloud. The Tuathaich shires, he said, had grown quicker than the census three years before had reckoned; the mill-towns of the north filled faster than its numbers allowed for, and a line that had seemed wide enough for two peoples to keep apart of their own custom might not stay wide enough forever. Better, he said, to mark it now, while it could be marked without offence, than to leave it to be marked later by a dispute nobody in this room would care to sit in judgment on.

Gormshuil, writing the minute, kept his words as near as her hand could follow them, which was her whole office and no part of her opinion, and she found, listening, that she could not fault the reasoning even as she found herself hoping the room would refuse it. She had grown up hearing the word Tuathaich said the way her mother's people said it, without heat, a name for neighbours who kept their own feasts and their own roads and had never, in her own lifetime or her mother's, come further south than the old drove tracks the maps still showed. A wall, even a ditch and a bank, seemed to her a strange answer to a peace that had held eight years already without one.

The debate that followed ran the better part of two hours, longer than the council usually gave any one matter, and Gormshuil's hand ached from it before it was done. A councillor of the western shires spoke for the proposal, saying a marked line was a kindness to both sides of it, since an unmarked boundary was a boundary only the strong could rely on, and the Tuathaich, having no seat at this table to speak for themselves, might in time be glad of a line the Crown had agreed to keep. Another, of the coastal shires, spoke against it on grounds nearer the treasury than the heart: that a ditch thirty miles long, surveyed and dug and marked at the mile, would cost more than the year's whole revenue from the north, for a peace that had cost nothing at all so far. A third said only, when her turn came, that she had ridden the drove tracks herself as a girl and had never in her life needed telling where one country ended and the other began, the change being plain enough in the roofs alone, in the smoke, in the dogs that barked at a stranger's coat, and that a bank of earth would tell a traveller nothing the land did not tell him already.

It was the last speaker, an old man of the northern shires himself, near enough the border to have buried a wife whose grandmother had been Tuathaich before the Severance closed the choosing, who said the thing that Gormshuil, writing it down, understood afterward to have decided the matter, though he said it quietly and without any plain claim to be closing the debate. They do not come south, he said. Not the drovers, not the walkers, not the families that had stayed on the Crown's side of the old truce line and taken the Rite. In eight years, not one shire south of the tracks had brought him a complaint of a Tuathaich crossing where he was not wanted, and he did not see the use of digging a line to keep out a people who had never once tried to cross it.

The council took no formal vote, the custom of the table being to let a matter stand or fall by whether anyone still pressed it once every voice had been heard, and by the time the old man had finished no one pressed it further, Seumas mac Coinnich Ghlais least of all, who rolled his map without visible disappointment and said only that he had thought it worth the asking, which, in the manner the council kept its records, was as near to a withdrawal as any proposal there ever received.

Gormshuil wrote the minute that night at the under-clerks' table by a single candle, the room otherwise empty, and found the writing of it harder than the sitting through it had been, because a minute was meant to be short and the debate had not been. She cut it, in the end, to four lines: that the boundary was proposed; that it was found needless, the two peoples keeping apart already of their own will; that the cost was weighed and set against the need; and, last, because she could think of no shorter way to say what the old man's words had actually settled, the sentence he had spoken, unaltered from his mouth. *They do not come south.*

She carried the fair copy to the table the next morning for the council's hands, as was the custom for any minute touching a matter of weight, and five of the twelve wrote their initials under it without pause, the old man's among the first. Seven did not, which she had expected, a councillor's silence on a matter already dead being no more than good manners toward a colleague who had lost a debate; what she had not expected, reading the seven names again after the table had emptied, was that not one of them had written a word against it either. No dissent was entered, and no one asked that the matter be raised again. Nor had any vote ever been called against the wall; a room had simply stopped speaking of it, and the wall went unbuilt on the strength of that silence alone. Gormshuil, copying the sheet a second time before she filed it, thought it a strange way for twelve people to decide anything, and a stranger way still for it to work.

She kept that second copy, though she had not planned to; she had cut a spare sheet from the same stock only because her first draft had blotted, and it was only after the fair copy was filed and the blotted one lay ready for the fire that she found she did not want to burn it. Smoothing it and folding it instead, she carried it home, and that evening, at her own table in the room she shared with two other under-clerks, she began a book: a small one, bound in plain board by a binder near the council's house who asked no questions of a clerk buying paper, its first page nothing but the folded minute pasted flat and, beneath it, in her own hand, the date and no more. She did not tell her clerk-master she had begun it. She could not, that first night, have said herself what she meant it for, only that a room's silence, eight years into a kingdom, seemed to her a thing worth keeping somewhere other than a filed sheet no one but a clerk would ever read again.

## III. Of the Walk to Allt an Àigh

Her mother came to the capital twice a year, at the turning of the seasons, and stayed a week each time in the room above a chandler's shop that had been theirs since Gormshuil was small, before the girl had a bed of her own at the council's lodgings and her mother had a house of her own two days' ride east, in a village near Ceann àrsaidh where the old custodian families still kept, alongside the Rite the state now taught, an older reckoning of their own. Her mother was of an Old Spirits house, one of the last in that shire to hold to it openly, and had never made a secret of it to her daughter nor much of one to anyone else, the council's law having found nothing in the old walking-faiths worth forbidding so long as they forbade nothing of the Crown's own in return.

That summer her mother's visit fell, by chance or by her mother's own choosing, in the week before the walk to Allt an Àigh, the holy well in the east whose water the old tellings said would bring good fortune to whoever drank it in the right quarter of the moon, and she asked Gormshuil, as she asked most years and was most years refused for want of leave, whether the council could spare its under-clerk a fortnight. That year, for reasons Gormshuil did not examine too closely at the time, the council could, the summer business being light and her clerk-master owing her, by his own reckoning, more days than he had ever paid.

They walked it together on foot, the way the walk was meant to be walked, four days out from the capital on the eastern road and a fifth along a drove track her mother knew from girlhood, past barley going gold and hedgerows loud with bees, sleeping two nights at farms that took walkers in for a coin or a prayer, whichever was offered, and one night at Achadh gharbh, where the village was raising a hall for the walkers.

The hall was not yet finished: a long low building the village was raising with its own hands, timber and turf, roofed that summer and still wanting the great hearth meant for its middle, with pallets already ranged along both walls. It was going up because the village had grown tired, its keeper told them over the evening fire, of finding walkers asleep in its barns and cattle-byres every autumn and had thought it easier in the end to build them a barn of their own. The keeper-to-be was an old woman with a ledger she was plainly proud of, a great cracked-spined book begun that very summer, in which she wrote, in a careful uneven hand, the shire and the name of every walker who slept a night under her roof, and she showed it to Gormshuil unasked, turning back page after page of names, telling her that it held travellers from better than twenty shires already and would hold fifty before the decade was out, north and south and east and west of the island both, the walk to Allt an Àigh being, so far as the old woman knew, the one road on which so many different shires' feet had ever worn the same track together.

Gormshuil watched her mother's face while the ledger was shown, and saw in it something she did not, that year, have words for, though she found words for it later, writing in her own book by candlelight after the walk was done: that her mother's faith, kept quietly in one shire for three generations, was written here into a stranger's ledger as plainly as a maor's tally of heads, and that the writing of it, rather than shrinking the thing, seemed somehow to have made it larger, as though a fact set down in another's hand carried further than the same fact kept only in memory.

They reached the well on the sixth day, a clear spring rising through pale stones in a fold of the land with nothing built over it but a low cairn some earlier walker had raised and every later walker added a stone to, so that it grew, the keeper at Achadh gharbh had told them, a little taller every year. Her mother knelt and drank first, cupping the water in both hands the way she had taught her daughter to do as a child, and Gormshuil, kneeling after her, found the water cold and very clear and tasting, she thought, of nothing at all beyond what water tastes of, and drank it anyway, watching the light move on it the way the old tales said it moved, in a sheen of colours that might have been the water's own trick of the pale stones beneath it or might, as her mother believed without ever quite saying she believed it, have been something else.

They walked the eastern road back together as far as Ceann àrsaidh, where her mother turned north to her own village and Gormshuil south to the capital, and at the parting her mother said only that she was glad the walk had been given her, this year of all years, and asked nothing further, not what the council's business had been that autumn nor why her daughter had come home from it a little quieter than she had gone. Gormshuil did not tell her of the minute, nor of the book she had begun to keep, not that year and, so far as any record shows, not for some years after; but she carried the folded page in the same satchel that had carried her drinking-cup to the well, and she thought, walking the last miles alone, that a line written to keep two peoples apart and a well walked to by fifty shires together were, in some way she had not yet the words to set down properly, answers to the same question, given in the same summer by two different rooms of the same kingdom.

## IV. Of the Sloc Mòr

She was back at the council's table before the month's end, and the business waiting for her there had none of the quiet of a well in a fold of the eastern hills. The mine had been the humans' before it was anyone's, a shaft sunk two generations back into a seam that ran the length of the central hills, worked day and night under a foreman's roster the humans had left behind them along with the shafts themselves, and the council, in the ten years since the Severance, had done little with it beyond keeping the roster running and taking what came up. That spring the surveyor's report that came to the table said, in the flat language surveyors used for things that would have startled anyone reading them plainly, that the yield of the mountain had come back to what it had given under the humans, and in the month just past had passed it.

The report went on to say what the council already half knew, that the working had not changed at all; the same faces were cut, by the same methods, at the same pace the humans had set, and the only thing new about the mountain's yield was the hands the profit of it now filled. Where a foreman's ledger had once gone east across the sea, it went now to a Crown clerk's chest at Cathair dhearg, and of what it held the council decided, that same sitting, to keep a fifth part for the Crown and grant the rest whole to whoever took on the working from here, the state having neither the men nor, several councillors said plainly, the wish to run a mine when a subject house could be found to run it instead.

The house found was Uisdean mac Ùisdein's, of Cathair mhòr, whose family had held an overseer's post under the humans at the very shaft now in question, and who came to the capital himself that spring to put his name to the grant, a broad, sure-voiced man in his middle forties who spoke of the mountain the way another man might speak of a field he had ploughed since boyhood, familiarly, without any great show of the honour being done him. The terms he signed were, Gormshuil noted, copying the grant fair for the record, the humans' own terms almost to the letter, a fifth to the Crown and the rest to the house that worked it, the rosters and the rates of pay carried over unaltered from ledgers the humans themselves had kept. Only the names at the top of the page had changed. She wrote the grant out twice, once for the council's own chest and once for Uisdean mac Ùisdein to carry home, and thought, setting down his name a second time in the same careful hand she had used for Seumas mac Coinnich Ghlais's minute the year before, that a kingdom not ten years old seemed readier, in the end, to copy an old arrangement than to make a new one, so long as the copying put the profit where the copying's makers wanted it.

A board was seated that same season to see the grant kept to its terms, three clerks and an engineer set at a table in a back room of the council's own house at Cathair dhearg, close enough to the chamber that Gormshuil, carrying papers between the two, came to know its members by their first names before the winter was out. It had no grander name at first than the mine's own board, and kept, in its early months, no records grander than a shift-roster and a weekly count of what came up the shaft, but it was the board, small as it was, that the council now answered to on any question touching the mountain, Uisdean mac Ùisdein himself, for all his grant, sending his own returns to it rather than to the council directly, the way a tenant answers his own landlord and not the landlord's lord.

## V. Of the Night Shift and the Blessing

The one term the council did not alter, in granting the mine away, was the roster, and it ran day and night both, as it had run under the humans, the mountain given no season and no hour it was not worked. A councillor asked, at the sitting where the grant was confirmed, whether the night shift ought not to be ended now that the working was the island's own and not a foreign company's, the mountain having earned, in her phrase, a right to the rest its old masters had never allowed it; the board's own engineer answered that the yield the council had just heard reported, the very number that made the grant worth granting, came in no small part from a shaft that never stood idle, and the matter was set aside rather than decided, in the same way, Gormshuil thought, writing it down, that the wall had been set aside the year before: not refused outright, only left, by a room that had heard both sides and found neither worth the cost of a plain answer either way.

The custodians of the vein, whose houses had kept the old duties of the shafts since long before the humans came, entered their protest that same month, a short paper carried down from the mountain by one of their own number and read into the council's record without much debate: that the working without rest broke a custom older than the humans' grant and older, the paper said, than the Crown that had now confirmed it, and that the custodians asked only that the protest stand written, the shift's ending being, they knew by then, no longer the council's to give back, so that no one after could say the breaking of the old custom had gone unmarked. Gormshuil copied it as carefully as she had copied the wall's minute, and found, reading it back to herself that evening, that she could not have said which of the two papers, the councillor's four lines or the custodians' half-page, asked the smaller thing of the room that received it, and got, in the end, the same answer from it: the fact allowed to stand, and nothing changed by the standing.

She rode east herself before the winter, sent with the council's own copy of the grant to be read at the shaft-head before the assembled hewers, a formality her clerk-master could as easily have sent another for, though she never learned, and did not ask, why he chose her. The mountain in winter was a grey country, its lower slopes cut with the black scars of the old workings and its upper ones already under the first snow, and the shaft-head, when she reached it, stood lit day and night as the reports had said it did, lamps burning at the pit-mouth against a darkness that would not, that season, lift from the mountain's face for more than a few hours together.

She read the grant aloud from a plank set up before the assembled men, her breath showing white in the lamp-light, and watched the custodians of the shaft, four of them in the grey coats their office wore, stand a little apart from the hewers while she read, their protest already filed and answered by the same silence that had answered the wall. When the reading was done and the men had gone back to the shift, the custodians did the thing she had come, without quite admitting it to herself, half hoping to see: they walked to the pit-mouth themselves, the four of them together, and spoke over it the words of an old blessing she did not know, in a low unhurried Dia-thìris she followed only in patches, calling on the mountain, so far as she could make out the sense of it, to hold and to give and to be, in its own time, allowed to rest.

She stood at the edge of the lamplight and watched them do it, and thought, watching, of the old man's words in the council chamber the year before, *they do not come south*, and of the custodians' own protest, entered and answered by nothing, and of the ledger at Achadh gharbh growing a name longer every year while the line that might have marked a border went undrawn; and she understood, standing there in the cold with the shaft's lamps burning behind the four bent grey-coated backs, that a kingdom could refuse a wall and grant a mine in the same season, could let one people walk where it pleased and work another mountain past the hour its own custodians asked it be let stand, and count both, in the flat language of its own records, as peace kept. The custodians finished the blessing and stood a moment longer at the shaft's edge with their heads bowed, and then, without any further word to the clerk who had come to read them their own dispossession, turned back to the mountain's business, and the lamps burned on at the pit-mouth into the falling dark, as they had burned the night before and would burn the night after, without pause, and without needing, any longer, anyone's leave to do it.
