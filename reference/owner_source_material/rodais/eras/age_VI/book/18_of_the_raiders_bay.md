# Of the Raiders' Bay

*SE 19 an t-Sultain – SE 20 an Lùnastal*

> *To the council at Cathair dhearg: we ask a second time for what we asked in the winter, being a boat with guns
> in it, or leave to arm our own. We have had no answer to the first asking. We do not think a third will find you
> any less occupied than the first two were.*
> — Nell Barrow's second letter for Cathair gheal, carried south by the maor's own rider and read once

## I. Of the Boats Lost off Cuan bheag

The storm that took Thomas Coyle came out of the west on the second night of an t-Sultain, in SE 19, with no more warning than a wind that had been rising since noon and a glass that the older men of Cuan bheag had been watching all afternoon with the particular silence of men who have seen a glass do this before and not liked what followed. Cuan bheag was a small place, a few miles up the coast from Caol mhòr, its harbour no more than a shingle beach and a stone jetty the humans had built and the town had never had cause nor coin to better, and its boats went out, as they had always gone out, in a fleet together, on the reasoning that a boat in trouble within sight of six others stood some chance of being seen. That night eleven men from seven of those boats did not come home, and among them was Thomas Coyle, a fisherman of Caol mhòr who had married into a Cuan bheag boat some years before and gone out of that harbour ever since, one-and-forty that year and married to Mary Coyle for near twenty of them.

Of the seven boats, the town could piece together only from what the sea gave back over the following week: two hulls, both broken, one oar with a name burned into the loom that nobody in Cuan bheag recognised and that turned out, when word of it reached Caol mhòr, to belong to a boat out of a village further north still. No lifeboat put out after them, because Cuan bheag had none, having never had the coin to build one nor, until that night, the occasion to feel the want of it as more than a thing other, richer harbours kept and it did not. The town buried what it had to bury within the week, in the Church's ground above the shingle, eleven stones cut in the same fortnight by the one mason Cuan bheag could call its own, working from first light to past dark on a debt no one had asked him to take on and that no one, afterward, offered to pay him for.

Mary Coyle heard of it at Caol mhòr two days after, from a carter who had it from a Cuan bheag man selling fish at the Caol mhòr quay and telling it, as such men do, to anyone who would stand still long enough to hear it told. She went up the coast road on foot that same afternoon, arriving after dark and finding no one at the harbour able to tell her more than what she already knew, which was that Thomas's boat had gone out and had not come back, and that of the six men who had crewed it only one had washed ashore, and it was not him. She stayed in Cuan bheag the better part of a fortnight, in a dead woman's spare room lent her by a neighbour, and helped where help was wanted, which was mostly in writing: the mason needed the names spelled correctly for his stones, and more than one of the widows could not write them herself and had never before had cause to see her husband's name set down anywhere but a boat's own gunwale, painted on by his own hand. Mary wrote all eleven out on a single sheet, in the order the boats had gone out in rather than the order the sea had given the men back, because that, she told the mason when he asked why, was the order in which they had last stood together, and it seemed to her the truer one.

Word of the wreck reached the Moot's house at Caol mhòr within the week, carried this time by the same carter, and Walter Hale, who was that year two-and-sixty and had been speaker of the Moot for seventeen of them, called a meeting of the Moot's own council on a Tuesday evening and put the matter to them plainly: Cuan bheag had no lifeboat, no village on that stretch of the coast had one, and the sea did not appear to be growing gentler with the years. The Moot voted, that same evening, to open a subscription for a lifeboat to be kept at Cuan bheag and worked by whichever crew of the town's own men would take the training for it, and Hale himself opened the list with five coins of the realm out of his own purse, entered in his own hand at the top of a sheet that by the month's end carried close on two hundred names, fishermen and farmers and the weavers of Doire ghlas alike, each giving what a poor coast could spare, which for most households was not much and for some was a day's wage entire.

The Moot's letter asking the council to bear half the cost went south with the same carter who had carried its letters south for years, and came back, four months later, with the council's answer written under the Moot's own question in a clerk's hand: that the council would meet a third of the sum, the matter having been considered by the twelve and the sum thought fair against what other calls on the treasury the year had brought. Hale read the answer aloud to the Moot without comment and without complaint, both having by then, as he told Ellen that evening, worn themselves out on lesser matters than this one, and the Moot made up the remaining sum from its own subscription list and a further asking that same winter, so that by am Màrt a boat had been ordered from the yard at Cuan shean, built to a plan the yard already had in hand for the humans' own harbour service, and paid for in full before its keel was ever laid.

The boat came up the coast under her own sail in an Cèitean, a squat white-painted thing with cork lashed along both gunwales in the humans' own fashion, and was put into the water off Cuan bheag's own jetty on a fair morning before most of the town, the mason among them, his eleven stones long since set and weathering. No priest of either the Church or the old Dia-thìreach faiths troubled to bless her, there being no agreed form for blessing a thing neither faith had built before, and the town settled the question by having the five widows of that autumn's wreck, Mary Coyle among them for the day though she had by then gone back to Caol mhòr, walk her down the shingle together and see her set afloat, on the reasoning, which nobody afterward disputed, that if any hands on that coast had earned the right to launch her it was theirs.

## II. Of the Thing off the Cape

Up the same north-western coast, past Cathair gheal and beyond it, lay a headland the fishermen called, among themselves, the cold cape, for the wind that came off it every month of the year but two, and below it a huddle of summer bothies kept by the fishermen of Achadh àrsaidh, a village of the middle country far to the south whose men had gone north each season, time out of mind, to fish waters that few other boats troubled, the ground there being poor and the currents ill-tempered, and they had, in consequence, told among themselves for longer than any living man could number, a tale of a thing that swam those same waters: a creature longer than any boat that had ever gone looking for it, seen always at a distance and never at the same distance twice, that some named the Sea-Watcher and some would not name at all, holding that a thing was less likely to trouble you if you had not troubled to give it a name of its own.

That autumn the tale was told, as it had been told before, to a chronicler who happened to be at the bothies on other business entirely. Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, the kingdom's own royal chronicler not two years and six-and-forty that season, was on her way back south from the same circuit that had carried her clerks and their forty chests out of the southern shires that spring, and had stopped a night at the bothies below the cold cape because the tide would not serve to carry her further that day. The bothies had shown her, when she asked, no parish book worth the copying and no grave-roll older than the last flood, and she had all but given up hope of the visit's earning its own night's lodging when an old fisherman of the place, who had fished the cold cape's waters the whole of his life and lost a brother to them, offered, over the one fire the house could spare a stranger, to tell her a tale instead, on the plain grounds that a chronicler ought to want tales as much as she wanted rolls, whatever her office called the difference between them.

He told it at some length, and with the particular pleasure of a man who has told a good tale many times and knows exactly where its pauses fall best: the shape seen always at a distance and never at the same distance twice, longer than any boat that had gone looking for it, that some in the village named the Sea-Watcher and some would not name at all, holding that a thing troubled you the less for not being given a name of its own. Gormshuil asked him, when he had done, how many men of the bothies had seen it with their own eyes, and he answered, without any offence at the question, that he did not know a man alive who had, only men who had known men who had, which was, he added, exactly how the tale had come to him and exactly how he expected it to go on from him; and at that, almost as an afterthought, he told her something else, that the flagless ships which had worked those waters some years now, the ones no honest boat cared to meet, seemed to know the cold cape's currents better than any stranger crew had a right to, and kept, he thought, some hidden anchorage among the rocks below it that no Dia-thìreach chart had ever marked.

Gormshuil wrote both things down that same night, in the same entry, the tale of the creature and the fisherman's word on the raiders together, and sat some while afterward deciding whether the two belonged in one book or two. She had by then kept the chronicle long enough to have settled, mostly, what its business was and was not: it was not her office, she had decided, to say whether a creature thirty feet long swam those waters or did not, only to say that a fisherman out of Achadh àrsaidh had told her so, on such a night, in such words, in the book that kept what people said. The raiders' anchorage she judged a different matter, being a thing a sailing master might use rather than a tale a fireside kept, and she wrote it a second time, separately, with a note that the council's own coastal watch be told of it, though she held out small hope, from what she had seen of the coastal watch's dealings with that coast already, that the telling would move it to do anything the coast had not already had to do for its own people. She sealed both pages before she slept, and rode south the next morning with the tide, carrying, among the parish books she had not been given, the one tale she had.

## III. Of the Flocks Counted and the Two Requests

Out past the cold cape, in the open sea to the west, lay Eilean dhubh, greatest of the outer isles, a place of grass and wind and more sheep than people, that the council's head-due had never yet been able to count with any confidence, no royal clerk having troubled, before that year, to cross to it in person. One was sent that same autumn, a young clerk new to the treasury's service and not greatly pleased at the crossing, which the islanders warned him would be rough and which was rougher than warned, the boat that carried him standing three hours off the landing before the sea would let it in at all. He found, once ashore and his stomach settled enough to speak, that the islanders had no more wish than he did to have a stranger stand about their pastures counting beasts that were not his to count, and offered instead to count the flocks themselves, by their own tally-sticks, which every household on the island kept notched to its own count and none had ever had reason to falsify against a due nobody from the south had come to collect in living memory, and hand him the sum entire, sworn to before the island's own headman. The clerk, who had crossed a bad strait to do a count he had no great appetite for doing twice, and who could see, besides, no way to check eleven hundred sheep across four townlands inside the fortnight the crossing allowed him, took the offer gladly, wrote the sworn sum into his own book exactly as given, and sailed home on the next fair tide grateful to be asked no more of it. The dues that year, and every year after, were set upon the island's own count, given once and never afterward disputed by either side, each having got, in its own way, exactly what it wanted of the arrangement: the council a figure it could enter in its books, and Eilean dhubh a treasury that troubled it, at most, once in a generation.

It was into that same autumn and the winter that followed it that the two requests went south from Cathair gheal, a walled town on that same stretch of coast, its harbour a shallow bay below the town wall that the flagless ships had by then worked for the better part of six years without any hand from the south troubling to move against them. Nell Barrow, a skipper's widow of the town some two-and-thirty years old that winter, had taken it upon herself, in the way of a woman with time on her hands and a grievance she did not care to sit on, to write the town's case out in full and carry it to the maor's own door for his seal and his rider. She had lost her husband to no raider, his boat having gone down in ordinary weather some years before this tale begins, but she had lost, in the six years since the flagless ships first came, a nephew taken off a boat within sight of the harbour mouth and never seen again, and the town's patience, along with her own, had by then worn thin enough that she no longer troubled to soften her letters for a council she did not expect to answer them gently in any case.

The first letter went south in am Faoilleach of SE 20, asking plainly for a boat armed and crewed at the council's expense, or failing that a licence for the town to arm and crew its own, the town having neither guns nor law to carry them and wanting, in Nell Barrow's own words, one or the other before it wanted anything else the council might think fit to send it. The maor read it over twice before he sealed it, and told her, not unkindly, that a request framed so plainly was as likely to be filed unread as answered, letters that gave a clerk no soft phrase to copy back being, in his experience, letters a clerk found easiest simply to set aside. Nell Barrow answered that she had written a good many soft letters in her time and had a drawer at home to show for it, every one of them answered in the same soft coin it was written in, which was to say not answered at all, and that she would try the plain kind once before she troubled to write a third of either.

No answer came within the two months she gave it, and in am Màrt she wrote the second letter, the one that has come down whole, the maor having kept a fair copy of it in his own office before sending the original on, on the reasoning, he told Nell Barrow plainly, that a letter written in that temper might not survive its own journey south undamaged by whatever hand first read it. It asked the same thing the first had asked, in fewer words, and ended, as she had written it, with the line that the maor copied out exactly and that has been kept ever since: that the town did not think a third asking would find the council any less occupied than the first two had. No answer came to the second letter either, that spring or after.

## IV. Of the Bay Cleared

The flagless ships came into Cathair gheal's bay in force on the night of 8 an Giblean in SE 20, three hulls where the town had grown used to seeing one or two at most, standing off the harbour mouth at dusk in a manner that gave every fisherman on the wall no doubt at all of what the night meant to bring. Nell Barrow was at the harbour when the watch first called it, having taken, since the second letter went unanswered, to walking the wall most evenings on the plain grounds that a thing seen early was a thing met on better terms than a thing met in the dark unlooked-for, and she sent a boy running through every street of the town for any man who owned a boat and anything that could be swung: a gaff, a gutting-knife, an old sword some grandfather's grandfather had carried off a battlefield two ages gone and that had hung over a hearth ever since doing nothing more warlike than gathering soot. She herself took down, from its nail beside her own door, the boat-hook her husband had carried the whole of his working life and that she had not touched since the day it came home without him, and found, hefting it in the dark of her own passage, that her hand still knew its balance better than it knew any weapon the town could have found her in its place.

What followed that night has been told in Cathair gheal more times than any single telling could now untangle from the rest, but the plain bones of it, as the maor's own report set them down within the fortnight, ran thus. Seven boats put out from the town before midnight, crewed by whatever hands could be found and armed with whatever the town could put in them, the tide low enough that every man among them knew the bay's own shallows better than any stranger crew ever could, raiders included. The larger of the three raider hulls, a black-sided ship the fishermen had for years called by a name of their own that the maor's report does not give, stood in closer than her own master judged the water, meaning to land a boat's crew on the quay itself, and ran her keel onto a bar that had shifted, that same winter, further out than any chart still above water on that coast had troubled to mark; and there she sat, fast and listing, while the two lesser hulls stood off in the dark uncertain whether to come to her or leave her.

The seven town boats closed on her before she could work herself free, coming in low and quiet with their oars muffled in sacking, as fishermen of that coast had muffled oars against a shy shoal since before any of them could say how the trick was first learned, and the fight, when it came, was closer and shorter and less orderly than any account of it afterward quite managed to make it sound: men going hand to hand along a listing deck in the dark, by lantern-light where a lantern held and by none where it did not, with gaffs and gutting-knives and, in one case that the maor's report set down without further comment, an old battlefield sword swung by a miller's son who had never before that night held anything sharper than a scythe. It cost the town two men dead outright and four more hurt before the black hull was cleared, one of the four losing the use of a hand he did not afterward recover; but by the time the black ship burned to her waterline, fired by her own boarders once her hold was found to hold nothing worth the saving, the two lesser hulls had already stood out for open water rather than come to a fight that plainly was not going as their own master had reckoned it would, and neither was seen off that coast again that year. Nell Barrow came back to the harbour wall at first light with a burn along one forearm, taken from a fallen lantern rather than from any blade, that she carried the mark of for the rest of her life, and told the town, before she would trouble to have it looked at, that the bay was clear; and clear, for that season and the two that followed it, it stayed.

## V. Of the Fine Paid

The council's answer to the night's work reached Cathair gheal before an Cèitean was out, and it was not the answer the town had spent six years asking for. The maor's report of the fight, forwarded south along with Nell Barrow's account of it and a plain list of the dead, came back with a ruling attached that the town of Cathair gheal had borne arms without the council's licence, contrary to the old rule that no Tuathaich town might arm its own boats without leave first asked and first given, and that the town was fined four purses of the realm-coin, to be paid at the maor's own office before the year's dues fell due. No word came, in the same letter or any after it, of a boat sent, a licence granted, or a guard set upon a bay that had needed one for six years and had at last, at some cost, made its own; the ruling's four lines named the offence and the sum and nothing besides, in the same hand and on the same kind of sheet, the maor afterward remarked to no one in particular, that had once fined a Ros bheag boat-builder for launching a hull a foot over its licensed beam.

Word of the fine reached Caol mhòr before an t-Iuchar was a week old, carried, as most such word was, by a Cuan bheag man selling fish at the quay who had it in turn from a carter out of the north, and Walter Hale, hearing it read aloud at the Moot's own table, said nothing for the length of time it took the clerk to finish reading and then asked only how much the town still lacked. It lacked, by the maor's own reckoning, some two-thirds of the sum, Cathair gheal being a poor town and its harbour trade thin that summer on account of the very raiders it had just driven off, the fish that had not been taken in six bad years being fish that brought in no coin toward a fine for taking them back. Hale carried the difference north himself within the fortnight, out of the same purse the Moot kept for such calls on it and that had, that year, rather less slack in it than he would have liked, the lifeboat at Cuan bheag having already taken more of it than the subscription alone had raised.

He came to Cathair gheal on a wet morning near the end of an t-Iuchar and found Nell Barrow, her forearm healed to a scar she did not trouble to hide, already at the maor's door before him with what the town itself had managed to scrape together, so that between her sum and his the fine was paid in full that same morning, coin by coin, in front of the maor's own clerk, who wrote the amount received in his ledger and said nothing at all about the six years, the two letters, or the boat the town had not been sent. Hale asked the clerk, as he watched the coins counted, whether the council's own ledger carried any line for what the bay had cost the town before that night as well as what it had cost the council after it, and the clerk, a decent enough man doing an office he had not chosen, answered only that his ledger carried what he was given to enter and no more, which Hale allowed was very likely true and no fault of the clerk's own making. The town, he said as he pocketed the last receipt, had already kept its own ledger of that cost, in stone and in scar, and needed no clerk to enter it a second time.

Far to the south that same season, at Cathair mhòr, the house of Mac Ùisdein raised a market cross at its own cost, cut on all four faces with the house's own name, and moved the town's wool and coal markets to stand at its foot, where they have stood ever since. The council's minute on the matter, when it was read at Caol mhòr in an Lùnastal along with the news of Cathair gheal's fine now settled, ran a good deal longer than the minute on the fine had, and named the sum the house of Mac Ùisdein had given a generous one. Mary Coyle, who had by then been back at Caol mhòr the better part of a year and had taken, since the wreck, to sitting at the harbour wall most evenings where she could see the boats come in, heard the two minutes read one after the other at the Moot's own table and said only that it seemed the council knew a great deal about crosses, and rather less about lifeboats, before she went back to counting the boats home.
