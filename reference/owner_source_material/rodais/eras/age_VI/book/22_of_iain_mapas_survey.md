# Of Iain Mapa's Survey

*SE 26 am Màrt – SE 27 an t-Iuchar*

> Received of Iain mac Ruairidh, called Iain Mapa, one survey of the faces of An Sloc Mòr, rolled and tied, this day. Filed.
>
> — Comhairle nam Mèinnean's receipt, {{date:V-0106}}, entire, as an Leabharlann renders it

## I. Of the Faces of the Sloc Mòr

Iain mac Ruairidh was thirty-two years old and had spent the better part of a year on his knees, or bent double, or flat on his belly on stone that was always either wet or freezing, with a lamp on a string round his neck and a chain that two other men held taut while he called the figures back to them over his shoulder. He was a chainman by his first trade and a surveyor by Comhairle nam Mèinnean's later reckoning, and he had come up through An Sloc Mòr the slow way, gallery by gallery, learning the mountain's faces the way another man might learn a woman's, by long acquaintance and no great ceremony about it. He thought of himself as careful rather than clever, which Comhairle nam Mèinnean valued more, or said it did.

What he had found, over that year, he had not gone looking for. He had gone into the eastern galleries with the ordinary work of the season, to true up an old survey against the new digging and settle a dispute between two overseers about where one man's cutting ended and another's began, and he had come out of it with a set of figures that would not lie flat no matter which way he turned them. The vein was thinning, badly nowhere yet: to the east it ran narrower than the old maps showed, and to the south it pinched again in the same proportion, as if the mountain had made up its mind about something years before and only now got round to telling anyone.

He checked it twice before he let himself believe it, because a man who tells Comhairle nam Mèinnean a thing it does not want to hear had better be right, and a third time because rightness alone, in his experience, was protection for no one. Working the eastern faces again at speed, chain out to its full length and back, in the wet cold of the spring with water finding its way down the back of his collar no matter how he stood, he got the same figures each time, to within an inch the chain itself could account for, and set them down in the plainest words he had: thinning to east and south, in a hand that did not shake, and did not argue.

The whole of it he drew fair on a single long sheet, rolled and tied with the plain string Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own store issued for the purpose, and carried himself the thirty-one miles from An Sloc Mòr to Cathair dhearg rather than send it by the carters, for a thing that mattered, in his experience, travelled better in a man's own hand than in anyone else's.

He had told no one at An Sloc Mòr what the figures showed, not the two men who had held his chain for him nor the overseer whose dispute had set him onto the eastern galleries in the first place, though both had asked, in the ordinary way men ask a surveyor coming up out of the dark what he has found down there. He had said only that the old maps wanted correcting, which was true as far as it went and told them nothing they could carry off and repeat, and he had told himself, walking the road south with the roll under his arm and the weather coming up cold off the hills, that this was caution and not cowardice, though he turned the distinction over more than once before he reached the capital and did not come to rest on it either way. The road ran two days by cart or a day and a half on foot at a hard pace, and he took the second, sleeping the one night at a carters' inn on the road where nobody asked his business and he offered none, and came into Cathair dhearg on the morning of the second day with the roll dry under his coat and his boots ruined by the last mile of it, which had run wet the whole way.

## II. Of the Receipt

Comhairle nam Mèinnean sat that season in three low rooms behind the old counting-house at Cathair dhearg, the same rooms it had sat in since Cead an t-Sluic Mhòir was first granted, with a single window between the three of them that let in less light than a man might have wished for reading survey work. Iain was shown in by a clerk who did not ask his business, because his business was written plainly enough on the roll under his arm, and he stood in front of a table at which three men sat, none of whom he had met before, though he knew all three by name the way a chainman knows the men who sign his wages.

He laid the roll on the table in front of them and untied the string himself, because it seemed to him the courteous thing, and waited for one of them to unroll it. None of them did. The man in the middle, who was older than the other two and dressed a shade better, put two fingers on the near edge of the sheet, as if to hold it down against a wind that was not blowing in that room, and left it at that.

"You've come far," he said, which was not a question and did not want an answer.

Iain gave him the short of it anyway, because he had walked thirty-one miles to say it and meant to say it whether it was asked for or not: the vein was thinning to east and south, the figures were on the sheet, the old maps wanted correcting, and a man ought to know it who was deciding where the next year's galleries went. He said it plainly, the way he had written it, without dressing it in anything worse or better than what the ground had told him.

The three men looked at the roll, and then at each other, and not very long at either.

"It will be considered," said the one in the middle.

Iain had walked thirty-one miles carrying a thing he believed, and he had thought, on the road, about a good many ways this hour might go, most of them worse than this one, some of them shouting. He had not thought about this one, a short sentence and a closed door behind it, and it took him a moment to find his own voice again, and when he did it was smaller than he had meant it to be, though it did not waver.

"I should like a receipt," he said.

The clerk at the side table looked at him as a man looks at another who has asked for something unaccountable in that room; no one told him no, and after a silence in which the three men at the table seemed to consult each other without a word passing between them, the youngest of the three nodded at the clerk, who took up a pen, wrote a few lines on a scrap no bigger than his own palm, and passed it across for the man in the middle to sign. He did not. He passed it on to the third man at the table, a broad, unhurried man named Ruairidh mac Ailein, who read what was written, found nothing in it he cared to argue with, and signed his own name under it without comment, in a hand a good deal quicker than the clerk's.

*Received of Iain mac Ruairidh, called Iain Mapa, one survey of the faces of An Sloc Mòr, rolled and tied, this day. Filed.* Nothing more. No word of what the survey said, no word of thinning or of east and south, nothing that a man reading it afterward, with no other knowledge, could have told from what it had cost to get.

Iain folded it once and put it in his pocket-book, where it lay against the ribs on the walk home closer than the coin he carried, and he thanked the room, which did not seem to expect thanks and did not offer any back, and went out past the clerk into the ordinary daylight of Cathair dhearg's street, which struck him, for a moment, as a strange place for nothing at all to have happened in.

The roll stayed on the table when he left it, and by the account an Leabharlann keeps of that room's business it was filed within the week, in a press with the two years' surveys before it, and shown to no one. Within the month Iain mac Ruairidh was told, without much explanation offered and none asked for, that he was wanted at the silver works at Muileann chiar, twenty-four miles east and a little south, where Comhairle nam Mèinnean had need of a man who could hold a chain straight and did not ask more questions than the work required.

He asked, once, of the clerk who brought him the posting, whether the move had anything to do with the survey he had carried in himself the month before, and the clerk, a younger man than the three at the table and not unkind, said only that he could not say, which Iain took to mean that the clerk could say and had chosen not to, and did not ask a second time. He asked nothing at all of Ruairidh mac Ailein, whom he passed in the corridor on his way out of the counting-house that same week and who nodded to him civilly enough, as one working man nods to another whose trade he has some respect for, and said nothing of the receipt he had signed, and Iain said nothing of it either, and the two of them went their ways down the same corridor without either mentioning the thing that stood between them, which was, Iain thought afterward, very much the manner of Comhairle nam Mèinnean in everything it did.

## III. Of the Rule Made at Cathair mhòr

Leabharlann Muileann chaol tells the rest of that year in fewer words than it gave to the receipt, and it is worth setting down what little it does say, because Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own account of itself, where Comhairle nam Mèinnean troubled to keep one, is thinner still.

Comhairle nam Mèinnean had sat at Cathair dhearg since Cead an t-Sluic Mhòir was first cut, close by the counting-house whose rooms it borrowed and the mint that struck the coin its shafts paid for. In am Faoilleach of the following year it moved its clerks and its presses the sixteen miles to Cathair mhòr, into rooms taken on lease above the town's own wool market, on the stated ground that a board charged with the mountain's business ought to sit nearer the mountain than it had. No one who wrote of the move at the time troubled to explain why sixteen miles nearer counted for so much, when thirty-one had done well enough for the years before, and an Leabharlann, which asked the question of every clerk still living who might have answered it, records only that none of them thought the question worth much of a reply.

With the move Comhairle nam Mèinnean set down, in the plainest of its own minutes, a rule that had not existed in writing before that year, though men who had dealt with Comhairle nam Mèinnean a long while said afterward that it had always been the practice and only now got its own sentence: that the surveys of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own men were to be kept at Cathair mhòr, in Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own presses, and were not to be sent on to Leabharlann Muileann chaol as the reports of other offices were sent, by the standing order that had governed the state's paper since Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill first asked every shire for its books. The rule gave no reason for itself beyond Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own convenience in having its working papers to hand, and it was written in four lines, and it was never once, in all the years it stood, brought before the council to be confirmed or struck down, because no one outside Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own rooms had reason to know it existed at all.

An Leabharlann, setting this down at the age's close with every paper the state ever sent it laid open on the table, notes only this: that from the year the rule was made until the year it was at last broken, Comhairle nam Mèinnean's surveys of An Sloc Mòr's faces went nowhere but into Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own presses, and that among them, filed and never circulated, lay the sheet Iain mac Ruairidh had carried thirty-one miles and a receipt had been the whole of his payment for.

No one on the council was asked to confirm the rule, and no one on the council thought to ask after it, for Comhairle nam Mèinnean's business had always been counted a small, dry corner of the state's affairs, more like a mill's own account book than a matter of law, and the men who sat on the council in those years had grain and roads and Fèill Muileann òg to occupy them, and little patience left over for a mountain that had, on the face of things, gone on giving as freely as it always had. The wool market's factor let the rooms to Comhairle nam Mèinnean gladly enough, for the rent was steady and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's clerks were quiet tenants who kept regular hours and troubled the market's own business not at all, and if he ever wondered what papers Comhairle nam Mèinnean kept upstairs that could not travel the sixteen miles on to Muileann chaol with every other office's, he left no word of the wondering behind him.

## IV. Of Small Trades and a Forbidden Night

Not every year's business was the mountain's. At Seann Vell on the capital shore, in those same seasons, the glass-houses that had lately begun blowing small flasks found a trade nobody had planned for them: the women of the town took to filling the flasks with waters they distilled themselves from bog-myrtle and meadowsweet and the honey pressed from heather, and selling them by the dozen to carters bound for the market towns of the western plain. Within a year or two the plain's own towns had taken the trade up on their own account, so that a woman travelling that country could buy the same scent from three different hands in three different towns and be told, by each, that it was made nowhere but where she stood. It was a small thing, and an Leabharlann sets it down chiefly because it happened in the same year as larger things that left less behind them: a magistrate's fine, a boat kept for a relic, a survey filed and forgotten. The glass-houses of Seann Vell are still standing, and the small trade in scent still runs from them, longer-lived by a good margin than the rule Comhairle nam Mèinnean made that same year and never told the council of.

Further north, at Baile chrom on the far cape where the lighthouse stood, the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane had for some years kept Oidhche Glaise in the open, on the headland below the light, as no keeper before her had kept it. In the summer of that same year a Dia-thìreach magistrate new to the district, who had heard the vigil described to him as an unruly gathering by men who had never troubled to watch one, forbade it as disorderly and had Hannah Crane brought before him and fined. She paid what she could of it herself and could not pay the rest, and word of the fine went south with the wool carts and reached Tional nan Tuathach before the summer was out. Tional nan Tuathach, which had by then grown used to paying for things the north's own purses could not quite stretch to, voted the balance of her fine out of its own small chest without much debate, on the motion of a councillor who said only that a woman fined for sitting on a headland in the cold and saying nothing to anyone was not, in his judgement, a threat Tional nan Tuathach need trouble itself to agree with.

The vigil did not stop. It went indoors, into a byre lent for the purpose by a family who kept the old rite themselves and asked no questions of those who came to it, and it was kept there, one night a year, in a silence the magistrate's order could not reach any better than it had reached the shingle, because a rule made against a sound cannot be made against its absence. Outsiders were still not told which night it was. An Leabharlann, which has asked, is not told either.

## V. Of the Boat Kept for a Relic

Far to the south, on the coast Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol had scoured clean a generation before, the harbour at Cuan dhearg was opened again that same season, with a new mole of dressed stone laid where the old one had gone under the water in a single night. The masons who built it were largely the sons of the men who had lost the first harbour, and more than one of them said, working the stone, that they were laying it heavier than their fathers had, against a wave that had not troubled the coast again in all the years between, and that nobody in Cuan dhearg had stopped thinking about all the same.

The first boat warped into the new harbour was not a new boat at all. Among the hulls that the wave had cast up into the fields behind the old town, and that had lain there through more than twenty years of grass growing up around their ribs, one had been found sound enough, when the harbour-master's men went out to look at what the fields still held, to be dragged back down to the water and caulked and floated. It went in first, ahead of every boat built new for the rebuilt harbour, and the harbour-master, a woman named for her own grandmother who had drowned in the wave without ever seeing a harbour again, kept it afterward as a relic rather than putting it back to work, mooring it at the head of the quay where every boat coming in or going out passed close by it. The fishermen of Cuan dhearg came to call it simply Bàta an Achaidh, and told strangers, if they asked, that it had come home the long way round, by way of the barley, and taken its time about it.

## VI. Of Muileann chiar

Iain mac Ruairidh had come to Muileann chiar at the start of the summer the receipt was given, with his chain and his lamp and the pocket-book that held, among its other pages, the receipt Comhairle nam Mèinnean had given him, and he found the silver works a smaller business than An Sloc Mòr and a stranger one, the veins running in thin bright seams through rock that gave up its secrets more readily than coal ever had, if a man knew how to read the seams at all. He was set to measuring the eastern faces against the old plans, much as he had been set to the same work at An Sloc Mòr, and the overseer who took him on said only that Comhairle nam Mèinnean had sent word he was careful, which was, the overseer added, more than Comhairle nam Mèinnean generally troubled to say of anyone.

The town itself he found smaller too, and quieter, built along a single burn that the silver-washers used and that ran, by the season, either a clean brown or a faint grey depending on how the works upstream were going, and the guild that held the silver had kept its charter through the humans' time and into the restored kingdom without much interruption to its own ways, so that the men he worked beside spoke of the vein as a thing with moods of its own, to be coaxed rather than merely measured, in a manner the coal men at An Sloc Mòr would have thought soft. He did not think it soft, once he had spent a season at it. The silver ran truer to the old plans than the coal had, faces holding where the maps said they would hold, and there were weeks that spring and into the summer when he went a full day without once thinking of the sheet he had carried to Cathair dhearg, which he counted, when he noticed it, a kind of rest.

It did not last the whole season. A letter came to him the following summer, forwarded on from An Sloc Mòr by a clerk who thought he might want to see it, from one of the two men who had held his chain for him the year before, asking, in a rough hand and rougher spelling, whether the eastern galleries had been given up yet, since the overseer at An Sloc Mòr had begun talking of opening new ground to the west instead, where the going was harder but the yield, by the overseer's own reckoning, steadier. Iain read it twice and wrote back a short answer that said he had no way of knowing what Comhairle nam Mèinnean meant to do with the ground he had surveyed, which was true, and did not say what he privately believed, which was that Comhairle nam Mèinnean meant to do nothing with it at all beyond keeping it filed, because a mountain that has been told, in writing, that it is running out does not stop giving simply because the men who own it have chosen not to read the letter.

He did not speak of the survey he had left behind him, not to the overseer and not to the men who worked the faces alongside him, and if any of them wondered why a chainman with a name for care had been moved on so quickly from a working as large as An Sloc Mòr to one so much smaller, none of them asked him to his face, and he offered nothing. What he did, on his first free evening at Muileann chiar, in the room he had taken above a cooper's yard, was to take the pocket-book out and lay the receipt flat on the table under the lamp, and read it through once more, though he had it near enough by heart already, and then he took a pin from the small tin he kept for mending his own coat and pinned the scrap of paper into the back leaves of the book he kept his working figures in, where it would not fall out and could not easily be lost, and where, he thought, it would still be found by whoever came after him, whether that was Comhairle nam Mèinnean or no one at all.

He did not write beside it what it meant to him, because he was not a man who wrote such things down even when he thought them, and the figures on the sheet he had carried to Cathair dhearg said, as far as he was concerned, everything that needed saying about the vein and about the mountain and about what Comhairle nam Mèinnean had chosen to do with the knowing of it. But he kept the receipt pinned in his book from that evening on, through every posting Comhairle nam Mèinnean sent him to afterward, a small square of paper that said only that a thing had been received and filed, and nothing at all of what the thing had been, and he carried it, in the years that followed, the way another man might carry a debt he meant, sooner or later, to see paid.
