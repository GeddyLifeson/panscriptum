# Of the Seventeen Suits

*SE 15 an t-Iuchar – SE 16 an Giblean*

> A law made exact is a law made for someone. I have never pretended otherwise, and I have not been asked to stop.
>
> — Eilidh nic Leòid, at the inn of {{place:burg:143}}, the night of {{date:V-0068}}

## I. Of the Court in the Heat

Ceann mhòr in an t-Iuchar smelled of tar and low tide and the fish-yards behind the harbour wall, and by the second week of the month the smell had climbed the hill to the old magistrate's house and settled into every room of it, so that the clerk who kept the maor's rolls there had taken, that summer, to propping every window on the seaward side and weighting his papers with whatever came to hand, a stone, an inkpot, a boot. Eilidh nic Leòid arrived on the Tuesday of the assize week with Murchadh Clàrc riding a length behind her, as he had ridden behind her for seven years now, and found the house's largest room already too warm to sit in with the shutters closed and not warm enough, once they were open, to keep the flies out.

She was nine-and-fifty that summer, and had ridden her own circuit of the south-west eight times, but never the northern one, which the council had added to her charge that spring, and never in such heat. She said as much to Murchadh while the room was set, the long table carried in from the maor's own house and set crosswise under the window so the light fell across it rather than into her eyes, the bench for the suitors arranged along the far wall where the draught, such as there was, would reach them first. "We will want water brought in through the morning," she told him, "and not wine, whatever the maor's clerk offers us by way of courtesy. A justice who drinks at her own bench in this heat will be asleep by the second cause and cross by the fifth." Murchadh, who had learned over seven years of riding behind her exactly which of her instructions wanted an answer and which wanted only a note taken, wrote it down and said nothing, and went to find the water himself rather than trust the maor's clerk to remember it.

The town's maor, a Dia-thìreach man named Griogair mac Ailein who had held Siorrachd Ceann mhòr for eleven years and lived, all that time, in the house the last human magistrate had kept, met her at the door with the courtesy due her office and a list of the assize's causes already fair-copied twice, and told her, walking her through to the room where the bench was set, that he had never in his eleven years seen so full a list brought before one sitting. "Nineteen causes," he said, "and of them seventeen the same kind of paper, near enough, that a man could read one and guess the shape of the rest before he opened it." He said it with the particular weariness of a man who had spent some years hoping the matter would settle on its own and had at last given up the hope, and Eilidh, taking the list from him and running her thumb down its length before she had even taken off her travelling cloak, told him only that she had come to read every one of the nineteen to its own end regardless of how much the rest resembled it, and that a paper's likeness to its neighbour was not, in her experience, a reason to read it any less carefully, whatever hope a busy maor might have of the opposite.

The room filled slowly through the Tuesday morning, the suitors of the first cases arriving early and standing about the yard in their best coats long before the bench was ready for them, the two Dia-thìreach households among the nineteen keeping somewhat apart from the seventeen Tuathaich ones by an old habit of the town that nobody troubled to explain and nobody, that morning, troubled to break. Among the second group, standing a little further back than the rest and dressed no better nor worse, Eilidh marked a grey-haired man in a coat cut in the humans' old fashion, a plain dark thing gone thin at the elbows, who carried no paper of his own and seemed, from the way the others near him spoke, to have come to watch rather than to plead. She did not know him by sight, and asked Murchadh, in a low word, whether he did. Murchadh did not either.

She opened the sitting a little after the noon bell, standing at the head of the long table with the window's light behind her and the room's heat already thick enough to see, faintly, in the air above the flagstones, and told the room, in the plain unhurried voice Murchadh had heard open eleven assizes before this one, that she would hear every cause on the list to the end, that no cause would be taken out of its order for anyone's convenience, and that the room's heat was a fact she regretted and could do nothing about, and would ask everyone present to bear with equal patience. A woman near the back, whose own cause stood fourteenth on the list, was heard by more than one witness afterward to remark that a justice who apologised for the weather before she had heard a single cause was, whatever else came of the week, at least honest about what she could and could not mend, which the woman meant, by every account, as praise.

## II. Of the Papers Brown at the Fold

The two Dia-thìreach causes went first, by the list's own order, and were settled within the hour, a boundary dispute between neighbouring farms that Eilidh resolved much as she had resolved the well-dispute at Muileann bhàn years before, by sending Murchadh out with a cord to measure the ground rather than trust either party's account of where an old wall had stood, and a debt case turning on a receipt that satisfied her within a quarter-hour of reading. Then came the seventeen, and the room, which had grown used through the morning to the ordinary rhythm of a small town's law, settled into a different and slower one.

Each of the seventeen came up to the bench carrying the same kind of paper, in substance if not in every particular: a title granted under the humans' own seal, in the humans' own hand, to a family that had worked the ground it named for one generation or two or three before the crossing closed, the ink long since gone the colour of weak tea and the folds worn soft and brown where the paper had been carried, folded and unfolded, from one generation's strongbox to the next. Eilidh read every one of the seventeen to its own end before she would hear a word spoken over it, turning each page toward the window's light when a line had faded past easy reading and asking, twice, for a magnifying glass Murchadh had thought to bring on a hunch rather than an instruction, and the suitors of the first several causes, expecting the swift hearing a busy assize usually gave a cause everyone in the room already knew the shape of, stood through the reading in a silence that had, by the fourth or fifth title, taken on something of the character of held breath.

She read the Dunlop title first among the seventeen, a grant of four fields above the town dated in the human count to a year before Eilidh herself was born, and asked the family's eldest, a woman of perhaps forty standing straight-backed in a coat clearly kept for occasions such as this one, whether the ground had been worked continuously by her own people since the grant was made. It had, the woman said, by her grandfather and then her father and now by her own husband and sons, without a season's break that she knew of, and she had brought, though nobody had asked her to, a second paper, a receipt for seed corn bought the spring after the grant, in case the court should want proof the ground had been worked from the very first year and not merely claimed. Eilidh took the receipt, read it as carefully as she had read the title, and set both papers down together on the table before she gave her ruling, which came, as it had come at Seann Bhral seven years before and would come sixteen times more before the week was out, in almost the words of her own earlier reasoning: that the humans' authority to grant land upon the island had ended with the humans' own leaving of it, and that the ground, in law, had passed with everything else the crossing left behind to An Crùn that now held the island; but that the working of it, the right to plant and reap and pass to a child what a family's own hands had made fruitful, remained with those who had in fact done that working, on the same terms, in substance, as before, saving only that any due An Crùn chose to lay on such ground in future would be laid on the Dunlops as on any other holder, and not excused them for the paper's age.

The room heard the same reasoning, in close to the same words, given over the Shaw title, the Bell title, the two Tullochs' adjoining strips that had once been one farm and were divided now between cousins who had not spoken to each other, by the account of the maor's clerk, in some years, and over nine more besides, each family's elder standing where the Dunlop woman had stood and hearing, in the end, that the ground was An Crùn's and the working of it theirs, a form of words that satisfied, by Eilidh's own later account to Hale that same night, no one entirely and left, so far as she could tell from the faces before the bench, few of the fifteen actually surprised.

## III. Of the Two Found for the Holders

It was the sixteenth of the seventeen, the Coombe title, that broke the pattern the room had by then settled into as firmly as the heat, and it broke it on a detail so small that Murchadh, reading the title over Eilidh's shoulder as she held it to the light, at first took it for a stain and not a mark at all.

In the margin of the Coombe grant, below the human magistrate's seal and dated some years after it, stood a second mark, a small pressed sigil Eilidh recognised before Murchadh did, having seen its like on older custody-books than any human title could carry: the countersign of a custodian, one of the old land-keeping houses that had held certain duties over the ground of the island since long before the humans came and had gone on, through all the years of the crossing, quietly witnessing such transfers of holding as came to their notice, without troubling to ask any human magistrate's leave to do it and without, so far as the record showed, being asked to stop. She asked the Coombes, an elderly man and his grown daughter who together made up the whole of the family now living, whether they knew what the mark was, and the old man said only that his own father had always called it the old blessing, and had told him once that a custodian had come to witness the grant because the ground touched, at one corner, a spring the custodian's own house had kept an interest in since before any human ever cut a title for it, and that his father had thought no more of the matter than a man thinks of a blessing said over a doorway, a thing done because it was always done and not because anyone expected it to matter.

Eilidh sat with the title a long while before she spoke again, turning it so the countersign caught the light at a different angle, and told the room, when she did speak, that the mark changed the ground she stood on to give her ruling, though not, she was careful to say, because a custodian's blessing carried any authority over Crown land that a justice's own bench was bound to defer to; it changed the ground because the mark was proof, of a kind no human seal alone could give her, that the grant had been witnessed and allowed, at the time it was made, by an office of the island's own older law, an office that had never answered to the humans and had gone on, unbroken, into the kingdom that now sat in judgment over its work. A title granted by human authority alone, she said, passed with that authority when the authority ended. A title witnessed also by an office of the island's own keeping, an office that had not ended and did not answer to An Crùn for the witnessing it had done, stood on ground the crossing's closing had never touched at all, and she would not now reach past that ground to take back what An Crùn's own predecessor, in a manner of speaking, had already let stand.

She found for the Coombes entire, ground and working both, and the room's held breath broke, for the first time that week, into an audible sound, not quite a cheer, that she let run its short course before calling the next cause. The seventeenth suit, the Pettifer title, carried the same custodian's mark in nearly the same place, a witnessing some years later by a different hand of the same office, and Eilidh, having found her reasoning once, applied it a second time with rather less deliberation than the first, though she read the Pettifer title through as slowly as she had read every other, a suitor's paper being no less deserving of a full reading for coming sixteenth or seventeenth than for coming first.

Fifteen for An Crùn, then, and two for the holders, and Griogair mac Ailein, watching from the back of the room where he had stood through the whole of the two days the seventeen had taken, said to Murchadh afterward, while the room emptied and the light through the west window had gone from white to gold, that he had sat under three magistrates and now two justices in his eleven years as maor and had never once seen a room learn, over the course of a single sitting, to stop guessing the ending before it came.

## IV. Of the Witness Heard

The grey-haired man in the old-fashioned coat had stood at the back through the first fourteen of the seventeen without speaking, and it was over the Tulloch causes, the two cousins' adjoining strips, that Eilidh first called on him, having noticed, by the fourth day of the sitting, that he was consulted in a low voice by nearly every family before its own cause was called, in the manner of a man whose word on such matters the town trusted a good deal further than it trusted its own memory.

"You have no standing before this bench," she told him, when he came forward at her word, "and I will not hear you as counsel for either side. But I understand you have kept Tional nan Tuathach's own accounts of northern land for some years, and I will hear you as a witness to the facts, if the families before me have no objection to it." Neither cousin objected, both having in fact sent for him themselves before the sitting opened, and Walter Hale, standing where the bench's clerk pointed him and turning his old-fashioned hat once in his hands before he set it down, told the court, in the plain unornamented sentences Ellen had long since stopped expecting him to shorten, what Tional nan Tuathach's own day-books showed of the ground in question: that the original Tulloch grant had been one farm and not two, that the division between the cousins had been made by their fathers in Tional nan Tuathach's third year and recorded nowhere but in a Moot minute of that year, Tional nan Tuathach having kept, since its own first winter, a habit of writing down such family arrangements precisely because no other body troubled to, and that the minute, which he had brought with him, gave the boundary the two cousins were now disputing in terms plain enough that a surveyor's cord could settle the rest in an afternoon.

Eilidh took the minute, read it as she had read every title that week, and asked him one question only, which was whether Tional nan Tuathach's habit of recording such arrangements was a general one or a thing done only where a dispute was already foreseen. General, Hale told her; Tional nan Tuathach had begun writing down family divisions of land in its very first year, at Walter Hale's own suggestion though he did not say so himself, on the reasoning, as old as Tional nan Tuathach's first minute-book, that a people with no deeds of their own to a magistrate's satisfaction had better at least agree, in writing, among themselves, since no one else was likely to trouble to keep the peace of it for them.

She found for the cousins jointly, confirming the division Tional nan Tuathach's minute recorded and no other, and told the room, closing that cause, that a body with no authority An Crùn recognised had nonetheless kept a better record of the ground in question than any office An Crùn did recognise, and that she saw no reason a justice's bench should refuse a fact merely because the hand that had written it down held no seal. It was, by Griogair mac Ailein's later account, the only moment of the whole sitting at which the room seemed to him to hold its breath for a different reason than heat or suspense, a number of those present having spent some years assuming that a court of An Crùn's own making would never trouble to notice, let alone credit, anything Tional nan Tuathach had ever written.

Hale did not speak again before the bench that week, the remaining causes needing no witness he could give, but he stayed through every one of them, standing at the back in the place the grey-haired man in the old coat had occupied since the first morning, and it was there, at the close of the last cause on the Friday, that Eilidh, gathering her papers while Murchadh saw to the fair copies, found him still standing, and asked him, since he seemed in no hurry to leave, whether he would take supper with her at the inn before the road took him back to Caol mhòr.

## V. Of the Inn at Ceann mhòr

The inn stood two streets back from the harbour, far enough from the fish-yards that the evening air, once the day's heat had begun at last to break, carried more of woodsmoke than of tar, and Eilidh took the table nearest the empty hearth, it being too warm a night for a fire but the quietest table in the room. Hale sat across from her with the particular stiffness of a man unused to being asked to a justice's own table, and it was some while, over bread and a dish of the harbour's own fish that neither of them had ordered by name and both ate readily, before either spoke of the week just past.

"Fifteen and two," Hale said at last, turning his cup a half-turn on the table without drinking from it. "I have heard the number said in the yard outside a dozen times since Tuesday, as though it were a kind of arithmetic a man could work out for himself given time enough. Fifteen for An Crùn and two for the holders, and every family in that room knowing, before you opened your mouth on their own cause, near enough what the answer would be."

"They knew the shape of it," Eilidh said, "because I gave them the shape of it seven years ago, at Seann Bhral, and have not since found cause to give them a different one. I do not think a family is owed a kinder ruling merely because the same ruling, given often enough, has begun to feel to them like a wall rather than a law. It is the same wall, whether they meet it the first time or the fifteenth."

"I know it," Hale said. "I have written it down myself, more than once, for Tional nan Tuathach's own book, in words a good deal less careful than yours. What I have never quite settled with myself is whether a law can be exact and just at once, when the two families it found in favour of won for a reason that had nothing to do with the land they'd worked or the years they'd worked it, and everything to do with a mark neither of them had troubled to notice on their own paper until a justice's eye found it for them. The Coombes did not earn that mark. Their grandfather happened to have a spring on his ground that a custodian's house had an old interest in. Fifteen other families worked their ground exactly as hard, exactly as long, and had no such spring, and lost."

Eilidh set down her own cup and looked at him a long moment before she answered, in the manner Murchadh had come, over seven years, to recognise as the look she gave a question she judged worth a full answer rather than a short one. "You are asking me whether the law is fair," she said, "and I have never claimed that it is, only that it is exact, which is a different thing and, I have come to believe, the more useful one of the two to a people who must live under a law made, in the first place, for someone else's convenience and not their own. I did not write Breith Leòid to be fair to the Tuathaich, Mr. Hale, whatever a fair reading of it may have done for two families out of seventeen by an accident of a spring and a custodian's old habit. I wrote it because a rule that changed from bench to bench, from year to year, from whichever justice happened to be riding through, was no rule at all, only eleven separate guesses in the same coat, and I judged, seven years ago, that even a hard exact rule, known in advance and held to without favour, was less cruel in the end than a kind one nobody could rely on from one assize to the next. I have not changed that judgment sitting in this room tonight, and I do not expect to change it before I die. But I will tell you what I have not told the maor, nor Murchadh, nor the council's own chests where my rulings are filed: I know exactly whom the rule was made for, and it was not made for the Coombes, nor the Pettifers, nor the fifteen who came before them. It was made to let a Crown that had just taken an island back from a people who could no longer defend it go on taking, in good order and without argument, what it had already decided to take. I made it exact so that the taking, at least, would be plainly seen for what it was, by anyone who cared to read a ruling closely enough to see it. Whether that is a kindness to the fifteen or only a kind of honesty that costs them exactly as much as a dishonest ruling would have, I have turned over more nights than this one, and I have not settled it yet, and I do not think I will."

Hale did not answer at once, turning his own cup a further half-turn, and when he spoke again it was to say only that he had not expected so plain an answer, and had asked, if he was honest, half hoping for a shorter and more comfortable one. "I did not come to Ceann mhòr for a comfortable answer," Eilidh said, "and I do not think you rode a day to hear one either, Mr. Hale, whatever you may have told your wife you were riding for." She allowed herself, for the first time that evening, something that Murchadh, had he been at the table, would have called nearly a smile, and added that Tional nan Tuathach's own habit of writing down every family's arrangement in plain words, a habit she had heard tell of before that week and seen proved to her own satisfaction in the Tulloch cause, was the nearest thing to her own trade she had met with outside a bench in seven years of riding, and that she did not think it beneath a circuit justice to say so to the man who had begun the habit.

They spoke on a while longer, of lesser things, the state of the coast road north of Àth leathan and the price fish was fetching that season at the Ceann mhòr quay, and parted at the inn's door with the plain courtesy of two people who had said, between them, rather more than either had expected to say and neither found cause to be sorry for it. Hale rode north for Caol mhòr the next morning before the assize house was properly awake. Eilidh, with Murchadh and the satchel of fair copies behind her, rode south.

## VI. Of the Road Home and the Book

The road from Ceann mhòr ran by {{place:burg:346}}, where the coast track from the harbour joined the old inland way, and Eilidh broke the first day's riding there, at an inn she had stopped at on her way north, remarking to Murchadh over supper that the innkeeper's memory for a justice's habits had grown, over the years, considerably more reliable than her own for the innkeeper's name. From Àth leathan she rode on by the western road to {{place:burg:47}}, where Breith Leòid itself had first been given seven years before at a bench she had not sat at since, and stood a while, before the light failed, at the harbour wall looking out at the water the ruling had, in its way, been made beside, though she said nothing to Murchadh of what she thought there, if she thought anything beyond the plain fact of having ridden her circuits nine summers now and being tired, that autumn, in a way the eight earlier summers had not left her. The way south from Seann Bhral was longer than either of the two stretches before it, and slower, the road turning inland for the better part of two days before it met the coast again above the fishing villages that fed Cathair dhomhain's own market, and it was on that inland stretch, riding through a country of low stone-walled fields she had ridden a dozen times before without troubling to remark on it, that she told Murchadh, more as a fact worth recording than as a complaint, that she did not think she would ride the Ceann mhòr road again the following summer, whatever the council's rota said, a season's riding at nine-and-fifty being, she judged, a different arithmetic from a season's riding at one-and-fifty, however little the distance itself had changed between the two.

She reached {{place:burg:26}} a little after the turning of the year, in am Faoilleach, and took to her own house on the harbour side of the town with a cough she told Murchadh was nothing worse than the road's dust and a season's riding, and for some weeks it seemed, even to Murchadh, to be exactly that. She kept up the fair copies of the Ceann mhòr rulings through the winter, sending one set to the council's chests at Cathair dhearg and a second, at her own instruction as always, to every maor's court of her circuit, with the note in her own hand that had gone out after every ruling of hers for seven years, that any cause brought on a like title should be decided on the like reasoning until the council itself said otherwise. That same winter she wrote a longer paper than any single ruling required, gathering into one book the whole of what she had held over nine years of riding, Breith Seann Bhral first and the Ceann mhòr sitting last, with every lesser cause between them that had turned on the same ground, so that a justice coming after her, she told Murchadh while he copied the fair pages by the lamp, would not have to ride to Cathair dhearg and dig through nine years of the council's own chests to learn what she herself already knew by heart.

The cough did not lift with the turning of am Faoilleach into an Gearran, and by am Màrt she had taken, for the first time in Murchadh's seven years beside her, to keeping her bed past the morning, dictating the last pages of the gathered book to him from a chair by the window rather than sitting at her own table to write them. She did not speak, in those weeks, of being ill, only of the work still wanting finishing, and Murchadh, who had learned long since which of her silences asked to be pressed and which did not, did not press this one. The book was finished, in substance, by the middle of am Màrt, needing only its final binding, and Eilidh, hearing from the physician who attended her by then that the winter had settled somewhere in her chest that a summer's warmth might yet clear, told Murchadh plainly that she did not think she would see the clearing, and asked him to send word north, before the roads grew too soft for a rider, to Uilleam mac Leòid, her brother's son, a young man of six-and-twenty who had ridden with her twice as a boy and had, she told Murchadh, more patience for the northern circuit's particular slowness than any other of the family she could call to mind.

She died at {{place:burg:26}}, {{date:V-0069}}, in her own house within sound of the harbour she had grown up beside, with Murchadh at her bedside and the finished book, still unbound, on the table beside her. She had not lived to be sixty.

The gathered book was bound within the month, in plain calf over boards, at the council's own expense and by the council's own binder, and given the name Eilidh had herself suggested to Murchadh in one of her last clear mornings, Leabhar nam Breith, the plain word for what it held and nothing grander. Uilleam mac Leòid, named to the northern circuit before Bealltainn, rode north for his first assize with the book strapped, as the satchel of blank record-books had once been strapped, behind his own clerk's saddle rather than his, on the grounds, he told the young man riding beside him, that a justice ought to arrive at her first assize with clean hands, an instruction he had had, he said, from an aunt who never in her life explained a rule twice when the first explanation had been exact enough to serve. Every circuit justice who rode after him carried a copy of the same book, and the reasoning it set down, fifteen for An Crùn and two for the holders and the whole long working that had brought her to that number, went on being read out, in courtroom after courtroom of the northern circuit, in words that were, for the most part, very nearly her own.
