# Of the Order of Worship

*SE 6 an Giblean – SE 7 an Gearran*

> There had never been one.
>
> — of the order of worship, entered at Seann Skell on the southern coast, {{date:V-0029}}

## I. Of the Letter that Found Him

Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was fifty-six years old and had kept the flame at a hearth-altar above Baile ghlas for thirty of them, a small stone house with one window and a roof that wanted thatching every third year, when the letter came that made him Àrd-choimheadaiche of the Rite. He read it twice standing in the doorway, because he did not trust it sitting down, and then a third time to see whether a second reading would change the words. It did not. The council's clerk had written that the Old Faith's keeping of Brìde's flame and Crom's stone was henceforth to have a settled order and a head to keep it, and that the head was to be Dòmhnall mac Thormoid, and that he was to remove to Seann Skell on the southern coast, where the greatest house of the Rite stood, and there was to write down, for the first time in all the long keeping of the fire, what the keeping was.

He had never asked for it. He said so to the two men who came to fetch him, and they said that was noted, and that it made no difference to the letter. Why him, of all the hearth-keepers of the south-west, he asked, and the younger of the two said that his name had come up at the council's table because a councillor's wife's mother had been named at his altar forty years before and remembered him kindly, and that this, as far as either of them knew, was the whole of the reasoning the office had behind it. Dòmhnall thanked him for his honesty and packed one chest.

He left his altar tended by his sister's son, a boy of fourteen who had never lit the flame alone and wept doing it the first night, though Dòmhnall did not learn that until later and would not have thought less of the boy for it. The road to Seann Skell took him the better part of a week, on a cart with a family of tinkers as far as Cathair dhearg and on his own two feet after that, and he arrived at the southern coast with his boots worn through at one heel and a headful of what he would say, none of which survived the first sight of the house he had been given to keep.

It stood on the cliff above the harbour, older than anything he had tended, its inner hall black at the roof-beams from fires longer than the memory of any keeper now living, and its custodians, eleven of them, of varying age and varying welcome, stood in a loose half-circle in the yard to receive him as though a stray dog had wandered in wearing a collar too fine for it. The eldest of them, a woman named Beathag who had kept the house's western flame for forty years, looked him over from his worn heel to his travelling cloak and said only: "So you're the order."

"I'm to write one," Dòmhnall said.

"There isn't one to write," said Beathag. "There's the keeping. It's always been the keeping."

He did not answer her that first evening, because he had no answer, and a man who has walked a week to a house that does not want him is wise to hold his tongue until the second day at the earliest. But her words stayed with him through the winter that followed, longer than anything the council's clerk had said, because she was, as far as he could tell, entirely right, and his charge was to write down a thing that had never had an edge to it, that had lived in gesture and silence and the particular way one keeper's hand cupped the flame against a draught that another keeper's hand did not, and to give it a form it would hold from then on whether the form fit or not.

The house gave him a cell off the hall that had belonged, Beathag told him without being asked, to a keeper three generations dead whose name was on none of the house's own lists, only on a beam where someone long ago had scratched it with a knife point. There was a table, a stool, a shelf that had once held something and now held nothing, and a window that looked west over the roofs of the town to the harbour, where the fishing boats went out before light and came back after it and paid the Rite no more mind than they paid the gulls. He set his one chest at the foot of the bed and did not unpack the whole of it for a week, thinking, though he did not say so to Beathag or to anyone, that he might yet be sent home before the spring turned, the office having been given him, as far as he could tell, by no better reasoning than a naming forty years gone and a council with a gap in its ledger it wanted filled.

He was not sent home. By am Màrt he had unpacked the chest to the bottom, and had begun, each morning before the house's other business, to sit at the table by the west window with the note-books Beathag brought him one at a time, as though she doubted his readiness for more than one at once, and did not think it worth telling him so.

## II. Of the Leyline and the Unbroken Line

He began where the old note-books began, in the vein-house's custody records copied down from ages before any council sat at Cathair dhearg, and in the fragments of disputation the druid schools had left when their halls stood open, arguing over whether the coal answered blood or answered effort, a question Dòmhnall found he could not resolve any better than the schools had, six centuries of masters having failed at it before him. A defence written for a law two ages dead held that the burning was never for the coal's own sake but for the people it would make of them; over it lay the copyists' later gloss, that this must have meant the line running down from the first king to the woman the record calls Aisling, though the defence said no such thing and could not have, being written before she was born. He sat with that contradiction for the better part of a month, turning it as a man turns a coin he cannot spend, and in the end wrote the doctrine to hold both readings open at once, because he could see no way, and no right, to close either.

What he set down, working through that spring and into the wet of the following winter by a lamp that Beathag grudgingly kept filled for him, was this: that the vein under the mountain was the holy thing, no sign nor dwelling of a greater power but the thing whole and entire, alive in some fashion no keeper was asked to understand or explain; that what was broken from it must be burned in measure or returned unburned, as the old law had always held; and that the vein answered a line, called the coal-touched, whose blood or whose office ran unbroken from a beginning no living record named. Whether it was blood or office he would not say, and said so plainly in the text, the first and only place in the whole order where he owned to an uncertainty. He called it the leyline, an old word the note-books gave him for the vein's own hidden path under the ground, and he set the two things, the line under the earth and the line of blood or office above it, side by side as the twin pillars the Rite would stand on from that day.

There was a night in that month, the wind coming hard off the water and the shutters loose in their frames, when he woke Beathag past midnight to ask her plainly whether he had any right at all to be the one deciding it, a hearth-keeper of thirty years who had never in his life set eyes on one of the coal-touched, writing doctrine about a line of people he could not name, the few of them the census would count living far from any altar he had kept. Beathag, who had been woken for less and minded it more, told him that she had never met one either, nor had any keeper of that house, and that this was rather the point of writing it down now than a reason to leave it unwritten, since a thing no one has seen is exactly the thing a house most needs a settled word about, so that when someone does see one, or claims to, there will be a doctrine standing to measure the claim against, rather than eleven keepers and eleven separate guesses. She went back to bed without waiting to see whether the answer satisfied him. It left something wanting, but it was the answer he kept, of everything said to him that whole year, closest at hand.

He wrote also, because Beathag made him, a page on the keeping proper: how the flame was lit from what the vein gave and never from any other fire; how the measure was to be judged, a handful and no more to a hearth on the year's turning days, and every ounce beyond it returned to the ground it came from; how a house without a custodian in living memory of its own line might still keep the flame, provided the keeping had never lapsed. Beathag read that page over his shoulder three times before she would let it stand, and struck out a line of his about the coal-touched appearing among ordinary households "as rarely as a comet," saying that she had never seen one, comet or coal-touched, and a keeper should not write what he did not know to be true merely because it sounded well. Dòmhnall struck it and did not argue. He had begun, by then, to trust her judgment a good deal more than his own, though he never told her so outright, being a man who found it easier to accept correction than to praise it.

## III. Of the First Reading

The order was read for the first time on a grey morning near the end of am Faoilleach, in the hall with the blackened beams, to the eleven custodians of the southern house and to a scattering of others who had walked or ridden to hear it: two hearth-keepers from villages along the coast, a Stone priest from Inis thais who had come out of curiosity rather than obligation and stood at the back with his arms folded the whole reading through, and a boy sent from Cnoc bheag to carry word home of what was said, though not, as it turned out, to carry any word of agreement.

Dòmhnall read it standing at the hearth, with the year's fire banked low beside him, and his voice, which had spent thirty years speaking to no larger a congregation than a single family at a time, cracked twice on words he had written and rewritten so often he no longer heard them as his own. He read the leyline and the unbroken line, the measure and the return, and the page on lapsed and unlapsed keeping that Beathag had made him write three times over until it said exactly what it meant and no more. He had thought, walking to the hearth that morning, that the hardest part would be the reading proper, and found instead that the hardest part was the pause after each page, when he looked up from the sheet to see who was still listening and who had begun, politely or otherwise, to study the beams; the boy from Cnoc bheag studied the beams throughout, and the Stone priest studied Dòmhnall, which he found, on the whole, the harder of the two to bear. When he finished there was a silence that ran long enough for him to think he had failed entirely, and then Beathag said, from her place at the front, "Well. Now there's one," in a tone that gave away nothing of whether she thought it an improvement, and the silence broke into the ordinary business of people filing out into a cold yard.

The Stone priest came to him before he left and said the houses of the Stone would send no witnesses to a settling that was not theirs to settle, the Stone in the south-west having never been Crom's stone at all, but that he for his part thought the writing sound, so far as it went, and would say as much when he was home. He was as good as his word. Within the month a letter came from the houses of the Stone, wishing the Rite well in its ordering and promising nothing further, which Dòmhnall had copied into the first page of a book he began that day, a plain ledger in which he meant to keep every letter the Rite sent or received from then on, since it seemed to him, watching the boy from Cnoc bheag ride off without a word to anyone, that a house with no written order and no written record of what was said to it and by it would spend the rest of its days answering the same questions with the same silence it had always answered them with.

## IV. Of the Cnoc bheag Letter and the Bell at Muileann naomh

The hawk-houses had not waited for the reading. Within a week of his coming to Seann Skell, the spring before, when the council's letter naming him was barely cold, a letter had come in a hand Dòmhnall did not know, sealed with the mark the hawk-houses used, a bird with its wings spread flat. It was not long. It said that the houses of Òrd an t-Seabhaig round Cnoc bheag had kept the old form of the Rite, the local altar answering to no custodian above it, since before any council sat anywhere on the island, and saw no cause to answer to one now; that any order of worship he might write would be, in the letter's own dry phrase, "handsome, and not needed"; and that the hawk-houses would go on as they had gone on, with their own flames and their own measures and their own silence about what either meant, and wished the southern house no ill in doing the same. Dòmhnall had read it three times, as he had read the council's letter, and found in it no anger at all, only a settled refusal so entire it had not thought the matter worth arguing. The boy from Cnoc bheag who stood through the reading that winter carried home only what the houses had already decided.

He was not compelled to force the matter, and had, in any case, no way to force eleven houses spread across a shire he had never set foot in, so he copied the letter into his book beside the Stone's, and let it stand. Beathag, reading it over his shoulder as she read everything, said only that she had kept the flame at this house for two reigns and had never once been told what to do by a hawk-house, and did not expect to start being told the reverse either. Dòmhnall came to think it the beginning of a quarrel that would outlast every one of the men and women then standing in that yard, though he did not write that down, not knowing it for certain and being, by nature, unwilling to set down as fact what was only his own guess at things to come.

One house had answered otherwise, and in the same first week. Word reached him from a wool-trader who had no particular reason to carry it beyond having passed through the town and thought it worth the telling: the hawk-house at Muileann naomh, far south of Cnoc bheag and close by the coast, had sent to say it would take the written order entire, whatever it proved to be when written, and had hung in its doorway a bell cast new for the purpose, the first bell any hawk-house had ever hung, to ring at the Rite's measured burnings. It rang it that summer at the burning of the year, a small crowd gathered in the lane outside to hear it. Dòmhnall walked out to the cliff path that evening and stood a long while looking south over water he could not see the far edge of, because it seemed to him a thing worth standing still for, that of all the houses that might have answered him, one had, before he had written a word, and it was not the one nearest the office that had made him, nor the one that owed the council anything, but a small house on a headland that had heard there would be words and trusted them unread.

He wrote to Muileann naomh with his own hand, the first letter of thanks he had ever composed in an office he still did not feel he had earned, and had it copied twice, once for his own book and once to be sent back by the same wool-trader, who took the coin for the errand without asking why a custodian of the southern house should write so plainly to a hawk-house when every other hawk-house on the island had disowned the office that same week.

## V. Of the Regalia and the Refusal at Court

In an Gearran, with the order read and the roads hard with frost, he rode north to Cathair dhearg, at the council's summons rather than his own wish, to be presented at court as the Rite's first Àrd-choimheadaiche and to make, as the summons put it, "such representations as the office might think fitting." The capital was larger and colder to his eye than anything the coast had prepared him for, its streets full of clerks and carters who did not so much as glance at a hearth-priest's plain grey cloak among the council's liveried men, and he spent his first evening there in a lodging house paid for out of his own purse, since no one had thought to arrange him one, working over the single representation he had carried north with him and turning it, on the ride and again that night, until he had it in a shape he thought could not be misheard: that the coronation regalia, which Òrd Mhacha at Cathair dhearg had kept since before the council itself existed, having kept it under the humans and before them, ought now to be shared in the keeping between Òrd Mhacha and the Rite that had, after all, only just been given a settled order to keep anything with.

He put it to the high priest of Òrd Mhacha across a table in a cold ante-room off the council's own chamber, a man some ten years his junior with the easy manner of a house that had never had to ask leave of anyone for anything since the founding of the kingdom, and a ring on his right hand that Dòmhnall, watching it catch the light through the whole exchange, took at first for a piece of the regalia and later decided was only his own. The high priest heard him out without interrupting, which Dòmhnall took, wrongly, for a good sign, and then said that Òrd Mhacha had kept the regalia through every age the record named, under the Hall and under the humans both, that the crown's own coronation rite ran through Òrd Mhacha's hands and no other's, and that he saw no ground on which a house not a year in its office, however sound its new writing, might claim a share in a keeping settled for ages. He asked, not unkindly, whether the southern house had any wish to crown a king, and when Dòmhnall said it had not, said that this, then, seemed to settle the matter on its own. He said all of it, Dòmhnall noted, without any unkindness whatsoever, in the tone of a man refusing a request he did not think worth the offence of refusing sharply.

Dòmhnall rode south again with nothing gained but the answer, which he entered in his book that winter beside the Stone's letter and the Hawk's, three plain lines with no comment added, because by then he had begun to see the sum of the office's first year, and did not think it wanted his comment to make its meaning plain. One house had disowned the order outright and told him so without malice. One house had stood aside from it with the courtesy of a neighbour who wants no quarrel and offers no help either. And one house, the oldest and best placed of the three, had simply outranked him, in a tone so mild it had taken him the whole ride home to recognise it for a refusal at all.

## VI. Of Caol mhòr, as the Library Keeps It

Far to the north, in that same year, at Caol mhòr where the Moot met in its long low room, Walter Hale asked the council's leave to open schools among the Tuathaich that would teach in the humans' own tongue rather than in Dia-thìris. It is not told at length here, having no part in the keeping of any hearth-fire, and belongs more properly to the record the Moot itself has kept; but the Library holds the council's answer, because it is short and because the age would be the poorer without it. The council wrote back that the kingdom's schools taught in Dia-thìris, and that it had no others.

The Moot's own minute of that answer, entered the same week in the hand of a clerk who did not trouble to hide what he thought of it, calls the council's word "clear, at least." Nothing further was asked, and nothing further was given, for a good many years after; and it is a small thing to set beside a bell rung at Muileann naomh or a refusal handed down across a table at Cathair dhearg, except that the same year gave the Rite its first written order and gave the north the same two words it would be given, in one shape or another, for most of the age that followed. The Library keeps both. It does not say they are the same. It only notes that they fell in the same twelvemonth, and lets a reader make of that what a reader will.

## VII. Of the Copies Sent Out

Before that Gearran was out Dòmhnall's clerks, two novices of the southern house lent him by Beathag, a boy called Cailean and a quiet girl named Sorcha who wrote a far steadier hand than either Dòmhnall or the boy, had made fair copies of the order entire, each one checked against his own working text line by line, for a written thing, he had come to understand over that first year, was worth nothing at all if every copy of it said something slightly different from every other. He sent one to every house of the Old Faith that still stood, the Hawk-houses included, though he did not expect an answer from most of them and received none; one to the Stone, with a note of thanks for the courtesy already shown; one, though he sent it without much hope, to Òrd Mhacha, on the plain ground that a house might refuse to share a keeping and still be sent the doctrine of a neighbouring one; and a last copy, made with more care than any of the others, wrapped and sealed and given to the same wool-trader who had first brought him word of the bell, to be carried south again to Muileann naomh, not because that house had asked for it, having a copy already from the first reading, but because it seemed to Dòmhnall, turning the last page under Beathag's lamp on the coldest night of that Gearran, a debt worth the paying: that of every house on the island, it was the one that had heard him first, and asked him nothing in return.

He wrote, in the last line of his book that winter, a summary he did not mean as one, only as a plain account of where the year had left him: that the order stood written now where it had never stood written before, that one house had taken it up entire and rung a bell for it, that one had set it courteously aside, and that one, the oldest and grandest, had heard it and gone on exactly as before. He banked the fire, closed the book, and sat a while in the dark hall listening to the sea below the cliff, which had gone on the same way through every age the Rite had kept it holy and every age it had not, and seemed to Dòmhnall, that night at least, entirely indifferent to which of the three answers the island had chosen to give him.
