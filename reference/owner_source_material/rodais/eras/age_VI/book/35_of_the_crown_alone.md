# Of the Crown Alone

*SE 58 an t-Sultain – SE 60 an Lùnastal*

> The charter names the Board a carrier of the Crown's people and their goods. A carrier that will take one man's coin and refuse another's answers to no charter at all.
>
> — Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, ruling given at Seann Bhral in the west, {{date:V-0203}}

## I. Of the Market that Passed the Fair

The news came north the way most news came to Caol mhòr that decade, a season behind the fact and by way of a carter who had heard it from a factor who had heard it from someone nearer the matter: that the market day at Baile Mòr dhomhain, on the deep river in the hill country, had for two years running drawn more carts than the fair at Muileann òg, the old fair the whole island had measured its trade by since before the restored kingdom had a name of its own. Daniel Frayne heard it from a wool-buyer at the Moot's own table, a man come down from the hills to sell what Baile Mòr dhomhain's own market no longer had room to take, and turned it over a while before he wrote it into the room's ledger of the year's business, under no heading that fit it well, since it was not a Moot matter and yet seemed to him, turning three-and-fifty that autumn, a thing the room ought to have set down somewhere.

"The vein feeds fewer hands each year," he said to Mary Coyle's old clerk, who still kept the room's figures though the chair was Frayne's own now, "and the hill country feeds more of them than it did. A market goes where the coin already is, and stops waiting on a fair that has had the same coin coming to it every year since anyone living can remember." The clerk said this was truer than the room had leisure to argue with that morning, there being three causes waiting and a letter from Muileann shean not yet opened, and Frayne let the matter go for the day, though he kept the wool-buyer's fact in his own private book beside the register of the hurt, a habit he had never broken since the winter he first began it.

The crown changed hands that same season, and of it the room at Caol mhòr knew only what any household on the island knew: that Eòghan's reign had ended, at two-and-sixty, the notice giving no word of how, and that Mairead, eight-and-thirty, had been crowned at Cathair dhearg by Macha's rite, as every ruler of the restored line had been crowned before her. The room's clerks noted the day and the name and went back to their own ledgers, the Moot having no place in a rite it was never asked to, nor coin to spare for a journey south to watch it, and Frayne, reading the notice a second time before he filed it, found in it nothing to say beyond what the notice already said, a fact and no more, the kind he had learned young to set down plainly and leave alone. He noted only, in his own book and nowhere else, that Eòghan had held the throne since Frayne was a young man at Agnes Pike's benches, and that the coin that winter would be minted new with another name on it.

The mint's own notice reached Caol mhòr some weeks after the crowning, later than the news of it had come by mouth, and told the room only what such notices ever told it, that the die was changed and the old coin still good until the new one was struck in sufficient number, a fact of no more moment to a clerk's ledger than the changing of a season, save that Frayne, weighing one of the last old coins in his own hand before he passed it across the table for a debt owed the Moot, thought it a strange thing that a face stamped on metal should outlast, in most households' own reckoning, the man who had worn it, no household at Caol mhòr troubling any longer to name Eòghan at its own table where once his name had been the one every household knew.

He had seen the fair at Muileann òg only a few times in his own life, the first with the wool carts down the Carters' Road, and he remembered it as a crowd too large for any one man to take the measure of, the factors' own tents strung the length of a field that seemed, to a boy from the north, to have no far edge at all. He asked the wool-buyer, before the man rode on, whether the fair still looked so to a stranger's eye, and the man told him that it did, near enough, to a stranger who had never seen it in its better years, but that a man who had stood in that field every autumn for thirty years running, as the wool-buyer had, could see the gaps now where the tents had once stood three deep, gaps the fair's own wardens filled each year with a wider aisle and called it room for the crowd to move, though the crowd had not grown to want the room.

The letter from Muileann shean, when he opened it, asked a different question of him entire, and one he had some part in answering. The mill-town in the north had that spring opened a school of its own for the sons and daughters of hewers the Guild would not bind, the Guild having voted, for the first time since its charter, to bind no new apprentices at all beyond the sons and daughters of its own masters, the vein by then thinning under enough of the old galleries that a guild grown used to feeding every willing pair of hands had, that year, more hands than it any longer had work to give them. Muileann shean's school meant to teach those turned away to read, to reckon, and to survey, on the reasoning of the shire's own maor, who had written the letter himself, that a hewer's child shut out of the pit might still find his father's own trade from the surface rather than the gallery, chain in hand instead of pick, if someone thought to teach him the chain. He asked the Moot for primers, there being none in the north fit for the purpose and no coin in the shire's own dues to have new ones bound.

Frayne read the letter twice and then a third time, standing at the window of the room he had sat in as a clerk for more years than he had sat in it as speaker, and thought of Agnes Pike, dead that same year, who had taught him his letters in a byre at Doire ghlas on slates the school could not always find chalk for, and of the room she had kept, and of what she had once told him, a boy of ten with more questions than sense, that a trade a man could carry in his own head weighed nothing on the road and could not be taken from him by any Board that ever sat. He wrote back to Muileann shean that he would bind the primers himself, at his own bench, and send them north with the first cart that had room, and did so within the month, forty copies of a reading-book he had set the type for himself twenty years before and kept the forms for since, the ink still smelling of the press when the bundle went out on the cart. The shire's maor wrote back in the spring to say that the eleven children the school had opened with had grown, within the year, past thirty, and that three of the first eleven had already gone south to the ports to hire themselves out as chain-hands, the surveying trade being one, as it turned out, the ports had more use for than the Board's own vein-towns did just then.

## II. Of the Doire ghlas Bolt and the Suit

The mills at Doire ghlas were three years building by the time they turned their first cloth, raised with the Moot's own money on ground the Church had let stand idle since the Severance, the first works the north had ever owned that owed nothing to the vein and nothing to any southern charter either. Frayne had sat on the room's own committee that voted the money out of the Moot's slender chest, a vote he had argued for on grounds he set down plainly in the room's minute, that a people who wove their own cloth need not buy the south's, and that a mill built with the north's own coin answered to the north's own reckoning and to no factor's. The first bolt came off the looms in the last week of an t-Iuchar, a grey twill with a white thread run through the selvedge, the same mark the weavers of Doire ghlas had first been let sell under their own name at the fair some years before, and the mill's own overseer sent word to Caol mhòr the same week, a short letter that said only that the cloth was good, better than the south's own factors had reckoned a northern loom could turn out, and that the mill stood ready to sell it wherever a buyer's coin was willing.

The buyer's coin, when it came, found no way to travel. The Rail Board's own factors at Àth leathan, the railhead nearest the north, asked to carry the first wagon-load down the coal line to the fair at Muileann òg, where the southern trade was and where the cloth would fetch a price the north's own markets could not match, refused the load without troubling to give a reason beyond the one the Board had given every Tuathaich cargo since the line was laid, that the line carried what the Board judged fit to carry and answered to no one for the judging. The mill's overseer wrote to Caol mhòr a second time, angrier than the first, and Frayne read the letter to the room himself, standing at the same table Mary Coyle had once stood at to open the register of the hurt.

"We have asked the Board before to carry what we make," he told the room, "and been told before that the asking was not the Board's to answer. I mean, this time, to ask a court instead, since a Board that will not answer a letter may yet answer a summons." The room's older clerks, who remembered every earlier asking and every earlier refusal, doubted a Dia-thìreach court would hear a Tuathaich complaint any more kindly than the Board had, and said so plainly, but the room voted the coin for the journey all the same, there being, as one of them put it, little enough left to lose in the asking that the mill had not already lost in the refusing.

Frayne rode west that autumn to Seann Bhral, the circuit's own western seat on the coast, three days' road from Caol mhòr and one he had never ridden before, carrying the mill's own ledgers and the Board's own letter of refusal, kept whole rather than answered, in the same satchel he carried his private book in. He was four-and-fifty that year, and told the clerk who took his papers at the courthouse door, a young man who had plainly never before entered a Tuathaich name on the circuit's own roll, that he had come to bring a cause and not a petition, the distinction, he said, mattering rather more to him than the clerk seemed at first to grasp it did.

The justice who heard it was Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, four-and-forty that year, a man who had sat the western circuit for a dozen years and had, by his own account given later to a clerk who asked him, never before had a Tuathaich cause brought properly before his own bench rather than settled or dropped before it reached him. He heard the Board's own factor first, a well-dressed man from Ceann leathan who argued that the line was the Board's own property, raised with the Board's own coin and the Crown's own charter, and that a carrier might choose its own cargo as freely as a merchant chose his own stock. Ruairidh mac Phàdraig asked him, when he had finished, whether the charter that raised the line had named the Board a merchant or a carrier, and the factor, after a silence the court's own clerk noted in his record without comment, allowed that the charter's own words called it a carrier, there being no way to answer otherwise with the charter lying open on the bench between them.

Frayne spoke for the mill himself, having no advocate the Moot's own coin could stretch to and no wish, he told the court, to hire one, since the facts of the matter were plain enough that a plain man could lay them out without a trained tongue to dress them. He read the Board's letter of refusal aloud, word for word, and set beside it the mill's own account of the coin it had offered to pay for the carriage, a rate no lower than any southern mill paid for the same road, and asked the court only the one question the room at Caol mhòr had sent him west to ask, whether a carrier chartered by the Crown to carry for the Crown's own people might refuse one people among them and take another's coin freely.

The factor pressed his own point a second way before the court rose that first day, saying that the Board had carried Tuathaich cargo before, wool by the cartload in years the road had allowed it, and that the refusal touched only the line, the Board's own rails being a different property from the Board's own carts and answerable, in the factor's own reading of the charter, to a different clause entire. Ruairidh mac Phàdraig had the charter read aloud a second time at that, the whole of it, clause by clause, by the court's own clerk, a reading that took the better part of an hour and left the factor, by its end, with no clause left to point to that named the rails apart from the carts, the charter's own drafters having written, as the justice observed when the reading was done, of "the carriage of the Crown's people and their goods" without once troubling to say by what wheel or what rail the carriage was to be made. Frayne, listening from the bench set aside for him, wrote the justice's own words into his private book that same evening, judging them, he told the room afterward, worth keeping whole rather than in his own paraphrase.

Ruairidh mac Phàdraig gave his ruling three days later, at the same bench, in words the court's clerk copied fair and Frayne carried home whole, that the Rail Board was a carrier of the Crown and bound, by the Crown's own charter, to carry for any who paid the rate it set, whatever the cargo's maker, and that it had no more right to refuse the Doire ghlas mill's cloth than it had to refuse a southern factor's grain. The Board appealed the ruling to the council at Cathair dhearg within the month, as Frayne had expected it would, and the appeal sat unanswered on the council's own table through that winter and the spring following, until it was let lapse in the summer without a word given either way, the council's own minute of the matter, when the room at Caol mhòr at last obtained a copy of it, noting only that the matter had not been reached in the session's business, a phrase Frayne read three times before he understood it meant the council did not mean to reach it at all.

"It is not a victory they have given us," he told the room, the letter open before him, "only a defeat they have declined to trouble themselves with undoing. I will take it. I have taken smaller things for larger names before now." The room's older clerks, who had doubted him at the start of the asking, said nothing against him that day, and one of them, the same who had first doubted a Dia-thìreach court would hear a Tuathaich cause kindly, wrote in the room's own minute, in a hand Frayne kept and read again more than once in the years after, that it was the first ruling of any Dia-thìreach court the Moot had ever won standing, and that a first was worth the keeping whatever came after it.

## III. Of the Copper and the Last Shafts

The same seasons that carried the Board's own refusal to Seann Bhral and back carried other news south of the north entirely, news Frayne read of rather than rode to. The house of Mac Ùisdein, which had sold half its own share in the Sloc Mòr some years before to answer a search that had found nothing, sold what remained of its working shafts to the Crown outright the next summer, keeping only its share in the spoil-heaps and the quarry-stone the mine's own waste still yielded, and the Board's own clerks, moving the last of the house's papers out of the stone house at Cathair mhòr, wrote in their minute that the vein was thenceforth worked in the Crown's name alone, no house's own charter standing beside the Crown's upon it any longer. Frayne, reading the notice at Caol mhòr, thought of Alasdair mac Ùisdein, whom he had never met, and of the note of hand that had begun the search, and set the thought down nowhere, there being no place in the room's own business for a southern house's own ruin, however plainly it answered a question the vein had been asking the whole kingdom for years. He thought too, reading it, of the Board's own surveyor who had drawn the search's last sheet and then resigned her post to teach at the druid school at Caol shean, a woman he had never met either and knew only by the name a Board clerk had once let fall to him in passing, and wondered, folding the notice away, whether a surveyor who had walked forty-one dry shafts for a house now selling what little the shafts had left it found any particular satisfaction in the selling, or only the same flat notice the rest of the kingdom was left to read.

Copper was found that same year in the hills behind Àth fhiadhaich, in the eastern country, and the Board opened a working there within the season, hiring men laid off from the Sloc Mòr's own thinning galleries at a wage the Board's own notice called fair and the men themselves, by what word of it reached Caol mhòr, called better than nothing, which in that season was the higher praise. It was not the vein, and no one at Àth fhiadhaich troubled to call it so; the town's own house of the Old Spirits, keeping the oldest folk rite on the island by the ford where its river fishers still blessed their nets, gave the new working no blessing when it was asked, the house's own keeper telling the Board's factor, by the account that reached the north secondhand and perhaps not word for word, that copper dug for a wage was copper dug like any other stone, and that a house which blessed the fishers' nets for their own sake, not the catch's, saw no cause to bless a working got up only to keep men fed who had lost a richer trade. The Board took the answer without argument and worked the copper regardless, blessed or not, the men themselves glad of the wage whatever the house at Àth fhiadhaich thought fit to say over it.

Frayne set both notices, the last shafts sold and the copper unblessed, into his own private book that autumn, beside the wool-buyer's fact about Baile Mòr dhomhain and the letter from Muileann shean, and read the four together one evening at his own table, the room at Caol mhòr empty around him and the lamp burned low. A kingdom whose founding wealth was failing, he wrote, might sell its last shafts to itself and dig for copper it could not bless and still call itself the same kingdom it had been the year before, since a crown changes nothing of what it stands over merely by changing the head it sits on; but a mill that had stood three years building and a court that had ruled for it once, however grudged the ruling, were the north's own doing entire, owing nothing to any southern coin or any southern charter's goodwill, and he judged, closing the book that night, that this was the larger fact of the two, whatever the council's own minutes chose to spend their ink on.

The first bale went out from Doire ghlas on the coal line in the last days of an Lùnastal the following summer, the ruling near a year settled and the Board's own factors at Àth leathan weighing it onto the wagon without comment, as they weighed any other cargo that paid the rate. Frayne rode down to see it loaded, having business in the west that week regardless and no wish to let the day pass unwitnessed, and stood at the wagon's side as the bale went up, the grey twill wrapped in sacking against the road's own dust, the white thread at its hidden selvedge the only mark that told a stranger's eye it had not come off a southern loom at all. Two of the mill's own weavers had ridden down from Doire ghlas to see the bale off, young women neither of whom had been born when Frayne first rode the Carters' Road with the wool carts, and one of them asked him, watching the sacking go up, whether the cloth would fetch a fair price at Muileann òg or a mean one, the same question the fair had answered every year of his own working life without ever quite answering it the same way twice. He told her he did not know, and that no clerk of the Moot's had ever known it in advance, only after, but that a bale which travelled at all, carried by right and not by grudging leave, was worth more to the room at Caol mhòr than any single season's price could be, whatever that price turned out to be. She said this sounded to her like a speaker's own answer, given to spare her a plainer one, and he allowed, laughing despite himself for the first time that week, that it was exactly that, and that a speaker who had spent thirty years in the trade owed her at least the honesty of saying so. The factor who signed the wagon's own manifest was the same man, by chance or by the Board's own thin bench of factors at that station, who had refused the first load the year before, and he gave the bale no more notice now than he gave the crates of nails beside it, which Frayne judged, watching the wagon pull away toward the south and the fair it would reach in two days' time, the truest measure the Board could have given of what the ruling had actually won them, the same plain weight any other cargo carried, asked for once and carried without a second asking.
