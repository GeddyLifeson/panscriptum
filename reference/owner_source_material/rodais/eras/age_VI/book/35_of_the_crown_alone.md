# Of the Crown Alone

*SE 58 an t-Sultain – SE 60 an Lùnastal*

> The charter names Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn a carrier of An Crùn's people and their goods. A carrier that will take one man's coin and refuse another's answers to no charter at all.
>
> — Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, ruling given at Seann Bhral in the west, {{date:V-0203}}

## I. Of the Market that Passed the Fair

The news came north the way most news came to Caol mhòr that decade, a season behind the fact and by way of a carter who had heard it from a factor who had heard it from someone nearer the matter: that the market day at Baile Mòr dhomhain, on the deep river in the hill country, had for two years running drawn more carts than Fèill Muileann òg, the old fair the whole island had measured its trade by since before the restored kingdom had a name of its own. Daniel Frayne heard it from a wool-buyer at Tional nan Tuathach's own table, a man come down from the hills to sell what Baile Mòr dhomhain's own market no longer had room to take, and turned it over a while before he wrote it into the room's ledger of the year's business, under no heading that fit it well, since it was not a Moot matter and yet seemed to him, turning three-and-fifty that autumn, a thing the room ought to have set down somewhere.

"The vein feeds fewer hands each year," he said to Mary Coyle's old clerk, who still kept the room's figures though the chair was Frayne's own now, "and the hill country feeds more of them than it did. A market goes where the coin already is, and stops waiting on a fair that has had the same coin coming to it every year since anyone living can remember." The clerk said this was truer than the room had leisure to argue with that morning, there being three causes waiting and a letter from Muileann shean not yet opened, and Frayne let the matter go for the day, though he kept the wool-buyer's fact in his own private book beside Clàr nan Leòn, a habit he had never broken since the winter he first began it.

The crown changed hands that same season, and of it the room at Caol mhòr knew only what any household on the island knew: that Eòghan's reign had ended, at two-and-sixty, the notice giving no word of how, and that Mairead, eight-and-thirty, had been crowned at Cathair dhearg by Macha's rite, as every ruler of the restored line had been crowned before her. The room's clerks noted the day and the name and went back to their own ledgers, Tional nan Tuathach having no place in a rite it was never asked to, nor coin to spare for a journey south to watch it, and Frayne, reading the notice a second time before he filed it, found in it nothing to say beyond what the notice already said, a fact and no more, the kind he had learned young to set down plainly and leave alone. He noted only, in his own book and nowhere else, that Eòghan had held the throne since Frayne was a young man at Agnes Pike's benches, and that the coin that winter would be minted new with another name on it.

The mint's own notice reached Caol mhòr some weeks after the crowning, later than the news of it had come by mouth, and told the room only what such notices ever told it, that the die was changed and the old coin still good until the new one was struck in sufficient number, a fact of no more moment to a clerk's ledger than the changing of a season, save that Frayne, weighing one of the last old coins in his own hand before he passed it across the table for a debt owed Tional nan Tuathach, thought it a strange thing that a face stamped on metal should outlast, in most households' own reckoning, the man who had worn it, no household at Caol mhòr troubling any longer to name Eòghan at its own table where once his name had been the one every household knew.

He had seen Fèill Muileann òg only a few times in his own life, the first with the wool carts down Rathad nan Cairtearan, and he remembered it as a crowd too large for any one man to take the measure of, the factors' own tents strung the length of a field that seemed, to a boy from the north, to have no far edge at all. He asked the wool-buyer, before the man rode on, whether the fair still looked so to a stranger's eye, and the man told him that it did, near enough, to a stranger who had never seen it in its better years, but that a man who had stood in that field every autumn for thirty years running, as the wool-buyer had, could see the gaps now where the tents had once stood three deep, gaps the fair's own wardens filled each year with a wider aisle and called it room for the crowd to move, though the crowd had not grown to want the room.

The letter from Muileann shean, when he opened it, asked a different question of him entire, and one he had some part in answering. The mill-town in the north had that spring opened a school of its own for the sons and daughters of hewers the Guild would not bind, the Guild having voted, for the first time since its charter, to bind no new apprentices at all beyond the sons and daughters of its own masters, the vein by then thinning under enough of the old galleries that a guild grown used to feeding every willing pair of hands had, that year, more hands than it any longer had work to give them. Muileann shean's school meant to teach those turned away to read, to reckon, and to survey, on the reasoning of the shire's own maor, who had written the letter himself, that a hewer's child shut out of the pit might still find his father's own trade from the surface rather than the gallery, chain in hand instead of pick, if someone thought to teach him the chain. He asked Tional nan Tuathach for primers, there being none in the north fit for the purpose and no coin in the shire's own dues to have new ones bound.

Frayne read the letter twice and then a third time, standing at the window of the room he had sat in as a clerk for more years than he had sat in it as speaker, and thought of Agnes Pike, dead that same year, who had taught him his letters in a byre at Doire ghlas on slates the school could not always find chalk for, and of the room she had kept, and of what she had once told him, a boy of ten with more questions than sense, that a trade a man could carry in his own head weighed nothing on the road and could not be taken from him by any board that ever sat. He wrote back to Muileann shean that he would bind the primers himself, at his own bench, and send them north with the first cart that had room, and did so within the month, forty copies of a reading-book he had set the type for himself twenty years before and kept the forms for since, the ink still smelling of the press when the bundle went out on the cart. The shire's maor wrote back in the spring to say that the eleven children the school had opened with had grown, within the year, past thirty, and that three of the first eleven had already gone south to the ports to hire themselves out as chain-hands, the surveying trade being one, as it turned out, the ports had more use for than Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own vein-towns did just then.

## II. Of the Doire ghlas Bolt and the Suit

The mills at Doire ghlas were three years building by the time they turned their first cloth, raised with Tional nan Tuathach's own money on ground An Eaglais had let stand idle since An Dealachadh, the first works the north had ever owned that owed nothing to the vein and nothing to any southern charter either. Frayne had sat on the room's own committee that voted the money out of Tional nan Tuathach's slender chest, a vote he had argued for on grounds he set down plainly in the room's minute, that a people who wove their own cloth need not buy the south's, and that a mill built with the north's own coin answered to the north's own reckoning and to no factor's. The first bolt came off the looms in the last week of an t-Iuchar, a grey twill with a white thread run through the selvedge, the same mark the weavers of Doire ghlas had first been let sell under their own name at the fair some years before, and the mill's own overseer sent word to Caol mhòr the same week, a short letter that said only that the cloth was good, better than the south's own factors had reckoned a northern loom could turn out, and that the mill stood ready to sell it wherever a buyer's coin was willing.

The buyer's coin, when it came, found no way to travel. Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own factors at Àth leathan, the railhead nearest the north, asked to carry the first wagon-load down Slighe a' Ghuail to Fèill Muileann òg, where the southern trade was and where the cloth would fetch a price the north's own markets could not match, refused the load without troubling to give a reason beyond the one Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn had given every Tuathaich cargo since the line was laid, that the line carried what Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn judged fit to carry and answered to no one for the judging. The mill's overseer wrote to Caol mhòr a second time, angrier than the first, and Frayne read the letter to the room himself, standing at the same table Mary Coyle had once stood at to open Clàr nan Leòn.

"We have asked Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn before to carry what we make," he told the room, "and been told before that the asking was not Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's to answer. I mean, this time, to ask a court instead, since a board that will not answer a letter may yet answer a summons." The room's older clerks, who remembered every earlier asking and every earlier refusal, doubted a Dia-thìreach court would hear a Tuathaich complaint any more kindly than Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn had, and said so plainly, but the room voted the coin for the journey all the same, there being, as one of them put it, little enough left to lose in the asking that the mill had not already lost in the refusing.

Frayne rode west that autumn to Seann Bhral, the circuit's own western seat on the coast, three days' road from Caol mhòr and one he had never ridden before, carrying the mill's own ledgers and Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own letter of refusal, kept whole rather than answered, in the same satchel he carried his private book in. He was four-and-fifty that year, and told the clerk who took his papers at the courthouse door, a young man who had plainly never before entered a Tuathaich name on the circuit's own roll, that he had come to bring a cause and not a petition, the distinction, he said, mattering rather more to him than the clerk seemed at first to grasp it did.

The justice who heard it was Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, four-and-forty that year, a man who had sat the western circuit for a dozen years and had, by his own account given later to a clerk who asked him, never before had a Tuathaich cause brought properly before his own bench rather than settled or dropped before it reached him. He heard Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own factor first, a well-dressed man from Ceann leathan who argued that the line was Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own property, raised with Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own coin and An Crùn's own charter, and that a carrier might choose its own cargo as freely as a merchant chose his own stock. Ruairidh mac Phàdraig asked him, when he had finished, whether the charter that raised the line had named Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn a merchant or a carrier, and the factor, after a silence the court's own clerk noted in his record without comment, allowed that the charter's own words called it a carrier, there being no way to answer otherwise with the charter lying open on the bench between them.

Frayne spoke for the mill himself, having no advocate Tional nan Tuathach's own coin could stretch to and no wish, he told the court, to hire one, since the facts of the matter were plain enough that a plain man could lay them out without a trained tongue to dress them. He read Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's letter of refusal aloud, word for word, and set beside it the mill's own account of the coin it had offered to pay for the carriage, a rate no lower than any southern mill paid for the same road, and asked the court only the one question the room at Caol mhòr had sent him west to ask, whether a carrier chartered by An Crùn to carry for An Crùn's own people might refuse one people among them and take another's coin freely.

The factor pressed his own point a second way before the court rose that first day, saying that Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn had carried Tuathaich cargo before, wool by the cartload in years the road had allowed it, and that the refusal touched only the line, Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own rails being a different property from Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own carts and answerable, in the factor's own reading of the charter, to a different clause entire. Ruairidh mac Phàdraig had the charter read aloud a second time at that, the whole of it, clause by clause, by the court's own clerk, a reading that took the better part of an hour and left the factor, by its end, with no clause left to point to that named the rails apart from the carts, the charter's own drafters having written, as the justice observed when the reading was done, of "the carriage of An Crùn's people and their goods" without once troubling to say by what wheel or what rail the carriage was to be made. Frayne, listening from the bench set aside for him, wrote the justice's own words into his private book that same evening, judging them, he told the room afterward, worth keeping whole rather than in his own paraphrase.

Ruairidh mac Phàdraig gave his ruling three days later, at the same bench, in words the court's clerk copied fair and Frayne carried home whole, that Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn was a carrier of An Crùn and bound, by An Crùn's own charter, to carry for any who paid the rate it set, whatever the cargo's maker, and that it had no more right to refuse the Doire ghlas mill's cloth than it had to refuse a southern factor's grain. Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn appealed the ruling to the council at Cathair dhearg within the month, as Frayne had expected it would, and the appeal sat unanswered on the council's own table through that winter and the spring following, until it was let lapse in the summer without a word given either way, the council's own minute of the matter, when the room at Caol mhòr at last obtained a copy of it, noting only that the matter had not been reached in the session's business, a phrase Frayne read three times before he understood it meant the council did not mean to reach it at all.

"It is not a victory they have given us," he told the room, the letter open before him, "only a defeat they have declined to trouble themselves with undoing. I will take it. I have taken smaller things for larger names before now." The room's older clerks, who had doubted him at the start of the asking, said nothing against him that day, and one of them, the same who had first doubted a Dia-thìreach court would hear a Tuathaich cause kindly, wrote in the room's own minute, in a hand Frayne kept and read again more than once in the years after, that it was the first ruling of any Dia-thìreach court Tional nan Tuathach had ever won standing, and that a first was worth the keeping whatever came after it.

## III. Of the Copper and the Last Shafts

The same seasons that carried Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own refusal to Seann Bhral and back carried other news south of the north entirely, news Frayne read of rather than rode to. Taigh Mhic Ùisdein, which had sold half its own share in An Sloc Mòr some years before to answer a search that had found nothing, sold what remained of its working shafts to An Crùn outright the next summer, keeping only its share in the spoil-heaps and the quarry-stone the mine's own waste still yielded, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own clerks, moving the last of the house's papers out of the stone house at Cathair mhòr, wrote in their minute that the vein was thenceforth worked in An Crùn's name alone, no house's own charter standing beside An Crùn's upon it any longer. Frayne, reading the notice at Caol mhòr, thought of Alasdair mac Ùisdein, whom he had never met, and of the note of hand that had begun the search, and set the thought down nowhere, there being no place in the room's own business for a southern house's own ruin, however plainly it answered a question the vein had been asking the whole kingdom for years. He thought too, reading it, of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own surveyor who had drawn the search's last sheet and then resigned her post to teach at Scoiltean nan Draoidhean at Caol shean, a woman he had never met either and knew only by the name a clerk of Comhairle nam Mèinnean had once let fall to him in passing, and wondered, folding the notice away, whether a surveyor who had walked forty-one dry shafts for a house now selling what little the shafts had left it found any particular satisfaction in the selling, or only the same flat notice the rest of the kingdom was left to read.

Copper was found that same year in the hills behind Àth fhiadhaich, in the eastern country, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean opened a working there within the season, hiring men laid off from An Sloc Mòr's own thinning galleries at a wage Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own notice called fair and the men themselves, by what word of it reached Caol mhòr, called better than nothing, which in that season was the higher praise. It was not the vein, and no one at Àth fhiadhaich troubled to call it so; the town's own house of Na Seann Spioradan, keeping the oldest folk rite on the island by the ford where its river fishers still blessed their nets, gave the new working no blessing when it was asked, the house's own keeper telling Comhairle nam Mèinnean's factor, by the account that reached the north secondhand and perhaps not word for word, that copper dug for a wage was copper dug like any other stone, and that a house which blessed the fishers' nets for their own sake, not the catch's, saw no cause to bless a working got up only to keep men fed who had lost a richer trade. Comhairle nam Mèinnean took the answer without argument and worked the copper regardless, blessed or not, the men themselves glad of the wage whatever the house at Àth fhiadhaich thought fit to say over it.

Frayne set both notices, the last shafts sold and the copper unblessed, into his own private book that autumn, beside the wool-buyer's fact about Baile Mòr dhomhain and the letter from Muileann shean, and read the four together one evening at his own table, the room at Caol mhòr empty around him and the lamp burned low. A kingdom whose founding wealth was failing, he wrote, might sell its last shafts to itself and dig for copper it could not bless and still call itself the same kingdom it had been the year before, since a crown changes nothing of what it stands over merely by changing the head it sits on; but a mill that had stood three years building and a court that had ruled for it once, however grudged the ruling, were the north's own doing entire, owing nothing to any southern coin or any southern charter's goodwill, and he judged, closing the book that night, that this was the larger fact of the two, whatever the council's own minutes chose to spend their ink on.

The first bale went out from Doire ghlas on Slighe a' Ghuail in the last days of an Lùnastal the following summer, the ruling near a year settled and Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own factors at Àth leathan weighing it onto the wagon without comment, as they weighed any other cargo that paid the rate. Frayne rode down to see it loaded, having business in the west that week regardless and no wish to let the day pass unwitnessed, and stood at the wagon's side as the bale went up, the grey twill wrapped in sacking against the road's own dust, An Snàthainn Geal at its hidden selvedge the only mark that told a stranger's eye it had not come off a southern loom at all. Two of the mill's own weavers had ridden down from Doire ghlas to see the bale off, young women neither of whom had been born when Frayne first rode Rathad nan Cairtearan with the wool carts, and one of them asked him, watching the sacking go up, whether the cloth would fetch a fair price at Muileann òg or a mean one, the same question the fair had answered every year of his own working life without ever quite answering it the same way twice. He told her he did not know, and that no clerk of Tional nan Tuathach's had ever known it in advance, only after, but that a bale which travelled at all, carried by right and not by grudging leave, was worth more to the room at Caol mhòr than any single season's price could be, whatever that price turned out to be. She said this sounded to her like a speaker's own answer, given to spare her a plainer one, and he allowed, laughing despite himself for the first time that week, that it was exactly that, and that a speaker who had spent thirty years in the trade owed her at least the honesty of saying so. The factor who signed the wagon's own manifest was the same man, by chance or by Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own thin bench of factors at that station, who had refused the first load the year before, and he gave the bale no more notice now than he gave the crates of nails beside it, which Frayne judged, watching the wagon pull away toward the south and the fair it would reach in two days' time, the truest measure Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn could have given of what the ruling had actually won them, the same plain weight any other cargo carried, asked for once and carried without a second asking.

## IV. Of the Widows' Bench

Mary Coyle died at Caol mhòr that same summer, in the second week of an t-Iuchar, in the house above the harbour she had kept since before the westerly took her husband off Cuan bheag, nine-and-seventy years old and five years out of the chair. Tional nan Tuathach's day-book gives the day and her age and the hour the bell was rung for her, and then, in Frayne's own hand rather than the clerk's, a single line more: that she had asked, some weeks before, that no one read anything over her but the names.

He understood the asking when he went up to the house the morning after and her daughter-in-law gave him the sheet. It was the sheet Mary Coyle had written for the mason forty-one years before, the eleven drowned men of Cuan bheag set down in the order the boats went out and not the order the sea gave the bodies back, the paper gone soft as cloth along its folds, and beneath the eleven, in the same hand grown older and less steady, the names of the widows who had sat on the bench by the door with her in the first years, each with the year she had died written beside it. Agnes Tulloch's was the last of them, three winters before. There was room on the sheet below it and nothing written there.

The burial was set for the fourth day, and Tional nan Tuathach's business stopped for it as it had stopped for Walter Hale's, without a vote taken to stop it. The boats came in from Cuan bheag on the morning tide, fewer than had come for Hale, for the northern crews were fewer than they had been, and the carters off Rathad nan Cairtearan came up with their wagons empty, it being the dead season between the wool and the harvest. Men came from Ros mhòr who had sailed with her to Seann Bhral and been turned away there, three of them, old now, and one of them carried the tiller of the boat she had sailed, which had been blessed fourth in the row at the first Fleadh na Mara and had lain on a Ros mhòr hard since her last season, and set it against the wall of the burying-ground without explaining it to anyone.

She was laid in the same ground as Hale, a little lower on the slope, within sight of Tional nan Tuathach's house and of the bench by its door. The priest who read over her read, as she had asked, only names: the eleven, and the widows after them, and then, because her daughter-in-law put the sheet into his hand at the graveside and said nothing, her own. It took less time than any burial the ground had seen, and the crowd, which had come expecting a speaker's burial and the long words that go with one, stood on afterward in the heat a while as if the thing were not yet done, until the Cuan bheag men began to walk back down to their boats and the rest followed.

Frayne did not speak at the grave. He spoke that evening in the room, when Tional nan Tuathach sat again for the first time since her death, and the minute keeps what he said nearly whole, for the clerk that night set down every word of it, as David Hale had once set down every word of hers. He said that the register of the western pits, which the room called Clàr nan Leòn and the carters called Mary Coyle's book, held that summer seventy-three names, and that every one of them had come to the room on a family's own word, carried by a neighbour or walked in by the widow herself, because Mary Coyle had held from its first page that Comhairle nam Mèinnean's count was Comhairle nam Mèinnean's business and the families' count was the room's. He said that the widows' rule she and Agnes Tulloch had moved in the twenty-fourth year still stood as a standing rule and not a courtesy, and that the bench by the door had been sat on that morning by four widows of the western pits, not one of whom had known her before she opened the book. He said that she had asked the south, in Tional nan Tuathach's name, for the timber at Àth leathan and for a plain reading of the factor rule, and been given silence on both, and that she had gone on asking because, as she told him once, a room that stops asking has already agreed to the answer.

Then he took from his coat the sheet her daughter-in-law had given him, and asked the room whether it would have the sheet kept in Tional nan Tuathach's own chest beside the register or given back to the family. The room voted, without a count, that it be kept, and that the space beneath the last name be left as she had left it. The clerk entered the sheet in the day-book as received from the family of Mary Coyle, speaker of Tional nan Tuathach from the thirty-third year to the fifty-fifth, and laid it in the chest with the census tallies she had pointed to on the morning she laid the chair down.

No notice of her death went south. Tional nan Tuathach sent none, having no office in the south that would have known what to do with it, and the south asked none. Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd for that year, when Tormod Pinn came to close it, holds no line for her; An Leabharlann's first knowledge of her death came late, in a carter's passing word to one of Mòrag nic Coinnich's clerks, and Mòrag, entering it, could give it no day. Tional nan Tuathach's book gives the day, the hour, the age and the names, and keeps them, with the rest of what the north has kept, in the north.
