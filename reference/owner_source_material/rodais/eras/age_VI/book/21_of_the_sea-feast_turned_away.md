# Of the Sea-Feast Turned Away

*SE 24 am Màrt – SE 26 an Gearran*

> We ask that the widows of the Cuan bheag drowning be heard in this Moot on the same footing as any man who has paid his shilling, and heard first, since it is their dead we are speaking of and not our own.
>
> — a motion of the Moot at Caol mhòr, {{date:V-0099}}, entire, as the Library renders it

## I. Of the Dock, the Twentieth, and the Names Given to Children

In these years the fair at Muileann òg was not the only place the shire's coin went to make stone stand up out of mud. At Ros gheal on the north-central coast the town gave over its whole spring to a dock built long enough to take three grain-ships lying abreast, for the Hawk country sent its barley and its oats down by cart no longer but by water, and the water paid better. The Hawk houses came down from Cnoc bheag to bless the stones before the first ship warped in, and asked, as the price of the blessing, a tithe of whatever the dock earned. The shire's factor haggled them down to a twentieth, and paid it, and has paid it every quarter since, so that to this day the clerks of Ros gheal call the due simply the Hawk's, and few of them could now say why a fishing town owes a grain due to a bird that flies inland.

It was a green early summer, that same year, when the shunting families of Muileann chrom, who lived apart in their rows beside the little engines and were counted apart in every roll the council kept, had a great crop of children born to them all at once, more than the row had cradles for. Two of that crowd were named Seachran and Brean, and of them, and of the rest born that season, this book says nothing more, for they had done nothing yet, and a chronicle that ran ahead of a child's life would not be a chronicle at all.

Further south that year, Seann Bhral on the southern coast — not the older Seann Bhral of the west, which is a different town and always has been — passed Ros dhomhain in the weight of fish landed, and its curing-sheds, which had been four, became eleven, and ran the whole length of the harbour so that a stranger walking it after dark could follow the smoke and not see the water at all. And near Seann Chwen, on the same coast, a travelling show pitched its poles for the first time, calling itself by a name that means, near enough, the Incomprehensible Travelling Circus, and the clerk who wrote its arrival into the local roll noted that he could not say what its acts were meant to show, nor, he added, could the crowd that filled the field to watch them, and came back the second night larger than the first.

These are the things the Library keeps of that year in the south: a dock, a due, a crowd of children too young to be named for anything but themselves, more fish than the year before, and a show nobody could explain and everybody paid to see twice. The clerk at Seann Chwen who could not say what the acts were meant to show tried, in his own defence, to set down what he had seen: a woman who walked a rope strung between two masts with her eyes bound in cloth; a man who ate fire from a spoon and spat smoke for a full minute after; a dog that counted to nine by barking and never, that the clerk could tell, miscounted. He gave it up as a bad job and wrote instead that the show had taken four shillings from Seann Chwen in one night and would likely take four more before it moved on, which was the kind of fact a roll could hold.

In the north the year is kept by a different kind of ledger. It begins with who stood up to speak.

## II. Of Mary Coyle Standing Up

The Moot's house at Caol mhòr had one door, low enough that a tall man ducked for it out of habit whether the lintel needed it or not, and inside, a table that had been a ship's hatch-cover before somebody planed it flat and gave it legs, the grain of it still showing the pattern of the rope that had once run over it in a coil. A stove at the far end took the worst of the chill off the room and none of the rest; men and women kept their coats on through every sitting from am Faoilleach to an Cèitean, and the breath of forty people in the small room fogged the one window until David Hale kept a rag on a string beside it, to wipe a clear patch for whatever daylight was left by the time the meeting rose.

Walter Hale sat at the head of the table still, an old man now, his hearing going, and he let the meeting run long past its hour because he had stopped trusting himself to know when an hour was up. He had chaired the Moot since before most of the room now sitting in it could walk, and the younger clerks had begun, that year, to write his rulings down twice, once as he gave them and once as David Hale judged he had meant them, in case the two should ever need reconciling.

Mary Coyle had come to every sitting of the Moot since the westerly took her husband and ten other men off Cuan bheag five years before, and for five years she had sat at the back near the door with the other widows. There were, that winter, eleven of them who came regular, in a pew of their own though nobody had built it as a pew, simply the bench nearest the door where a woman could slip out early if a child needed fetching. She had passed notes up when she had something to say, because that was how it was done, and nobody had told her different, and she had not thought to ask. Agnes Tulloch, who sat beside her most nights and had lost a brother on the same boat, used to fold the notes into the smallest square her fingers could manage before they went forward, as if a small enough fold might make the asking smaller too.

That night the matter before the Moot was the grain-ships. Ros gheal had agreed a due with the Hawk houses that the widows of Cuan bheag had heard nothing about until the shilling was already gone from the shire's purse, a purse that took its silver, in part, from a levy on every fishing family in the north, widows included, for the upkeep of the harbour lights. The man reading the account, a Moot clerk named for the evening only, ran through the figures in the flat unhurried voice of someone who did not expect to be stopped, and would not have thought to stop himself if he had known what was coming.

"We pay into that purse the same as any man," Mary Coyle said, standing, "and we have never once been asked what it should buy."

The room did not so much fall silent as forget, for a moment, what it had been doing. Nobody at the table had heard her speak before without a note going up first, folded small, for a man near the front to read out in her stead, in his own voice, so that the words arrived at the table already once removed from the mouth that made them.

Hale cupped a hand to his better ear. "Say that again, and stand where I can hear you."

She came round the table, past the stove, past the clerk with his flat voice stopped mid-figure, and said it again, louder, close enough now that Hale did not need the hand at his ear. "We pay into that purse the same as any man. Every quarter, out of what little the season leaves us. And no one has asked a widow of Cuan bheag what she would have that money spent on, though it is our dead the harbour lights are meant to warn other men's boats away from the rocks that took them."

"That is a fair question," Hale said, when he had turned it over, "and I do not know that anyone has ever put it to this table before."

"No one has," said Mary Coyle. "I have sat at the back of this room five years and watched other men's notes read out and voted on, and I thought, tonight, that I would rather be wrong to my own face than right in somebody else's mouth."

There was a laugh at that, not unkind, and one or two of the older men who had known Thomas Coyle rapped their knuckles on the table the way they would for a man's speech they approved. David Hale, the old man's grand-nephew, kept the day-book at the Moot's elbow, and he wrote down what she said nearly word for word, because it was the first time in the minutes that any woman's speech was set down whole rather than summed in a line — "the widow Coyle spoke" — as the clerks had always done before, when they troubled to note a woman's speaking at all.

It was resolved, before that sitting rose, that the widows of the drowned should be heard in the Moot on the same footing as any man who had paid his shilling, and heard first when the matter touched their own dead. Agnes Tulloch, from the bench by the door, was the one who moved that it be written down as a standing rule and not a courtesy for one night only, and old Hale, who had chaired the Moot longer than either woman had been widowed, said he could not see why it had taken the room so long to think of it, which was as near as he came, in front of the whole Moot, to admitting that it had not occurred to him either.

David Hale copied the motion twice, once into the day-book and once onto a loose sheet that Mary Coyle folded into her shawl and carried home, because she did not yet trust a book she could not open herself to keep a thing said.

She did not sit at the back again.

## III. Of the Muster for Seann Bhral

It was old Hale himself, turning the matter over by his fire that winter, who put the thought in her head, though he never claimed it after. "Your grandfather's grandfather," he said, "used to tell of a feast the crews kept before ever they came to this coast, at the turn of a year, for the safe return of the boats. He said it came from the harbours of the people who lived here before us, the ones who worship the sea itself, or a lord of it. He said our own folk learned it from them, on the passage over, long before any of us were born on this side of the water."

"Do we still keep it?"

"We keep the *shape* of it. A blessing at the boats, in an Gearran, before the season proper starts. But the old feast, the one it comes from — that they keep still, he said, at a house on the west coast. Seann Bhral. Not this one." He tapped the table, meaning the near one, the fishing port to the south they had all heard of that year on account of its curing-sheds. "The other Seann Bhral. The first one."

Mary Coyle turned it over for the better part of a month, and what she found, asking among the older crews, bore Hale out: that the house of Manannan's order at Seann Bhral in the west was reckoned the oldest on that coast, and that some memory persisted, thin as a rope worn to its last strands, that the Tuathaich forebears had once been welcome at its winter feast of the sea, in the years just after the Crossing, before the island had settled into a kingdom with a north side and a south.

She put it to the Moot in am Faoilleach: that a fleet of the northern boats should sail west-about, round the capes, to keep the feast where it had first been kept, as their own people's memory said they were owed the right to do. The Moot argued it two full sittings before it agreed. Nobody spoke against the sea-feast itself; half the room thought it a fool's errand all the same, to sail eleven boats round the worst capes on the island in the dead of winter for a welcome that was not certain, and said so, plainly, and were not wrong to wonder it. Mary Coyle did not argue that they were wrong. She said only that a certainty was not what she was asking the Moot to send her after, and that she would go herself whatever the vote, with whoever chose to come, and the Moot, having heard that, voted to send its blessing after her rather than let her sail without it.

It was not a large fleet in the end. Eleven boats put out from Caol mhòr and Cuan bheag and Achadh àrsaidh on a morning cold enough to freeze the spray on the gunwales before it dried, laden with little beyond themselves and the coin for the harbour dues at the far end, a keg of salt fish apiece to see them there and back, and, in the bottom of Mary Coyle's own boat, the folded sheet from the Moot's day-book that named her the one the north had sent. She sailed in the leading boat, not because she could handle a boat better than any man aboard — she could not, and said so plainly to the skipper who offered her the tiller out of courtesy the first morning, and never offered it again — but because it had been her word that sent them, and she judged, rightly, that a thing said ought to be stood behind by the one who said it, in the bow if not at the helm.

The passage took the better part of four days, coasting inside the worst of the weather, putting in at night wherever a harbour would have them, which was not everywhere; the harbour-master at Ros bheag, on their second night's road, would not let them tie up at the quay proper and sent them instead to a mooring buoy off the point, with a lantern for courtesy and nothing warmer than that. Two nights they lay at anchor in open bays with the watch kept turn and turn about against the swell, wrapped in whatever oilskin and wool could be spared, and men who had fished together twenty years found out, in those two nights, things about each other's snoring and each other's praying that they had somehow never learned in twenty years of ordinary weather. On the third a squall out of the south-west put green water over the bow of the smallest boat and near enough finished it before the crew bailed her clear with anything that would hold water, boots included; Mary Coyle, in the boat behind, watched the smaller craft vanish twice into the trough of a wave and come up twice still afloat, and said afterward that she had made herself a promise in that quarter-hour that she kept the rest of her life and never once told anyone the wording of. Nobody was lost. It was, more than one of them said afterward, the hardest four days most of them had known since the Crossing itself, which none of them remembered and all of them had heard told often enough to measure everything else against.

## IV. Of the House That Said the Sea Was the Same

They came into Seann Bhral on the eve of the feast, eleven boats riding low with salt on everything, and moored where the harbour-master's boy showed them, at the outer wall, away from the fishing fleet proper. The house of Manannan's order stood above the harbour on its own terrace, older than anything else in the town by the look of its stonework, its doorposts carved with a wave-pattern worn nearly smooth by five ages of hands touching it on the way in.

Word of eleven Tuathaich boats standing in had gone up to the house before the last of them was moored, carried by a harbour boy quicker with his feet than his manners, and by the time Mary Coyle and two of the older skippers climbed the terrace steps a small crowd of the town's own fisherfolk had gathered at the wall below to see what would come of it, some curious and some, by their faces, already certain.

The keeper who came out to them was a tall, spare man, not unkind in his manner, who heard Mary Coyle out to the end without once interrupting her, which she took, wrongly, for a good sign. She had rehearsed the asking the whole four days of the passage, turning the words over at the tiller and in the night watches until she judged she had them worn smooth, and she gave it to him now much as she had made it to herself: that the Tuathaich forebears had kept the feast of the sea at this house once, in the first years after the Crossing, before ever there was a kingdom with a north side to it; that the memory of it was thin in the north now but not gone; and that eleven boats had sailed a hard four days on the strength of that thin memory, asking nothing more than to stand at the harbour and see the rite done, and perhaps to say a word of their own over it, if the house would permit that much.

"You have the right of the history," he said, when she had finished. "It is true that in the first years the harbours took in whoever came off a boat, and asked no questions of blood before they let a man to the fire. I do not deny you that. But the order that keeps this house now keeps it for those who hold the rite of Manannan as it is held today, by right of blood and by the custodian's book, and that is not a thing four days' hard sailing can put right, however justly you sailed them."

"We are not asking for the rite," said Mary Coyle. "We are asking for the feast. Our own priests can say what needs saying over it."

"The feast is the rite," the keeper said, not unkindly, and that was the whole of his answer, said once and not repeated, though she asked it three further ways before she judged that a fourth would only make a hard thing harder to carry home. "The sea is the same sea it always was," he told her at the last, seeing her face, and meaning, she understood after, to soften it rather than close it further. "It is the house that is not the same house it was."

One of the skippers who had climbed the terrace with her, a hard, plain man named for nothing gentler than his trade, asked the keeper straight out whether it was blood he meant, or coin, since the house looked in no want of either. The keeper did not take offence at it. "Both, in their way," he said. "The order keeps this house on tithes from families who have held the rite four ages and more. If I let in every boat that could show a claim on the old welcome, I would have no house left to keep it in by the second winter, and no order behind me either. I do not say that gladly. I say it because it is true, and I would rather tell you a true hard thing than a soft one that costs me nothing."

She did not answer that, then. She has been reported, by more than one of the crews who stood behind her on the quay that evening, to have looked a long while at the carved doorposts before she turned back to the boats, the wave-pattern worn smooth by hands she would never be able to count and none of which were now hers to add to, and to have said nothing at all until she was aboard, at which point she said only that they would sail on the morning tide, and slept, or lay still enough to be thought asleep, through a night none of the rest of the eleven boats' crews slept much through at all. Below on the harbour wall a few of the town's own fisherfolk had stayed on past dark, and one woman among them, whose name the Library has not kept, came down to the boats with a jug of something hot before she went home, and said only that she was sorry for it, which was more than the house above had said, and which Mary Coyle remembered, she told people afterward, longer than she remembered the keeper's own words.

## V. Of the Feast They Made Their Own

The passage home took five days against a headwind that had not troubled them going out, and nobody aboard the eleven boats said much of anything about Seann Bhral for the length of it, beyond what was needed to work the sail and the helm. It was on the third day, off the northern capes, that one of the Achadh àrsaidh men, an old hand who had said little the whole voyage, asked Mary Coyle plainly what she meant to do now, since the west would not have them.

"Keep it anyway," she said. "Not their feast. Ours."

She did not mean, and said she did not mean, to invent a rite out of nothing; she meant the blessing of the boats that the north already kept in a thin, half-remembered way each spring at the little stone church above Cnoc ghorm, and to make of it, deliberately and in full, the feast that Seann Bhral had refused them the whole of. It was decided among the crews before they raised Caol mhòr again, and the decision cost nothing but the saying of it, which by then Mary Coyle had some practice in.

Word of it went round the north coast faster than the eleven boats themselves had sailed, carried by every crew that had been on the passage and told, in every kitchen it reached, a little larger than it had left the last one; by the time an Gearran came round, boats were promising to come from as far as Inis mhin's country, a fair way further than any of the eleven had gone west. On the second Sunday of an Gearran, in SE 26, the boats of the north coast that could be got to Cnoc ghorm in a day's sail or a morning's cart were drawn up on the shingle below the church, more than fifty of them by the count David Hale kept for the day-book, far more than had ever gathered at Cnoc ghorm for the old thin blessing in any year before. The priest of the Eaglais who kept that parish, a young man new to the charge and glad of the crowd whatever brought it, said the old words over each hull in turn, dipping a bundle of dried heather in a bucket of harbour water and shaking it across bow and stern while the crews stood bareheaded in the cold, their breath going up white over the shingle in a line as long as the boats themselves. Mary Coyle's own boat, the one that had carried her both ways to the west, was blessed fourth in the row, and she stood at its bow through the whole of the rite with her hands folded and her shawl pulled close, and said nothing to anyone until it was done.

Afterward there was food on trestles set up in the churchyard, brought by whoever had a little to spare that week, and someone had got a fiddle working despite the cold, and the thing that had begun as eleven boats sailing west in the middle of winter ended, that evening, as a crowd on a beach that did not, by any account written down afterward, look like a people who had been turned away from anything at all.

The Library keeps this much of what followed: that the north kept its own Fleadh na Mara at Cnoc ghorm every year after, on the same Sunday, without asking leave of any house on the west coast again, and that within a generation more boats came to it than had ever, in the memory of anyone living, gone to the feast at Seann Bhral. Whether the house on the west coast ever heard how it had grown, the Library does not know, and has found no one who thought to write and tell it.
