# Of Now Written

*SE 64 an Dàmhair – SE 67 an Lùnastal*

> A column of figures is no argument, until it has been read aloud in a room that never wished to hear it read. After that it is a fact the room must carry, and carries as long as the room keeps its own minutes.
>
> — Daniel Frayne, private book kept at Muileann leathan, {{date:V-0217}}

## I. Of the Clerks Who Came for the Figures

The census clerks who rode north to Caol mhòr that autumn were the first Tional nan Tuathach's room had ever seen come asking rather than come telling, and Daniel Frayne, nine-and-fifty that year and speaker long enough that he no longer troubled to count the seasons of it, found the change stranger than anything the clerks themselves said to him. Two men came, both Dia-thìreach, both younger than his own eldest clerk, riding a cart with a chest of blank forms lashed under a tarpaulin against the rain that had followed them the whole road from Cathair dhearg, and they asked at the door of the room, with a courtesy Frayne had not met from any southern office in thirty years of dealing with them, whether Tional nan Tuathach kept a count of its own people that the third census might use, the shire's own maor having told the council's census office that no southern clerk had yet set foot in the northern towns to take one and none was like to before the printing date the council itself had chosen.

Frayne sat with them the better part of a day, the room's ledgers open on the table between them, and gave them what Tional nan Tuathach had: the school registers Agnes Pike's and Alice Hale's houses had kept since before he could write his own name properly, what the vigil-keepers of Baile chrom would lend of their own books, the bindery's own tally of every family in Muileann leathan that had ever brought him a book to mend, and Tional nan Tuathach's own count of heads taken every third year since the room was first opened, a count no southern office had ever asked to see before that autumn. The younger of the two clerks copied the figures into his own book in a fair, careful hand, and asked, when he had done, whether Tional nan Tuathach would swear to them before a justice if the council required it, a question Frayne answered by telling him that Tional nan Tuathach had sworn to smaller things for larger names before that day and would swear to this one gladly, there being nothing in the figures the room had any cause to soften.

The rain that had followed the clerks up from Cathair dhearg did not let up while they sat at the room's table, and Frayne, watching it run down the small window behind the younger clerk's shoulder, thought it a fitting enough weather for the asking, the road north having never been an easy one for a southern office to trouble much with in any season, wet or dry. He offered the two men what the room's own chest could spare of Caol mhòr's own bread and its own beer, poor fare beside what a council office kept for its travelling clerks, and the elder of the two, a man near Frayne's own age who had ridden the circuit for the census twice before in the south and never once in the north, said over it that he had heard the road called worse than he found it, and that a man might have told him, before he set out, that the room at the end of it kept better order in its own ledgers than half the southern shire halls he had visited that same season. Frayne took the saying for the courtesy it plainly was and no more, having heard courtesies enough from southern mouths over thirty years to know the weight a wise man gave them, but he set it down in his own book that night beside the rest, judging that even a courtesy, once written, outlived the mouth that had spoken it.

"They will print what we give them," Frayne told the room's own clerks that evening, when the cart had gone south again with the chest of forms filled and the blank ones still lashed beneath the tarpaulin for want of use, "and I do not know yet whether that is a kindness or only a shorter road to the same silence we have always been given. But we have never before been asked, and a room that keeps a minute-book keeps this in it: that in the sixty-fourth year of the reckoning, we were asked." He wrote the day into his own private book that same night, the figures given and the manner of the giving both, a habit he had kept since the winter he first began it and had no thought of breaking now that the figures, for once, were wanted rather than merely tolerated.

The census was published that same autumn at Cathair dhearg, in the last week of an Dàmhair, and it was the first in the kingdom's history to be printed and sold openly at the fairs rather than filed unread in the council's own cellar, a small reform the notice that announced it called a token of An Crùn's own confidence in its people, though the room at Caol mhòr, reading the notice a season later than the south had read it, found in the phrase nothing that the figures, once they arrived, did not answer more plainly than any notice could.

## II. Of the Reading and the Two Words

The figures reached Caol mhòr early in an t-Samhain, a bound sheaf of printed columns the council's own press had run off in numbers enough to sell at every market town on the roll, carried up the last stretch of road on a carter's own cart because the autumn's rain had closed the higher road to anything lighter, and the room that gathered to hear them read was fuller than it had been in years, benches carried in from the vestry next door and the fire built up past its usual evening measure against a cold that had kept the windows white with frost since the first hard nights. Tional nan Tuathach's clerk who read the columns aloud, a woman who had kept the room's own books since before Frayne himself had held the chair and had buried, by her own account, two speakers and meant to outlast a third at the same table, read them in the flat voice a clerk keeps for figures that trouble her more than she means to let the room see. Frayne sat in the chair Mary Coyle had once sat in, his hands laid flat on the table the way he sat when he meant to keep them still, and listened to every column the census had chosen to take, the counted heads, the counted hearths, the counted trades a family owned to, read out in turn for the northern towns and set beside the same columns for the south.

In every column the clerks at Cathair dhearg had troubled to take, the northern towns stood at about half the measure the southern shires showed for the same count, a fact the room had known in its own bones for two generations and had never once seen printed in the kingdom's own hand before that winter. Nobody in the room was surprised at the figures themselves; what silenced them, Frayne wrote afterward, was hearing figures they had always known made into a fact the council itself had signed its own press to. The clerk read the last column and set the sheaf down, and the room kept a quiet long enough that the fire's own settling in the grate was the loudest thing in it.

Frayne drew the minute-book toward him when the quiet had held as long as he judged it should, and wrote two words in it, and let the room see him write them before he spoke a word himself: *Now written.* He offered the room no gloss on the two words, then or after, though more than one clerk asked him in the years following what he had meant by them and got, from each asking, much the same answer, that a figure Tional nan Tuathach had always known and the kingdom had never printed was one kind of fact, and a figure both Tional nan Tuathach and the kingdom now held in the same paper was another kind entire, and that the difference between the two kinds was the whole of what he had meant to set down and no more than that.

One of the room's younger clerks, a man barely into his own second year at the table and too new to the work to keep the stillness the older hands kept by long habit, asked Frayne afterward, walking out with him into the frost, whether the two words meant the room ought now to expect the south to answer for what it had printed. Frayne told him no, that a fact set down was not a promise kept, and that a room hoping for the one from the other had not yet learned the difference the room's own long history ought to have taught it by then; but he told the young man, too, that a fact once printed could not be unprinted, whatever the council chose to do or not do about it, and that this was worth having even where a promise was not, a distinction the young clerk turned over some while in the cold before he owned that he took the sense of it, if not yet the comfort.

The census carried, as the first one had, a line for fuil-ghuail, a number given without gloss or comment, and Tormod Pinn, copying the published figures into Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd at Muileann chaol that same winter as he had copied every census before it, entered the line exactly as Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill had entered it a generation before him, without a word added either side of the number. He was eight-and-seventy that winter and had kept Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd three-and-twenty years, and the clerks who worked beside him at Muileann chaol said afterward that his hand had grown no less steady for the years, only slower, so that a page that had once taken him an evening now took the better part of two.

In the south-west that same season, Òrd na Cloiche published, for the first time in their own province's memory, a count of their own people to set beside the kingdom's, a reckoning kept by the province's own scribes rather than by any council clerk, and the two counts, when word of them reached Muileann chaol, agreed within a hundred souls of each other, a closeness Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd noted with the same flat hand it gave every other column that winter, though more than one clerk at an Leabharlann remarked, reading the entry, that a province which could check the kingdom's own figures against its own and find them true had been given a trust the north had never once been offered.

## III. Of Tormod Pinn's Last Winter

Tormod Pinn had come to the chronicler's desk at Muileann chaol in the forty-second year of the reckoning, called Pinn for the speed of his own hand rather than for any name his father had given him, and he had kept, since the day Gormshuil laid the pen down, the same habit she had left him: the list of what the record did not hold, copied into the front of every year's book and added to but once and never struck from in three-and-twenty years of keeping it. He had told an Leabharlann's younger clerks, more than once over those years, that a chronicler's business was to keep the list honest rather than to shorten it, and that a man who struck a line from another's list without first filling the gap it named had done nothing but tidy a shelf he had no right to tidy.

A junior clerk who copied returns beside him that last winter said afterward that Tormod Pinn had grown, in his last years, given to reading a page twice where once he had read it once, from a wish, the clerk judged, to be sure of a figure before he trusted his own hand to it, a carefulness the younger clerks about him took at first for slowness and only later, once his desk stood empty, understood for what it had been. Tormod kept to his own desk through most of that winter and into the spring following, reading, as he had read every season for twenty years, the returns the shires sent up and the figures the census had newly given him to set beside them, and the clerks who passed his door those months said he looked, by the spring, thinner in the face than the winter before, though none of them thought to trouble him with the saying, a chronicler's own health being, by an Leabharlann's long custom, a matter he was left to keep to himself unless he chose otherwise. They found him at his own desk on a morning in an t-Sultain, the returns for that year still open under his hand and his pen laid down beside them rather than dropped, in much the manner, an old clerk remarked who had served under Ailean Leabhar too, that a man of an Leabharlann seemed always to be found, as though the desk taught a certain way of leaving it.

He was buried at Muileann chaol within the week, in the small burying-ground behind an Leabharlann that held every keeper and chronicler the house had kept since Ailean Leabhar's own day, and Mòrag nic Coinnich, who had known him the whole of his three-and-twenty years at the desk, spoke over him only that he had kept the list honest and struck nothing from it that was not yet earned, words an Leabharlann's own younger clerks copied down and kept, by their own account, truer to the man than any longer eulogy could have been. He was nine-and-seventy at his death, by an Leabharlann's own reckoning of his years, an age no chronicler before him at Muileann chaol had reached at that desk, and the clerks noted the fact in the margin of his own last page, the only line in the whole of his tenure that he had not written himself.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh took the desk before that autumn was out, three-and-fifty years old and the first woman to hold the chronicler's chair since Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, chosen by Mòrag nic Coinnich from among the house's own senior clerks for a steadiness of hand the keeper judged the desk would want after Tormod's long, quiet tenure. She had kept an Leabharlann's own gazetteer returns seven years before that summer and told the clerks who worked under her, on her first morning at the chronicler's own desk, that she meant to change nothing of the desk's furniture nor its hours, only its habit of silence where the record had none to give, a saying the clerks who heard it did not at first take her full meaning of, until her first year's book was closed and its foot-note read. She kept his habit of the list at the front of the year's book, unchanged, and added to it a habit of her own that no chronicler before her had kept: a foot-note at the close of every year's entries, set apart from Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd proper, naming what the record could not learn that year and why, where the why could be told at all.

Her first such note, written at the foot of her first year at the desk, concerned the northern figures the census had printed. The clerks who had gone to take them, she wrote, had been told the figures at Caol mhòr and had not troubled to count them for themselves, and the figures they were told were Tional nan Tuathach's own, given freely by its speaker to two clerks who asked. So the kingdom's first honestly printed census was honest in the north, she set down, only because the north had done the counting the south's own clerks had been sent to do and had not done. Frayne read the note some months later, a copy of it sent north by a clerk of an Leabharlann who thought the room at Caol mhòr ought to see what its own giving had been made into, and kept the copy in his private book beside the day he had first given the figures, judging, he told the room, that a chronicler who would write such a thing plainly, of her own kingdom's own clerks, was a chronicler the north might yet have some use trusting.

## IV. Of the Hawk's Rolls

In the same season that Tormod Pinn's desk stood empty, and before the first of the shire chests had been sent for, a cart came to Muileann chaol from the north-central coast that no one at an Leabharlann had asked for. It carried three coffers of plain oak bound in iron, and a letter under a seal an Leabharlann's clerks had not seen on any paper in the house before: a hawk with its wings spread, the same that had stood on the arms of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag since the council granted it near thirty years before. The coffers held the birth-rolls of Òrd an t-Seabhaig of Siorrachd Ros gheal, every child named at their altars since the houses had first kept a roll, some of the oldest leaves in a hand an Leabharlann's copyists had to send for the lamp to read.

The letter was short. It said that the houses had heard an Leabharlann meant to call in the records of the shires, and that the houses were not a shire and would not be called; but that they had kept their rolls a long time in damp halls and a flooded year or a careless fire might end them, and that they would send them to be kept, as the hall at Seann Dunn sent its naming, on one condition. The rolls were to be filed under Òrd an t-Seabhaig and not under Òrd Bhrìde, whatever an Leabharlann's own custom was for filing the rites of An Creideamh Sean together; and if the keeper did not agree to that, the carter had orders to turn his horse round and bring the coffers home.

The carter was a young man of Ros gheal who had not been further south than the grain dock in his life, and he waited in an Leabharlann's yard through the whole of an afternoon while the question went up the stair, sitting on the tail of his cart with his hat on his knee and answering the copyists' questions about the grain-ships with the patience of someone who had been told to answer anything but the one thing he had come about.

Mòrag nic Coinnich read the letter at her own desk and sent for the register of an Leabharlann's filing, which Ailean Leabhar had begun and she had kept, and which set the papers of An Creideamh Sean's orders, Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail and Òrd an t-Seabhaig and the houses Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail had taken in, under one heading, as Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's own order of worship set them, Òrd an t-Seabhaig being in Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's reckoning a house of An Creideamh Sean that had not yet come under its custodian. She had filed Òrd an t-Seabhaig's letters so for five-and-twenty years without anyone asking her otherwise, and no one at Seann Skell had ever asked her either way. She sat with the register open a while, the clerk who had brought it said afterward, and then asked him what an Leabharlann's warrant said it was for. He told her: to receive, to order and to keep whatever was sent to it. She said that the houses were sending, and that the order of a thing sent was the sender's to name if the sender cared to name it, since a keeper who changed the name on a paper to suit his own shelves had begun, however slightly, to write it.

She wrote the answer herself, three lines, and gave it to the carter before dusk. The rolls would be filed under Òrd an t-Seabhaig, under that name and no other, in a press of their own; the houses might send a witness to see them so filed if they chose; and an Leabharlann kept a copy made in its own hand of all it held for any sender, and sent the copy back to any sender who asked, as it did for any shire. The carter read it, or had it read to him by the copyist he had been talking to, and unloaded the coffers himself before he would take a bowl of anything.

It was the first time in its history that an Leabharlann had filed a paper under a name its sender set against the name an Leabharlann's own register gave. Mòrag entered the fact in the register in the plainest terms she could find, and beneath it, in a hand the clerks said was not quite her ordinary one, that Òrd an t-Seabhaig of Ros gheal had kept their children's names longer than an Leabharlann had kept anything, and that she did not think an Leabharlann had any business teaching them what to call themselves.

No word came from Seann Skell. A copy of Mòrag's three lines went there in the ordinary way, with an Leabharlann's other notices of the year, and was answered, if it was answered, in a letter an Leabharlann never received. At Ros gheal, the carter reported when he came south again the next spring with a fourth coffer the houses had found in a shut room at their oldest altar, the houses had read the three lines aloud in their hall and said nothing of them afterward, which in Oirthir an t-Seabhaig, he said, was how a thing was accepted.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, new at the chronicler's desk that autumn, entered the rolls' coming in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd as an Leabharlann entered it, under the name the houses gave, and added nothing, it being, she judged, a thing that had been rightly done and needed no foot-note to say so.

## V. Of the Chests Called In

Mòrag nic Coinnich, keeper of the Library these six-and-twenty years, had long held that a history kept in forty scattered shire-chests was no history at all but forty separate hopes that someone, someday, would trouble to read them together, and in the year after Tormod Pinn's death she sent her own clerks out along the roll to call every shire's record-chest in to Muileann chaol, the maors' own returns, the town chronicles where any town kept one, the vigil-books and rite-rolls the shires' own faiths had filed apart from An Crùn's business, all of it, whatever a shire had troubled to keep, to be copied or kept whole as an Leabharlann judged fit.

Most shires sent what they held without complaint, some gladly and some only because a council notice had told them to, but at Seann Tarr the shire's own keeper, an old man who had held the chest's key since before Mòrag herself was born and had never in that whole tenure let a stranger's hand touch the chest without his own standing by it, burned the chest in his own yard the night before her clerks arrived rather than let it travel south. He met the clerks himself at the gate the next morning, the ash still warm behind him and his own coat smelling of the smoke he had not troubled to wash from it, and told them, when they found only ash where the chest had stood, that the shire's record belonged in the shire and would stay there in whatever form he judged fit to leave it, a plain answer given without heat and without apology, the clerks noted, as though the burning had been no more than the ordinary close of an ordinary evening's business. He was fined by the council for the burning, a fine an Leabharlann's own clerk noted he paid without argument and without apology either, and the chest, and whatever it had held of Seann Tarr's own two centuries, was lost entire, the only shire record of the whole calling-in that an Leabharlann never had the chance to read.

Cathair naomh, in the north, sent its own rolls in two carts that same season, together with the town's own chronicle, kept by a single family through four generations without a council's asking, the crates packed by the family's own eldest daughter, who rode the two carts south herself rather than trust them to a hired carter, and told Mòrag's clerks, unpacking the crates at her own hand once the road was done, that her grandmother's grandmother had begun Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd on the back of an unused rent-roll for want of paper better suited to it, and that every hand since had kept to the same plain page rather than buy finer stock the family judged an extravagance no plain chronicle wanted. Mòrag nic Coinnich, going through it at her own table with a clerk standing by to lift out whatever the crates held, entered it in an Leabharlann's own register as the best-kept shire record the calling-in had yet returned, a judgment she let stand even after every other shire's chest had reached Muileann chaol and been weighed against it. Seann Toll sent its own chest willingly and asked, when the clerks came for it, for a copy of everything the chest held to be returned to the shire once an Leabharlann had made its own, an asking no shire before it had thought to make, and Mòrag's clerks made the copy as asked, a full transcription bound in the same hand and set beside the original before either left the shire's own hall, and the copy they made for Seann Toll became, in the years after, the pattern every later shire's copy was cut to, an Leabharlann having found, in the asking, a better custom than the one it had begun with.

An Leabharlann's own holdings doubled that year with what the calling-in returned, and it took into one keeping, for the first time since its founding, the lesser archives several provinces had kept apart from An Crùn's own rolls, so that from that season the history of Dia-thìr was written and held in one place at Muileann chaol and not scattered, as it had been since An Dealachadh, across forty shire halls each keeping its own account of its own corner and no other's.

No Tuathaich record was called in that season, nor any season after it, the calling-in's own notice having named only the shires the council's roll accounted for, and the north's own record stayed where it had always stayed: Tional nan Tuathach's minute-books at Caol mhòr, the school registers Agnes Pike's and Alice Hale's houses had begun, the churches' own registers, the vigil-books of Baile chrom, all of it in private keeping and none of it asked for. Frayne, reading the calling-in's notice when word of it reached the room the following spring, called the room together and put the question to them plainly himself, whether Tional nan Tuathach ought to offer its own books south now that the council had shown, by the calling-in, a wish to gather every record the kingdom held: a question, he told them, the room had never once been asked by any southern office and had, this once, the choosing of for its own.

The room debated it the better part of an evening, the older clerks arguing that a book offered was a book the council might one day demand rather than merely receive, and the younger arguing that a history kept only in the north was a history the kingdom's own printed figures would go on outweighing however true the north's own count remained. "We gave them the figures once," the room's oldest clerk said, near the debate's close, "and they made of the giving a mark against us, however honestly it was meant. I will not vote to give them the books as well and trust the giving to fare better." Frayne, who had sat the whole evening without speaking his own mind either way, told the room only that he had not called them together to be told which way to vote, and that he would set down whatever the room decided and no more of his own judgment beside it, a promise the room's own minute of that night bears out.

The room's minute of that evening, in Frayne's own hand, records no vote taken by show or by count and no reason given beyond the two words its own clerk set down at the sitting's close, words that answered the calling-in more plainly than any longer argument the room could have offered instead: *kept north.* No cart went south from Caol mhòr that year or any year following, and Tional nan Tuathach's own books, whatever the kingdom's Library now held of every other shire's record, stayed where they had always been kept, in the room at Caol mhòr, in the hands that had written them.
