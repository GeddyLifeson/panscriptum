# Of the Justice's Circuit

*SE 7 an t-Ògmhios – SE 8 an t-Sultain*

> Exactness in a law makes it possible to keep, whatever else it fails to make it.
>
> — Eilidh nic Leòid, of the ruling given at Seann Bhral in the west, {{date:V-0038}}

## I. Of the Two Counts of Doire ghlas

The state took its own measure that summer, for the first time since it had taken the island back. Clerks rode out from Cathair dhearg with rolls under their arms and a single instruction copied into every one of them: count the houses, count the heads in them, and ask, where it seemed proper to ask, what a household held to. Most gave the answer the clerks expected and wrote down without comment. On the far north-western cape and on the small islets off it, where the fishing was thin and the priests came once a year if the sea allowed it, a good many gave none at all, and the clerks, finding no word in Dia-thìris that fit a household with nothing to declare, wrote against their names *gun chreideamh*, without a creed, and rode on. It went into the roll as a fact and stayed there as one, unexplained.

Of the coal-touched the roll says less than that. It notes, in the same flat hand it gives to everything, that there are very few, and says no more; whoever wrote the line seems to have felt that a thing so seldom seen was better left where it was found than dwelt on, and the clerks who came after him have kept to the same rule ever since, as though the silence were its own part of the counting.

The Tuathaich towns of the shire of Doire ghlas were counted from the road. The census clerks sent that far north had no love of the errand and less of the weather, and a man on horseback, going by at a trot with a rain-cape over his rolls, will miss a house set back from the way, or a family lodging three to a room above a shop, or a child asleep and not thought worth waking to be added to a list. The Moot at Caol mhòr, which had kept its own tally of its own towns for five years by then and trusted it a good deal more than it trusted a stranger on a horse, took its count again that same season, going door to door as it always did, and found close to a fifth more souls than the state's roll gave it. Neither side wrote to the other about the difference. The state's roll went into the treasury's chests at Cathair dhearg; the Moot's went into the chest under the long room's table at Caol mhòr; and there the two counts of Doire ghlas have sat ever since, each one true by its own reckoning, and neither one correcting the other, because no one anywhere on the island had been given the office of making them agree.

It is against that count, taken and not reconciled, that the year's other business must be set: for it was the same summer, while the clerks were still riding the coast roads with their rolls, that the council took up a question it had let stand unanswered for six years, namely what law a man in Muileann bhàn or Inis thais or a dozen other towns a hard week's ride from the capital was actually to be given, when the only courts that sat regularly sat at Cathair dhearg and heard, for the most part, only what Cathair dhearg's own householders brought before them.

A shire far from the capital, in those years, had law of a kind: the maor's word, the custom the old families kept among themselves, and, failing both, whatever a man could make a neighbour agree to without troubling anyone above him. It served well enough for a boundary or a debt, so long as the parties were willing to be bound by an unwritten thing, and served very badly the moment one of them was not, there being then no bench nearer than the capital to appeal to, and few enough householders with the means or the year to spare for such a journey. The council had heard this said to it more than once, by maoir who did not care to be magistrates as well as tax-gatherers, and had done nothing about it, there being always some nearer business to attend to first, until the same summer that sent its clerks out with the census rolls sent out, in the same weeks, a second set of letters altogether.

## II. Of Cathair dhomhain and the Custody-Book

Eilidh nic Leòid was one-and-fifty that summer, and had never married, and had spent the whole of her working life, from the age of fourteen, in the counting-house of Cathair dhomhain, where her father before her had kept the custody-book of the old vein-house on the hill above the harbour. It was not, by then, much of an office; the house had lost its galleries in the mountain to the humans' Company three generations back, and the custody-book was, by then, a relic, a great bound ledger recording who had held what right to what gallery and on what terms, going back further than any living hand had written in it, kept up now for the sake of the town's older families and their disputes over what their grandmothers had been owed. Her father read her the book before she could write her own name in the margin of it, and by the time she could write she was correcting his entries, gently and then less gently, when a heading was carried forward wrong or a right was recorded as belonging to a house it had in fact passed out of two holders before.

It was not, she used to say afterward, that the custody-book taught her the law of the land, for the land's law and the vein's custom were two different bodies entirely, and she had had to learn the first as a grown woman with the second already fixed in her. What the book taught her, she said, was that a right written down carelessly was worse than no right at all, because a careless entry gave a man something to point to and be wrong about, whereas an honest blank at least told him plainly that no one had settled the matter yet. She carried that conviction into every clerkship she held after her father died and the vein-house closed its custody-book for good, first as a copyist for the harbour court at Cathair dhomhain, then as its recorder, then, for eleven years, as the court's own justice in all but the name, since the man who held the name preferred his garden to his bench and was glad enough to let her hold both.

The council's letter naming her first among the circuit justices of the kingdom reached her on a wet morning in an t-Ògmhios, carried by a rider who had come the long way round the coast road because the direct way was under water from three days of rain, and who was, by his own account, glad to be shot of the errand and out of his boots. It told her that eleven shires of the south-west were to be hers, that she was to ride them in a fixed order once in every twelvemonth, hearing what causes came before her at each stopping-place, and that she was to carry with her, and return each year to the council's clerks at Cathair dhearg, a written record of every judgment given, so that a ruling made at one stop should be known, and if need be answered, at the next. She read the letter twice, standing in the doorway of the harbour court with the rain still coming down behind the rider's shoulder, and found in it, she told her clerk afterward, the first office in her whole career that asked of her exactly what her father's custody-book had always asked of her without her having to explain the likeness to anyone.

For the riding she was given a horse she did not trust, a clerk of two-and-twenty named Murchadh Clàrc who trusted the horse even less than she did, and a satchel of blank record-books stamped with the council's new seal, the ink of which was still tacky on the first three she opened. She set out from Cathair dhomhain on the first fine day after the rains broke, with the satchel strapped behind Murchadh's saddle rather than her own, on the grounds, as she told him, that a justice ought to arrive at her first assize with clean hands and let her clerk arrive muddy.

## III. Of the First Assize and the Wells at Muileann bhàn

Her first sitting, held at Cathair dhomhain before she had properly left the town, was a small thing: three causes, a boundary dispute between neighbouring orchards settled by measuring the ground herself with a length of knotted cord rather than trust either party's account of where the old hedge had run, a debt of thirty measures of barley that she found for the lender on the strength of a receipt neither party had thought to burn, and a complaint against a fishmonger for short weight that she dismissed for want of any evidence beyond the complainant's own scales, which she made him bring into court and weigh against the council's standard, and which proved, to his visible discomfort, to be the ones at fault. Murchadh recorded all three in a hand Eilidh found, by the end of the first week, considerably steadier than his seat on the horse.

She reached Muileann bhàn in the last week of an t-Iuchar, a mill-town on the southern plain that had, that same year, raised the first public waterworks the restored kingdom had seen anywhere on the island: a stone tank set on the hill above the town, fed by a leat off the mill-race, with lead pipes laid down to four wells in the streets below so that a household no longer had to send a child to the river with a yoke and two buckets every morning of its life. The Guild of Hewers had sent surveyors down from the vein-towns to watch it built, being curious, as diggers of every kind are curious, about a different way of moving water through the ground than the ways they already knew; and the town was, on the whole, proud of the thing, which had cost it three years of subscription and the loan of every mason within a day's walk.

The quarrel Eilidh was asked to hear had grown, as such quarrels do, out of the very success of the works. The well nearest the mill had been dug, by an oversight nobody now owned to, on land that belonged in custom, though not in any deed either party could produce, to a widow named Beathag nic Mhurchaidh, whose late husband's family had held the ground for water rights time out of mind, long before any tank or pipe was thought of, on the strength of a spring that had run there before the leat was cut and had since gone dry, the leat having drawn off what fed it. Beathag held that the well was hers to charge for, as her husband's family had always charged a penny a season for water from the spring, and had begun setting a boy at the well-head to collect it. The town's new well-warden, a mason named Dùghall who had helped lay the pipe with his own hands, held that a well fed by the town's own works, built with the town's own subscription, belonged to the town and to no single household, spring or no spring, and had had the boy sent home twice already, once with words and once, the second time, with something closer to a shove.

Eilidh heard them both in the mill-yard, there being no proper courtroom at Muileann bhàn and the mill-yard being the largest covered space the town possessed, with half the street come to watch and the miller's dog asleep across the threshold through the whole of it. She asked for the old spring's history first, and got it from three old men in turn, none of whose accounts agreed on the exact year the spring had first been charged for, though all agreed it long predated the leat. Then, and this took longer, she asked exactly which lengths of pipe the town's subscription had paid for and which the mill itself had given out of its own purse, since the mill's owner turned out to have laid the last stretch of pipe to the disputed well at his own cost, out of a wish, he said plainly, to see his own household's well filled first. She sent Murchadh out with the cord she had used on the orchard boundary to measure the actual distance from the tank to each of the four wells, and had the mill's ledgers of the subscription brought into the yard and read aloud, penny by penny, while the dog slept on.

By evening she had her ruling, and gave it standing on the mill-step so that the whole yard could hear it without crowding round the bench. The spring's old custom, she said, had died with the spring; a right to charge for water that no longer ran could not be transferred, by any reasoning she could construct, onto water that ran by an entirely different road and had been paid for by an entirely different purse. But the town's claim to own the well outright went too far the other way, for the ground the well stood on was still, by every account given her, Beathag's ground in custom if not in deed, and a town could not simply dig where it liked and call the digging ownership. She ruled that the well stood on Beathag's land and would go on standing there, that Beathag was owed no charge for water she had not drawn and no longer could; in place of the dead spring's custom she was owed the town's word that the ground would never be built over or fenced from her without her leave, so long as she or her heirs held it. It satisfied neither party entire, which she told Murchadh that night, over a supper the mill's owner sent up to their lodging by way of thanks for a long day's hearing, was generally the surest sign a ruling had been made fairly rather than to please the loudest voice in the yard.

## IV. Of the Head-Due and the Road South

The head-due was gathered that same autumn for the first time in the shires of her circuit, eighteen purses on every five thousand heads, and Eilidh found herself, more than once along the road, sitting as much a witness to the gathering as a justice riding past it: a maor's clerk at a crossroads with a table and a strongbox, a queue of householders each paying by the count of their own roof, and here and there a dispute over whose child was old enough to be counted and whose was not, which the clerks settled among themselves and never thought to bring to her bench at all, the sum being too small, as one of them told her frankly, to be worth a justice's time. She noted it in her own record-book all the same, as a thing seen rather than a cause heard, on the reasoning that a circuit justice who wrote down only what came before her formally would, in twenty years, know a good deal less of her own shires than one who wrote down what she passed on the road between.

She had not been a full year in the office before she understood that the greater part of a circuit justice's work was arithmetic of a duller kind, not judgment at all: how many days a shire's roads would cost her against how many causes waited at the end of them, and which stopping-place could be shortened without a householder somewhere going a twelvemonth unheard. A second book, kept apart from the record of rulings, held that reckoning alone, filled with sums that never once appeared in anything the council's clerks would read; and she thought, more than once on a wet evening with the sums still not coming right, that her father's custody-book had never asked this particular exactness of her, there having been only the one vein-house to reckon and no road between its rooms longer than a stair.

She rode from Muileann bhàn through Inis thais, where the sitting was brief, the town's own harbour court being well enough kept that little came to her beyond two cases the local magistrate had been glad to hand up rather than decide himself, both involving a fishing boat's ownership after its owner's death without clear heirs, and on through the smaller courts of the coast to Doire shean, a fishing village under the old faith where she sat for two days in the porch of the town's temple, there being, again, no other roofed space large enough, and heard, among lesser things, a case brought by three households against a fourth over the right to dry nets on a stretch of shingle that had, within living memory, belonged to no one in particular. She settled that one, as she had settled the well, by asking after the custom rather than the law, since no written law reached so far down as a stretch of shingle, and found, as she generally did when she asked patiently enough, that the custom was known to everyone in the room and had simply never been written where a stranger could find it.

It was on the road between Doire shean and Seann Bhral, in the last days of an Lùnastal, that Murchadh asked her, not for the first time, whether she found it strange to be making law rather than only keeping it, having spent so many years reading a book that recorded rights already settled long before she was born. She thought about the question longer than he expected an answer to take, the horses walking slow on a coast track gone soft with the last of the rain, and said at length that she did not think she was making anything at all; she thought she was doing to the whole of the kingdom's law what her father had asked her to do to the vein-house's custody-book from the time she could hold a pen, which was to write down plainly what was true, so that the next hand to open the book would not have to guess. Whether that came to the same thing as making law, she said, was a question for someone with more patience for words than she had ever had for anything but figures.

## V. Of the Ruling at Seann Bhral

Seann Bhral in the west stood on its harbour with the tide out when they rode in on the ninth of an t-Sultain, the mud flats stretching grey below the town wall and a scorpion carved over the old counting-house door that nobody now living could account for, it having been there, by every telling, since long before the humans ever came ashore. The cause that had been waiting on her docket the longest was already known to her by name before she reached the town, a maor's clerk having ridden ahead a week earlier with the papers so that she might read them on the road rather than in the court itself: a dispute over four fields on the plain above the harbour, held for two generations by a Tuathaich family named Tanner under a title that had been cut, in the humans' own hand, by a Company surveyor before the Severance, and claimed now by a Dia-thìreach household that held, under a much newer seal, the Crown's grant of the same ground, made without, it was plain from the paper's own date, any knowledge on the council's part that the land was occupied at all.

The maor of the shire, a heavy, careful man named Artair mac Lachlainn who had held the office four years without once being asked to sit in judgment on anything larger than a dispute over a fishing weir, met her at the town gate the evening before and walked with her as far as her lodging, saying little beyond the courtesies until they reached the door, when he stopped and told her plainly that he would be glad if she found for the Crown's grant entire, since the household that held it was a cousin of his own and had waited two years for a ruling he had not felt able to give himself. Eilidh thanked him for his honesty, much as Dòmhnall mac Thormoid, in a house far to the south, had once thanked two strangers for theirs, and told him that she would hear the cause the next morning exactly as she heard every other, and that he was welcome to sit through it if he wished, though not to speak in it. He came, and sat at the back of the room, and did not speak.

She sat the tenth of an t-Sultain in the counting-house, the old scorpion looking down from the lintel on a room fuller than any she had yet sat in on her circuit, for word of the cause had gone ahead of her along the coast and half the shire's Tuathaich households, it seemed, had found some reason to be in Seann Bhral that week. She read the Tanner family's title through twice, in the humans' own script, which she had learned enough of years before, from an old harbour clerk at Cathair dhomhain, to make out slowly without help; she read the Crown's grant beside it, in Dia-thìris, cut clean and recent and utterly silent on the question of who already stood on the ground it named; and she asked the Tanner family's eldest son, a man near her own age who had farmed the land since boyhood and buried both his parents in a corner of it, a single question, which was whether the family had ever paid a tithe, a due or a rent of any kind to any authority for the ground since the Severance, and receiving the answer that they had not, because none had ever been asked of them, sat a long while in silence before she gave her ruling.

The ground, she said, had passed to the Crown; on that the two titles could not both be right, and the humans' authority to grant land on the island had ended with the humans' own departure from it, whatever the survey's ink still claimed. But the right to work that ground, to plant it and reap it and pass it to a child, had never in fact been taken from the family that held it, no clerk having ever come to take it, and she would not now invent a taking that had not happened merely because a newer seal existed that assumed one. The land belonged to the Crown. The working of it belonged, and would go on belonging, to the Tanners and their heirs, on the same terms, in substance, as they had always worked it, the only change being that any due the Crown chose in future to lay upon such ground would be laid on them as it was laid on any other holder, and not excused. She thought, reading it back to Murchadh that evening for the fair copy, that she had found the most generous reading the law would bear, and she believed it, turning it over that night in the room the harbour master had found her, a fair judgment as well as an exact one, though she noted, writing the last line of the record before she slept, that she had not asked the Tanner son what he thought of being told the ground under his own dead parents now belonged, in law if in nothing else that would ever touch him day to day, to a Crown he had never once been asked to swear to.

## VI. Of the Copy Sent to Every Court

The ruling was fair-copied twice more before the month was out, once for the council's own chests at Cathair dhearg and once, at Eilidh's own instruction, for every maor's court in the eleven shires of her circuit, with a note in her own hand that any cause brought on a like title should be decided on the like reasoning until the council itself said otherwise. She had no authority, strictly read, to bind any bench but her own; a circuit justice's ruling was her ruling and no other's, and she knew it. But she reasoned, writing the covering note by candlelight in the harbour master's borrowed room with Murchadh already asleep in the chair by the door, that a law applied differently in every court that heard it was no law at all, only eleven separate guesses wearing the same coat, and that if the council would not yet say plainly what the rule should be for land such as the Tanners', she would at least see to it that every bench in reach of her own hand said the same thing until it did.

The copies went out through the autumn, one by carrier to each of the eleven courts, and were entered, in most of them, without comment, a clerk's initials in the margin and a date, the way any ruling was entered that nobody expected to argue with. At two courts, the record notes only that the copy was received; whether it was read is not told. The copy did not travel north beyond her own eleven shires that year, the courts of the north-west being no part of her circuit and answering, in any case, to no bench of hers; but it was cited, before the season turned again, in a boundary case at a town she had never ridden through, a clerk there having got hold of a copy from a neighbouring court and finding, on reading it, that it settled a question his own bench had been putting off for want of any better guide. Eilidh did not learn of that particular citing for some years, and when she did, from a passing remark of a fellow justice at a council dinner she attended with visible reluctance, she said only that it did not surprise her; a rule written down plainly, she had told Murchadh more than once on the road that autumn, has a way of travelling on its own feet long after the hand that wrote it has stopped watching where it goes.
