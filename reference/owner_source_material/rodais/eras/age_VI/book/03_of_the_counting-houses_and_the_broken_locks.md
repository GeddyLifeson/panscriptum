# Of the Counting-Houses and the Broken Locks

*SE 2 an t-Ògmhios – an t-Iuchar*

> *Is san scrìobhadh a tha e, chan ann san t-sùil.*
> It lies in the writing, not the seeing.
>
> — a saying of the counting-clerks of Cathair dhearg, of no known author

## I. Of the Boxes at Cathair dhearg

Ailean mac Iain had spent the first summer of the restored country learning that a thing not written down might as well not have happened. He spent the second summer learning what it cost to write some things down and not others, and that the difference between the two lessons was most of what a clerk's life was for.

He was set, on the second morning of an t-Ògmhios, to a room at Cathair dhearg that had been the humans' counting-house, a long stone hall behind the harbour offices with high windows and a floor still marked in chalk where the humans had ruled lines for sorting coin. The war had been through it twice, once when the council took the town and once when a company of the humans' own guard had tried, briefly and badly, to hold it back, and what the fighting had not burned the damp had got into afterward. Ailean stood in the doorway on his first morning with a list in his hand of what he was to do, and could smell, under the mildew, a fainter smell of char that told him some of the shelves nearest the door had gone up before anyone thought to put them out.

The master set over the room, a heavy, patient man named Ruairidh whom Ailean never once heard raise his voice, gave him the whole of the northern wall to clear: ledger after ledger in the humans' close, slanting hand, bound in board covers gone soft with damp, each one a year or two of some tally he could only guess at from its weight and its ruled columns. "Box them," Ruairidh said. "Don't read them. We haven't the hands to spare for reading, and half of what's here is charred through by the middle anyhow. Box them, mark the shelf they came from, and let the reckoners sort out later what's worth opening."

Ailean had not told Ruairidh, and did not tell him now, that he could in fact make out a fair part of the humans' hand, better than most of the clerks the council had brought south that summer. As a boy in the hills he had gone twice a week to a reader kept by Teachdaireachd na h-Eaglaise in the nearest of the human mission-houses, less because his people held with the humans' errand than because the reader taught letters to any child sent to him and asked no coin for it, and Ailean's mother had wanted her son able to read whatever came his way, in whatever hand it came in. He had sat on a bench with three human children and two others of his own people and learned his letters twice over, in two hands and two tongues, and had not thought since that it would ever be worth anything to anyone but himself.

It was worth this much now: that he could have told Ruairidh, truthfully, what several of these ledgers were before he boxed them, and chose instead to box them as he had been told, unread, because a clerk who is asked to box a thing and instead reads it aloud to his master has made himself the kind of clerk nobody keeps for long. He carried the first stack to the crate the carpenters had left at the door, laid the boards in flat, and wrote on the crate's side, in his own hand, *Tithe rolls, north wall, unread*, which was as much truth as the label had room to hold and rather more than most of the crates around him got.

By the third day he had the rhythm of it: lift, glance at the spine for a date if one showed, set it in the crate, mark the tally on his own sheet, lift again. Some of the ledgers, near the seat of the old fire, crumbled at the spine when he lifted them and had to go into the crate as loose leaves, gathered as best he could in something like their order, with a note on the crate that the order was his own guess and not to be trusted past that. He thought, more than once that week, of how a fire that burns a thing halfway leaves a worse record than one that burns it whole, because a whole loss at least tells you plainly that there is nothing left to ask about, while a half loss goes on pretending, for as long as the crate is shut, that the rest of the truth is only waiting to be found.

He did not say this to Ruairidh either. Nowhere did he write it down. It sat with him the way the two words in his margin from the year before had sat with him, a private accounting kept against the day he might have a use for it, and he went on boxing.

There were four others set to the room besides him, none known to Ailean before that summer, drawn south from as many different hill parishes as there were clerks, and of these only a stooped, quick-handed man called Tòmas ever spoke to him beyond what the work required. Tòmas had been a mason's clerk before the war, used to tallying stone rather than paper, and he handled the ledgers as though they might crumble at a wrong word as readily as at a wrong touch, which by the third week several of them did. On the day the fire-damaged shelf gave up its last crumbling stack, Tòmas straightened over the crate with a fistful of loose leaves gone soft and brown at the edges and said, to no one in particular, that it seemed a strange kind of victory, to win a war and then spend a summer sweeping up the loser's paper.

"Somebody has to know what was owed and to whom," Ailean said, "or the whole of it starts again from nothing, and starting from nothing costs more than sweeping."

"Does it," Tòmas said, not quite a question, and went back to his crate, and Ailean found he had no ready answer for the plain doubt in it, only the private one he did not offer: that starting from nothing might in fact have cost the kingdom less, in the end, than beginning from a room like this one, but that no one who had been given a crate and a chalk stub that summer was in any position to choose between the two.

## II. Of Ros dhomhain's Sheds

In the second week Ailean was sent west along the coast road to Ros dhomhain, where the humans' harbour offices stood larger than Cathair dhearg's and the war had touched them less, and where the council wanted its tariff tables copied out before the summer's shipping began under the new hands.

Ros dhomhain in that first restored summer was still mostly a humans' town wearing a Dia-thìreach flag: the sheds along its long stone quay built to the humans' measure, the cranes still swung by the humans' gearing, the harbour-master's house with its clock stopped at whatever hour the last of the humans' officers had walked out of it. A clerk of the council's own, a hill man named Dùghall who had been set over the harbour's books for want of anyone else willing to learn its workings, met Ailean at the gate and walked him through the sheds himself, apologising for the state of the ledger room as though the mess were somehow his own doing.

"They kept a tariff table for every port on the coast," Dùghall said, laying a bound sheet across the counting-desk, "duties on grain, on timber, on the coal that went out before the works closed, on I don't know what else, half of it in figures I can't make head or tail of. The council wants it copied into our own hand and our own tongue before the boats start moving again, so a captain putting in here need not wonder whether the man taking his dues even knows what he's owed."

Ailean spent nine days at that desk, copying figure for figure and clause for clause, turning the humans' close columns into a Dia-thìris he had to build word by word as he went, for there had never before been a need to say, in his own tongue, what a duty on timber was or how it was reckoned by the load rather than the piece. He found that a language could be made to hold a new thing without breaking, provided a man was patient enough to lay the words down one at a time and careful enough not to claim, in the laying, that the thing had changed because its name had. The tariff the humans had set stood exactly as it had stood before; only the ink was new.

Dùghall watched him work on the last evening, reading over Ailean's shoulder with the frank curiosity of a man who has never learned to read closely enough to be embarrassed by his own slowness at it. "It's the same duty," he said at last, as though the thought had only just settled in him. "We've only put our own coat on it."

"For want of another," Ailean said, which was the same answer Dùghall would go on giving, in one form or another, for as long as he kept the harbour, though neither of them knew that yet.

He carried the finished copy south himself rather than trust it to a courier, folded flat inside his coat against the coast road's dust, and thought, walking, of the label he had written on the crate of tithe rolls: *unread*. The tariff table he carried now had been read most carefully of all, word by word, clause by clause, and copied out exact, and he could not have said with any honesty that the reading had changed a single thing it was made to do.

## III. Of the Labelled Box

The last of his three counting-houses was the one at Seann Skell in the west, the humans' great harbour on the coast below the capes, where the fishing fleets still came in under a flag they had never learned to read and the old harbour bell rang the hours by a humans' clock nobody had yet thought to reset.

The counting-house there had taken the least harm of the three, for the town had changed hands with barely a shot fired, its garrison marching out under terms before the council's companies were even in sight of the walls, and Ailean found its shelves whole where Cathair dhearg's had burned and Ros dhomhain's had been picked over by two summers of harbour clerks before him. He worked through it alone for the better part of a week, boxing what he was told to box, tithe rolls and customs rolls and the wage-books of men whose names meant nothing to him, until he came, on a shelf set apart from the rest behind a locked grating that had taken the smith half a morning to force, on a set of rolled sheets larger than anything else in the room, each one bound with a strip of the humans' own linen tape and marked, in a surveyor's careful print, with a district's name.

Setting his own crate aside, he unrolled the first of them on the long table by the window and stood over it a long while before he thought to send for Ruairidh.

It was a map, but more than a map: every field about Seann Skell laid out with its boundary walked and measured, every holding numbered, every stream and track set down to a scale marked plainly in one corner, in a hand so exact that Ailean, who had grown up on a hillside no one had ever thought worth surveying at all, felt for a moment that he was looking at the land laid out entire, folded small and put under glass. He had seen the council's own maps, such as they were: rough things, drawn from memory and hearsay, good enough to show a captain which valley led where but not good enough to say whose field lay on which side of a boundary stone. Nothing in his life had prepared him for what lay unrolled on that table.

He counted the district sheets as he unrolled them, laying each flat under a weight of loose stone from the yard before it could curl back on its own roll, and found there were eleven in all, enough between them, he judged from the scale marked in the corners, to cover the whole of the coast from the north-western capes down past Seann Skell itself and some way inland besides, though how far inland he could not tell until he had them all open together, which the table was not long enough to allow. He knelt on the floor instead and laid three of them edge to edge, matching a stream on one sheet to its continuation on the next by eye, and felt, doing it, something closer to the excitement of a boy given a puzzle than to the flat patience with which he had boxed the tithe rolls all that month.

Ruairidh came, looked, said nothing for a while, and then said what Ailean had already half thought himself: that there was nothing else like this on the whole of the island, that whatever the council's own men might one day draw of the shires it meant to rule, they would draw it from this, because there was nowhere else whole enough to draw it from.

"Box them apart from the rest," Ruairidh said. "Not with the tithe rolls. These go to the council's own clerks, not the reckoners." He thought a moment longer, and added, in the tone of a man handing over a decision he would rather not have had to make himself, "Write something on the crate so whoever opens it next knows what it's for."

Ailean found a fresh crate, laid the rolled surveys in it one district at a time, each still bound in its own tape, and stood with the lid in his hand and a stub of chalk, thinking what to write. He could have written what the crate held, plainly, as he had for the tithe rolls: *district surveys, Seann Skell in the west, entire*. It would have been true and would have said nothing that mattered. He thought instead of the council's own rough maps, of the valley that showed a captain the way but not the boundary, of a kingdom that had just that summer begun calling itself a kingdom with no whole map of its own to be one by, and he wrote, in the end, six words that were not a description of what the crate held so much as a statement of what it was for: *The shires will be drawn on them.*

He looked at the words a moment after he had written them, chalk-white on unpainted wood, and understood, standing there with the lid half over the crate, that he had just written down the truest sentence he had put to anything since the margin of the council's own proclamation the year before, and that it troubled him in the same way that sentence had, because a kingdom that drew its shires on the humans' district lines, using the humans' own survey of the humans' own farms, was not drawing a kingdom fresh out of the ground it had won. It was tracing one that had already been drawn, by other hands, for other purposes, and calling the tracing its own.

He shut the lid, nailed it, and marked the crate for the council's own clerks rather than the reckoners, as Ruairidh had told him. Nobody who read the label in the months and years that followed, as the shire lines were in fact drawn from what the crate held, exactly where the crate had said they would be, ever asked who had written it or what he had meant by it.

## IV. Of What the North Tells

It is told at Muileann chaol, though not from any minute the council kept, for it kept none, that in those same weeks, at Ceann mhòr and Cathair gheal and Inis àrsaidh on the north-western coast, a different kind of counting-house was opened, and opened rather more quickly than the ones Ailean was working through.

The humans' harbour officers at those three towns had sailed with the last ships of An t-Aiseag and had taken their keys with them, whether from forethought or from simple haste nobody left behind could afterward say, and the harbour stores at all three towns stood locked through that spring with the last of the humans' stock still inside them: rope, canvas, salt, tackle, and at Cathair gheal a whole loft of nets that had never been fished. No maor had yet been set over any of the three towns, the council's attention being wholly on the south that season, and for a while the stores simply stood, locked and unclaimed, while the towns around them went short of exactly the things the stores held.

It did not stand long. A fisherman of Ceann mhòr, whose name the north's own telling does not agree on, though every version gives him a hammer and a bad temper, walked down to the store one wet morning, tried the lock with his hand, tried it again with his shoulder, and then went home for his hammer and came back and broke it. He did not, by any account the north tells, take anything for himself that first morning. He broke the lock, opened the door, and stood back to let the town see what was inside, and it was the town, not the man with the hammer, that decided within the hour how the rope and the canvas and the salt would be shared out, house by house, by much the same rough justice that had already, that same year at Caol mhòr, decided how a thin catch of herring ought to be shared.

Cathair gheal and Inis àrsaidh followed within the fortnight, each in its own way: at Cathair gheal it is told that the whole loft of unfished nets went out to every boat in the harbour on a single tide, so that the fleet that year fished with nets it had never paid a coin for and never afterward wondered whose they had once been; at Inis àrsaidh, smaller than the other two and poorer, the store held little enough that the sharing was done inside an afternoon, what the town remembers of it is the reckoning rather than the goods: who had a boat and who had none, and so who needed rope and who needed salt more, drawn up on the spot by three women of the town with no office to do it and no one afterward to say they had done it wrong.

The north tells this part, when it tells it at all, with something close to relish, in a way it does not tell the winter of A' Bhliadhna Acrach that came so soon after: broken locks, a hammer, goods shared out by the plain justice of a fishing town rather than doled out by any office south of the water. No maor ever came to ask who had struck the first blow, for by the time one did come north, in the winter of the year after, there was nothing left locked to account for, and the town's own memory of the thing had already hardened into the kind of story a place keeps because it likes the shape of it: the strangers left, and took their keys, and the sea folk broke in with their own hands and helped themselves, and no one has ever been the worse for it.

Ailean, boxing tithe rolls at Cathair dhearg that same month, heard nothing of it at the time, and no clerk's crate anywhere in the south held so much as a line about it, because no one south of the water had thought there was anything there worth a clerk's crate.

## V. Of the Proclamation

The council styled the state a kingdom on the twenty-seventh of an t-Iuchar, from the steps of the counting-house at Cathair dhearg, the very building whose northern wall Ailean had spent the earlier weeks of that summer clearing crate by crate.

He was given no part in the reading. He stood in the crowd instead, in the press of clerks and harbour men and off-duty guards that had gathered in the square below the steps once word went round that the council meant to say something, close enough to hear the words plainly and too far back to see more than the tops of the twelve heads on the platform above him. A herald, not the crier who had read the council's word at Caol mhòr the year before, read out a proclamation that named the state, from that day, Rìoghachd Dia-thìr, and spoke of the island won back and the homeland made secure and the years of settling now, in the herald's own word, *complete*. It was not a long proclamation. It named no king, for none had yet been crowned, and no new law, for none had yet been made; it was, as far as Ailean could judge standing in the crowd with the sun on the back of his neck, mostly a naming, the council giving itself a title it had in truth held in every particular but the word for some months already.

He thought, listening, of the crate at Seann Skell with its six chalked words, and of the tariff table at Ros dhomhain copied out in a new tongue to say exactly the old thing, and of the seal the council's engraver had that same week begun cutting for the state's own letters, a seal that would go, when it was finished, onto documents no different in kind from the ones he had spent the summer boxing unread. A kingdom had been proclaimed from the steps of a building that had been, until that spring, a foreign counting-house, using a survey drawn by foreign hands to mark out the shires it meant to rule, keeping in force, for want of another, a tariff the foreigners themselves had set. He did not think any of this made the proclamation false, exactly. He thought it made the proclamation a kind of label, not unlike the one he had chalked on the crate of surveys, true as far as it went and silent about everything that had gone into making it true.

Beside him in the crowd stood a woman he did not know, a fishwife by her basket and her salt-reddened hands, who asked the man on her other side, in the flat voice of someone asking merely to have something answered rather than because the answer mattered to her trade, whether a kingdom paid its dues any different from a council. The man did not know. Nobody near Ailean seemed to know, and he thought that this, more than the herald's words or the twelve heads on the platform, was the truest measure of what had been proclaimed: a name new enough to need explaining and an arrangement old enough that nobody in the crowd expected the explaining to change anything they would be asked to pay.

The crowd around him cheered when the herald finished, in the ragged, willing way of a crowd that wants something to cheer and has been given, at last, an excuse. Ailean did not cheer. He stood with his hands in his coat, watching the twelve on the platform receive the cheering as though it had been meant for them personally rather than for the word, and thought that a state could change its name from a war-council to a kingdom on a single afternoon and be, in every working part beneath the name, exactly the state it had been the afternoon before: the same tariff, the same surveys, the same crates going into the same rooms marked in the same hands, only now under a seal cut fresh and called royal.

He walked back to the counting-house that evening, after the crowd had gone, and let himself in with the key Ruairidh had given him, to check, he told himself, that the last of the week's crates were properly nailed before the carters came for them in the morning. The northern wall stood bare where the tithe rolls had been, swept and empty for the first time since he had begun. The crate marked for the council's own clerks, the one with six words chalked on its lid, still stood by the door where he had left it, waiting to be carried up to whichever room the shire-lines would in the end be drawn in.

Reading the words over once more by the last of the evening light through the high windows, *the shires will be drawn on them*, he thought that whatever the herald had proclaimed that afternoon from the steps outside, the truer proclamation of the summer was chalked in his own hand on a crate nobody but a handful of clerks would ever see, and that the kingdom the crowd had cheered for would be built, quite literally, field by field and boundary by boundary, on lines that other hands, under another flag, had walked and measured first. This did not, to his mind, make the kingdom any less a kingdom. It made it the kind of kingdom that proclaimed itself in the open, on a summer afternoon, with bells and a herald and twelve men receiving a crowd's cheers, and settled itself, in the meantime and mostly unseen, in rooms like this one, on paper like this, in a hand like his own.

He locked the counting-house behind him and went home by the harbour road, past the sheds where the council's idle ships still lay, and did not write any of it down that night. There was, he thought, nothing in it that the crate had not already said better than he could, in six words and a piece of chalk.
