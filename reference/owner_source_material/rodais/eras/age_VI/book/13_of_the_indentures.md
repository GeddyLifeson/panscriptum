# Of the Indentures

*Part Three: Of Guilds, Law and Library*

*SE 14 am Faoilleach – an Dùbhlachd*

> *Scrìobh a làmh an t-ainm; a làmh fhèin a reub e.*
> His hand wrote the name; that same hand tore it.
>
> — said of Seumas Crùbach at {{place:burg:282}}, of the loosing of the indentures, {{date:V-0062}}

## I. Of the Yard in Winter

Harry Marlow was seventeen that Faoilleach, four years into his time at Seumas Crùbach's yard on the hard at {{place:burg:282}}, and had stopped, some while before, counting the years the way a boy counts them and started counting them the way a bound man does, by the work left to learn rather than the work already behind him. The yard in am Faoilleach kept short hours and long fires. There was little to build that no one wanted mended first, and the mending, that winter, was slow work done by lamplight with the wind coming straight off the firth and finding every gap in the shed's south wall that four winters of caulking had not yet closed.

Seumas Crùbach was forty that year, a big slow-spoken man with one leg shorter than the other from a fall off a stage twenty years before, which was where the name had come to him and stayed, and he had taken Harry on, sight unseen, on a passing word about a boy at Doire mhin who could true a plough-beam by eye. He had not been disappointed in the boy, and said as much, rarely, and never twice. What he had taught Harry in four years he taught the way most of the yard's masters taught, by making him hold the far end of things, the adze, the clamp, the long batten sprung round a rising frame, until holding them stopped being a thing he thought about and became a thing his hands simply did, the way Lachlann's chainman up at the Sloc Mòr, had either of them ever met the other, might have said his own trade was learned. Seumas kept a small library of nothing at all, no book of the trade beyond a single dog-eared tide-table nailed inside the shed door, and taught out of his hands and out of the hulls standing round him, which he held to be library enough for any craft worth calling one.

At the far end of the hard, past the two hulls under repair and the third laid up on blocks for the winter, stood a fourth that nobody paid to have mended, an old human vessel left derelict on the sand since before Harry's time, her stem stove in on a falling tide some years back and never worth the labour of hauling clear, so that she had simply stayed, settling a little deeper into the shingle each winter, her timbers greying and her caulking gone to powder. Harry had taken to her in his second year the way another boy might have taken to a book, going down to her in the stolen half-hour before the yard opened or after it shut, first only to look, and later, once he had begun to trust his own eye, to measure. It had troubled him from his first winter at Cuan shean that the humans' way of raising a hull and the yard's own way came at the same hull from opposite ends: the yard's hulls went up frame first, rib after rib stepped and faired before a single plank closed them in, so that a man could stand inside the naked skeleton of a boat and read her whole form before she held water; the humans, by every account Harry had ever been given, built the other way about, plank on plank from the keel up, shaping the hull's skin before her bones were more than a notion in the shipwright's head, a method the yard's men called quick and unsound in the same breath, the way a man might call a short cut both quick and unsound. He had asked a stranger once, years before, at his father's forge, which of the two ways was truer, and been told only that he would have his answer soon enough. No master of the yard had troubled, in four years, to build a hull the humans' way and show him. The old wreck on the hard was the nearest thing to an answer the yard possessed, and Harry went to her, that winter and every winter since his second, with a rule and a scrap of slate, learning by his own measure what no man at Cuan shean would trouble to teach him.

The other apprentices of the yard, four of them that winter besides Harry, three Dia-thìreach boys bound in the ordinary way and one Tuathaich boy younger than Harry, out of a fishing family further down the coast, thought little of Harry's evenings at the wreck and said as much, the biggest and idlest-handed of the Dia-thìreach boys, who would himself be sworn that spring, telling him more than once that a man learned nothing from a dead hull that a live one, worked under a master's eye, would not teach him twice as fast and half as cold. Harry did not argue the point, having found argument a poor tool against a boy who had never gone down to the sand himself to see what there was to learn there, and went on measuring. The younger Tuathaich boy alone, of the four, sometimes came down with him in the last light, to hold the lamp steady rather than to measure, having no head yet for it, and to ask, now and again, some plain question neither of them yet had the words for, and it was in answering those plain questions, more than in any book, that Harry first found words of his own for what his hands already knew.

## II. Of the News from Ros dhomhain

The news came up the coast the way most news came to Cuan shean that year, by a coaster putting in for water and staying an extra tide for the gossip, and it was old news already, three weeks stale, by the time it reached the yard: that the shipwrights of {{place:burg:27}}, who had built hulls for the whole southern coast since before the kingdom had any name of its own, had taken a charter of their own from the guild court at {{place:burg:19}}, and bound thirty apprentices in a single day on the slipway to mark it, the largest binding the south had seen since the Crossing. Seumas heard it out at the shed door from the coaster's mate, a wiry man with a Ros dhomhain accent thick enough to cut, and asked, when the telling was done, only how many of the thirty were Tuathaich boys, which the mate could not say, not having troubled to ask, and thought an odd question to be asked at all.

It was not, that winter, an odd question at Cuan shean, or anywhere along the coast where a Tuathaich boy had ever been bound to a Dia-thìreach master's craft. The charters were going up everywhere that year, guild after guild taking its letters from the court at Cathair dhearg the way a town takes a market day, each charter naming its own trade's custom and each custom, without quite saying why, drawn a little narrower than the last. It was told, that same winter, of the chandlers of {{place:burg:88}}, upriver of the capital on the Abhainn fhionn, that their new charter won them the sole right to fit the Crown's patrol boats, a right that would outlast the boats themselves by years neither the chandlers nor the Crown then troubled to reckon; and of the lamp-makers of {{place:burg:137}}, who made between them every coal lamp burned in every shaft on the island, that their charter set upon their work a mark, a flame struck over a hammer, stamped from that winter on every lamp that left their sheds, so that a hewer a hundred miles off, holding a strange lamp up to the light, could read in that small stamp the whole history of where it had been made and under what oath. Of all the marks the guilds set that year, the humans' own traders, who copied little from the island and less from its guild law, were said to have copied that one alone, finding it, whatever else they made of the rest, a useful thing to know a lamp by.

What the charters did not say, most of them, was what was owed a boy already bound before the charter was read, and it was this silence, more than any single line in any single guild's letter, that sat that winter in the yards and the smithies and the weaving-sheds of every coast the humans' children worked. A rope-maker's apprentice at {{place:burg:27}}, four years bound already and thought by his own master fit to be sworn with the thirty, brought his case that winter to the same guild court that had chartered the shipwrights, standing before three benchmen in a hall built for the hearing of debts and disputed cargoes and asking only to be sworn along with the rest of his master's household. He was heard out in full, the court giving him, by the account that reached Cuan shean, a fairer hearing than a rope-maker's apprentice commonly got, and refused in the same sitting, the eldest benchman reading the charter's own words back to him slowly, that its custom of binding was kept for those of Dia-thìrich blood and named no other, and that a court sworn to the charter could no more stretch its words for one boy's sake than a shipwright could stretch a keel too short for the hull laid on it. The boy's own master, it was told, said nothing in the hearing to help him, having, by then, no words left in the charter's own language that would have served. The Moot at {{place:burg:95}}, hearing of the ruling by the same slow road all northern news travelled, wrote that winter to the guild court at Cathair dhearg asking plainly what was to become of the indentures already standing across every trade the charters touched, and had its letter filed among that year's other business, without an answer, a fact the Moot's own minute-book set down at the foot of the page in a hand that Harry, had he ever seen it, would have known at once for its plainness, whoever held the pen.

Seumas said little of any of it that winter, being a man slow to speak of a thing until he could see the whole of it, and Harry noticed, without being told to notice, that his master had begun, some time in am Faoilleach, to look at him a beat longer than the work required before turning back to whatever the two of them were doing, the way a man looks at a thing he expects, before long, to lose.

## III. Of the Eight-and-Twentieth of am Màrt

The letter came to Cuan shean on the twenty-eighth of am Màrt, carried by the same maor's clerk who rode the coast road twice a year with the shire's other business, and it said, in four lines that took the clerk less time to read aloud than it had taken him to find the yard, that the custom of binding, restored to every chartered guild of the island that winter and spring, was kept henceforth for Dia-thìrich hands alone, and that any indenture standing between a guild master and a Tuathaich boy, wherever on the coast it was held, was from that day loosed. The same letter, or one much like it, went that week to {{place:burg:47}} in the west, where the Leòid ruling had been given years before on land and not on labour, and to every yard and shed along the coast where a Tuathaich boy had ever taken a master's name along with a master's craft.

Seumas read it through once himself, standing in the shed door with the clerk still waiting for some word to carry back, and read it a second time slower, though there was nothing in four lines that a second reading could change. He asked the clerk, once, whether the rule reached back to a boy four years bound already or only forward to one not yet taken on, and was told that it reached back, the letter's own words being *any indenture standing*, and that the guild court at Cathair dhearg had been plain enough about it not to leave a master room to argue the other way. The clerk did not stay long after that, having other yards on the coast road still to reach before dark, and Seumas stood a while in the doorway with the letter in his hand after the clerk's horse had gone, before he went in to find Harry at the bench, planing a stem-piece for the second hull, and told him, without any lead-up at all, what the letter said.

Harry heard it out standing, the plane still in his hand, and said nothing for a space long enough that Seumas repeated the last of it, as though the boy might not have caught it the first time. He had caught it the first time. What he could not, in that first space, find words for was less the fact alone, which he understood plainly enough, four years of a trade being no small thing to have taken from a man by four lines read out at a shed door, than the look on Seumas's face while he said it, a look Harry had not seen there before, one hard to name exactly, somewhere between the grief a man shows at a death he cannot prevent and the shame a man shows at a thing he is made to do and cannot, however plainly the fault lies elsewhere, entirely forgive himself for doing.

The indenture had hung, these four years, in a frame behind the shed's one good window, Harry's name and Seumas's own set down together in a clerk's careful hand and witnessed by the harbour-master of that year, long since moved on to other work. Seumas took it down himself that afternoon, unhooked it from its nail without a word to Harry about doing it, and tore it, not neatly, the paper being stiffer with age than either of them had judged it, so that the tear ran crooked across both their names before it came free in two pieces in his hands. He did this with his back half turned, and did not, in the doing of it or after, look at Harry directly, occupying his eyes instead with the two torn halves of the paper, which he folded once, carefully, as though a careful folding might undo some part of what the tearing had done, and set on the bench rather than in the fire, where four years' worth of another man's kept faith seemed, to him at least, to deserve better than burning.

"You'll have heard the wage a free hand gets on this coast," he said at last, still not looking up from the paper. "Half a bound man's, and no guild mark to show for it, and no standing in the court if a debt goes bad on you. I'll not pretend to you it's a fair trade for what the four lines took." He paused, and Harry, watching him, understood that whatever came next had cost his master some effort to arrive at. "But the bench is still yours if you'll have it on those terms, and I'll not have another boy stand where you stood these four years and call the standing his by right, when it was never given him by anything but a paper that's ash and torn corners now."

Harry said, when he had found the words for it, that he would have the bench.

## IV. Of the Day-Labour

He stayed, through that spring and the summer after it, at half the wage a bound man drew and none of the standing, which was, in the small ways a yard measures such things, no small change. He ate at the same board as the sworn men still, Seumas seeing to that much himself, though he sat at its foot rather than its middle, by a custom nobody troubled to name to him and that he learned quickly enough on his own not to ask named. More often than before he was sent to fetch and carry rather than to fair a plank, the yard's other hands, sworn men now under the new charter Seumas himself had at last to take from the guild court that spring to keep trading at all, being wary of being seen teaching their craft too openly to a hand the charter no longer counted theirs to teach. And the whole year went by without a guild mark of his own to stamp on any work, so that a stem-piece he had fair-shaped from rough timber to finished curve left the yard, when it left it, under Seumas's stamp alone, the hands that had shaped it going unrecorded in the wood.

The half-wage told in smaller ways too, ways that took the year to add up to anything a man would trouble to complain of. He mended his own boots that year rather than paying the cobbler at the harbour end, and went without the tobacco he had taken up, against his father's wishes, in his third bound year, and put off, twice, buying a coat the winter weather said plainly he needed, until Seumas, noticing the third winter of the same worn coat without being told why it had lasted so long, set a length of oiled wool on the bench one morning with no word of where it had come from and none asked. Harry took it, and said only that he would pay him back for it come the spring herring money, and Seumas said that he would take the payment if it was offered and would not trouble himself if it was not, which both of them understood, without either saying so, to mean the same thing twice over.

What the charter did not take from him, having no way to reach it, was the hour before the yard opened and the hour after it shut, and it was in those hours, through that spring and on into an t-Samhradh, that Harry went back, oftener now than in any year before, to the old wreck on the hard, measuring her frames rib by rib with a patience the yard's own work no longer quite gave him room for, and setting down what he found in a small book of his own, kept apart from any book the yard or the guild would have troubled to read.

The wreck had given up her secrets slowly and in the wrong order over Harry's three prior winters at her, the way any old and broken thing does to a boy working alone and by guesswork, and it was only the autumn before this one, on a night he did not afterward forget, that a plank sprung loose at the turn of her bilge showed him, in the shape its edge had been shaved to before ever it was fastened, the thing the yard's own frame-first men had no cause to know: that a human shipwright did not lay his planks flat and force them to the hull's curve after, as Harry had half supposed, but shaved each one, before it ever touched the keel, to a curve the eye alone had judged against the planks laid before it, so that the whole skin of the hull was faired plank by plank, from the keel up, long before a single frame was stepped inside her to hold the shape true. It was, when he had it, a small enough thing to have taken three winters over, and he had sat a long while on the cold sand that evening with the loose plank across his knees, turning it in the last of the light, before he trusted himself to write it down. This spring and summer, freed of bound hours earlier in the day than any year before, he set himself to proving the finding against every strake still fast to her, plank by plank, to be certain the one loose piece had not misled him.

By an Lùnastal of the year the letter came, he had her lines, so far as four years' rust and rot had left them to be had, set down whole from stem to stern in the small book he kept apart from any book the yard or the guild would have troubled to read, and had learned, mostly by his own error corrected against his own measure, why a hull built plank first and frame after held together at all, a discipline the yard's own frame-first men, working the surer way, never had cause to learn.

A trader out of {{place:burg:29}} put in at Cuan shean that an t-Sultain, working the coast on his way north with a hold half full of cloth the northern towns still bought though the coin that paid for it grew scarcer each year, and heard, over an evening's ale, something of Harry's case from one of the yard's own sworn men, not unkindly meant, and said to Harry himself, the following morning, that a free hand with steady hands and no guild behind him might do better for himself at one of the northern harbours, where a man's craft was reckoned, he said, by what it was worth rather than by what paper stood behind it, there being, in the north, no guild court to stand behind either. Harry heard him out, and thanked him for the telling, and did not go. He gave the trader no long reason for staying, having none yet fully worked out even to himself, beyond the plain fact that the old wreck's lines were not yet whole in his book, and that a craft learned by his own measure, wreck by wreck and rib by rib, seemed to him, that autumn, worth more than a paper standing behind him in a court three hundred miles off that had never once, in four years, stood behind him in the court three miles from where he slept.

The year ended as every year of his apprenticeship had ended, with Harry setting aside from his wage, such as it now was, the sum he sent north each Dùbhlachd to his father's forge at {{place:burg:5}}, a habit begun in his first bound year and kept now, half a bound man's wage or not, without his troubling to weigh whether a free hand owed the habit any less than a sworn one had. It was a smaller sum that year than any year before it, the half-wage making sure of that, and he wrote no long letter to go with it, his father Joseph Marlow being, by all Harry had ever seen of him, no reader of long letters in any case. What he wrote, folded small inside the coin, was four words only, set down in the same hand the guild's own clerk might have used to close out an account: *the bench is still mine.*

Seumas kept the two torn halves of the old indenture on the bench through that whole year, moving them from shelf to shelf as the shed's other clutter grew round them but never once putting them to the fire, and it was noticed, by more than one hand in the yard, that when a stranger asked him that Dùbhlachd how his boy did, meaning Harry, he answered as he always had, that the boy did well enough, and did not trouble, in the answering, to correct the word *boy* to *hand*, or the possessive that went before it to anything less than it had always been.
