# Of the Bare Entry

*SE 28 am Faoilleach – SE 29 an t-Ògmhios*

## I. Of Achadh mhin's Fields

The clerk who carried the shire's return up from the south-west that winter was young enough to think the news in it worth a second horse, and rode the last stage in the dark to save the half-day it would have cost him to wait for light. Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill read it at her own table at Muileann chaol by a single lamp, the household long abed, and found nothing in it that a fresh horse had been worth, which was not the same as finding nothing in it at all.

Achadh mhin's fields bore again. It was the plainest sentence the shire clerk had ever sent her: the barley had come up along the old strips, the salt long since rained out of ground that had lain under Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol's tide-mark for a generation, and three of the households that the council had moved off it in the year of the wave, or their children in their place, had asked and been granted their old ground back, measured out to the same lines the humans' surveyors had cut before any Dia-thìreach clerk had thought to draw a shire on top of them. The shire had gone to the trouble of the old humans' plat rather than draw new bounds of its own, since the old lines were the ones the families still walked by memory, their grandparents having farmed inside them, and a boundary a family can find in the dark by the line of a hedge is worth more than one a surveyor can find at noon with a chain.

She wrote the entry in four lines, gave it the category the record gave such things, and noted, because she always noted it when she could, the day the return had reached her and not only the day the fields were sown. It went into Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd between a drought at Baile chiar and a wedding at Doire shean, one line among the ordinary traffic of a kingdom's year, and she thought no more of it that night than she thought of any entry once it was written and the ink dry.

One of the three households was named in the return, since the shire clerk had thought it worth the ink: a family that had farmed the northernmost of the drowned strips before the wave, moved inland to a cousin's holding for a generation, and come back that spring to find the old boundary stone still standing where the tide had left it, tilted but not thrown down, and had righted it themselves before troubling the shire for a measurer at all. Gormshuil kept that detail out of Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd's own entry, which had no room for a family's name among a shire's ordinary returns, but she copied it into the private book she had kept these several years and showed to no one, on the ground that a record built only of what the office required would in the end tell a reader less about the kingdom's year than the office already knew, and that somebody, someday, might have use of the difference.

She thought more of it three weeks later, when the second courier came, this one no less young but riding no faster than the roads allowed, since nothing in his bag would be helped by haste and everything in it would be hurt by a torn seal. Gormshuil had kept Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd ten years by then and knew the weight of a packet by its wrapping before she broke it: a shire's ordinary return came loose in a merchant's satchel; a council packet came sealed twice, once by the clerk who wrote it and once by whoever above him had read it and agreed it should go. This one was sealed twice.

She broke it standing, which she did not usually do, and read it standing too, which she did even less, and when she had read it through she sat down at the same table where she had entered Achadh mhin's barley and found that her hand, reaching for a fresh sheet, was steadier than she had any right to expect, and steadier, she thought afterward, than the news deserved.

## II. Of the Bare Entry

Brìghde was dead, and the packet gave no more of the matter than that. It gave the day. No cause was made known, in words that made plain the office writing them expected the phrase to be believed and did not much mind whether it was. Eòghan, Brìghde's cousin, had been crowned within the week by Macha's rite, as the rite required when a throne could not sit empty past a certain number of days, and a line added at the foot, in a different, hastier hand, said that the council's own sitting on the matter had not passed as quietly as the crowning that followed it.

Gormshuil sat a long while with the sheet in front of her and wrote nothing. She had, in ten years, buried one king and crowned another, and had let one phrase into the record for it, weighed for a night and kept for good; she had told herself then that a chronicler who let herself one ornament, chosen with care, was safer than a chronicler who let herself none and then, some later year, gave way all at once to a worse one under worse pressure. She believed that still. It was not, she found, sitting with the lamp burning down and the packet's two seals broken on the table in front of her, an answer to the question the second death put to her, because the second death was not the same question as the first.

*Mar a thàinig Niall* had cost her nothing to write because it had cost the kingdom nothing to hear. A king had died in his middle years, and had been followed by a daughter nobody contested; the sentence she had borrowed had been light because the year it described had been light. This year was not light. A queen had died young, of a cause the office that wrote to her did not name and, by the turn of the sentence, did not intend ever to name, and the crowning that followed her had gone through a council sitting hard enough that someone thought the fact of its hardness worth adding in a different hand, though not worth explaining. A chronicler who reached for a soft phrase now, having reached for one once before, would not be choosing an ornament. She would be choosing a habit, and a habit chosen at the one moment the record most wanted to speak plainly was a habit chosen exactly wrong.

She wrote three drafts before she wrote the one that stood. The first ran to six lines and said, without quite saying it, that a young death was a hard death for a chronicler to leave unremarked, which she struck through before the ink was dry, on the ground that a reader wants to know what happened and has no use for what the chronicler found hard. The second ran to two lines and said only the day and the fact, and she looked at it a long time before striking it too, because a chronicle that gave a queen's death two bare lines when it had given the last one a name for what came after read, to her own eye, less like restraint and more like a door pulled shut on purpose, and a shut door draws the eye of anyone who later comes looking for what was behind it, which was exactly the attention she did not want to invite and could not, in honesty, say her entry was free of inviting regardless of how she wrote it. The third she began and left unfinished on the table past midnight, because it had begun arguing with its own middle before it reached its fact, laying out the case for saying more before it had said the thing plainly. An entry that argues before it states is no record at all, only a brief for one side of a question nobody had asked her to judge.

What she wrote in the end, on the fourth attempt, past the second hour of the morning, gave the day of Brìghde's death, the fact that no cause was made known, the day of Eòghan's crowning by the rite, and nothing else. Nothing was said of the council's hard sitting on the succession. Nothing was said of what the harder hand in the packet's second seal had wanted her to know without quite writing it down. Four bare lines carried a queen's death and a king's crowning together, the same length Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd would have given a lesser succession in a lighter year, and she signed the page, and did not strike it, and sat with it in front of her until the lamp guttered rather than trim it and give herself more light to doubt it by.

She knew, setting it into Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd the next morning in her own clean hand rather than trusting the copy to a clerk, exactly what the bareness would do. A reader coming to that page a hundred years on, or ten, would read four short lines after a long entry for the coming of Niall's own reign, would notice the shortness before noticing anything the lines actually said, and would ask, as she herself had asked of shorter entries in older books, what the silence was standing in for. She could not stop that asking. She had decided, over the third draft struck through on the table, that she did not want to. An entry padded to look full said, to anyone who read enough of them, precisely as much as an entry left honestly bare, and said it more dishonestly, since it dressed a silence as a fullness and hoped the reader would not weigh the two the same. She would rather the silence stood for what it was and let the reader do their own weighing than build a small deception into the kingdom's own record to spare a future reader the discomfort of wondering.

"You wrote nothing of the hard sitting," said Tormod, the following week, laying a fair copy beside her for checking, having read the packet himself as her clerks always did before she did, since a clerk who did not read what he copied was of no use copying it.

"I wrote what the office sent me and what I could stand behind," she said. "The hard sitting is the council's to enter in its own minutes if it chooses to. It is not Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd's business to tell a reader what to make of a silence it did not create."

He asked her, not for the first time in ten years, whether she thought a chronicler owed the truth or the record, as though the two were ever different questions, and she told him what she had told him before, that the two were the same question asked twice, and that the day she found them pulling apart from one another was the day she would know it was time to lay the office down, whatever the council thought of the timing.

## III. Of the Votes Recorded

The council's own minute of the sitting reached her some weeks after, forwarded by the same office that had sent the first packet, on no request of hers; she took this to mean that somebody above the clerk who copied it wanted the fact of it in the kingdom's permanent keeping even if nobody wanted it dwelt on, which suited her well enough, since dwelling on it was not Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd's office to begin with.

It was the first minute of its kind the council had ever kept, in the restored kingdom's whole span since the twelve first sat at Cathair dhearg. Every crowning before it, Niall's own included, had gone through the council's sitting as a formality entered without a division, the twelve rising as one body to swear the fresh oath the rite required of them and the clerk noting only that the oath was sworn. This time the clerk noted a division. He named the two who had stood against seating Eòghan, Tormod mac Iain Òig and Sìleas nic Artair, and he named nothing else: not the ground either had stood on, not the word either had used to explain a vote that no councillor within living memory had ever needed to explain before, not whether the two had spoken together or apart, not whether the ten who carried the sitting had answered them in the room or only outvoted them and moved on to the day's next business. Four lines, the same length she had given the queen's death and the king's crowning together, and the fourth line was the plainest of the four: *the two did not sit again.*

She entered it as it had come to her, changing nothing, since a council's own minute was not hers to soften or to sharpen, only to keep; and she found, doing so, that the four bare lines of the council's record sat oddly well beside her own four bare lines for the crowning, as though the two offices had reached, from opposite ends of the same season and without a word passing between them, the same conclusion about how much a hard year could safely be let say of its own hardness in the kingdom's own hand.

A later return told her of the two seats, and she entered it separately, since the record kept things in the order they reached her and not the order a reader might wish for a tidy story: before the year turned, the council filled both places, a landed man's son from the western shires taking the one and a widow of a coastal house the other, and neither newcomer, so far as any packet reaching Muileann chaol ever said, was asked in council to account for the vote that had opened the seat they now held. Tormod mac Iain Òig went home to his own lands, and his name does not appear in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd again for some years. A separate note on Sìleas nic Artair, which Gormshuil entered without comment because it was not hers to comment on, told that she kept a house of her own kin's Na Seann Spioradan faith with more attention than the council's business had ever left her time for.

"Two names and no reasons," Tormod said, copying the fair page, "is a strange thing for a first of anything."

"It is the shape the council chose to give it," Gormshuil said. "I did not choose it for them. I only kept it."

She thought, though she did not say it to him, that the council's silence and her own were not quite the same silence, however alike the two entries read on the page. Hers was chosen with a night's argument behind it, three drafts struck through before a fourth stood, and she could have told anyone who asked exactly why she had chosen it. The council's, she suspected, watching the fair copy dry, had not been chosen at all, only arrived at, the way a room full of people who would rather not explain a hard vote will find, without deciding to, that four lines are as much as anyone in the room is willing to put their name to. A chosen silence and a fallen-into one looked the same on a page a hundred years on. She did not think they ought to, and knew of no way, within the office Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd gave her, to mark the difference for a reader who had not sat, as she had, with the packet's two seals broken open under a guttering lamp.

## IV. Of the Ordinary Traffic

The ordinary traffic of that year went on around the bare entry, before it and after it, and she entered it as she entered every year, in the order it reached her. The kingdom did not stop for its own hard season, and Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd, she told Tormod when he asked her whether the bare entry ought not to stand a page to itself, was not the kingdom's mourning-book but its day-book, and a day-book that left a page empty for one death would soon be asked to leave pages for others, until it was more empty page than book.

The return from Baile chiar had come in with the winter packets, in the same week as Achadh mhin's barley, and it told of the dry summer just gone in the weavers' country, the washing-pools behind the fulling-sheds gone so low that the wool could not be scoured and the looms stood idle three weeks for want of clean fleece to put on them. The guild's own clerk had written it, and he wrote it as a guild writes a loss, with the days counted and the bolts not woven set down in a column, and a line at the foot asking the maor whether the market-due on cloth might be eased for the season in proportion. Gormshuil entered the drought and the idle looms and did not enter the asking, which was the maor's business and not Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd's; she noted, because it was the kind of thing she noted, that the guild had counted the bolts it did not weave to the last half-ell and had not counted the weavers it had not paid at all.

The return from Doire shean, in the south-west, came in the same packet and was of another kind entirely. It told of a wedding at the house of Òrd na Cloiche there, the harbour-master's daughter to a clerk of the order's own counting-room, kept by Òrd na Cloiche's rite at Òrd na Cloiche's door, and set down by the harbour-master himself because, he wrote, it was the first wedding in the town in his memory at which the whole of the fishing fleet had come in on the same tide to stand on the quay for it, and he thought an Leabharlann might care to know what a harbour looked like with every boat in it at once. It was a small thing and she entered it as a small thing, one line, and it sat in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd a little below the drought at Baile chiar, with Achadh mhin's barley between them, and a little above the queen's death, and she did not move it afterward. A reader who came to that page afterward, she thought, would find in it what a year was: a loss, a wedding and a death, and none of them waiting on the others.

In the autumn came the fifth copy of the naming from the hall at Seann Dunn, longer by eleven names than the fourth, the hall having taken in that year three households from along the coast that had kept their dead at their own hearths until then. She entered the day it came and the count of names, as she had entered the first four, and on the same day entered the return of the northern circuit, where Uilleam mac Leòid, riding his twelfth year with Leabhar nam Breith behind his clerk's saddle, had heard a Tuathaich family's claim to a grazing at Àth àrsaidh and found for the family in the words of his aunt's ruling, citing the book by its page. It was the first time the circuit's own return cited the book in its own words, and she let the citation stand in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd whole, since it seemed to her that a ruling which could now be cited by page was a ruling that had become, in some sense she could not yet set down plainly, law.

The last return of the year came from Comhairle nam Mèinnean at Cathair mhòr, and it was the shortest she had yet received from that office, a statement of An Sloc Mòr's yield for the twelvemonth given as a single figure and nothing beside it: no faces named, no survey cited, no comparison with the year before. The figure was lower than the year before by a margin she noticed only because she had the year before's figure in her own hand on the shelf above her, and she entered it as it came, a single figure, and set beside it, in the smallest hand she had, the year before's, and no word between them. Tormod, copying the fair page the next morning, asked her why she had set two figures where the Board had sent one. She told him the Board had sent one because the Board chose what it sent, and Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd had two because Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd had kept the other; and that if a reader afterward wished to know why the two differed, the reader would have to ask the Board, since Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd had not been told.

She closed that year's book on the last day of an Dùbhlachd with the lamp trimmed low and the stream below the town frozen at its edges, and wrote at its foot, as she did at the foot of every year, only the count of entries and the day. There were more entries than any year before it. It had not, she thought, felt like a fuller year, and she did not know, putting out the lamp, whether that was the year's doing or her own.

## V. Of the Fire at Inis bhàn and the Letter of the Schools

The spring brought her a return of a plainer kind, one that asked nothing of her but the keeping. Fire had taken the warehouse quarter of Inis bhàn on the Abhainn ìseal, in the north-central country, and burned two days before the town beat it back street by street with buckets and wet sacking and whatever water the river would give up to a chain of hands; the loss was heavy, three storehouses and a dozen dwellings above them, but the dead were none, every soul in the burning quarter having got clear ahead of the flame or been carried clear by neighbours who did not wait to be asked. The town's Hawk house, which had never before opened its doors past the households sworn to it, took in every family the fire had put out of a roof, sworn or not, and kept them through the worst of the spring cold until new roofs could be raised; the return noted this without comment, as the town seemed to have taken it without much comment, a Hawk house doing in a hard week what a Hawk house existed to do and nobody troubling to call it remarkable.

What the town did trouble to call remarkable, and what its return spent three lines on where the fire had had one, was the bell the town bought that summer to stand where the old warehouse bell had burned with its tower under it: cast at the founders' yard at Cathair dhearg to the town's own order and no other's, and rung once on the quay before it was ever hung, so that half the town could say afterward they had heard it new before anyone else on the river did. It carried, by every account that reached an Leabharlann after, further up and down the Abhainn ìseal than any bell in the kingdom, farmers three miles off swearing they set their day by it whether they meant to or not, and Gormshuil, entering the fire and the bell in the same short passage, found herself giving the loud bell nearly as many words as the two days' burning, and let the imbalance stand once she had looked at it, on the ground that a town chooses what it wants remembered of its own hard year as much as any office in Cathair dhearg does, and a chronicle that overruled a town's own choice of what to dwell on was claiming for itself a kind of authority the office had never in fact been given.

In the north that same spring a child was born whom Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd of that year does not mention, and could not have: Dubhan, born mortal into the confinement An Dealachadh had made, decades after the war that had shut the island against those from whom his line descended. No return carried his name south, and no packet brought it to Gormshuil's table.

The letter that closed her year, and that she entered last of the season's business though it had reached her some weeks before the summer's warmest days, came from Tional nan Tuathach, over Walter Hale's own hand, and it was neither bare nor argued with itself in the writing, which she noticed and was glad of, having spent the better part of the winter learning again how much could ride on four plain lines. It gave, in Hale's own flat count, the number of schools the north now kept for its own children, eleven in all, from Agnes Pike's byre at Doire ghlas to a room over a chandler's shop at Ceann mhòr that had opened only that autumn, and it gave, after the eleven, a second number that the letter itself called worth Tional nan Tuathach's separate notice: three of the eleven, Agnes Pike's among them, had begun that year to teach some measure of Dia-thìris alongside the humans' letters and Tional nan Tuathach's own minutes. No order from the council asked for it, and no maor gave leave for it; parents at all three schools had simply come to the teacher and asked, wanting their children able to read a proclamation, a ledger, a shire's boundary-line, in the tongue the kingdom wrote its business in, whatever tongue the household spoke at its own hearth.

Gormshuil read the letter twice, the second time more slowly than the first, and entered it whole rather than in the short hand she gave most returns, letting the eleven schools and the three among them stand in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd in something near Hale's own words, since a fact this plain needed no chronicler's shortening and would have lost by it. She thought, setting the pen down on that entry longer and more fully than she had let herself set it down on the year's harder ones, that a record could be trusted to keep a great silence honestly only if it could also be trusted to keep a small fullness honestly, the one discipline standing or falling with the other; and that whatever a reader made, a hundred years on, of the four bare lines she had given a queen's death, they would at least find, not far past them in the same book, an entry that gave a plain northern letter every word it had asked for, and could judge for themselves which kind of year the chronicler had thought she was keeping.

She copied the letter's last line into her own hand before she filed the whole of it away: *eleven, and three of the eleven now teach both.* It went into Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd exactly as Hale had written it, and she let it close the year's entries, the bare and the full both behind her now.

Before she put out the lamp she opened the private book again, the one no clerk copied and no council packet ever asked to see, and set beside the year's two hardest entries a line of her own that would never go south to Cathair dhearg or stand in any fair copy: that a kingdom which could give a school's letter every word it asked for, and a queen's death four lines and no more, kept two records under one hand and one table, and that she had not yet decided, in ten years of keeping both, whether the difference was a fault in her or a fault in the year. She closed the book on the question unanswered, as she had closed it before, and set the next return, still unread, on the corner of the table for the morning.
