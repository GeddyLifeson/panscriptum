# Of the Coin with the Coal in its Seal

*Part Two: Of Coin, Rite and Mountain*

*SE 5 an Giblean – SE 6 am Màrt*

> *He turned the king's silence into iron, and the iron into bread.*
> — a Library clerk's plain-prose rendering of a Tuathaich song made of Joseph Marlow, sung at Doire mhin

## I. Of the Boxes Under the Beds

The proclamation came up the coast road to {{place:burg:95}} on a wet morning in an Giblean, nailed to the market cross by a maor's rider who did not get down off his horse to read it and did not wait to be asked what it meant. Walter Hale read it himself, over the shoulders of two fishermen who could not, and then read it again, more slowly, because the second time he thought he must have mistaken it.

The humans' coin, every shilling and crown and worn copper penny that had crossed the water in a Company man's pocket or a Company ship's strongbox, was cried down. Within the year it would cease, by the council's own word, to be money at all. In its place the kingdom would strike its own.

It was, Hale wrote in his day-book that night, a fair enough thing for a kingdom to do, and he did not doubt that whoever had signed it south had done it for reasons that made sense at a council table. He had sat at enough tables himself, in his time, to know that most decisions look reasonable from the chair that makes them. What he set down under that, in a smaller hand, was the thing the council table could not see from where it sat: that in every house along the coast road, under a hearthstone or a floorboard or the false bottom of a meal-chest, there was a box, and in the box was what a family had put by, coin by coin, against a bad winter or a daughter's wedding or the year the boats came home empty, and that by the council's own word every one of those boxes now held metal, plain and simple, where savings had sat the day before.

He went round Caol mhòr with his book that week on his own account, nobody having asked him to, because it was the kind of thing his hands did while his mind was working out what to do about it. Ellen watched him counting other people's coin at the kitchen table in the evenings and said nothing, which after eighteen years of marriage he understood as her particular way of telling him she thought it was the right work and a fool's errand both at once. David, twelve that spring and already better with figures than his father, added the columns for him without being asked. Alice, younger, wanted to know why the king's men couldn't just say a coin was still a coin if everybody agreed to go on believing it, which Hale thought, turning it over afterward, was as good a question about money as he had ever heard asked, and one he had no answer for that a child would find satisfying.

By an t-Ògmhios the tally stood at something over four hundred pounds in the old coin, spread thin across better than two hundred households, no single sum among them large, every one of them real. It was, Hale wrote, the whole of what the north had managed to keep back from thirty years of tariffs, tithes and bad harvests, and the council had voided it with a proclamation that took a rider less than a morning to nail up and ride away from.

An old woman at the harbour end of the town, whose name the day-book gives only as Widow Tull, brought him her box unasked one evening, tipped it out on his table and put to him, plainly, as a question she wanted answered by a man who kept figures for a living rather than as any complaint, whether the king's men meant to give her anything at all for it or only to tell her, in a kinder voice than the rider's had been, that it had never truly been hers. Hale had no answer that satisfied either of them, and wrote down only her question, word for word, under that week's total, because he judged it worth more than anything he could have said in reply.

He did not write, because he did not yet fully believe it himself, what the number was going to feel like a year on, when the old coin was worthless and the new coin scarce and the distance between the two had to be crossed by every household in the north on its own, with nothing from the south to help it over.

## II. Of the Mint at Cathair dhearg

Far south, at the old counting-house in {{place:burg:19}} that the humans had built for their own tariffs and the council had taken whole, the kingdom's new coin was already being made, and the Library's later telling of it, drawn from the mint's own first ledgers, has the particular pleasure of a thing done well by a man who liked doing it.

Fearchar mac Dhòmhnaill, whom the counting-house clerks and afterward the whole kingdom called Fearchar Bonn, had been a die-cutter's apprentice under the humans and a die-cutter's journeyman after them, and when the council needed someone who understood a press and a punch and the temper of metal under a hammer, his was the only name anyone put forward twice. He set up his mint in the humans' own strongroom, with the humans' own scales, and he spent the better part of a winter grinding coal dust fine enough to work into an ink that would not smear, because the council had decided, on the advice of Òrd Mhacha, that every coin of the realm should carry the seal of the mountain that fed it, pressed dark into the die's deepest cut, so that a coin clipped at its edge would show the coal's own colour where the silver had been shaved away and could not be passed off as whole.

It took him four tries to grind the dust fine enough and three more to find the binder that would hold it to the die without fouling the strike, and by the time he had it right he had ruined enough good silver in the trying that a lesser man would have been called before the council to explain himself. Fearchar Bonn was not called before anyone. He set the first true coin of the Rìoghachd Dia-thìr on the counting-house table in front of the head of the treasury and said, by the mint's own later account, only that it was done, and the treasury man turned it over twice in the light from the window and said it would do very well.

The coin was honest, in the sense that its weight was true and its seal could not easily be forged, and it was holy, in the sense that Òrd Mhacha's blessing had been said over the first striking and the coal in its ink was reckoned a small piece of the mountain carried out into every purse in the kingdom. The south, which had lost little in the exchange beyond the trouble of getting used to a new face on its money, liked it both ways, and said so, and the coin passed from hand to hand at Cathair dhearg's markets within the month as though it had always been the coin of the realm and the humans' silver had been some passing foreign currency, tolerated but never quite trusted.

Hale never saw a struck coin until the autumn, and when at last one came north in a maor's satchel and he was shown it across a table at the Moot's house, he held it longer than the showing required, turning it as Fearchar Bonn's treasury man had turned the first one, tilting it to the light to find the dark line at the rim where the coal ink sat in the die's cut. It was, he wrote, a well-made thing, and he did not doubt Fearchar Bonn had earned the pride the mint's clerks plainly took in him. What he could not get past, holding it, was that not one soul south of the moss had asked, before they cast their honest and holy coin, what the north was to use in its place while it waited its turn to be honest and holy too.

The maor's man who had brought it north, a young clerk out of Cathair dhearg glad enough of a fire and a bowl after two wet days on the road, told the room, when Hale asked him plainly how a household of the south was managing the exchange, that he did not rightly know, since his own family's savings had never been more than a season's wage put by and the treasury had made that good within the month through his father's own maor's office, the same as every household along that stretch of coast. He meant no unkindness by it either, and said as much himself when he saw how the room had gone quiet around him; he had simply never had cause to learn that a maor's office might exist for thirty years in one shire and never open a like window in another.

## III. Of the Letter Received and Filed

The Moot met on it twice before it agreed what to write, which for a room that had lately settled the winter's grain in a single wet afternoon was, Hale noted, a measure of how much harder it is to argue a household's coin than its bread. Householders who had said nothing when the granary was shared out by need rather than by holding found their voices readily enough when it was their own box under discussion, and the argument ran two full evenings by the light of the Moot's tallow before it settled on a single ask, plain enough that no one south could pretend to misunderstand it: an exchange, pound for pound, of the humans' cried-down coin for the kingdom's new, at a rate to be set by the council and honoured by the council's own mint.

The first evening was mostly given to two men who wanted the letter to say a great deal more than that, one arguing that the Moot should ask outright why the council had cried the coin down without so much as a word north first, the other that it should say nothing of blame at all and only ask, plainly and humbly, for the exchange, on the ground that a letter which scolded a council was a letter a clerk could file with an easier conscience than one which merely asked. Hale, called on at last to say which he favoured, said neither, and said instead that a letter's business was to get an answer, not to be right, and that he had sat across enough humans' counting-house desks in his own time to know that a plain ask, however humbly worded, stood a better chance of being read to the end than a clever one. The room took his word for it chiefly because he was the one among them who had once made his living writing such letters to men who did not have to answer them, rather than for any special skill in the arguing of it, and by the second evening the four lines he had first set down stood nearly unaltered, save that someone thought to add the date, so that whoever received it south would know, to the day, how long it had taken to reach them.

Hale wrote the letter himself, in the humans' hand because he had never learned to write another, and read it back to the room twice before anyone would let him seal it, and the room's chief objection both times concerned the waiting rather than the wording, since a letter carried south by cart took the better part of a fortnight to reach Cathair dhearg and a further stretch of unknowable length to come back, if it came back with anything more than an acknowledgment of its own receipt. David, who was learning his letters that year off his father's shoulder as much as off Ellen's teaching, copied the fair draft out a second time so that one might go with the winter carter and a second wait for the spring roads, on the reasoning, which Hale thought sound, that a petition sent twice by two different hands was harder for a clerk to lose than a petition sent once.

The letter went south in am Faoilleach, in the coat pocket of a carter named Fionnlagh who had made the run twice before and grumbled both times about the state of the road past Àth leathan, and it reached the council's chests, in the ordinary course of the council's business, in the hands of an under-clerk who had by then been eight years at his desk and had learned, as under-clerks do, to read a letter's whole business in the time it took to unfold it.

Ailean mac Iain read the Moot's letter standing at the chest where such things were kept, noted its date, its seal, and its single plain ask, and set it down among that quarter's petitions with the others, because there was nothing in the council's own instructions to him that told him to do anything else with a letter that asked the treasury for money it had not budgeted. He wrote across its top, in the short clerk's hand the chests were full of, the two words the room north would come, in time, to know by heart though none of them would ever see the paper it was written on: *received; filed.*

Hale, hearing of it months later from the same carter who had carried the letter down, judged there was no unkindness in it at all, and said so to Ellen the night he heard it, turning the coin Fearchar Bonn had struck over in his fingers by the fire. A clerk with a chest full of petitions and no standing order to answer this one had done, with perfect correctness, exactly what his post required of him, and that plain correctness was, Hale thought, a far worse thing to reckon with than unkindness would have been, since unkindness at least could be argued with.

## IV. Of Doire mhin in the Snow

He walked to Doire mhin himself in an Gearran, ten miles on a road half snow and half the black mud the snow left when it thawed at noon and froze again by dusk, because a letter had come up from there that he judged wanted seeing rather than reading, and because, if he was honest with the day-book that night, he wanted to see with his own eyes a thing he had only heard told twice removed.

Joseph Marlow's forge stood at the mill end of the town, close enough to the millrace that its wheel turned a second, smaller wheel off the same water when the smith needed more draught than his own arm could give the bellows, and Hale found him there in the smoke and the orange dark with a crucible set deep in the coals and a leather sack at his feet that clinked, when Marlow toed it, with a sound Hale had heard often enough that winter in his own kitchen, counting other men's boxes at his own table.

It was dead coin, Marlow told him, without being asked, because by then half of Doire mhin had brought him theirs and he had stopped waiting for a man to explain what everybody in the room already knew. It was worth nothing as money and it was worth exactly what it had always been worth as metal, which was a good deal, if a man had a use for metal and a fire hot enough to give it one. He had melted down his own box first, to see it done before he offered to do it for a neighbour's, and cast the silver, once it ran, into the long flat shape a ploughshare wants before the grinding, and he showed Hale the first of them, cooled and dressed on the bench, still bearing along one edge the ghost of a king's profile that the mould had not quite erased.

What he charged for the work, Marlow said, turning the share so the forge-light ran along it, was the price of his charcoal and nothing beyond it, because he reckoned, and said plainly enough that Hale wrote it down that night nearly word for word, that a man should not be made to pay twice for coin the council had already taken off him once. He had two sons and a leather apron between them and more dead coin waiting in the yard than he could turn in a season, and he did not expect, he said, to grow rich on it, and did not much want to. His eldest, Harry, thirteen and long-armed for his age, was working the bellows that afternoon with the steady unbothered rhythm of a boy who had done it every day of a hard winter and stopped noticing the work as work; Marlow told Hale, over the ring of the hammer, that the boy was bound in the spring to a shipwright's yard at {{place:burg:282}}, on the recommendation of a Cuan shean man who had passed through Doire mhin the autumn before and watched Harry true a plough-beam by eye without a level, and that he, Joseph, would miss the second pair of hands at the bellows a good deal more than he let the boy see.

There was a song already going round the forge by then, though Marlow himself would not sing it in front of a stranger and looked faintly embarrassed when Hale asked after it, and it was left to one of the neighbour women waiting her turn with a sackful of her husband's savings to hum a few bars of it under her breath while she watched the crucible: a plain tune, easy to carry, with a line in it about a king's silence and a smith's fire that Hale did not catch whole that day and would not hear entire, set down properly, for some years yet.

Harry left off the bellows only when his father told him plainly to go and warm his hands, and stood a while at Hale's elbow looking at the finished share on the bench with the particular attention of a boy already halfway gone from a place in his own mind. He asked Hale, not his father, whether Cuan shean's yards worked in the humans' way of building a hull, plank first and frame after, which he had heard was faster but less sound, or the frame first, which he had heard was slower and better, and seemed disappointed when Hale, who had spent his working life on a quay and not in a yard, could not tell him. Joseph Marlow, hearing the question over the ring of the hammer, said only that the boy had been asking some version of it all winter and would have his answer soon enough without a stranger's help, and there was in the way he said it both the pride of a man who meant to lose his best pair of hands gladly and the plain arithmetic of a man who did not know yet how he would manage the forge come spring without them.

He wrote in his book only that the town of Doire mhin, given nothing from the south but a proclamation and a fortnight's warning, had found between one forge and the next winter's ploughing a way to turn what the council had taken worthless and left worthless into something a field could use, and that no one had asked the council's leave to do it.

## V. Of the Chits at Inis thais, and the Turn

He heard the other telling of it that same winter, at second hand from a Ros bheag man who traded down the west coast and had it, in his turn, from a merchant of Inis thais who came up as far as Caol mhòr each spring with dried fish and left with wool. At Inis thais, on the south-western coast where the new coin was slower to arrive than almost anywhere else in the kingdom, the town's merchants had not waited on the mint at all. They traded through the winter on paper: chits written and countersigned on the strength of the great house of Òrd na Cloiche that stood in the town, whose word for a debt no one on that coast had ever doubted, and when the new coin did at last come south to them in any quantity, the chits were carried to the house's own door and redeemed there, in full, less a tenth kept back by the house for the keeping of the paper and the trust that had stood behind it through the lean months.

It struck Hale, turning the two tellings over together by his own fire that Gearran, that the south-west had solved exactly the trouble the north was drowning in, and solved it without troubling the council for a single word of leave, because the house at Inis thais had something the Moot at Caol mhòr did not: a name old enough and trusted enough on its own ground that its paper could stand in for coin until the coin came. The Moot had no such name. It had only its letter, and the letter had been received and filed.

He asked the Ros bheag trader, before the man went on his way south again, whether the tenth the house of the Stone kept back struck anyone at Inis thais as dear, and the trader laughed, not unkindly, and said that a tenth kept back by a name a man trusted was cheaper, by every account he had ever heard on that coast, than a whole savings box kept back by a name he had never been asked to trust at all. Hale wrote that answer down too, and thought about it longer than the length of it seemed to want.

He set the coin Fearchar Bonn had struck down on the table beside the ploughshare Marlow had given him, part payment, the smith insisted, for the walk out in the snow, and looked at the two of them a long while before he wrote anything at all. What he wrote, when he did, he did not show the Moot until the spring, and even then he showed it to no one but David, who copied it fair into the minute-book without comment, because his father had asked him not to make more of it than the words themselves made.

It was received and filed. He had written those three words himself now, on his own page, more than once that winter, and he understood at last, holding the coin in one hand and the share in the other, that they were not a single clerk's failing, to be argued round or appealed past with a better-worded letter, but the whole shape of the answer the north would get to nearly everything it asked of the south for as long as the north went on asking and did nothing else. A letter received and filed cost the council nothing and settled nothing and could be sent again next year with the same result. A forge lit, and a chit signed on a trusted name, cost the town that lit it or signed it everything it had to give, and settled the thing that same season.

He did not write that the Moot should stop asking. He wrote, and it is this line the Library keeps of the whole winter's business, that a people waiting on an answer that never came would do better to build, while it waited, the thing it had asked for, because the waiting, one way or another, would be a very long wait, and the ground did not stop wanting to be ploughed in the meantime.

The first furrow of that spring at Doire mhin was cut, by the last day of am Màrt, with one of Joseph Marlow's shares behind a borrowed ox, and it turned a clean black line the whole length of the field, and if it carried, ground fine into the iron somewhere under the coulter's edge, the ghost of a king across the water whose face no one now living on that coast could any longer have named, no one who watched it turn troubled to look for it there.
