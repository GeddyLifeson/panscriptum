# Of the Silver at Depth

## I. Of the Faces below the Old Working

Iain mac Ruairidh was past forty, and eleven years at the silver works at Muileann chiar, where Comhairle nam Mèinnean had sent him the summer it gave him his receipt. He had stayed on long enough that the townsfolk no longer called him Comhairle nam Mèinnean's man from the west but simply Iain, the surveyor, as if the works had grown him themselves out of their own rock, and the old workings he had been sent to measure ran shallow, and the new ones, the ones he measured now, did not.

The silver at Muileann chiar had always been worked from the surface down, the old shafts no deeper than a tall house is high, sunk by hands that had learned the seam close to daylight and never troubled to ask what lay beneath it, since a seam that gave silver at arm's length gave a man no reason to dig further for it. It was Iain's own chain, run down a new shaft cut on the strength of nothing more than a hunch one of the guild's own captains had carried for thirty years and never had the coin to test, that first proved the seam ran richer the deeper it was followed. Every worked vein Iain had ever measured before had thinned with depth; this one thickened, the ore darker and heavier in the hand the further down the new gallery went. He checked the figures three times before he wrote them fair, the same habit that had served him at An Sloc Mòr, and found them holding each time, to within the chain's own inch.

He carried the new figures to the guild hall himself rather than send them by a boy, walking the short distance from the shaft's mouth to the hall's door with the roll under his coat against the rain, and found the guild's own masters gathered already, word of the deep gallery's yield having outrun his own walking by the better part of a morning. The old silver guild of Muileann chiar had kept its charter since before An Dealachadh, a charter the humans' own Administration had let stand unaltered through the whole of their years on the island, on the plain ground, it was said, that silver troubled nobody's coal and a guild that troubled nobody was a guild worth leaving be. The charter had lapsed with Riaghaltas nan Coigreach's own going, along with every other paper the humans' hand had signed, and the guild had gone on working the shallow seam these thirty-odd years on custom alone, its captains meeting and its journeymen bound by no writing any court south of Muileann chiar would trouble to uphold if a dispute ever came to one.

He stood in the guild hall's doorway that morning longer than the walk from the shaft had left him breath for, the masters' own talk running ahead of him faster than he could get a word into it, one captain already reckoning aloud what a deeper gallery might be worth to the guild's own share and another asking, more soberly, whether the timbering that held for a shallow working would hold for a deep one, a question Iain thought worth asking and had not yet an answer for. He set the roll down on the hall's long table and let them argue over it a while before he spoke, on the plain habit, learned at An Sloc Mòr, that a room arguing over a survey it has not yet properly read will argue better once it has read it, and unrolled the sheet himself when the noise had thinned enough to let him.

"It will hold," he told the captain who had asked about the timbering, "if it is timbered the way I have drawn it and not the way custom drew the shallow shafts, and not an inch cheaper," and the captain, who had expected a surveyor's usual hedging, looked at him a moment before answering that he supposed a man who had walked the figures three times over had earned the right to say so plainly. A guild working a seam that ran to nothing was a guild the council could safely leave uncharted; a guild sitting on ore that ran the other way, richer with every fathom, was a different matter, and the charter that came back from Cathair dhearg that spring, under the restored custom the council had by then given a dozen older trades, bound the guild's captains to An Crùn's own tithe on every bar struck and gave the guild, in return, the courts' own force behind its bindings. Iain's figures went south with the petition that asked for it, unrolled this time and read entire by three men who had not troubled to unroll his first survey eleven years before, a fact he noted to himself without saying it aloud to anyone at the guild hall, and pinned the noting, in his own private book, beside the older entry it answered.

## II. Of the Blanks and the Gold below Baile dhìreach

The deep gallery's silver came up faster, once the new shaft was properly timbered, than the mint at Cathair dhearg could refine it, more ore reaching the capital in a single season than the mint's own furnaces had ever been built to answer, and the council, rather than build the mint larger at An Crùn's own cost, gave the refining out to the villages of the eastern hills, whose smiths had worked what little silver came their way for generations on nothing grander than a hearth-forge and a patient hand. The villages took the guild's rough bar and worked it down to the mint's own fineness and measure, paid by the pound of finished metal rather than by the day, a bargain the council's clerks reckoned cheap and the villages, who had never before been paid for anything by An Crùn's own hand, reckoned otherwise.

It was at Baile dhìreach, lowest of the eastern villages and nearest the stream that ran off the hills above it, that a smith named for no particular skill beyond his father's trade before him found that the same stream which turned his forge's own wheel carried gold in its gravel, fine grains of it caught in the same pan a boy might use to wash grit from a well. He said nothing of it for a season, working the guild's silver bar as he had been bid and washing his own pan only of an evening when the forge was banked, until the gold he had put by, a modest handful, was worth more than he could easily account for to a maor's clerk who came asking why a village smith kept coin he had no wages to show for. He told the clerk the plain truth rather than invent a lie he would have to keep, and the clerk, to the smith's own surprise, thanked him for the telling and carried the matter south rather than fining him for the withholding.

The council added the stream's gold to the villages' own charge that same year, washed and weighed alongside the silver bar and struck into the same blanks the mint's own dies pressed for Bonn na Rìoghachd, so that a coin drawn at random from any shire's till that year might carry, unmarked and unremarked, a grain of Baile dhìreach's own stream in its weight. The first blanks struck under the new arrangement went west from the eastern hills in am Faoilleach, carried by the same clerk who had first asked the smith his question, riding a road he had ridden a hundred times before on the guild's ordinary business and reckoning, this once, that he carried something worth minding on the way. He handed the blanks to the mint's own master at Cathair dhearg with no more ceremony than a carter handing over a load of grain, and the master weighed them, found the sum true to the fraction the villages' own reckoning had promised, and signed for them without comment, which the clerk took, riding home, for the highest praise the mint's own hand was ever likely to give anybody.

The villages were paid by weight that spring and every spring after, the payment carried up from Cathair dhearg in a locked box a maor's rider brought once the quarter's blanks were counted, and the smith who had found the gold in his own pan, whose name the villages kept among themselves though no royal book troubled to record it, was said by his neighbours to have washed that same stream every evening of his life after, long past the years his own forge still needed the wage, on the plain habit, he told anyone who asked him why, of a man who has once found a thing worth finding and sees no reason to stop looking for it again.

## III. Of Mòr nic Dhòmhnaill

Domhnall the elder, Mòr's own father, had refined silver for the old guild longer than any other hand at Muileann chiar, a careful worker who could tell the fineness of a bar by its ring on the anvil before the assayer's own scale confirmed it, and his daughter had grown up at his elbow the way a smith's own daughter grows up at a forge, learning the work by watching it long before anyone thought to teach it to her outright. She was past thirty herself the year Iain's survey proved the deep gallery, unmarried by then longer than the town thought usual for a refiner's own daughter, having kept her father's books and weighed his bar since her mother's death without much time left over, she told anyone who asked her plainly why, for the kind of courting a town like Muileann chiar expected of its women.

Iain had known her some years before the guild's charter was renewed, the two of them meeting first over a dispute about the true weight of a bar the guild's own scale and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own scale had returned two different figures for, a dispute Iain was sent to settle and Mòr was sent, by her father who trusted her hand more than his own that week, to argue her family's side of. She proved him wrong in front of three of the guild's own captains, walking him through the weighing twice to show him where his own scale's beam sat a hair's breadth off true, and he thanked her for it rather than taking offence, a response she found rarer in a man of Comhairle nam Mèinnean than she had been led to expect and said so to her father that same evening.

They were married in am Faoilleach of the year the deep gallery's charter was signed, a plain ceremony at the guild hall rather than any grander house, the same long table that had held Iain's survey the season before cleared and scrubbed and laid with what the guild's own wives could put together on short notice, bread and the guild's own store of dried fish and a cask a departing wool-factor had left against a debt nobody now troubled to collect. Mòr's father gave her hand himself, his own voice steadier at the giving of it than Iain had ever heard it over a disputed bar, and the guild's captains stood witness in place of any kin Iain had thought to bring from the west, since his own people were a hard two days' distance and a man of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's postings did not run to much visiting. One of the captains, the same who had doubted the timbering, stood and said, when the guild's own custom called for a witness to speak, that he had watched a great many surveys come through that hall and never one that had married into it before, which the room took for the nearest thing to a blessing the man was ever likely to give anybody, and Mòr laughed longer at it than the joke quite earned, which Iain took for the truer blessing of the two.

Mòr kept her own name at the guild's books after the marriage, as a refiner's daughter's name was worth more on a ledger than a surveyor's, and the two of them kept, that first year, two households' worth of habits under the one roof, Mòr rising before first light to weigh the night's cooling bar while it was easiest read and Iain gone before she had finished to whatever gallery the week's work called him to, so that David the guild's own boy, who carried messages between the forge and the shaft-head, joked that he served two masters under one roof and never caught them at the same meal twice running. Iain told her on their first evening as man and wife that he had reckoned, walking east from An Sloc Mòr those eleven years before, that he was walking toward a posting and had not once reckoned he was walking toward a wife, a confession she took, she told him, as the nearest thing to a compliment a surveyor's tongue was ever likely to manage, and answered that she had reckoned, watching him lose an argument over a scale's true beam in front of three of her father's own captains, that she was watching a man honest enough to be worth the losing of an argument to, which she meant, she told him, as the higher compliment of the two.

## IV. Of the Two Towns Called Dùn thais

There were two towns on the island that answered to the one name, Dùn thais, one on the west coast within a morning's ride of Ros bheag and the other far to the east, seat of its own shire and its own dues, and the confusion the two had caused the royal record since the drawing of the shires had never yet troubled anyone enough to be untangled, since neither town's business had often crossed the other's on any page a clerk at Cathair dhearg was likely to read in the same sitting. That changed, in its small way, the same year the eastern hills' first blanks went to the mint, when both towns' names reached the capital in the same season's post.

The western Dùn thais asked, that spring, for a court of the sea to be set over its own harbour, to judge salvage and wreck along a stretch of coast that had gone without any such ruling since An Dealachadh, its fishermen having settled such disputes among themselves by custom until a wrecked hulk driven ashore the autumn before proved too rich a prize for custom alone to divide without hard words on both sides of it. The council granted the court within the season, and its first sitting, held on the strand with the hulk still canted where the tide had left it, awarded the wreck whole to the crews who had driven it aground rather than let it lie for whoever claimed it first, a ruling the fishermen carried home more pleased with the court's plain justice than with the timber it won them.

The eastern Dùn thais, in the same season, opened a covered market for the hill farms that had traded in its square uncovered since the town was first walled, a long timbered hall raised at the shire's own charge to keep the autumn's grain dry through a market day, its first trading held under a roof that smelled, the farmers said, more of new-cut wood than of the produce it sheltered. The clerk at Cathair dhearg who filed the eastern town's petition for its market alongside the western town's petition for its court, both arriving within the same week and both signed only "Dùn thais" without further marking, entered the two matters against the one town's name in the royal record before a second clerk, reading the file a fortnight later, caught that a market and a sea-court could hardly belong to a single harbourless hill town in the same season and set the error right by hand, a correction that held for exactly as long as it took the next season's post to bring the next confusion.

Neither town troubled itself over the mistake once it reached their own ears, each reckoning, in the way of a town used to being the only Dùn thais its own shire had ever needed to distinguish, that the fault lay in the capital's own carelessness and not in any likeness between the two of them, and the royal record went on confusing the pair a while longer yet, court and market alike filed sometimes rightly and sometimes not. A factor from the western town, riding south that same year on business of his own, was twice asked by clerks at Cathair dhearg after the covered market's own takings, a question he could not answer and thought, at first, a poor joke at his expense, until he understood that the clerk before him held no joke at all, only a file that had never once been told there were two towns to keep apart. He carried the tale home to the western harbour more amused by it than aggrieved, and it was told at that town's own hearths for years after, growing a little in the telling each time, that the capital itself could not tell a sea-court from a market roof so long as both bore the one name.

## V. Of the Petition from Baile chiar

Doire chaol, the village laid out south of the capital in the first years after An Dealachadh for the families that served the royal household, had grown in the years since from a scattering of houses built for clerks and stewards into a town proper, its own market crowded of a market day and its own disputes running thicker than the maor of Baile chiar, whose shire the village sat within, had ever troubled to answer for when it was smaller. The maor wrote to the council that same year, plainly and without much ornament, that Doire chaol's business had outgrown a seat five miles distant and asked that the village be given a maor of its own rather than go on being governed, badly and at second hand, from a hall it took the better part of a morning to reach on the village's own worst roads.

The council read the petition alongside a dozen others that season and granted it before the summer was old, the only shire cut new from an older one since the original drawing had first set the island's bounds, Siorrachd Doire chaol entered onto the roll with its own arms, its own dues, and a maor of its own choosing rather than the Baile chiar man's deputy who had kept its peace, badly, these many years. The Baile chiar maor himself, reading the grant, was heard by his own clerk to say only that he was glad of the lightening, having governed a village he could not properly see to for longer than he cared to own, and sent the new shire's first dues south the same season with a note wishing its new maor better luck of it than he had had.

## VI. Of the Shepherd above Doire uaine

The hills above Doire uaine had been grazed thin by shepherds for as long as any flock had run there, save one stretch of high ground the shepherds themselves would not put a beast to, a shoulder of hill where the old writings the town's own keeper still had by heart told of a thing that was dead and did not rest, the Marbh-bheò the writings named it and no shepherd living had ever seen with his own eyes, though every one of them could point to the ground it was said to keep. It was custom rather than any written rule that kept the flocks off that shoulder, the kind of custom a hill country keeps without troubling to ask itself why, and it had held, the keeper reckoned, since before her own grandmother's telling of it.

A shepherd new to that country, a young man down from the north hired that season for want of local hands enough to cover the lambing, put his own flock to the shoulder's grass one evening rather than walk them the longer way round it, the grass there thicker than anywhere else on the hill and the light already going, and did not come down again. His dog came down alone the next morning, and the flock scattered wide over ground it had never before been let to graze, and the town searched the shoulder three days running without finding more of him than a crook left standing where the ground pitched steep, driven into the turf as a man might drive it who meant to climb rather than fall.

The magistrate who wrote the matter into the shire's own book, a careful man who had no more taste than his own clerks for setting an old story down as a court's own finding, entered wolves as the cause, a beast or beasts unaccounted for having come down off the higher ground that season, a finding the town's own shepherds neither argued nor believed, since no wolf had been seen on that hill in living memory and none was seen after, but a finding that let the book close on the matter without asking the court to rule on a thing older than any court the island kept. The keeper who held the old writings by heart said nothing against the magistrate's entry when it was read to her, only that the shoulder would go on being grazed thin and empty whatever the book called the cause of it, and the shepherds, who had never needed the book's word to keep off that ground before the finding and needed it no more after, went on keeping off it exactly as they always had.

The young shepherd's crook was brought down off the hill and given to his own family, who had walked three days from the north to claim what little of him the shoulder had left to claim, and the mother, standing at the magistrate's own door with the crook in her two hands, asked him plainly whether he believed his own entry, wolves being a thing she had shepherded thirty years herself without once losing a beast to. The magistrate, who had asked himself the same question more than once in the writing of it, told her only that the book wanted a cause it could hold, and that a mother burying a son deserved a finding she could carry home and set before a shire court if ever she wanted to, which a hillside's old writing, however faithfully kept, was never going to give her. She took the crook and the finding both, and thanked him for neither more nor less than the truth he had given her, and walked the three days back north with her son's own crook wrapped in her shawl the whole way.

## VII. Of the Copy Iain Kept

Iain wrote the guild's new charter into his own private book the same evening it was signed, in the plain hand he kept for entries he meant to be able to find again without searching, beside the receipt Comhairle nam Mèinnean had given him eleven years before and the deep gallery's own figures, set down fair the week he first proved them. He had kept the habit since his first survey, a second record held against the day Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own copy might be lost, mislaid, or simply not shown to the hand that had earned the showing of it, and he had never yet had cause to open the book for any purpose grander than his own satisfaction that the figures, wherever Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own copies went, still existed somewhere a man could put his hand to them.

Mòr found him at it one evening that autumn, the book open on the table between them while she totted the guild's own weekly weighing at his elbow, and asked him, not for the first time, why a man of Comhairle nam Mèinnean troubled to keep a second copy of business Comhairle nam Mèinnean itself was paid to keep straight. He told her what he had told nobody else in eleven years of the keeping, that the vein at An Sloc Mòr had thinned exactly as his first survey said it would and no living soul at Cathair mhòr had ever once told him so, the figures simply vanishing into Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own files the day he handed them over and never once being spoken of to him again, so that a man might work eleven years for a board that had proved him right and never learn it from any mouth but his own reckoning. The silver at Muileann chiar ran the other way, richer with every fathom where the coal at An Sloc Mòr had run thinner with every one, and Iain told her, closing the book, that he meant to go on keeping his own copy of both truths for as long as Comhairle nam Mèinnean went on keeping its own counsel about either of them, a thing that seemed to him, though he did not put it in those words to her, less like caution now than like the plainest kind of accounting a man could keep against a kingdom that told him only what it chose to.

He put the book away on its shelf, silver charter and coal survey held the same distance from his own reaching hand, and walked out with Mòr to the stream below the town, where the light was going the way it went in that country at that season, thin and long over the hills, and said nothing more of Comhairle nam Mèinnean that evening, the two of them standing a while at the water's edge before the cold turned them back toward the forge's own banked warmth and the guild's ledgers waiting, unfinished, on the table they had left.
