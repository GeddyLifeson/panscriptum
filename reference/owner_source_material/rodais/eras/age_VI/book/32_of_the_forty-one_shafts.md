# Of the Forty-One Shafts

*Part Six: Of the One Sheet and the Empty Floor*

*SE 50 am Màrt – SE 52 an Lùnastal*

> Forty-one shafts sunk between Dùn dhearg and Baile ruadh, and not one struck the vein. It had been tried before, long ages since, on the same ground, and the Library alone remembered.
>
> — the Library's own reckoning of the search, entered at Muileann chaol

## I. Of the Piles and the Six Schools

It is told at Muileann chaol, of the fiftieth year of the Severance Era, that the sea came into Inis chrom on the twenty-third day of am Màrt and did not trouble to knock. A storm tide out of the south-west rode the mouth of the river higher than any tide the town's own sailors could put a season to, and it went through the net-lofts and the rope-walk of the lower town the way a hand goes through a spider's web, leaving the timber standing in places and lying flat in others, and no reason a man could point to for the difference. Above ten families lost the whole of what they kept below the high-water mark, salt fish and cordage and a season's mending all gone together, and the harbour silted so that the deeper boats could not come in for the better part of a month, riding instead at anchor beyond the bar while their crews rowed the catch ashore in whatever smaller boat the flood had left them.

The town did not ask the Crown for its rebuilding. It asked its own carpenters, who had watched other towns on other coasts set their stores on piles against exactly such a tide and had always reckoned the cost of doing likewise not worth the bother of a flood that had never yet come. It came now, and the lofts and the rope-walk went up a second time on driven piles a fathom above the old floor, the timber cut from the woods inland and floated down the river on the spring flood, raft after raft of it, and the work was finished before the autumn herring, so that the only loss the second season carried was the labour. A sailor of the Stone's own order blessed the first pile driven and the last, and said, between the two blessings, that a town built twice on the same ground within one lifetime was a town that had learned what the sea meant to teach it only the once. The lofts stand so now, and the town's own telling of it, kept short and without much wonder in the keeping, is that the sea taught Inis chrom once what it would not trouble to teach twice.

In the east that same year Cnoc leathan opened a school in every one of its six parishes, on the twenty-first day of an Giblean, the first shire on the island to do so entire and out of its own dues rather than the Moot's purse or a chartered guild's charity. The sixth and last of the six, at a parish so small it had never before had cause to keep a room set apart for anything but a market day, opened with eleven children on its benches and a teacher hired from Baile chiar who had never taught so few in one room in her life, and said so to the parish's own maor, who told her that eleven taught well was worth more to the shire than sixty taught badly, and that the shire's own dues would stretch to the teacher's wage whatever the room held. The council at Cathair dhearg, hearing of the six schools together, sent a clerk to ask how a shire so far from the capital's own notice had found the coin, and was shown the accounts, plain columns in a plain hand, the dues that would otherwise have gone toward a road never built or a bridge never wanted, turned instead toward six rooms and six teachers and six sets of slates bought at Baile chiar. The clerk copied the accounts and carried them south, and nothing more was asked of Cnoc leathan, which went on keeping its six schools without further notice from the capital in either direction, the parish's eleven benches filling to twenty within two years and the teacher from Baile chiar staying on past the term she had first been hired for.

These are told first because they belong to the same year and to no great matter of state, and because the Library holds that a year should be known by what stood at its edges as well as by what stood at its centre. The centre of that year, for the mountain country and for the house whose name the mountain's yield had made, was a debt taken out against a hope, and it began in the month after Cnoc leathan opened its sixth school, when a grandson of the first holder of the Sloc Mòr rode to Cathair mhòr to ask the Mine Board for surveyors.

## II. Of the Borrowing

Alasdair mac Ùisdein was thirty-four that spring and had held his house's share in the Sloc Mòr for eleven years, since his father's death, without once having to ask the Board for anything beyond the ordinary courtesies a concession-holder was owed: a seat kept at the Board's own table, a first look at the yield figures before they went to the fair, a nod from whichever clerk was on duty when his own name was read from the roll. He came into the Board's chambers on the twentieth of an Cèitean with a paper already drawn, and laid it on the clerk's table before he had properly sat, which the clerk who received him thought, afterward, told him more about the man's temper that morning than anything Alasdair said aloud.

"My house holds the richest working the island has," he said, "and my house's own surveys, going back three generations, say plainly that the working thins to the east and to the south both. I am not the first Mac Ùisdein to read those figures. My grandfather read them and said nothing. My father read them and said nothing. I mean to be the first to act on them beyond reading them again."

The clerk asked him, since it was the clerk's own place to ask it, what he proposed the Board do that three generations of his own house had not already tried, and Alasdair told him that no Mac Ùisdein had ever gone looking for a second vein, having always had coal enough from the one they held not to trouble themselves over whether the island kept another, and that a house rich enough to ignore the question for three generations was, in his own reckoning, precisely the house that ought now to be asking it, before the first vein ran too thin to pay for the asking.

The act he meant was a search for a second vein, and the paper he laid on the table was a note of hand against his house's own share, coin enough to keep six surveying parties in the field a full season, more if the season wanted it. The Board did not need to be asked twice to lend its own surveyors alongside the hired ones, the search being, as the Board's own minute of that week put it without much modesty, "a matter touching the whole kingdom's coal and not the house of Mac Ùisdein's alone," and the Crown lent its name to the asking, which cost it nothing and bound it to nothing either, a fact the Board's clerks understood without needing it written down. A search that found a second vein would be the kingdom's triumph before it was Alasdair's; a search that found nothing would be a debt on one house's own books and no other's.

Catrìona nic Neacail was thirty-two that year, a surveyor of eight years' standing who had come up, as most of the Board's surveyors came up, through the vein-towns rather than through any school that troubled to teach the trade from a desk, chain-hand first at Muileann chiar and then at the Sloc Mòr itself before the Board took her on its own roll, and she was named to the search in the same week the note of hand was signed, one of eleven the Board sent east with their chains and their levels and their own field-books already half filled with other men's work on other ground. She had not asked to be sent. She had, she told the surveyor riding beside her on the road out of Cathair mhòr, a man twenty years her senior who had surveyed half the roads the Board now kept its own maps by, no particular hope of the search finding anything the house's own three generations of figures had not already told them, and she said this plainly, the kind of fact a careful woman kept in front of her so as not to be surprised by it later.

"Then why go?" the man asked her, not unkindly, the two of them riding the east road past the last of the coal country's own smoke before the ground opened out into hill and moor neither had surveyed before.

"Because the Board pays me to go where it sends me," she said, "and because a search that finds nothing is still a search that someone has to walk the ground for, chain in hand, before the kingdom can be told plainly that nothing is there. I would rather be the one who walks it than the one who is told, years on, that nobody ever properly looked."

He said nothing further to that for the better part of a mile, and then told her, as the hills above Dùn dhearg came into sight for the first time, that he had surveyed for the Board twenty years and had learned in that time to tell, within a week of any new ground, whether it would answer or not, and that this ground, by every sign a man's eye could put to it before a chain was ever laid across it, gave him no particular feeling either way, which was, he said, the worst kind of ground a surveyor could be sent to, since hope at least gave a man a thing to be disappointed by.

## III. Of the Forty-One

The ground the search was set to was a stretch of hill and moor running from the rise above Dùn dhearg north-east to the red-earth fields about Baile ruadh, some thirty miles by the road and rather less as a bird would fly it, ground that had never held a working shaft in living memory and that the house's old surveys marked only with the word *unproved*, which in a surveyor's hand meant no more and no less than that nobody before had troubled to find out. The first shaft went down above Dùn dhearg in the early summer, cut through stone that gave every sign a hopeful man could ask of it, layered and dark and not unlike the stone that ringed the Sloc Mòr's own outer galleries, and for the better part of a fortnight the diggers worked it with the particular good humour of men who believe the next stroke of the pick may be the one that changes everything, singing at the windlass in the evenings and wagering small coin among themselves on the very day the vein would show.

It was not. Nor was the second, sunk three miles north-east of the first on a rise Catrìona herself had chosen for the lie of its strata, walking the ground twice with a level before she would mark it, nor the third, nor any of the eleven that went down that first season between the hill above Dùn dhearg and the moor's edge north of Baile ruadh. Each shaft was logged in the field-books the Board's clerks kept, depth and stone and water and the day it was judged dry, and each log ended the same way, in Catrìona's own hand more often than any other surveyor's on the search, with the single word the Board's own form asked for and no surveyor on the ground had yet found a kinder way to write: *barren*.

The camp the search kept that first summer, a scatter of tents and one timber hut on the moor's own edge, held eleven surveyors and near sixty diggers hired from the vein-towns and the shires round about, men glad enough of the season's wage whatever the ground gave up, and a cook who fed the whole of it from two great pots and a fire kept burning from first light past dark. Catrìona took her own meals with the diggers oftener than with the other surveyors, having learned young that a chainman's grumbling told a surveyor more about the ground than another surveyor's own reckoning ever did, and it was from a digger of Dùn dhearg, an old man who had cut peat on those same hills as a boy, that she first heard the moor's own name for the ground they were searching, *am Fearann Falamh*, the empty ground, a name the moor's own shepherds had used long before any Board surveyor came near it and had meant, the old man told her, nothing more mysterious than that no shepherd had ever found a spring on it worth the digging.

The second season brought thirty more shafts, spread wider now across the same stretch of country as the house's coin and the diggers' patience allowed, some sunk within sight of the first season's covered mouths and others carried further out onto ground the first year's surveyors had judged too poor to trouble with, and Catrìona kept, apart from the Board's own field-books, a private tally in the back pages of her own, a line for each shaft with its number and its depth and the date it was abandoned, so that by the close of that second summer she could tell a man asking, without troubling to add the column, that the search stood at thirty-eight shafts sunk and thirty-eight shafts dry. The diggers' songs had thinned by then to nothing much, and the wagers had stopped after the twentieth shaft or thereabouts, no man on the ground caring any longer to put coin against a stone that had shown them, thirty times running, exactly what it meant to show.

The thirty-ninth found nothing. The fortieth, sunk on ground so unpromising that even the diggers who cut it said afterward they had expected no better, found nothing either, the water coming in at a depth that told Catrìona, reading the borings by lamplight in the timber hut that evening, that the ground below it held no more promise than the ground above, and by the time the forty-first was marked out on the moor south of Baile ruadh, in the last days of the second summer, there was a temper on the ground that Catrìona had not felt on the earlier shafts, the particular tiredness of men who have done careful work for two years running and have nothing to show a passing stranger for it but a row of covered holes and a moor's own name proving truer with every one of them.

The forty-first shaft went down through an t-Sultain and into an Dàmhair, the diggers working it in weather no earlier shaft had asked them to work through, the first frosts in the ground by morning and the windlass rope stiff with them, and it was Catrìona who stood at its head on the morning the chief digger came up to tell her, not with any particular ceremony, his hands still black to the wrist from the last boring, that the water table had been struck at a depth where no vein had ever been found on the whole island and that the ground below it, so far as the borings could tell, held nothing but the same barren rock the other forty had held. She wrote the word in her own field-book, *barren*, in the same hand she had written it forty times before, and then sat a while on the shaft's own spoil-heap in the cold before she rode back to report it, a thing she judged, that particular morning, the ground had earned of her before she left it for good, whatever the Board's own form asked.

## IV. Of the Same Week

It happened, as such things happen more often than either chroniclers or surveyors care to admit, that the very week Catrìona rode into Cathair mhòr with the word of the forty-first shaft, Mòrag nic Coinnich at Muileann chaol was working her way through a press of Holy Age rolls that had lain unread in the Library's lower room since before her own keepership began, part of the long cataloguing she had set herself the year she took the keepership, tallying each roll against a list that had never once been made and setting aside, as she went, whatever the list did not already answer for. She came upon, near the bottom of a press she had nearly finished with that week, a list from the Holy Age, the ink browned and the vellum stiffened with age, that named, in terms she had to read twice by the light of her own lamp before she trusted them, the same hills above Dùn dhearg and the same moors south of Baile ruadh, searched once already, and found barren once already, by surveyors whose own names the roll no longer troubled to keep, the last line of it worn to little more than a shape where a hand had once written a plain word she could still, holding the vellum close, make out as the same word Catrìona had been writing all that season: barren.

She sent word south the day she found it, not to the Board directly, which was not the Library's habit, the Library keeping no correspondence with the Board as a body and preferring, where a fact touched one person's own work, to send it to that person plainly, but to Catrìona herself, whom she had never met but whose name stood at the head of more of the search's own logs than any other surveyor's, a short letter that said only what the roll said and asked nothing beyond that it be read. Catrìona received it the same week she filed her own report of the forty-first shaft, the two papers arriving on her table at Cathair mhòr within two days of each other, one saying that the ground before her had just failed to yield what two years and forty-one shafts had asked of it, the other saying that the same ground had failed the same way once already, ages before either of them, and that somewhere in the Library's own keeping the failure had been sitting the whole time, waiting for someone to think to ask.

She read Mòrag's letter twice before she set it beside her own field-book, and told the clerk who had brought it in that she did not know, that morning, whether the Library's find made the two years' digging easier to bear or harder, and the clerk, who had carried enough of the Board's own bad news in his time to have an opinion on the matter, said that a thing known twice over and forgotten once already was, in his own reckoning, harder to bear than a thing simply not known, since the not-knowing at least left a man free to hope the next shaft might be the one. Catrìona did not answer him directly. She copied Mòrag's letter into her own field-book, word for word, beside the entry for the forty-first shaft, and let the two stand together on the page, which was, she told the clerk afterward, the nearest thing to an answer she had, and wrote beneath the two a line of her own, that she meant, before the Board's own record of the search was closed and filed past reading, to see the Holy Age roll sent a copy south so that the next surveyor sent to that ground, if any Board ever sent one again, would not have to wait on a keeper's own private cataloguing to learn what the last search had already cost.

It was in that same season, before the search's papers were fully closed, that Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain's letter to the Board, written before the digging had properly begun and filed unread at the time in the ordinary way of letters the Board judged it had no immediate use for, was found again by a clerk sorting the Board's own correspondence for the year, and read this time with more attention than it had been given the first. She had written, when the search was still no more than Alasdair's note of hand and the Board's own minute, that the search asked the wrong question, that her school had always asked why the vein answered some hands and not others, and that in all the kingdom's asking about the mountain, in all the ages of digging it and forty years of the Board's own surveys, nobody had thought to ask why there was only the one vein at all, on the whole of an island otherwise so rich in stone and metal both. The clerk who found the letter a second time carried it to Catrìona along with Mòrag's, and Catrìona read it standing at the same table, and said to the clerk, when he asked her whether it changed anything, that it changed nothing the search could act on, a school's question and a surveyor's chain answering to different masters, but that she meant, all the same, to keep a copy of it beside the other two, so that whoever next read her field-book entire would have all three papers together and could judge for themselves what the season had actually found, and what it had failed, three separate ways, to ask.

## V. Of the Fence

The reckoning that followed the search fell to others than Catrìona, who had done the digging and left the accounting to the Board's own clerks, though it touched the ground she had walked all the same. The town of Dùn dhearg, whose hills had held a dozen of the dry shafts among the forty-one, brought its claim to the circuit court that summer, the shire's own advocate walking the shafts himself with two shepherds who swore, plainly and without much heat, that grazing spoiled by a digger's spoil-heap was grazing lost for longer than any digger troubled to reckon, one of them telling the court that a ewe would not graze within a stone's throw of a fresh spoil-heap for two full seasons after the diggers moved on, whatever grass grew there, and the other adding only that he had lost more lambs to the search's own open ground in two years than to wolves in the ten years before it. The court found for the town, and the house of Mac Ùisdein paid a third of the damages that same season and, its coin by then thinner than its note of hand two years before had allowed for, sold half its own share in the Sloc Mòr back to the Crown to meet what remained, on the fifth day of an t-Iuchar in the fifty-second year, a clerk of the Board carrying the deed south to Cathair dhearg the same week it was signed. Its stone house at Cathair mhòr, kept in the family since the first holder's own day, its hall still hung with the same crest the first Mac Ùisdein had cut for it, was let to the Board that same season, which moved its offices into it the following spring, so that a house built to hold one family's wealth came to hold, instead, the ledgers of a search that family's own coin had paid for and not answered, and a Board clerk was heard to remark, carrying the first press of files up the house's own stair, that the family had at least left the fires drawing well.

The shafts themselves, forty-one dry mouths in the ground between two towns that had asked for none of them, were left as the diggers had left them when each was judged barren in turn, covered loosely where the diggers troubled to cover them and not at all where the season's weather or the Board's own thinning purse left the work half done. On the moor south of Baile ruadh, where the ground was softest and the covering poorest, sheep began falling into the old shaft-mouths before the first winter after the search was out, three that the shire's own reckoning could put a number to and likely more that no reckoning troubled to count, and before that summer was out a child of the town, out after a stray lamb on ground she had walked a hundred times without harm, went down one of the same shafts and was not found again until the following day, past any help the finding could give her. The parish's own book at Baile ruadh holds her name and the day, and nothing more than that; her mother, it is told locally and nowhere written, would not walk that road again for the whole of the year that followed, and took the longer way round by the mill instead, whatever the weather asked of her for it.

The shire of Baile ruadh met the week after, in the same low room where its own dues had once been argued over school-money the shire had never had cause to spend, and voted, without much debate that the record troubled to keep, to fence every one of the shafts on its own ground at the shire's own cost rather than wait on a Crown that had shown, in the matter of Dùn dhearg's grazing, no great haste to pay for what its own search had spoiled. The shire's claim against the house of Mac Ùisdein for the fencing's own cost was entered the same week, and has never yet been paid. The fencing went up through that same summer, plain posts and wire strung by the town's own hands round each covered mouth, one shaft a day where the ground allowed it and two where a family gave its own labour free for a shaft that had taken a measure of their own, a fence for every hole on the moor, holes that had cost two years, a house's half-share, and one child, and had found, at the end of it, nothing but the stone the Holy Age's own forgotten surveyors could have told them, had anyone thought, before the digging began, to ask the Library what it already knew.

Catrìona nic Neacail rode past the last of the fences on her way south that autumn, on Board business that had nothing further to do with the search, and stopped a while at the rail nearest the road, where a boy of the town, too young to remember the digging, was mending a gap the last winter's frost had opened in the wire. She did not tell him what the shaft behind him had cost, in coin or in the other coin, and he did not ask her, taking her, from her dress and her horse, for no more than a Board rider passing through on ordinary business, which by then, in every particular the boy could see, she was. She rode on before the light went, the fences falling behind her one by one in the grey of the evening, each one holding nothing but the dark the search had proved, and thought, coming down at last onto the coal road with Cathair mhòr's own smoke ahead of her, that a kingdom which kept a Library at all and still had to be told twice, by its own ground, what that Library already held, was a kingdom that had not yet learned to ask its questions of the right book first.
