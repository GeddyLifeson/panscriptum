# Of the Empty Floor and the Banked Fire

*SE 68 an t-Ògmhios – 18 an t-Ògmhios SE 71*

> A floor raised and left bare speaks only years afterward, once the ledgers show plainly what was left unsent.
>
> — Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, foot-note to the chronicle, entered at Muileann chaol, {{date:V-0228}}

## I. Of the Fourth Floor

The new wing at Muileann chaol was raised of the grey stone that came up out of the Sloc Mòr's own spoil, cut and dressed by masons who had learned the trade cutting galleries rather than walls and cut it, by old habit, in blocks the size a gallery's own timbering would have wanted. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, three years into the chronicler's chair by the summer it was finished, walked its three built floors before a single chest had been carried up to them and thought that a library housed in a mountain's own leavings kept, in its stone if nowhere else, an honest account of where the kingdom's wealth had gone. The first floor took the provinces' returns, shire by shire, in presses built to the same measure so that no shire's chest stood taller or grander than its neighbour's. The second took the town chronicles, where any town had troubled to keep one, and the third the vigil-books and rite-rolls the shires' own faiths had filed apart from the Crown's business. The fourth floor, built to the same measure as the three beneath it, stood empty, its presses raised and its shelves cut and planed and never loaded, and Mòrag nic Coinnich, walking it with her that first morning, told Beathag only that a floor was easier built now than added later, and that the Library had learned, over its long keeping, to build for what it did not yet hold.

The clerks who carried the chests up from the carts that autumn worked the first three floors in the order the wing had been given them, a chain of hands up three flights of a new stair that still smelled of its own mortar, and more than one of them, setting a chest down at last in its press, remarked to the clerk behind him that the fourth flight climbed to nothing and asked, half in jest, whether they were to carry chairs up there instead of chests to save the stair the indignity of standing idle. Beathag heard the jest twice that autumn from two different mouths and wrote neither of them into the chronicle, judging a chronicle no place for a joke that would want explaining to a reader fifty years on, though she kept the sense of it, she owned in her own private book, longer than she kept most things said to her that season.

Beathag asked Mòrag plainly, since the keeper had not offered it, what the fourth floor was built to hold, and Mòrag answered that it was built for the north's own record, should the north ever choose to send it, and that she had asked the masons to raise it all the same, whatever the Moot's own answer proved to be. The two women stood some while at the head of the empty stair, the fourth floor's own door unlocked and its room bare of everything but light coming in low through windows cut the same size as the floors below, and Beathag wrote that evening, in the private note she had kept since her first year at the desk, that a room built and left empty said more of the kingdom's own habits than any room the kingdom had troubled to fill. She had kept, since Tormod Pinn's death, his own front-of-the-year list of what the Library had never been sent, unchanged; she added now, at the year's close, her own foot-note beneath it, a habit no chronicler before her had kept, and she wrote of the fourth floor only that it stood ready and that no cart had come, a sentence she judged plainer than any she could have set beside it.

The calling-in of the shires' chests that had filled the wing's first three floors was two years finished by the time the wing stood ready to take them, and Beathag, reading the whole tally that summer for the first time set out floor by floor, found in it a fact the calling-in's own notice had never troubled to state outright: every shire the council's own roll accounted for had sent something, willing or unwilling, whole or in part, and the one record asked for and not received was the one record never asked for at all. She set that down in the same plain hand, and kept the note in the book the Library's own younger clerks were beginning, by then, to read before they read the chronicle proper, a small book that had, by the wing's second year, outgrown the drawer she had first kept it in.

## II. Of the Arrears at Àth leathan

The Rail Board's own minute that summer gave one line to the branch line at Àth leathan, closed for the falling yield of the western pits, and Beathag, copying the minute into the chronicle as the Board had written it, found nothing in the line to trouble a reader who did not already know the western hills. The pits did not close with the branch. The Board's overseers were withdrawn, their wages costing more by then than the coal they oversaw was fairly worth to a house working the vein in the Crown's name alone, and the Tuathaich hewers who had worked those pits under the overseers' watch for two generations went on working them under pit captains of their own choosing, men chosen from among their own number for the trust of the rest, in a manner the Board's own charter had never troubled to forbid because it had never once thought a Tuathaich pit might be left to govern its own affairs.

The last overseer to leave Àth leathan, a man named in no book Beathag ever read but remembered at the pit-head for years after by the hewers he had watched, is said to have handed the pit-head scale's own key to the hewer standing nearest him without a word of ceremony either way, and to have ridden south the same afternoon without looking back at the headgear behind him. The captains the hewers chose kept the Crown's fifth by the Leòid ruling, as the ruling required of them, weighing every cart at the pit-head and setting the fifth part by before a cart went anywhere, and sent word south each season of what was owed, in a hand as careful as any overseer's had ever been. The Board took the payments when a carrier troubled to bring them and entered them in its own books as arrears, a word that named a debt owed rather than a due paid, though the hewers at Àth leathan had never once failed to send what the ruling asked of them. Beathag, reading the Board's own ledger a season later at a clerk's asking, wrote in her private note that a kingdom which could not tell the difference between a debt and a payment made in good faith had chosen, in the choosing of that one word, to keep a grievance where none was owed, and that she did not know whether the choosing had been made in malice or only in the ordinary carelessness of a Board that had stopped looking at what it did not have to look at.

In the second summer after the branch closed, the pit captains at Àth leathan sent the season's first coal south by cart rather than by rail, the road being longer and the going slower but the loading and the sending both theirs to order, down through the western hills to the yards at Seann Bhral, where the coal factors bought it at the pit-head price and asked no questions of the hand that had cut it. It was the first coal out of Àth leathan to travel a road the Board had not chosen for it, four carts and a spare axle carried against the road's own roughness, and the carters who drove it said afterward, at Cnoc thais and at the pits above it, that the going had cost them three days more than the rail would have and had cost the Board nothing at all, the Board having washed its hands of the branch and of everything the branch no longer carried. The factors at Seann Bhral, the carters said too, weighed the coal twice over before they paid for it, as though a cart come down from a pit with no overseer's mark on its papers were a cart to be doubted until the scale itself had spoken; but they paid, each time, the full pit-head price, and asked, in all the seasons of the carts coming, no question beyond the weight.

A younger clerk at the Library, reading the Board's own minute beside Beathag that same season, asked her whether the word ought to be struck from the ledger and a truer one put in its place, since the chronicle, of all the kingdom's books, might have the standing to ask for it. Beathag told him no, that a chronicle's business was to copy the word the Board had chosen and let the choosing stand against the Board's own name, not to soften it for a reader who ought to be trusted with the plain fact of it; she had learned the habit, she told him, from a chronicler who struck through his own bad guesses rather than hide them, and a chronicler who mended another office's bad words was practising a different trade entirely. The clerk did not raise the matter again, though he copied, that same week, the word arrears into three further ledgers of his own without once, by his own later account, writing it easily.

## III. Of the Register at Cnoc thais

At Cnoc thais, in the same western hills, the pits passed to their own hewers in the same season and by the same withdrawal, and the man chosen to keep the accounts was Sam Varley, four-and-fifty that year, who had worked the pit-head scales under the Board's own overseer for eleven years before the overseer left and had kept, in that time, a private tally of his own beside the Board's, out of a habit he could not afterward explain except to say that a scale watched by one pair of eyes alone was a scale worth checking against a second pair. He opened, that first season, a book of the pit's own accounts, the earliest page of it headed in his own plain hand with the day and the pit's own name and nothing else, a book the town's own register still keeps and still calls by no finer title than the day it was begun.

His small son Tom, not yet old enough for the pit, ran the errands the book wanted: a figure carried to a captain waiting at the pit-head, a weight called down from the scale-house to the table where his father sat with the ledger open, a message sent to the next valley when the carts were due and had not come. Sam kept him at it through that first summer and every summer after, and once, when the boy asked why the figures mattered so much to a book nobody south would read, told him plainly, without looking up from the page, that a book kept honest for nobody was worth more than a book kept honest for someone watching, since the one habit would hold when the other failed. Tom did not, by his own account, take the full sense of the saying that summer, standing in the scale-house door with the cold coming in behind him and his father's pen not once pausing over the page; but he carried the figures all the same, and carried them straight, and Sam never once had to send a second boy after the first.

That same season, on the southern road a country away, the far-off house that the carters called An Sligeanach Fad' às passed from the family that had kept it three generations to a family of drovers new to the trade. The old keepers left the way the tellers of that road say a house is properly left, the last of them sweeping the hearth clean before he handed the new family the key, and the drovers built a second hall beside the first for a trade the old house had never troubled to serve; a hall built, the carters who watched it rise said, faster and cruder than the first, of green timber that would want a year's settling before a door in it hung true. Within a few years the house that had once fed a carter dried deer and black water and asked no questions had become the largest house on the old mining roads, its yard crowded of an evening with beasts and men from every valley the roads still served, its new hall louder than the old one had ever been in three generations of quiet keeping. Beathag noted the sale in the chronicle in a single line, the house's changing hands being, by the Library's own measure, a small thing beside a branch line closed or a pit passed to its own hewers, though she wrote, in her private note that year, that a house grown fat on the drove trade while the coal roads emptied told its own kind of truth about which trade a failing kingdom still had use for.

The book's first year closed, as every year after it would close, on the last evening of an Dùbhlachd, when Sam sat up late by the scale-house lamp to draw the final line beneath the season's figures and set the total against what the captain's own count made of it, the two sums agreeing, that first year, to within a single basket-weight neither man troubled to chase down further. Tom, sent to bed an hour before, came down again in his nightshirt to see the line drawn, having asked, the week before, whether a book's first year ought not to be watched closed the way a house watches its own hearth banked for the night, and Sam let him stand by while the ink dried, saying nothing of the asking either way, though he did not send the boy up again until the page was blotted and shut.

## IV. Of the Failing Grounds

The herring did not come, that year or the year following, to the grounds off the mouth of the river below Seann Dunn, and the east-coast boats, finding their own waters empty a second season together, went north to fish the grounds off Caol mhòr instead, grounds the licence law had kept for Dia-thìreach boats since the licences were first written and that few east-coast boats had troubled to fish while their own waters answered. The law gave them the right, and they used it, in numbers the law had never anticipated, and the Tuathaich boats that worked the edges of those grounds were pushed farther out by hulls they did not know and could not, by any law written, turn away.

The boats of Inis mhin, pushed from the grounds nearest their own harbour by the crowding, took to the deep water beyond the northern isles, a water no boat of that coast had fished within living memory for the roughness of it and the distance a crew had to keep from land overnight, days on end with no headland in sight and the swell running longer than any inshore man cared to trust. Two boats were lost there in the first year, gone out and not come back, and the harbour kept, after that, a stricter watch on the weather than it had ever kept before, a horn sounded from the point whenever the glass fell fast; but none was lost in any year following, the crews having learned, in the losing of the two, what the deep water asked of a boat that meant to fish it and return. Before the second summer was out the catch that Inis mhin brought in from that water had passed what Caol mhòr brought in from all the grounds about it, a fact the chronicle sets down without comment, since a comment would only have said what the figures said more plainly: that a people pushed from what the law protected had found, in what the law did not trouble to reach, a living the law had never thought to grant them.

## V. Of the Keeper Laid Down

Mòrag nic Coinnich laid down the keepership of the Library in the autumn of the seventieth year, two-and-seventy that season and thirty years and more in the chair, the longest any keeper had yet held it, longer than Ailean Leabhar's own founding tenure, and she named, before the council that confirmed such things, her own deputy Raghnall mac Mhuirich to come after her, a man fifty that year who had kept the Library's own catalogue in order through every calling-in and every new wing raised over it without once, by Beathag's own account, losing a single shelf's place in the reckoning. Mòrag's own last act at the desk was to walk the fourth floor once more with Beathag beside her, the floor no less empty than it had stood the day the wing was finished, their two lamps the only light in a room built for four hundred chests that had never held one. "I built the room," she said, when Beathag asked her, at the head of the stair, whether the emptiness troubled her after thirty years of watching it. "The asking who fills it belongs to Raghnall now, or to nobody, and a room built stands built either way." She left the key to the fourth floor's own door with Raghnall the same week, unremarked among the other keys, a room being, in her own long judgment, worth more built and empty than never built at all.

Raghnall took the chair that same season and changed, in his first year, nothing of the Library's own hours nor its own custom, a steadiness the clerks under him took, at first, for a want of ambition and came later to value as the surest thing the house had left it, in a season when little else about the kingdom's own coal held steady. He asked Beathag, in that first year, whether her own foot-notes ought to be gathered separately from the chronicle proper, a question she answered by telling him that a note kept apart from what it corrected was a note easily lost, and that she meant to keep hers bound into the very pages a reader could not skip past. He did not ask her again, and the foot-notes stayed where she had put them.

That same autumn the price of coal-fuel at the fair of Muileann òg doubled over what it had stood the season before, the Board having cut its own sales to spare the lowest galleries the pumping its dwindling profit could no longer answer for, and the fair's own court, whose long custom it was to fix a ceiling on any good the crowd grew angry enough about, set a ceiling price on coal-fuel that same week, cried three times from the fair's own steps as the custom asked. It found, within a season, that no factor troubled to sell at the price named, the coal simply not reaching the fair's stalls at all rather than reaching them cheap, so that a buyer who came asking for coal-fuel at the court's own ceiling was told, at stall after stall, that none was to be had that day, a true answer and a hollow one both. The ceiling stood written in the court's own book and broken in every stall beneath it, and the wardens who had cried it from the steps did not trouble to cry its breaking, a silence Beathag noted in the chronicle as plainly as she had noted the ceiling.

## VI. Of the Watchmen and the Banked Fire

At Cnoc fhiadhaich, where the spoil-heaps of the old coal galleries still stood unlevelled above the town, the Board posted watchmen the following spring against the households that had taken, in the doubled price of the fair, to picking coal off the heaps to burn in their own grates rather than buy what they could no longer pay for. The watchmen the Board hired were men of the town, chosen for their knowledge of the ground and paid a penny a day above a labourer's wage for the watching, and set two to a shift, one at the north heap and one at the south, with a lantern between them against the winter dark. The chronicle records, in a line Beathag set down without any gloss beside it, that the watchmen saw nothing, every night the posting lasted, though the heaps grew visibly lower by the season and the town's poorer grates burned warmer than the price of the fair could otherwise have bought them. A clerk who rode out from Muileann chaol to note the posting for the Library's own record wrote home to Beathag that he had watched the north heap an hour himself one evening and had seen, in that hour, three separate baskets carried down from it by three separate doors, none of them the watchman's own; she kept his letter but did not print it, judging a clerk's single hour no fair measure of a whole season's watching, whatever it seemed to prove.

In the capital that same season, and worse as the summer came on, the streets of the poorer quarters burned their lamps shorter hours than the council's own lighting order required, the keepers of those streets having found, by the doubled price, that an order written for a coal that cost half what it now cost was an order the poorer wards could no longer afford to keep in full. A lamp lit at dusk and let go out before the middle watch left a street dark for the hours a labourer walked it home from a late shift, and more than one such labourer, Beathag's own clerks reported to her, had taken to carrying a rushlight of his own rather than trust the council's order to see him the whole way. The council's own notice on the matter, when one came at all, named the shortening a saving rather than a want, a word Beathag copied into the chronicle exactly as the notice gave it and set beside it, in her own private note that year, that a saving and a want looked much alike from a street too dark to tell the one from the other.

Her foot-note for that year, entered the following spring at the close of the chronicle's own account of it, named what the record could not learn: how many grates in the capital's poorer wards had gone unlit that winter for the price of what should have warmed them, a figure no census had troubled to ask for and no shire had troubled to keep, so that the Library, which now held in one place the fullest count the kingdom had ever kept of its own coal, its own dead, and its own dues, held no count at all of its own cold. She wrote the note in the same hand she had written every note before it, and changed, in the writing of it, nothing of the desk's own hours nor its own custom, as she had told Raghnall, on his first morning at the chair beside her, that she meant to do.

She climbed the fourth stair once more before she sealed the year's book, alone this time, Mòrag being by then a season retired from the desk, and stood again in the room the masons had built and the masons alone had ever filled, the light coming in as low and as even as it had the first morning she had seen it. A shire's chest could be called in by a letter and a cart, she wrote that evening, and a fair's own price could be set down in a court's own book whether the coal answered to it or not; but a floor built for a book that was never sent stayed exactly what it had been built as, a question asked once and left standing, and she judged, closing the year's account, that the Library had grown practised, in seventy years of the kingdom's keeping, at building rooms for what it did not yet have the courage to ask for outright.

In the north Dubhan was at his work still, as he had been for ten years, boiling and burning what the pits threw away. Nobody in the capital was watching him, and nobody in the Library knew his name. On a night near midsummer in the seventy-first year, the tellers of the north say, he banked his fire and slept beside it, as he had on a hundred nights before.

Here this book ends, the fire banked in the north and the kingdom asleep around it, the Library's fourth floor standing empty still, the ceiling broken in every stall that sold beneath it, and no word set down of what the morning brought.
