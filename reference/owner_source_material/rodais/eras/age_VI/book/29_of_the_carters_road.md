# Of the Carters' Road

*SE 41 am Faoilleach – SE 42 an t-Iuchar*

> Entered this day, on the word of the widow of Achadh dhomhain, one name: her husband, lost under the western pits in the spring gone by. No other book will hold it. This one will.
>
> — the first entry of Clàr nan Leòn, {{date:V-0150}}, as the Moot's clerks kept it

## I. Of the Road the North Named

The Rail Board's refusal stood through the whole of that winter, and the wool of the north went south the only way left to it, by cart, five days on the road from Muileann leathan to the fair at Muileann òg, and Daniel Frayne rode with the second train of carts that left in am Faoilleach, having asked leave to go himself, the Moot having sent no one before him and nobody troubling that winter to say him no. He was thirty-five that winter, a clerk of the Moot since before he could properly be called a man, and he had spent most of those years at a table at Caol mhòr with other men's words in front of him and had not, until that winter, ridden further from the northern shires than a day's walking would carry him.

The road the carts took was no road the Board's own maps troubled to draw, being nothing more than the old way the Tuathaich had always known between one northern town and the next, worn by feet and cart-wheels rather than laid by any surveyor's chain: out from Muileann leathan by Cnoc ghorm, where the church built for the Ros bheag bell stood with its door open to the carters of a market morning, and on by Àth fhada, where the ford that gave the town its name ran shallow enough in that season for the carts to cross without unloading, and down at last by the old way to Inis àrsaidh, where the road proper began and the wool went on by a track the fair's own toll-keepers knew and taxed. Frayne rode the whole of it on a borrowed horse he handled worse than he handled a pen, and said as much to the carter beside him on the second day, who laughed and told him a Moot clerk's hands were worth more to the north whole than broken on a green horse's account, and to hold the rein looser.

It was at Àth fhada that the first house opened its door to the carters as an inn proper rather than as a household doing a neighbour's kindness, the family that kept it having fed carters at their own table for two winters running before one of them, a woman near sixty who had buried a husband under the western pits and raised four children on what the mine paid a widow, reckoned that a house that fed twenty men a night on its own charity might as well be paid for the feeding. She hung no sign, having none to hang and no coin to cut one, but she kept a lamp lit in her window past the hour any other house in the town kept one lit, and the carters came to know the lamp before they knew her name, and called the house by it. Within the season two more households on the road had done the same, at Cnoc ghorm and at a crossing short of Inis àrsaidh, each lit lamp answering the one before it, so that a carter riding that road by night in the second winter of the wool's going by cart could find his bed by the lights alone, without once asking a stranger the way.

Frayne asked the widow at Àth fhada, on the second night he lodged under her lamp, what the carters called the road they rode, having heard no one name it before that journey and having wondered, riding it, whether a way so plainly worn by use had ever been given a name at all. She told him plainly that the north had always called it the Carters' Road, and had called it so since before the wool went by cart at all, back to the years when the same track carried salt and grain between the northern towns before any fair at Muileann òg had reason to want either, and that the name had simply grown to fit the newer use as a coat is let out to fit a growing child, without anyone troubling to cut a new one. He asked her, being a clerk and unable to leave a fact alone once he had it, what the south called the same road, and she told him, without much interest in the answer, that she did not know the south troubled to call it anything, the road not existing on any map south of Cnoc ghorm that she had ever heard of a southern man consulting. Frayne wrote the two facts down in the small book he carried for the Moot's business, the north's name and the south's silence side by side, and thought, riding on the third morning with the carts strung out ahead of him on a road no southern chart would own, that a thing could be entirely real to one half of an island and entirely absent from the other half's account of the same island, and that this seemed to him, turning the thought over as the horse found its own footing better than his hands found the rein, the truest thing the journey had shown him.

The weather held fair the first three days and broke on the fourth, a cold rain out of the west that found every gap in a carter's coat and turned the road's worn ruts to standing water by the time the train came down off the higher ground short of Inis àrsaidh, and Frayne, who had never in his years at a Moot table learned what it was to sit a horse in rain that did not let up, understood before the day was out why the carters rode in wide-brimmed hats no Caol mhòr clerk troubled to own. The lead carter, an old man who had driven the same road for the wool before Frayne was born and for salt and grain before that, told him, when Frayne asked how the carts would cross the low ground with the water standing as it was, that a road worn as long as this one had been worn found its own way round a flood the way a stream finds its own way round a stone, and that the carts would sit a night at the crossing short of Inis àrsaidh and go on when the water dropped, which they did, losing half a day to the weather and none of the wool to it.

It was at that same crossing, waiting out the rain under the third of the road's new lamps, that Frayne fell into talk with a family of the shunting households travelling the other way, north from the fair with a season's wages and a cart of goods a southern factor had let them buy against the wool not yet sold, and heard from them, for the first time in his own hearing rather than at second hand in a Moot report, how a family that had never owned land of its own reckoned the road's five days against a season's whole earning. The husband told him plainly that the road cost the north more than the south ever troubled to count, a cart and a team and five days of a man's labour spent getting wool to a fair that would have carried it in a single day by rail had the Board's own rule allowed it, and that the toll the carters paid in time was a toll no ledger at Cathair dhearg would ever be asked to enter against the fair's own profit. Frayne wrote the figure down as the man gave it to him, a season's wages set against five days twice over for every cart the north sent south, and thought, riding on once the water had dropped enough to let the carts through, that a fact set down in a clerk's small book travelled no further than that book's own covers unless someone with the standing to be heard troubled to carry it further.

The carts reached Muileann òg on the fifth day, as they always did, and the wool was sold by the factors the fair's own rule required, at a price the carters reckoned fair enough for the road behind them and mean enough for the road they knew the profit was truly measured against, and Frayne stood at the fair's own edge a while watching the factors' clerks weigh and mark the bales before he turned to find the inn the carts' own drivers used, a house that took Tuathaich custom without comment because its owner had learned, over enough seasons, that a carter's coin spent as well as any other man's. He rode home the same road he had ridden out, five days back by the same lamps, the water dropped now and the going easier, carrying nothing the Moot could point to as the fruit of the journey save the two facts in his small book, the north's name for the road and the south's silence about it, a season's wages weighed against five days twice told, and a callus on his right hand he had not had going out.

## II. Of the Boy Who Watched

In the north that same winter a boy who had been born three years before, in the town the copper works had given its name to, was old enough to walk to the pit-head with the other children and watch the shift change, the hewers coming up black and bent from a day at the western faces while the men who kept the Board's own count stood clean at the gate with their ledgers and their unhurried hands, and to understand, before he had words enough to say it to anyone, that the two kinds of men before him would go on being two kinds for as long as either lived, one bent a little further every year and the other not visibly bent at all. It was an aristocracy that stood very near the undying, and the boy, watching it from the pit-head wall with the other children of the shunting families, took the fact in the way a child takes in weather, without yet asking why it should be so, only that it was so, and that the coal his own father cut was never going to be the coal his own father owned any share of. Of this nothing more is told; the age that watched him grow knew him only as a line in a census and a name in a chronicle's later hand, and left him, that winter, at the pit-head wall, watching.

## III. Of the Register Begun

Mary Coyle called the Moot's clerks to the room at Caol mhòr in an Dùbhlachd of that same year, Frayne among them, home from the carters' road some months and back at his usual table, and told them plainly what she had told no one outside the room before that morning: that the Board kept no count of the Tuathaich dead and maimed in the western pits beyond what the census swept up in its own careless net, a number without a name attached to it anywhere a hewer's own family could ever put a finger to, and that the Moot, having no Board of its own to answer to and no council above it to forbid the keeping, would begin a book that named them one by one, on the families' own word since no other word was going to be offered.

"We have no rolls to draw the names from," Frayne said, the first objection any clerk in the room troubled to raise aloud, "and a name taken on a widow's word alone, with nothing to check it against, is a name a southern court would throw out before it read the second line." Mary Coyle answered him that she had not asked the room to keep a book a southern court would honour, only a book that would be true, and that a widow's own word, given in a room with her neighbours to hear it, was truth enough for any book the Moot troubled to keep, whatever a court south of Caol mhòr chose to call it.

The book was opened that same week, a plain ledger bound at Muileann leathan and carried north still smelling of the binder's glue, and the first name entered in it was the husband of the widow at Àth fhada whose lamp Frayne had lodged under not a year before, brought to the room by a neighbour of hers who had walked the road south on other business and thought, hearing the Moot's new business spoken of, to carry the widow's name along with him rather than let it wait for a season she might not trouble to make the journey herself. Frayne wrote the entry in his own hand, the widow's husband's name and the pit he had died under and the season it had happened in, and set the pen down a moment before the ink was dry, having understood, in the writing of that single line, that he had just done the one thing his years at a Moot table had never yet let him do, which was to put a Tuathaich name into a book that meant to keep it rather than to file it and forget it. He told Mary Coyle, handing her the ledger to read over, that he did not know how many names the book would want before it was done, and she told him that neither did she, and that not knowing was the whole of the reason for keeping it.

The register grew through that winter and the spring after, a name and a place and a season at a time, entered on no evidence stronger than a family's own remembering and checked against nothing but the room's own trust in the family that gave it. Frayne kept the second book himself, a working ledger where a name was first set down rough before it was copied fair into Clàr nan Leòn proper, and he found, within the first month of the keeping, that the work asked more of a clerk than the careful hand he had brought to it out of habit. A name given at second hand by a neighbour wanted checking, where checking could be had, against whatever other neighbour or kinsman might confirm the season and the pit, the Moot trusting the neighbour's word well enough and asking only that a register meant to outlast the men who kept it be made as right as a clerk's own care could make it; and more than one entry that winter was set down first as one thing and corrected within the season to another, a season misremembered, a pit's own name given wrong by a family that had moved twice since the death and forgotten which working had taken the man.

Mary Coyle read every entry before it went into the fair copy, a habit she kept up through that whole first year though the room's other business left her little enough time for it, and told Frayne, the evening he brought her the twentieth name, that she meant to go on reading each one herself for as long as her eyes held out, since a book kept for the dead deserved at least one pair of eyes that troubled to read it whole rather than trusting the work entire to a clerk's own hand, however careful that hand had proved itself. She asked him, that same evening, whether the work weighed on him more than the Moot's ordinary business had, and he told her, after a silence longer than the question seemed to want, that it did, and that he had not expected it to, having spent enough years copying grimmer reports than most clerks ever saw without the copying troubling his sleep before. What troubled him now, he said, was not the dying, which the north had always known and grieved in its own way, but the knowing that a name written nowhere else might, if his own hand slipped or his own memory failed some winter years off, be lost a second time, this once for good; and Mary Coyle told him that this was exactly why the book was bound in leather rather than stitched in paper, and kept in the Moot's own strongbox rather than on any clerk's open shelf, so that no single hand's slipping would be given the chance to lose it.

By the year's end the register held above thirty names, carried to every western pit's own captain that the Moot's clerks could reach and copied out a second time, against fire or flood, into a spare book kept apart from the first at a house in Muileann leathan that had never once been troubled by a southern search, so that a name lost to the Board's own count might yet be found in Clàr nan Leòn, the register of the hurt, kept at Caol mhòr and sent south to no court, no Board, and no council that had never once asked to see it.

## IV. Of the Chronicle Laid Down

Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill had kept the royal chronicle at Muileann chaol for four and twenty years by that spring, longer than any keeper before her had held the office, and she told the Library's own keeper, Mòrag nic Coinnich, in an Giblean of SE 42, that her hand had begun to shake on a long day's writing in a way it had not shaken when she was young at the work, and that a chronicle written in a shaking hand was a poorer gift to the years after her than a chronicle handed, in good time, to a younger one. She wrote her last entry that same week, a plain list, kept in the same hand she had kept every list before it, of what the chronicle under her care had gone four and twenty years without setting down: the northern towns' own accounts of themselves, the humans' old treaties in their own tongue, and the Board's surveys that three men at Cathair mhòr had once left unrolled on a table. She struck nothing from the list once it was written, and asked no one's leave to write it, and set the pen down for the last time as chronicler on a page that named, more honestly than any page she had written before it, the shape of everything she had not been able to write.

Tormod mac Sheumais came after her, a clerk of the first chronicle circuit who had spent the better part of ten years riding between the southern towns copying the returns the circuit's own justices sent him, and who wrote, once he sat at the desk Gormshuil had kept, in a hand so quick that the Library's own copyists took to calling him Tormod Pinn before his first full season at the office was out, the name holding to him for the rest of his years at the work. He copied Gormshuil's last list into the front of his own first book, word for word, and struck nothing out of it either, telling Mòrag, who asked him why a new chronicler would open his own book with an old one's account of what was missing rather than with a fresh page of his own, that a list of gaps was worth more copied whole than left behind to be found and re-written by whoever came after him with less patience for the finding.

## V. Of the Keel at Ros mhòr

The shipwrights turned out of the southern guild yards in the years the guilds first closed their doors to Tuathaich hands had scattered along the northern coast in ones and twos since, mending what boats the north already owned and building nothing new, the timber and the coin for a proper yard being beyond what any one of them could raise alone. It was at Ros mhòr, in am Faoilleach of SE 41, that enough of them came together at last to lay a keel of their own, the harbour there deep enough for a hull of any size the north was likely to want and far enough from the southern yards that no guild master troubled to ride up and ask under what charter they worked.

Harry Marlow stood at the head of them, grey now and further from the boy who had first been bound apprentice at a southern yard than he had ever expected to find himself, and it was his own hand that laid the yard's first keel, cut and set to the lines his old master had taught him rather than to any line a southern yard would trouble to build now, the humans' own lines, low and full in the bow, that had gone out of southern fashion the year the guilds decided a Tuathaich hand had no business building anything a Dia-thìreach captain might one day sail. He told the men working beside him, the first morning the keel was laid true and pinned, that he had spent thirty years building other men's boats to other men's rules, and that he meant to spend whatever years were left him building his own boats to a rule he could stand behind, whatever a southern guild chose to call the result.

The hull went up through that year on evenings and Sabbaths, the yard's men keeping their day's wage at whatever work would still have a Tuathaich hand and giving the boatyard what hours were left over, timber bought a plank at a time from whatever coin the yard's own men could spare and coin from Ros mhòr households who wanted no share in the finished boat beyond the knowing that it had been built. A southern factor riding through that spring, on business that had nothing to do with boats, stopped long enough at the harbour to look the frame over and told Harry Marlow, not unkindly, that a hull built on Sabbaths and evenings by men paid nothing for the building of it would want two years where a guild yard wanted one, and Marlow told him that two years of a man's own boat was worth more to the man than one year of another man's, an answer the factor turned over a while before riding on without troubling to argue it further.

It stood ready for the water in an t-Iuchar of SE 42, swifter in the eye of anyone who had built a boat before than anything the southern yards had launched in a long season, low in the bow and quick to answer, the humans' old lines proved again on northern water by hands the south had told, not many years before, that such lines were no longer theirs to build. Harry Marlow put his own shoulder to the launching with the rest of the yard's men, the rollers greased and the tide judged to the hour by a man who had judged tides at a southern yard before any of the younger hands beside him had been born, and the hull took the water clean, riding upright and true from its very first wave without the roll a badly trimmed hull will sometimes show a crowd on its first meeting with the sea. The men who had cut its timber and pinned its keel stood on the strand at Ros mhòr and watched it ride, the first boat the north had built entire to its own name since the yards first turned them away, and said little, having spent thirty years among them learning that a boat that answers true says more for itself than any man standing on the strand beside it ever could; and Harry Marlow, wading out to lay his own hand once more on the hull's cold planking before the crew took her out past the harbour mouth, told no one in particular, though more than one man on the strand heard him say it, that his father had cast dead coin into ploughshares in a smithy at Doire mhin and lived to see the north plough its own furrows with them, and that he meant, himself, to live long enough to see the north sail its own waters in hulls it had built with no southern guild's leave asked for and none given.
