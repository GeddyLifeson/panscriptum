# Of the Vigil in the Open

*SE 8 an Dàmhair – SE 9 an Giblean*

> "We do not tell outsiders which night. If it were known, it could be watched for. If it could be watched for, it would not be a vigil. It would be a show put on for whoever came to watch it."
>
> — a clerk of an Leabharlann's plain rendering of what was said at Baile chrom, when asked

## I. Of the Line to Muileann chrom

The railway the humans had built out of the central mountains to the sea had lain broken in three places since the fighting of An Dealachadh, its cuttings full of blown sand, its rails rusted red as the hills they crossed. In {{date:V-0039}} the council mended it as far as Muileann chrom, no further, for the money ran only that far and the mountain gave the rest. A Rail Board was made to run it, seated at Cathair dhearg: one clerk, two engineers who had learned their trade watching the humans work it and never quite forgot the habit of looking over their shoulders for a foreman, and the humans' own timetable, copied fair into a Dia-thìreach hand and hung on a board at the Muileann chrom terminus, where the line now ended, under a header someone had lettered too large for the space it had. The trains ran to it or near enough. Nobody who rode them thought it strange that a kingdom six years old should keep the hours of a people who no longer lived on the island. It was the only timetable there was.

The bridge below the capital, over the wide brown water the humans had spanned in stone, was hallowed anew that same autumn. A priest of Macha stood on the parapet in the cold and named it in Dia-thìris, and a new stone was cut and set beside the old one, which nobody had thought to move and which nobody, in the end, moved. The humans' name-stone was turned instead, quietly, by two masons working an evening shift so that its face looked down at the river rather than out at the road, and there it has stood since, reading nothing to anyone who does not go down onto the towpath and look up. An Leabharlann keeps no record of whose idea that was. It keeps only that it was done, and that no minute anywhere calls it defacement, and none calls it honour either. The bridge carries the road it always carried. It does not know, or does not say, which of its two stones it answers to.

Further south that winter the shire of Ceann mhin closed its old ground at Cnoc dhìreach to new burial, the vines by then having split so many of the old stones that no clear plot could be found among them without turning up someone else's dead first. The families who had people there still walk out on Na Ceithir Fèilltean to clear the growth from the names they can still read, and some say, in the way such things are always said of ground closed to the living and kept for the dead, that the place is not so empty after dark as a stranger walking the road would think. An Leabharlann records the closure and no more. What is or is not walking among the broken stones at Cnoc dhìreach after dark has never been a question that came before any court, and an Leabharlann keeps no case it was not asked to try.

These are the small things a kingdom does in its eighth and ninth years, once its wars are over and its first census taken: it mends a line as far as its money reaches, it turns a stone rather than break it, it shuts a gate on ground too full to hold more. None of them was much remarked at the time. All three are remembered now only because of what else was happening, in that same autumn and the spring that followed it, on a headland in the far north-west that no engineer's timetable reached and no priest of Macha had ever stood on.

## II. Of Baile chrom

Baile chrom stood where the coast turned, a scatter of grey houses above a shingle beach with its boats drawn up past the tideline and its nets strung to dry on frames the fishermen mended every spring with their fathers' twine, because good twine was dear and their fathers had known how to make bad twine last. Above the houses, where the land rose to a bare shoulder before it fell into the sea, stood the lighthouse the humans had built, one of the five, its lamp lit each dusk by an old keeper the council paid by the year and otherwise forgot, its outer stair still sound, its door unbarred because nobody had thought a lock worth the iron. The Tuathaich called it the old light and let their children climb the outer stair on dares while the keeper slept. From its gallery, on a clear evening, you could see the whole run of the coast north and south and, on the clearest evenings of all, a grey smudge that the old people said was cloud and the young people said might be something else, and neither would say more than that.

In the town itself the Tuathaich kept An Eaglais their forebears had carried over the sea at An t-Aiseag, and kept it as quietly as a people keeps a thing the state has not troubled to forbid: openly enough to build with, not so openly as to be troubled. Its congregation met in a long low building that had been a net-store before it was a church and looked, from the road, very much like a net-store still, its cross cut small and set over the door where a passing maor's man would need to be looking for it to see it. Within, on a shelf behind the altar-cloth, in a box no larger than a loaf, the town kept what its people called A' Chiad Churach: the telling of Naomh Breandan's crossing to Inis nam Beannaichte, in whatever form it had come to them, written or carved or kept only in the memory of whoever held the office of keeping it. The Dia-thìreach priesthood, on the rare occasion the question reached them, called it superstition and left it there. An Leabharlann, which has asked twice across the age, has never been shown it, and knows only that it is kept, and where is not a question an Leabharlann asks twice of a people who have already answered it once by not answering.

Hannah Crane held, that autumn, the office of vigil-keeper for Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise, an office within An Eaglais and yet not quite of it, older than the building it was kept in and answerable to nobody south of Muileann fhionn. She had held it eleven years, since the woman before her had died in a hard winter and left it to her the way such things were left in Baile chrom, by a word said once to the right person and never written down. She was a fisherman's widow herself, her husband taken by the sea the year before she took the office, and she kept a cottage two doors from the net-store church with a black cat that came and went as it pleased and a garden that grew, in the thin northern soil, more onions than anything prettier. Nobody thought her a solemn woman. She laughed easily, swore when she stubbed a toe on the hearthstone, and was known to the length of the coast for a temper that came up fast and went down faster. None of that stood against her holding the office. The office asked one thing of its keeper and one thing only, which was silence kept on one night of the year, for Sìne, an Gèadh Sìtheil, whom the sect named for the wild goose An Eaglais elsewhere named an Spiorad Naomh and pictured, when it pictured it at all, as something gentler and less inclined to fly a thousand miles into a headwind without resting. Which night was Hannah's to choose and Hannah's alone to keep, and the whole of the office's discipline lay in choosing it so that no one, not the town, not the maor, not the bishops themselves, could learn it in advance.

## III. Of the Night in the Open

For as long as anyone at Baile chrom could tell it, the vigil had been kept indoors: a room shuttered dark, the keeper alone in it from dusk until whatever hour before dawn she judged the silence had been held long enough, the town going about its evening outside the shutters as though nothing were happening behind them, which was, in its way, the whole of the point. A silence nobody could see being kept was a silence nobody could interrupt, and a silence nobody could interrupt was a silence that could not be taken from the keeper by any hand stronger than her own. It had held through An Aois Choigreach, through An t-Aiseag and the years it stayed open, through its closing and the marching north, and through eight years of the kingdom that had followed. Hannah Crane, in the autumn of her fortieth year, decided to keep it another way.

She did not say why, not to the town and not, so far as an Leabharlann has ever learned, to anyone at all, though the reason she gave herself, alone, walking the shingle in the evenings of that Dàmhair while she turned the decision over, was simple enough that she might have said it to anyone who asked and been believed: that a silence kept behind a shutter, in a kingdom that had not yet troubled to ask what was behind the shutter, was a silence that cost nothing and proved nothing, and she had held the office eleven years without once being tested in it, and wondered, walking the shingle with the wind coming hard off the water, whether a thing untested for eleven years could truly be said to have been kept at all. No one learned the date she had settled on. On a Sunday at the end of am Faoilleach, standing at the net-store door after the service, she told the town only that this year the vigil would be held on the headland, in the open, where any who wished could see that it was kept, though none would be told beforehand when to look.

The town took it as the town took most things Hannah Crane did, which was without much comment and a good deal of private opinion kept for the hearth afterward. Some thought it foolish, an office risked for no reason a sensible woman could name. Some thought it overdue, and said so more freely once the thing was done than they had said it before. The maor of the shire, lodged in the town's old customs-house and a stranger to it still, sent no word at all, which the town read, correctly, as meaning the maor had heard nothing worth his attention, Oidhche Glaise being to him and to every officer of An Crùn before him no more than a line in an old report that called the Tuathaich's private observances harmless and not worth the ink to describe further.

There was talk of it at the net-mending that winter, the frames drawn up under the eaves of the net-store church where the work could go on out of the rain, and Hannah sat among the menders as she always did, her own needle no faster or slower than anyone else's, and let the talk run where it wanted. An old man named for nobody an Leabharlann has recorded said he had kept his own counsel through worse changes than this one and would keep it through this, and went on splicing. A younger woman, newly married into the town from Muileann fhionn and still finding her feet in its customs, asked outright whether the keeper was not afraid, sitting alone on a headland all a winter's night with nothing but the old light for company, and Hannah told her that she had been afraid of a good many things in her life and had found, on the whole, that fear was a poor reason to leave a thing undone that wanted doing, which was as near as she came, that winter or after, to explaining herself to anyone. The younger woman said no more of it, but she was on the headland herself, at a careful distance, the night it happened, and never told even her own husband that she had gone.

An Eaglais's own priest at Baile chrom, an old man who had baptised half the town and buried the other half's parents, asked her once, walking back from a burial in am Faoilleach, whether she meant to go through with it, and when she said she did, told her only that the office was hers to keep as she judged best, and that he had held his own for forty years by staying out of what belonged to another keeper's office. It was the nearest thing to a blessing she had from anyone that winter, and she told the linen-woman once that she held those few words dearer than anything the bishops ever wrote her.

On the night of {{date:V-0043}}, the fourteenth of am Màrt, Hannah Crane walked up from the town after dark, alone, with nothing in her hands, and climbed the shoulder of land to where the old light stood with its lamp burning high above her against a sky just clearing of cloud, and sat down on the turf below its base with her back to the cold stone and the sea in front of her, and kept the silence there until the sky began to lighten in the east some hours later. She lit no fire, for the vigil's discipline forbade it as much in the open as behind a shutter, and she had thought, walking up, that the cold would be the worst of it, and found, sitting through it, that the cold was the easiest part; it was the not-knowing whether anyone watched, and the knowing, at the same time, that this was precisely what she had chosen, that occupied the long middle hours.

The tide turned somewhere below her in the dark, though she could not see it, only hear the change in the water's noise against the shingle, a longer pull where before it had been a shorter one. Once a light showed far out, low on the water, there and gone before she could be sure of it, a fishing boat working later than it should or something else entirely, and she did not move toward it or away from it, because the vigil's whole discipline lay in not moving at all, in sitting where she had sat down until the sky told her she was free to stand. Her feet went from cold to numb to a kind of distant ache that she stopped attending to somewhere past midnight, the way a held breath stops feeling like holding after long enough. The wind dropped toward the small hours, and the sea below the headland went quiet enough that she could hear, once, far off, the calling of birds moving in the dark, though whether geese so early in the year or something else she never afterward said, holding to the vigil's other discipline, which is that what is heard on the night is not told either.

She thought, in the stretches when thought was easier than not thinking, of the woman who had held the office before her, dead now more winters than Hannah cared to count, who had kept it always behind the shutter and had never once said, in all the years she taught Hannah the small duties of it, whether she thought the shutter a true part of the keeping or only a habit nobody had troubled to question. Hannah had never asked her outright, and there was no one left to ask now, and she found, sitting with her back to the cold stone, that she did not mind the not-knowing as much as she had expected to. Some things an office hands down are the office's own; some are only the last keeper's caution, worn smooth by years into looking like doctrine. She could not have said, that night, which the shutter had been, and thought it likely no one, not the bishops with their three signatures nor the woman who had taught her, could have said either.

She was not entirely alone on the headland, though she did not know it until near dawn. The maor's man, a clerk's assistant from Cnoc ghorm sent up the coast on some unrelated errand of the shire's and lodging that week at Baile chrom, had climbed a lower rise a quarter-mile off, drawn by nothing more than an idle curiosity about where the vigil-keeper had gone after her Sunday announcement, and had sat there through most of the night himself, at a distance, watching a shape against the lighthouse's base that did not move and a fire that never came. He wrote nothing of it in the shire's returns, there being, as he told it afterward to anyone in Baile chrom who would stand him a drink, no line on any form he carried for a woman sitting still on a hillside all night doing nothing anyone could call an offence, and no instruction from the maor to make one. He watched, and in the morning he walked back down to his lodging and slept until noon, and the shire's record of that week at Baile chrom says only what it always said, which was nothing.

## IV. Of the Letter Called Irregular

Word of it reached An Eaglais's bishops by the summer, carried, as such things travelled the coast, by a preaching brother who had heard it told at Cnoc ghorm and thought it worth passing on, half as news and half as a question he did not know how to put more directly: whether an office so old and so private might be kept, from now on, in a way the bishops had not sanctioned and had, until that telling, not known existed. The bishops wrote back to Baile chrom a single letter, addressed to the keeper of the vigil and signed by three names none of the town had heard before, which called the new manner of keeping it irregular and asked, without commanding, that the keeper consider returning to the custom her office had held before her.

Hannah Crane read the letter twice at her own hearth, folded it, and put it in the box behind the altar-cloth alongside the thing the box already kept, which seemed to her, she told the woman who helped her with the church's linen, as good a place as any for a letter that asked a question and made sure, in the asking, never quite to become an order. She did not answer it. Nobody at Baile chrom, then or later, learned of any bishop travelling north to see for himself what irregular meant, and the vigil was kept in the open again the following year, and the year after, on nights Hannah told no one in advance. The word irregular is the only word An Eaglais's own record has ever set against it. An Leabharlann, which keeps the letter's copy among the north's returns, has never been asked to explain why a kingdom that fined a fishing town for arming its boats without a licence let a woman sit on a hillside once a year unwatched and unfined, and offers, where it is asked, no better answer than that some things a state forbids because it has looked at them and judged them dangerous, and some things it lets stand because it has never looked at them at all, and a vigil kept once a year on a headland the maor's writ ran over but the maor's attention never quite reached was, for many years, the second kind.

A church was built that same year at Cnoc ghorm, the largest the north had raised since An t-Aiseag closed, its stone hauled up from a quarry two shires south on carts Tional nan Tuathach paid for by subscription, and into its tower, when the tower was finished, the town hung a bell that had come across the sea long before in the belfry of the old chapel at Ros bheag, carted north in the kingdom's first spring and kept crated in a barn for want of anywhere fit to hang it. It rang for the first time on a market morning, and the sound of it, the town said afterward, carried further than anyone had thought a single bell could carry, out past the fields and down to the shore road, where carters driving in from Muileann fhionn heard it and knew, before they came in sight of the tower, that Cnoc ghorm had a church worth the name at last.

The two buildings, the small net-store church at Baile chrom and the great new one at Cnoc ghorm, stood twelve miles apart on the same coast and answered, in their different ways, to the same three signatures on the same distant letter, though the Cnoc ghorm parish, larger and nearer the shire's own seat, kept its custom to the letter without ever being asked to think twice about it, and thought no more of Baile chrom's irregularity than a shrug when it was mentioned at all. The north had not, in truth, agreed among itself on the matter; it had learned, in its years under the crown, a great deal about which disagreements were worth carrying to a bishop and which were better left where they lay, and a vigil-keeper's choice of headland over hearthroom, so long as the vigil itself was kept, fell without much debate into the second kind.

## V. Of the Geese Going North

Muileann fhionn, the mill-town the humans had built to watch the northern landing and left, like everything else, to whoever wanted the keeping of it, was granted that same spring a right to hold a market for the inland country round about, the second such right any northern town had been given since the kingdom was proclaimed, after Ceann mhòr's. The Tuathaich drovers of the hill farms, who had until then walked their cattle and sheep the long way south to sell where a buyer could be found, began instead to bring them to Muileann fhionn on set days, and from Muileann fhionn the droves went on south by the old tracks to Ceann leathan and the farms beyond it, so that within a few years the town's green stood ankle-deep in trodden mud every market morning and its inns did a trade the mill alone had never given them. Nobody troubled to write down whose idea the market right had been. It answered a need every farmer in the hills had felt for years, and answering it cost the council nothing, which was, that spring, coming to be understood in the north as the surest sign that a thing would be granted rather than filed.

The green at Muileann fhionn had been common ground since the humans' day, used for nothing more than a fair-weather pasture and the odd game of ball among the mill hands, and it took the town's carpenters most of a month to lay hurdles enough to pen the first market's beasts, working from a plan one of them had seen used at a southern fair and adapted, by guesswork more than instruction, to hold cattle rather than the finer stock the southern fair had been built for. The first morning brought fewer drovers than the second, and the second fewer than the third, word travelling up the hill tracks the way word always travelled in the north, by one farmer telling the next at a boundary wall, until by midsummer the green stood full on every market day and the innkeepers of Muileann fhionn, who had kept rooms half the year for millers and the other half for nobody, found themselves turning trade away for the first time in the town's memory.

By the time the market opened its first season the geese had already gone. They had come down the coast past Baile chrom every autumn within living memory and gone north again every spring, in lines that broke and re-formed against a wind that never seemed to be blowing the way they wanted it to, and no one in the town, watching them from the shingle or from the shoulder of the headland where the old light stood, had ever thought them anything but geese, going where geese go, at the season geese go there. Hannah Crane watched them from the same turf she had sat on in am Màrt, on a clear evening near the end of an Giblean, with the black cat winding round her ankles and the wind, for once, at her back, and said nothing to the woman standing beside her that a chronicler could set down as meaning more than the words themselves; only that they were early this year, or seemed it, and that the light held longer with each evening now, and that it would be a fair spring if it kept on the way it had begun.

The town below her went on with what a town's evening is made of, suppers and doorstep talk and the last of the day's work put away before dark, none of it troubled by what had been kept, once more, on a night nobody there had been told in advance, on the headland above them, in the open, where anyone who cared to climb up and watch was free to watch, and see nothing more than a woman sitting still at the foot of a lit tower until the sky came up grey in the east. That was the whole of what the vigil had ever asked to be seen doing. It was the whole of what, that year and for years after, it was seen doing. The bishops' letter stayed folded behind the altar-cloth. And the geese went north, as they always had, answering to no maor's writ and no bishop's letter and no line in any timetable a Rail Board ever hung on a wall, and were gone from the coast within the week.
