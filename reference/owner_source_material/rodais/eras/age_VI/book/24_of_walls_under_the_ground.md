# Of Walls under the Ground

*SE 30 an Giblean – SE 32 an Cèitean*

> Found this season at Làrach an Dùin: walls of dressed stone, older than any building known to us, and no writing on them anywhere. Entered as of Seann-Dhaoine, purpose unknown.
>
> — An Leabharlann's register of finds, {{date:V-0120}}, entire, as it stands at Muileann chaol

## I. Of the New Hall at Cathair mhòr

Taigh Mhic Ùisdein had been building in stone the better part of two years before it was finished, and when it was finished the town said there was no house like it between Cathair dhearg and the sea, which was very likely true, since no one else in the kingdom had a mountain of spoil sitting idle at the mouth of his own working to quarry it from for nothing. Uisdean mac Ùisdein had taken the stone that came up broken and unsellable from An Sloc Mòr's own galleries, cart-load on cart-load of it over two winters, and set masons to dressing it into a hall with a roof of slate and a stair of the same grey stone climbing to a gallery that ran the width of the house. Comann nan Gualadairean held its feast there the week it was finished, tables the length of the hall and torches in iron brackets the smith had made to match no pattern but his own fancy, and men who had gone down An Sloc Mòr's shafts for twenty years sat under a roof built, as more than one of them said that night with a dry look at the man beside him, out of the very rock they had spent those twenty years bringing up.

Ailean Leabhar was not at the feast. He heard of it after, from Mòrag nic Coinnich, who had gone as an Leabharlann's own witness because somebody from Muileann chaol always went to a thing of that size, whether an Leabharlann thought it worth the going or not, on the ground that a house built to outlast its builder was exactly the kind of thing a chronicle regretted not having a clerk present for once the builder and the house were both fifty years gone. She told him the hall smelt of new mortar and old smoke in about equal measure, and that Uisdean had stood at the head of the highest table and said almost nothing, letting the guild's own master speak for the evening, which she thought, knowing the man a little, was either modesty or the surest kind of confidence there was, and that she had not been able to decide which in one evening and did not expect a second evening to settle it for her.

The licences went out that same season, and drew less notice at the time than the house did, though they would draw more before the year was out. The southern fishing grounds off Caol mhòr had been open water to whoever put a boat on them since the shires were first drawn, Dia-thìreach and Tuathaich boats working the same banks without much comment from either side, since fish did not trouble to sort themselves by the terms of a truce their forebears had made a generation before either fleet was born. That spring the maor's office at Caol mhòr began issuing licences for the grounds, in writing, and kept them for Dia-thìreach boats. The Tuathaich fleet was left the northern grounds instead, where the fishing ran thinner and where, as every man on the quay at Caol mhòr already knew without the office troubling to say it, the raiders' own boats worked too, since a coast with no licence on it was a coast with no one to complain of who fished it.

The first boat turned away under the new rule was a Tuathaich craft out of Caol mhòr itself, her master a man named Sam Ridley who had fished the southern banks since before the licences were dreamed of and who brought his catch in to the maor's own quay the day the rule took force, as he had every fair-weather day for eleven years, and was told at the scales that the catch could be weighed but the boat that had taken it had no paper for the grounds it came from and would need one before it went out again, and that papers for those grounds were not, at present, being written for boats of his fleet. Sam Ridley said little at the scales and less on the walk home, by every account that reached Tional nan Tuathach's own minutes at Caol mhòr that summer, but Tional nan Tuathach entered the day and the name all the same, since a first of anything was worth the ink even when the man it happened to had nothing further to say about it.

Doire shean, in the south-west, built its own harbour that same season, the first new harbour raised anywhere in the kingdom since An Dealachadh without a coin of An Crùn's in it. The house of Òrd na Cloiche at Doire shean lent the money against the town's own dues, as the order's houses had taken to doing where a town's need outran a maor's patience for petitioning Cathair dhearg, and the masons who built the mole worked to Òrd na Cloiche's own measure rather than An Crùn's, a fact the harbour-master noted with some satisfaction in his first return and the council, so far as any packet reaching Muileann chaol ever said, noted not at all. The house took its first quarter's dues off the new quay before the mole's last course was even dry, a plain figure entered in the town's own book beside the sum it had lent, and the return that reached Muileann chaol set the two figures side by side without comment, leaving a reader to work out for himself, as Ailean did when the return crossed his own table, how many years of quarter-dues it would take the town to owe the house of Òrd na Cloiche rather less than it had first borrowed from it.

Of the feast in the new hall he had one further account, sent him some weeks after Mòrag's own telling by a mason who had worked the dressing of the stone and had not been asked to the tables he had spent two winters cutting, a plain enough matter and no fault, by the mason's own words, of Uisdean mac Ùisdein's, since the feast had been called for the guild and the guild held no cutter of stone among its own sworn members, whatever the man's hand had done for the walls that housed the supper. The mason wrote it to an Leabharlann without complaint, only as a thing he thought worth the record's keeping, that he had dressed the stone of a hall he was not asked to sit in, and Ailean entered the letter whole rather than in his own shorter hand, on the ground that a fact stated this plainly, and without complaint attached to it, needed no keeper's shortening, and would only have lost by it.

## II. Of the Trenches at Làrach an Dùin

Ailean Leabhar was five-and-fifty that spring, keeper of the Library fifteen years, and he had learned in most of those fifteen years that the finds which mattered least to the kingdom's own business were very often the ones that cost him the most sleep, a fact he had stopped finding strange some time before he had stopped finding it troublesome. He went down himself to Làrach an Dùin on the south-east coast that year, which was not his habit, since a keeper who left Muileann chaol every time a dig opened would have had no library left to keep by the end of a season; but the diggers' first letter had used a word he did not care to take on trust from a letter, and the word was *older*.

The camp sat above a low headland where the ground had been broken by trenching since the early spring, a scatter of canvas and turf-roofed huts and a cook-fire that never quite went out, and the diggers, four of them under a foreman named Cailean who had cut trenches for an Leabharlann at three sites before this one and thought he had seen most of what the ground on Dia-thìr had to show him, met Ailean at the head of the longest trench with the particular quiet of men who wanted to be asked rather than to volunteer.

"Show me," Ailean said, and Cailean showed him.

The wall ran the length of the trench and further, a coursed stone founded so deep that the diggers had given up trying to find its footing and had cut sideways instead to follow the line of it, dressed on its face in a manner Ailean had not seen in any ruin an Leabharlann held a record of, the stone fitted so close along its joints that a knife blade would not have found the gap between one block and the next. There was no mortar in it anywhere they had cut. There was no carving on it anywhere they had cut, no sign scratched or cut or painted, nothing that a scholar could call a letter or a mark meant to be read by anyone who came after, only the wall itself, running on under the turf in a line the trenches had not yet found the end of.

"How old," Ailean said, not quite a question, since he already knew no answer he was given would satisfy him.

"Older than anything I've cut into," Cailean said, "and I've cut into the watch-fort ruins and thought those old. Whatever built this built long before any of them, and built better."

Ailean spent four days at the camp before he went home, most of them on his knees at the trench's edge with a lamp and a notebook, and what he found in those four days was less than what he did not find, which was the harder thing by far to carry back to Muileann chaol and set down in an Leabharlann's own hand. No writing. No image of a god or a beast or a man. Not a hearth-ash, not a broken pot, not a bone the diggers could say for certain was worked by a hand rather than gnawed by a fox, nothing that a scholar of any faith on the island could point to and say *this tells us who they were, or what they wanted, or what they called the stream at the foot of their own wall*. Only stone, laid with a patience and a skill that put every mason Ailean had ever watched at work to shame, and then, at some point no trench had yet reached far enough to find, left.

He wrote the entry himself rather than leave it to a clerk, sitting at the foreman's own trestle by lamplight on his last night at the camp, and he wrote it short, because the alternative to short was to write a page arguing with his own ignorance, and an entry that argued with itself before it stated its fact was no entry at all, only a chronicler's private trouble dressed up as a finding. *Walls of dressed stone, older than any building known to us, and no writing on them anywhere. Of Seann-Dhaoine, purpose unknown.* He read it back twice, struck nothing, and let it stand.

## III. Of Làrach an Teampaill

He rode east from Làrach an Dùin rather than straight home, since the season's letters had brought him word of a second matter that could not, in honesty, wait for a second journey, and reached Làrach an Teampaill in the district's own damp evening light with the ruin already half in shadow, a low mound of fallen stone that the local telling called a temple though nobody living, Dia-thìrich or Tuathaich, could have said whose.

He was met, before he had so much as dismounted, by three elders of Na Seann Spioradan houses of the district, who had heard by the same letters that had reached him that an Leabharlann meant to open the ground, and who had come to the ruin themselves to be there when he arrived rather than trust the matter to a written petition that a keeper at Muileann chaol might set aside unread. The eldest of the three, a woman named Beathag who kept the district's own hearth-book and had walked, by her own account, to Làrach an Teampaill on every quarter-day of her life since she was old enough to be trusted with the road alone, spoke first and spoke plainly.

"We are not asking you to leave it standing," she said. "It will stand or fall as the ground under it chooses, and we have never asked an Leabharlann to prop up a wall it has no business propping. We are asking you not to open it. Whatever is under that stone, it is not ours to say what it is, and it is not yours either, and a hand that goes into the ground to find out makes a claim on the finding that neither of us has the right to make."

Ailean stood at the edge of the mound a long while before he answered, the light going out of the sky behind Beathag's shoulder and the ruin's own shadow lengthening across the ground between them, and he thought, standing there, of the wall at Làrach an Dùin four days behind him, laid by hands that had left no word anywhere for anyone who came after, and of the entry he had written by lamplight, *purpose unknown*, and of how little that entry had actually told a reader once the words were counted, and how much labour the not-knowing had cost him to set down honestly rather than paper over with a guess dressed as a finding.

"I can measure it without opening it," he said at last. "The shape of the ground, the fall of the stone, what stands and what has come down, none of that needs a spade. If that answers what you're asking, I'll take the measure and leave the ground as I find it."

Beathag looked at him a moment before she answered, the way a person looks at an answer they had expected to have to argue harder for. "It answers it," she said. "We had not thought to hear it offered before we asked twice."

"I've dug one wall this week that had nothing to tell me but its own patience," Ailean said. "I don't think this one owes me more than that either, and I'd rather leave it its patience than spend a season proving I was right to want an answer it never meant to give me."

He spent two days at Làrach an Teampaill with a chain and a level rather than a spade, walking the mound's own outline and setting down, in a hand as careful as any he gave an Leabharlann's own registers, the line of the fallen wall, the height of what still stood, the lie of the ground where a doorway might once have opened toward the morning sun or might, for all his measure could tell him, have opened toward nothing a living scholar would recognise as a reason. He entered it, back at Muileann chaol a fortnight later, in four words that cost him nearly as much thought as the four he had given the wall at Làrach an Dùin: *measured, and let be.*

## IV. Of the Obelisk at Cnoc bheag

The third of the season's ruins asked less of him and gave, in its own way, nearly as little back. A standing stone north of Cnoc bheag, cut with lines of a script no scholar at Muileann chaol or anywhere else on the island had ever been able to read, had stood in open pasture since before any record an Leabharlann held began, cattle rubbing their flanks against it and children chalking their own marks beside the old ones until a passing shepherd or a stricter mother scrubbed the chalk away again.

Ailean sent a scribe rather than going himself, a careful young woman named Ùna who had trained under him at copying an Leabharlann's oldest and most damaged leaves and who took to Carragh Cnoc bheag's lines with the same patient hand she gave a torn page, kneeling at the stone's foot with a board and a soft pencil and a square of oiled cloth against the weather, and copying every stroke exactly as it stood, gap for gap where the stone had worn smooth and weathered edge for weathered edge, rather than smoothing a guessed line into a broken one the way a less careful hand might have been tempted to.

It took her the better part of three weeks, working only in fair weather, and when the copy came back to Muileann chaol Ailean set it against every script an Leabharlann's own shelves held a record of and found nothing that matched it closer than a chance resemblance any two alphabets might share by accident. He filed the copy as he had filed the walls and the temple's own measure, under the same plain heading, and sent word to the council, through the maor of Cnoc bheag, that the stone was worth a fence against the cattle if nothing else, since a thing that had stood unread for longer than the kingdom's whole span deserved at least that much keeping, whatever it turned out, in the end, to have been trying to say. The council found the money for the fence before the summer was out, four posts and a rail set well back from the stone's own foot, and the shepherds of the district, who had rubbed their cattle against it without much ceremony for as long as any of them could remember, grumbled for a season about the lost scratching-post and then, by the following spring, had mostly stopped grumbling.

## V. Of the Lighthouse's Shadow

He had one other matter that season that was not his own finding, brought to him only because an Leabharlann kept the copies of everything a shire's return chose to enter, and it came from the far north-west, from the keeper of the lighthouse at Baile chrom, a Dia-thìreach man fifteen years at the post and still reporting, as his warrant bade him, what he saw by land as well as by sea.

He reported, in a hand Ailean thought more careful than most such reports troubled to be, that Oidhche Glaise sect kept its yearly vigil that spring in the lighthouse's own shadow, a knot of shapes standing silent in the dark at the foot of the tower through the small hours, gone before the light came up enough to put names to any of them, and that he had made no move against them, having no order that told him a silent vigil on open ground was any business of a keeper's whose charge was the light and not the shore beneath it.

The report went south to Cathair dhearg with the season's other returns, and Ailean, copying it into an Leabharlann's own register as he copied every such return that reached him, noted what came back from the council in answer, which was nothing at all. No order to the keeper, no word to the magistrate at Baile chrom who had fined the sect's own vigil-keeper once already for holding the same watch in the open, no instruction of any kind that a clerk at Cathair dhearg troubled to set down where a copy of it might reach Muileann chaol. He entered the silence as plainly as he had entered the walls and the temple, since a council's not answering was, in its own way, as much a fact of the kingdom's year as anything it had chosen to say, and a Library that kept only the answers and not the silences would, in the end, be keeping the smaller half of the record.

## VI. Of the Salt-Pans and the New Deputy

The last of the season's business reached him from the east coast rather than the west, a plainer matter than any of the others and, for that reason, one he was glad to set down last. The flats below Àth bheag had been dug for salt-pans that spring, shallow beds cut into the tidal ground and left to the sun and the slow work of the sea, and by the time the first return reached Muileann chaol the town was already supplying salt up the coast as far as the eye of the return's own writer cared to follow it, a trade that had begun, so far as anyone at Àth bheag troubled to say, from nothing more than a man noticing that the flats held the tide long enough after each ebb to leave a white crust worth the gathering, and a second man asking why nobody had thought to help the flats do more of it on purpose.

It was on the road home from that last errand, in the early days of the summer, that Ailean told Mòrag nic Coinnich what he had been turning over since the trench at Làrach an Dùin, which was that fifteen years of keeping an Leabharlann alone, riding out to every dig and every dispute and every return that seemed to want a keeper's own eye on it rather than a clerk's, had begun to cost him more than the walking, and that a Library with only one person who could speak for it was a Library one bad season from having nobody who could. Mòrag had come to Muileann chaol as a girl of the district and had worked under him the better part of a decade, careful with the older leaves in a way few of the younger clerks troubled to be and quicker than any of them at telling, from a shire's ordinary return, which lines wanted a keeper's own attention and which could wait for the season's slower copying.

"You want a deputy," she said, not quite a question, when he had finished saying rather less than that directly.

"I want someone an Leabharlann can trust to speak for it when I can't be in two places at once," he said, "which this season proved I very often can't. I'd want it to be you, if you'll take it."

She said she would think on it, which he had expected, and came to him three days later with her answer, which he had also expected, though not quite so soon: that she would take it, on the condition that an Leabharlann's own register note plainly, from the first day she signed anything in its name, that she signed as deputy and not as keeper, since a record that let the two blur together, even out of kindness to a woman doing the work well, would in the end tell a later reader less truth than a record that kept the two apart. He agreed to it before she had finished the sentence, thinking, as he agreed, that she had learned the office's own discipline rather better in ten years than some keepers managed to learn it in a lifetime, and that a Library with a deputy who thought that way about her own title was a Library considerably less likely to need worrying over the next time a keeper rode east and did not come home for a fortnight.

He set Ùna's copy of Carragh Cnoc bheag into an Leabharlann's own presses himself that same week, alongside the measure of Làrach an Teampaill and the short entry on the wall at Làrach an Dùin, three findings that told, between them, almost nothing of who had raised the stone or built the wall or asked that the temple's ground be left to its own patience, and a great deal, if a later reader troubled to notice it, of what a keeper of the Library had judged worth doing with a question he could not answer. He had measured what could be measured, copied what could be copied, and filed the not-knowing as carefully as he would have filed a certainty, so that whoever came after him, with better tools or a longer patience or simply more time than a single season allowed, would find the ground exactly as he had left it, and the question exactly as open.
