# Of the Moot and the Council of Twelve

*SE 2 an Lùnastal – an t-Sultain*

> *Chan eil inbhe againn. Scrìobhaidh sinn co-dhiù.*
> We have no standing. We shall write it all the same.
>

## I. Of the Long Room at Caol mhòr

They came on foot and in carts, at the tail end of a wet an Lùnastal that had done nothing for the barley and less for tempers, from Doire ghlas and Muileann leathan and the scattered crofts along the coast road, householders every one of them, a man or a woman standing for each roof that had smoke going up it that summer. Nobody had called them. Word had gone from house to house the way word goes in a country too poor yet to have a bell for it, mouth to mouth along the quay and up the drove paths, that there would be a meeting at Caol mhòr on the first fair day of the month to speak of the winter coming, and enough people believed it mattered enough to walk to find out that the harbour-house's long room, used till then for nothing grander than mending nets on a wet afternoon, could not hold them all. Those who could not get inside stood in the doorway and along the wall outside it, passing back what was said a sentence behind the saying of it.

Walter Hale stood at the back with his hat in both hands, working the brim between his fingers the way he had worked it as a boy waiting outside a schoolmaster's door, though he was forty-five now and had not stood outside a schoolmaster's door in thirty years. He had come because Ellen had told him to come, flatly, over the breakfast she made him eat before he went, on the grounds that a man who kept a private ledger of the town's dead and did not trouble to say so out loud to the town was only half doing the thing he had set himself to do. He had not argued the point. Once Ellen made it in that voice, he rarely did.

The room smelled of damp wool and fish-scale and too many bodies for the size of it, and for the better part of the afternoon it did nothing but argue, which Hale had expected and had come prepared to sit through. They argued first about whether there was anything to be done at all, a question that took the better part of an hour to settle in the negative, since a country that has just been marched into a homeland it did not choose and left to make what it can of a summer already half spent is not naturally disposed to agree on anything, least of all on whether agreeing is worth the breath. They argued next about who among them had the right to speak for the rest, which took longer, since half the room held that no one man could speak for households as different as a fisherman's and a smith's and a schoolmaster's widow's, and the other half held that a hundred voices arguing at once would carry no further than the harbour wall and be no more use than the gulls.

It was in the middle of this second argument, with the light going amber through the one window and a woman near the door saying for the third time that she had not walked nine miles to listen to men quarrel about who should do the talking, that somebody said Hale's name.

He did not afterward remember who said it first. It went round the room the way a bucket goes hand to hand along a fire-line, picked up by whoever's hand was nearest and passed on without much thought given to it, a name is offered, another voice takes it up because it is easier to agree than to argue further, and by the time it had gone twice round the low room it had stopped being a suggestion and become, without any vote taken or asked for, a fact that the room seemed to have arrived at together. Hale had kept a slate; Hale had kept a book of the hungry year's dead, though few enough of them yet knew how thorough a book; Hale had once been paid, under the old administration, to write things down in a hand that held up in a court of law, which was more than any other soul in the room could claim. The bucket stopped with him.

"I'm no speaker," he said, which was true, and which nobody in the room seemed inclined to hold against him.

"You needn't speak," said the woman by the door, whose name was Seonag and who had lost, though Hale did not know it yet, a sister at Doire ghlas that winter. "You need only write down what the rest of us say, so it's said once and stays said."

It was a man further back, one of the pursers the guard had walked north with the rest, who put the harder question into words. "We've no standing," he said, loud enough to carry to the doorway and the backs pressed against it. "Not in their law. They needn't hear a word of it, whatever we write or don't."

"Then we'll say it where we can hear it ourselves," Hale said.

He had not planned to say it. It came out of him the way the count of the graves at Doire ghlas had come out of him in the church porch that spring, not chosen so much as arrived at, a thing his own mouth said before the rest of him had finished deciding whether he believed it. But once said it could not be unsaid, and the room, which a moment before had been on the edge of deciding the whole meeting was a waste of a fair day's light, went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when it has just heard the one sentence it did not know it was waiting for.

He went to the trestle at the front, where somebody had set out an inkhorn and a stack of paper meant for nothing more than tallying the fair day's trade, and he sat down on the bench behind it, and he opened, because it was the only book to hand, a ledger that a Caol mhòr chandler had brought along half-hoping to sell blank stock to whoever needed it. It had nothing written in it. Hale wetted the pen, and, because a clerk's hand does the thing a clerk's mind has already decided before the mind has finished deciding it, wrote the day at the top of the first leaf in the small, upright script that Ros bheag had trained into him twenty years before, and under the day, because a minute needs a heading and he had been keeping one kind of book or another for three months now and had a feel for what a heading ought to say, he wrote: *A meeting of the households of the north, at Caol mhòr.*

"There," he said. "It's written now."

There was no ceremony to it beyond that, no vote taken on whether the thing they had just made should have a name, and for the whole of that first afternoon nobody troubled to give it one. It was some weeks before anyone began calling it the Moot, a word one of the pursers remembered from an old book of the humans' law, half meaning it as a joke against themselves and half not, and the name stuck the way names stick that fit a thing better than the thing's makers had planned for. It had no standing in Dia-thìreach law. No statute recognised it, no seal was cut for it, no clerk south of the guard-line had ever heard the word spoken. It met all the same, in the long room and then, when the long room proved too small, in a barn lent for the purpose by a Caol mhòr net-maker who asked nothing for the use of it beyond a promise that the floor would be swept after. It kept minutes from that first wet afternoon onward, in Hale's hand and then, as the years went on, in other hands after his, and the minutes ran unbroken, that first ledger and the books that followed it, from that day to this.

## II. Of the Book with Nothing in It

The Moot's first business, once the matter of a name had been set aside as not worth the afternoon it would have cost, was the winter.

It did not take long to agree what the winter would need, because the whole room had lived through the last one and none of them wished to live through its like again unwarned. Grain, first, and more of it laid by earlier than the last time, since the stores that had come over the water in other years came no longer and would not be coming this one either, whatever the councillors at Cathair dhearg might privately believe about a harvest that had not yet been cut. A guard kept on the roads, against no enemy anyone could name, only because a household walking alone with a cart of oats on a bad night was safer for having somebody else's eyes on the road with it, and the memory of what had happened to nobody-knew-how-many households on the road to Doire ghlas the winter before sat close enough under the surface of that meeting that nobody needed to say it outright for everyone to understand it was being said.

And the dead of Doire ghlas, though the dying lay a season behind them by then, went into the minutes too, because Hale stood and told the room, plainly, in the flat clerk's voice he used for anything he did not trust himself to say any other way, what he had counted on the rise above the town and what the names pinned in the church porch had since told him, and asked whether the Moot, being now a thing that existed and could vote on matters, should not vote that the count be kept as the north's own record, since no one south of the guard-stone had asked to keep it and none, so far as anyone in the room could tell, so much as knew there was a count to be kept. The vote, when it was taken, by a show of hands that Hale counted twice to be sure of the number, as though the habit of counting things twice had become, for him, something closer to a creed than a caution, carried without a voice raised against it.

The granary took longer to settle than the counting had, because a granary meant not only agreeing that grain should be laid by but agreeing whose grain it would be and on whose word it would be shared out when the laying-by season turned to the eating one, and a room full of householders who had spent a hungry year learning exactly how much a neighbour's honesty was worth in a bad month was in no hurry to hand that question over lightly. It was settled in the end, after the better part of another hour's argument, on terms near enough to what Caol mhòr's own fishermen had already worked out for themselves with the catch, unspoken and unminuted, the autumn before: each household to give what it could spare into the common store, in proportion to what it had, and the store to be kept under lock in the harbour-house cellar with three keys held by three householders chosen for the keeping and answerable to the Moot for every sack that left it. Peg Carrick's father was one of the three, being a man the whole harbour street trusted with a scale and a slate both, and a smith from Doire ghlas the second, and the third, after some argument, a widow named Mòrag Tulloch who had buried two children the winter before and whom nobody in the room, having heard that, was minded to argue against.

Hale wrote it all down as it was settled, article by article, in the hand that would come, in time, to be recognised the length of the north as the Moot's own, a hand that grew no more elaborate with the years but only surer, the letters set a fraction closer together as though the writer had learned, sentence by sentence and winter by winter, that paper was dearer than time and both were dearer than either had seemed to him on that first wet afternoon in the long room. By the time the light had gone from amber to grey in the one window and somebody had thought to light a lamp, the ledger meant for a fair day's tallies held four pages of minutes where that morning it had held none, and the room, tired now in the particular way a room is tired after it has done a thing it did not know, walking in, that it had come to do, began to empty into the wet evening a household at a time.

Hale stayed longest, blotting the last page by lamplight with a scrap of blanket someone had left behind, and it was only then, alone at the trestle with the door standing open on the harbour street and the smell of the sea coming in cold off the water, that the size of what the afternoon had made began to settle on him properly. He had written the day at the top of a chandler's stock ledger because it was the only book to hand and because a habit three months old in a private book had by then worn into something closer to instinct than a choice made anew each time. He had not, sitting down at the trestle, thought of it as making anything at all, only as doing the one thing he knew how to do in a room that had asked more of him than he had expected. It was only afterward, walking home along the harbour street with the ledger under his coat against the damp, that he understood he had not merely taken minutes of an afternoon's argument. He had opened a book that the north had needed opening for a year and more and had not, until that afternoon, known it needed, and the book, once opened, was not the kind that closed again.

## III. Of the Letter South

The granary filled slower than the Moot had hoped and faster than Hale, privately, had feared, and by the second week of an t-Sultain there was grain enough under Peg Carrick's father's lock to make the winter a different proposition from the one before it, though nobody in the north was fool enough to call it plenty.

It was at the Moot's third sitting, with the barn swept and the harvest mostly in and the evenings drawing down early enough that they met now by lamplight from the start, that the question of the letter came up. Somebody, it might have been Seonag, who had taken to sitting near the trestle at every meeting since the first as though she had appointed herself to watch that the writing stayed honest, asked plainly what use it was, keeping so careful a book of grain laid by and dead counted and guards posted, if nobody south of the guard-stone ever came to know a word of it. The council at Cathair dhearg governed the whole island, by its own reckoning; if it did not know the north kept a granary, or a count of its winter dead, or a book at all, then whatever the Moot wrote might as well, for all the good it did beyond Caol mhòr's own harbour wall, have been written on water.

Hale had turned the same thought over more than once himself, in the quiet after each meeting, and had come to no answer he much liked. But the room wanted a letter, and a room that had made him its clerk without asking whether he wanted the office was not one he felt he had much standing to refuse, so a letter was drafted, in his hand, read aloud twice over for the room's approval and amended twice more before it satisfied anybody, and copied fair onto the best paper the chandler could be persuaded to part with.

It said, in the end, less than Hale would have liked and more than some in the room thought wise. It told of the households met at Caol mhòr and the book they had opened, and, in four careful lines that Hale rewrote three times before the room would let them stand, of a winter the year before in which the north had buried its own dead without troubling the south for help it had learned not to expect, and asked, without accusation, only as a plain matter of fact laid before whoever in the south might trouble to read it, that the council take note that such a winter had been, and such a book now existed of it. Nothing more than that was asked. Several in the room had wanted more, a plea for grain, a plea for a seat at whatever table the council kept its business on, and Hale, who had spent twenty years under an administration that answered every plea with either silence or a form, argued them down to the four lines on the grounds that a letter asking for nothing could not be refused, only ignored, and a letter ignored still stood, in a file somewhere, as proof that it had been sent.

The letter went south in the last week of an t-Sultain, carried by a carter named Robert Carrick, Peg's younger brother, a boy of fifteen who had never been further from Caol mhòr than Doire ghlas and who set off down the coast road with the letter sewn into the lining of his coat, on the theory, which was Ellen's and which Hale had not argued with, that a boy robbed on the road would lose his purse before he lost his coat. He carried, besides the letter, a week's bread and a small store of the Moot's coin against the tolls the humans' old roads still charged where some town had troubled to keep them up, and he was gone the better part of a fortnight, longer than the distance alone should have taken, because the road south of Doire ghlas was a road the boy did not know and he lost the better part of a day twice over finding it again.

He came back at last on a wet evening in the first week of an Dàmhair with an empty satchel and very little to report beyond the fact that the letter had been handed, at the harbour-house in Cathair dhearg, to a clerk who read the first line, looked at the boy for a long moment as though trying to decide what manner of thing had been put in front of him, and told him it would be seen to. Robert Carrick had asked, because Ellen had told him to ask and he had promised her he would, whether there would be any word sent back. The clerk had said there would be, in time, and had not looked at him again.

Hale wrote that, too, into the minutes, in the same flat hand he used for grain and for graves: that the letter had been delivered and received, and that no answer had come with the boy who carried it, and that none was expected soon. It was the first entry of its kind in the Moot's book, though it would not be the last, and Hale, reading it back to the room at the next sitting, found himself thinking, though he did not say it aloud, that a letter received and not yet answered was at least a letter received, which was more certainty than the north had owned of the south's attention a season before, and less than he had privately hoped for when he set Robert Carrick on the road.

## IV. Of the Twelve at Cathair dhearg

The letter, when it reached Cathair dhearg, reached it into a house that was only a month old itself.

Ailean mac Iain had not expected to be in the room when it was read. He had turned twenty-six that summer, a hill-born clerk with a tally-clerk's habits and no family in the capital to speak for him, and it was more by the accident of who had been standing nearest the council chamber's door on the morning the roll of under-clerks was read than by any design of his own that he found himself, on the twenty-third of an t-Sultain, sworn alongside a dozen others into the newest of the state's offices, with his hand on the same worn custody-book the custodians used for oaths of less consequence and a councillor he did not yet know by name reading out the form of words in a voice that suggested he had read it too many times that morning already to trouble himself with the weight of it.

He was sworn beside a woman a few years his senior named Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, a council clerk's daughter from the capital who had, by her own account, wanted the office since she was old enough to understand what a minute-book was for, and who stood through the swearing with a stillness Ailean found himself envying, since his own hands would not keep from working at the seam of his coat the whole time the words were said over him. She caught him at it afterward, in the corridor outside the chamber while the twelve councillors were still being seated within, and said, not unkindly, that a clerk who fidgeted through his own oath would fidget through worse, and that he had best learn to sit on his hands before the council learned to notice them. He liked her for it, in the plain, immediate way a man likes someone who has told him a true thing without troubling to soften it, and the liking lasted the whole of the years they afterward worked in the same office, though neither of them, on that first morning, had cause to know it would.

The council was seated that same afternoon, twelve chairs set round a table too large for the room that held it, drawn, as the proclamation naming them had said, from the custodian families that had kept the island's old law through the humans' years and from the captains who had led the companies of the Severance war. Ailean knew two of the twelve by sight before the day was out: Fearchar mac Raghnaill, the captain whose plea that his company's roll not be burned Ailean had himself carried into the council's chests the summer before, and a custodian woman named Beathag nic Artair, whose family had kept the old rites at Cathair dhearg since before the humans came and who sat through the whole of the seating with the particular patience of someone who has waited a long time for a thing and does not intend to let it show. The other ten he learned in the weeks that followed, among them Griogair mac Tormoid, a custodian of the western shore, and Cailean mac Sheumais, who had commanded a company at the fords of the western hills and spoke, when he spoke at all, in the clipped, unhurried way of a man used to being obeyed without needing to raise his voice for it.

Their minute-books were opened that same afternoon, a matched pair of ledgers bound in the capital's own leather and lettered in gold on the spine, a considerably finer thing than the chandler's stock-ledger that had opened the north's book a month before, though Ailean, watching the first councillor's minute set down in a clerk's careful copperplate, found himself thinking, and did not say so to anyone, that fine binding was no guarantee a book would be read any more carefully for it.

It was into the third week of the council's sitting, once the business of shires and seals had settled into something like a routine, that the letter from the north came before them. It was read aloud, as every petition that reached the council chamber was read aloud, by the clerk whose office it was to read such things, in a voice that gave the four careful lines no more weight than it had given the tariff schedule read just before it. The room took it in the way a room takes in something it has not been asked to think about before and does not much wish to start now: with a general stillness, a shuffling of papers that was not quite inattention and not quite attention either, and, from one of the twelve, a question.

"What," said Cailean mac Sheumais, into the small silence that followed the reading, "is a Moot?"

Nobody in the chamber answered him with any certainty, since nobody there had heard the word before that morning, and the clerk who had read the letter aloud, when pressed, could offer only that it appeared to be some manner of gathering the northern households held among their own houses, though on whose authority or to what settled purpose he could not say. Fearchar mac Raghnaill, who alone among the twelve had walked the north roads himself, in the winter the companies stood down, said only that he had heard the word once, from a harbour clerk at Caol mhòr, and thought no more of it at the time than a man thinks of any word he does not expect to need again. Beathag nic Artair asked whether the letter required an answer, and was told, by the same clerk, that nothing in it asked for one in so many words, whereupon the council, having no further question to put to a document that asked nothing and explained no more of itself than its own four lines, moved the letter to be filed, and the next matter on the day's roll was called.

Ailean, sitting at the clerks' table with the council's own minute-book open before him and Gormshuil's pen scratching steadily at his elbow, wrote the disposal down as he had been taught to write every disposal down, in the plain unadorned hand the office required: *Received and filed.* He thought, writing it, of the winter before, of the count he had heard was being kept somewhere north of the guard-stone, though he had never seen it and knew no more of it than the letter itself now told him, and of the plain fact, which the four lines had stated without complaint and the council had received without comment, that a whole country's worth of hunger and burial had just passed through the chamber in the time it took to read a paragraph and had left behind it nothing more than three words in a minute-book. He did not think, writing it, that he was doing anything remarkable. He was doing what the office required of him, in the hand the office required, and it would be some years yet before he came to understand that a clerk's three words, set down without comment in a book that outlived every hand that wrote in it, could carry, for whoever read them afterward, a weight the clerk himself had never meant to give them.

The light was long gone from the chamber's high windows by the time the day's roll was finished and the councillors rose from the table, and Ailean stayed, as the newest and least senior of the clerks, to see the ink dried and the books put away, the same office that fell, thirty-odd miles north and a month earlier, to Hale in his barn with a lamp and a borrowed scrap of blanket. He closed the council's minute-book last of all, the gold-lettered spine catching what little lamplight remained in the room, and set it in its place on the shelf built for it, beside eleven others no fuller and no emptier than it was.

Two books had been opened that autumn, a month apart and thirty-odd miles between them, each by a clerk who had not chosen the office so much as had it settle on him in the course of an ordinary afternoon, each in a hand that would be read by people neither clerk could yet imagine. Neither book knew of the other's existence that first night, and neither Ailean nor Hale, closing his own book by his own lamp in his own town, had cause to think of the other man at all. But both books were shut, that night, for the same reason and in the same kind of dark: because the day's writing was done, and because a book, once opened, has to be closed again before the next day's can begin, whatever it is that the day has found worth setting down.
