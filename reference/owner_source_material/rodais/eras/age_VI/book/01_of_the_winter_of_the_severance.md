# Of the Winter of the Severance

*Part One: Of the Settling*

*1 am Faoilleach, AE 151 (as memory) – 3 an t-Ògmhios, SE 1*

## I. Of the Last Ships

It is told at Muileann chaol, from the boxed rolls of that winter, how the Age of Strangers ended: not in a battle
won, for the battle had been won already, but in an emptiness where ships had been.

Five beacons stood on the capes in those days, from Baile chrom round to Tobar dhearg, kept against the humans' crossing, and each kept its own
daybook. On the first morning of that year the keepers wrote the same four words, one after another down the coast,
as if the news had to be said five times before anyone could believe it: *Cha tàinig long.* No ship came. It had not
come the morning before either, nor the morning before that, but this was the morning the keepers stopped expecting
one, and a daybook that stops expecting a thing is the surest record there is that the thing has ended.

Over the eastern water, wherever it led, the last of the humans' ships had gone out and not come back for more of
their own. Those left on Dia-thìr, some thousands of them, were put on the roads north under the victors' guard, in
the worst cold of the year, and the columns that had been an army's business all autumn became a walking business of
old men, women, children and the sick, strung out between the escorts on foot. Of the day itself little is told
beyond the counting, for a column on the move makes a poor subject for a tale, and a hungry one makes a worse one.
It is remembered only that the mud on the north roads that winter was said to be knee-deep the whole way, and that it
was still called so by the old people two generations after, though no one living had walked it.

The victors, for their part, spent that same winter in a kind of astonishment that outlasted their war. They had
fought the humans' administration for years, in the hills and the pit-towns and along
the coast roads, and had grown so used to the fighting that the winning of it left them, for a season, with less to
do than they had ever had before. The captains who had led companies through the last hard campaigns found
themselves that am Faoilleach standing in doorways at Cathair dhearg with nothing pressing to command, while the
council's few surviving clerks tried to make sense of an administration's worth of papers left behind by men who had
sailed away rather than burn them. It is said that for the first fortnight after the last ship went out, no order of
any weight was issued from the council at all, since no one among them could agree who now held the standing to
issue one.

That was the Severance, by the name it wears in every book since. Of what it cost the two peoples who lived through
it, this book has little new to add: the columns that went north, the count kept badly or not at all of who did not
finish the road, the emptiness the south felt at first as victory and only slowly, over years, as something else. It
begins instead with a clerk on a horse, going the same road the columns had gone, three months behind them, with a
satchel of blank paper and a tally to keep, sent because the council, having no better idea what a peace required of
it, had decided at least to count.

## II. Of the Guard at the Heads of the Roads

Ailean mac Iain was twenty-five that spring, a hill-born man of the council's service, named, as many of his
generation were, for a king no one now living had seen crowned. He had spent the war years copying muster-rolls in a
tent behind the lines, and he had never yet ridden further north than the market at Baile Mòr mhòr. When the council
sent its guard up to stand at the heads of the north roads, where the columns had gone in winter and where it now
meant to keep the ones still left from wandering back south, Ailean rode with them as tally-clerk, to count what
passed and write it down.

He found the work simpler than he had feared and duller than he had hoped. A guard-post was set at each of the roads
that ran north out of the settled country, a captain and a dozen men at each, and Ailean's business was to ride
between them with a book, entering what the guard reported: how many had come through going north that week, how many
turned back, how many were sick past walking and left to be carried by their own people. In three weeks he filled
eleven pages and crossed out nothing, for the council had told him plainly that a clean page was worth more to them
than a fast one.

The road itself taught him more than the tally did. He rode it in the last hard weeks of winter, when the hedges
were black and the burns ran the colour of strong tea, past farms whose byres stood empty because their stock had
gone to feed the war and their hands had gone with the columns or with the companies, one direction or the other. At
one such farm, a half-day short of the first guard-post, an old woman came out to the gate to ask him, in Dia-thìris
too plain and too quick for a hill man's ear, whether the boy who had once kept her sheep would be let come back for
them now the fighting was done. Ailean told her, truthfully, that he did not know, and wrote nothing of the
exchange in his book, since it fitted none of its columns. He remembered it all the same, years after he had
forgotten most of what the columns held.

At the guard-posts themselves the captains kept him at arm's length at first, taking him for one more of the
council's young men come to watch them rather than to help them, until they saw that his count matched theirs to the
man, week on week, and stopped troubling to check it. One captain, an old campaigner named Griogair who had lost two
fingers to a humans' picket some ten years before the Severance, told him over a fire one night that a line held by
soldiers was no line at all, only a line waiting for the soldiers to be needed elsewhere.

"Write that in your book too," Griogair said, "if your council wants the truth of it as well as the count."

Ailean did not write it in the book the council had given him. He wrote it, that once, in the back pages he had
begun keeping for himself, where a fact that would not sit easily in a column could still be set down in its own
words. It was the second such page, after the one on the empty road, and neither of them was ever asked for by
anyone above him.

At the post on the Caol mhòr road he watched the last of the columns go through: forty-one souls, by his count, two
of them dead before evening and buried where the guard let them dig, the rest walked on into the low hills with
nothing behind them but the wagon-ruts freezing over. He wrote the number in his book and, beneath it, a line the
council had not asked for: *and the road behind them empty after.* His captain read it over his shoulder, said
nothing, and let it stand. It is the first sentence of Ailean mac Iain's that survives in his own hand, and it says,
in its way, everything the tally could not.

The guard he rode with was not there to keep the columns moving. By the time Ailean reached the last post, in the
thin sun of early spring, the moving was long done, and the guard's business had become a different one: to see that
the line held, now that it had been drawn. He wrote as much to the council in his weekly letter, and got back a
single line telling him to keep counting.

## III. Of Caol mhòr and the Two Words

At Caol mhòr, on the twenty-second day of am Màrt, the council's word on the north was read aloud from the steps of
the old harbour-house, to whoever of the town would come and stand for it. Ailean had ridden down from his post two
days before at the guard captain's word that something was to be read, and stood at the back of the crowd with his
satchel, meaning only to note the numbers of who attended.

It was a wet, grey morning, the kind that makes a crowd of separate people into one shivering animal, and the man
sent to read wore the council's colours and a voice trained for a hall, not a quay. Below the steps the crowd stood
packed between the fish-sheds and the harbour wall, fisher-folk mostly, with a scattering of the guard's own men and
a few Dia-thìrich come up from the south with the columns and never gone home. The tide was out, and the mud-flats
beyond the harbour mouth lay bare and grey under the same colour of sky, so that Ailean, watching from the back, had
the sense of a crowd gathered at the edge of nothing much, to be told what it already half knew.

He read that the homeland of the Tuathaich (for that was already the word growing up for the humans' children born
and to be born on the island) was fixed by the council's law in the north, from the head of the roads to the sea,
and that it would be held there. He read it once through in Dia-thìris, which perhaps a third of the crowd followed,
watching him with the particular stillness of people waiting to be told the same thing again in words they could
use, and then said that the council had also ordered it read in the humans' tongue, for those who had none of the
other.

There was a pause on the steps while someone went to fetch the man who had been found for that. He came up out of the
crowd itself: a lean man in his middle forties, in a harbour clerk's coat gone thin at the elbows, with ink worked so far into
the creases of his knuckles that no washing would ever get it out again. He had been a clerk of the humans' harbour
office at Ros bheag before the crossing closed, and someone at the council's post had remembered it and sent for him
the night before. His name was Walter Hale.

He took the paper, read it through once to himself with his lips moving and no sound coming, and then read it aloud
to the crowd in their own tongue, flat and without warmth, the way a man reads a thing he has been handed rather than
a thing he has made. His voice carried well enough over the harbour, better than the first reader's had, though he
gave it none of the other man's polish; he read it the way a clerk reads a cargo manifest, one item following the
next without pause for effect, and the crowd took it in the same spirit, with no cheer and no protest, only a low
murmur that ran back through the ranks as each heard it from the one in front and passed it on.

Ailean, near enough to hear both readings, followed the English against his memory of the Dia-thìris and found it a
fair rendering, word for word, until the very last clause. Where the council's paper said the homeland was *fixed*
and would be *held*, Hale's English said the land was *settled*, a milder word, one that might have meant no more
than a people finding a place to live, chosen freely, as a family settles a new farm. Ailean had spent enough weeks
by then reading a column of figures against another to know the trick of a small discrepancy, the kind that never
shows in the total and never fails to matter to whoever is on the wrong side of it.

The reading over, the crowd went its ways in the rain, and most of the council's men with it, well pleased that the
thing was done. Ailean stayed by the steps to note the count. Hale was folding the paper to hand it back, and Ailean,
more from a clerk's habit of getting a thing right than from any larger thought, said that he believed the English
had softened the last clause.

Hale looked at him for a moment before he answered, as if deciding whether the question was worth an honest reply.

"Settled," he said, "is what a man does to a place he has chosen. This wasn't chosen." He handed the paper back. "The
word your council used was *held*. That's the truer one. I'd have read it that way if I'd had the choosing of it,
only I hadn't."

He said it without heat, the way a clerk corrects another clerk's figure, and turned to go back into the crowd before
Ailean could answer him at all. Ailean called after him to ask how he came to know the council's own choice of word so
exactly, since the paper he had been handed carried only the English.

Hale stopped and looked back. "I read your tongue as well as mine," he said. "Better than most of your own would
credit a harbour clerk with. I had the whole of it in my head before I opened my mouth, and I chose which of it to
give them." He said no more, and did not wait to be asked why he had chosen as he had. The crowd had already begun to
close round him, some wanting a word repeated, one old man wanting to know only whether the fish market at Doire
ghlas would still be his to trade at, and Hale gave his attention to each in turn, in the plain unhurried way of a
man used to being asked things by people with no one else to ask.

Ailean stood a moment in the rain with the paper's copy still in his satchel, then took it out, and in the margin
beside the clause on the homeland, in his own small hand, wrote the two words Hale had given him: *not settled —
held.* It was not council business, and no one had asked it of him. He never afterward could say clearly why he had
written it, except that it seemed to him, even then, a thing worth having down somewhere, in case no one else ever
thought to write it at all.

That same week the word went round Caol mhòr, and from Caol mhòr down the western roads to Ros bheag, that the humans' own
chapel there, shut since the crossing closed and its priest gone north with the rest, was to be shut for good and its
bell taken down. It was Hale's own town, the one he had clerked its harbour in before the war took the harbour's
trade away, and it was Hale who first told Ailean of it, two days after the reading, coming to find him at the guard
captain's lodging with no more introduction than the paper he still carried.

"You'll want this in whatever it is you keep," Hale said, and told him the bare fact: that the council's men were at
Ros bheag that week with a cart and an order, and that the chapel there, which had stood shut but whole since the
crossing closed, would not stand whole much longer. He did not ask Ailean to do anything about it. He seemed, if
anything, faintly amused that a Dia-thìreach clerk should think it any business of his at all, and said as much,
though without unkindness: that the council's word was the council's word, and a bell was only a bell, and he had
buried enough of his own to know that the loss that mattered was never the one you could put a cart to.

The council's men who did the work gave no reason beyond an order from the south, and took the bell away on a cart
bound, it was said, for wherever in the north the humans' faith might still be kept. Ailean rode the two days down to see it for
himself, more from the clerk's habit of checking a thing reported than from any claim on it, and found the chapel
already stripped: a low stone building with a slate roof gone green, its door standing open on an empty floor swept
bare of even its dust, and the belfry above it holding nothing but a square of paler stone where the bell's cradle
had hung. He met the cart on the road back, three carters and a soldier, the bell wrapped in sacking and roped down
like a prisoner against the cart's jolting, and stood aside to let it pass. He wrote it into his tally as he wrote
everything else that crossed his page that spring: a fact, dated, without comment. It was the only entry in eleven
pages he later wished he had written differently.

## IV. Of Cathair dhearg and the Rolls

He was recalled south in early summer, his counting at the roads' heads judged finished now that the columns had
stopped and the line held itself. He rode into Cathair dhearg on the second of an t-Ògmhios, into a town crowded and
loud after the quiet of the guard-posts, every street full of men newly told they would not be paid again as
soldiers. The market square, which he remembered from before the war as a place of stalls and cattle, was given over
that week to nothing but companies waiting their turn to be stood down, sitting on their packs in the sun with the
particular patience of men who have already fought the hard part of a thing and have only the paperwork of it left.

He found lodging above a cooper's shop near the counting-house, the same room he had kept before he rode north, and
was at his old table again within the day, as if the guard-posts and the wet road and the chapel at Ros bheag had
been someone else's spring entirely. The council's clerks who had stayed south through the winter treated him, at
first, as a man returned from an errand rather than from anything that had changed him, and he found he had no
ready way to tell them otherwise. He kept the margin copy of the Caol mhòr reading folded in the bottom of his
satchel and did not show it to anyone.

The council had ordered the war-companies of the Severance stood down, all of them, the whole force that had held the
island through the last campaign against the humans' administration and seen it end. It was thought a simple order
to carry out: muster each company once more, read the council's word, take back what arms belonged to the council
and not the man, and let the rest go home. Ailean was set to the muster in the old drill-yard behind the counting-
house, with three other clerks and a table each, taking down names as the companies came through and were released.

It went as such things go, quickly and without much feeling, until the company of Fearchar mac Raghnaill came to
Ailean's table.

Fearchar was a broad, grey man who had held that command since before Ailean was old enough to hold a pen, and his
company, hill men mostly from the same country Ailean's own family came from, stood in ranks behind him in
silence while he asked one thing only. He did not ask for pay, though none of his men had seen a full month's since spring,
and he did not ask for arms, though half the company owned nothing else worth the name. He asked that the roll of
the company's names not be burned with the loose paper of the muster, as the clerk beside Ailean had already begun
doing with the smaller companies to clear the yard faster.

"We fought under that roll," Fearchar said. "Every name on it stood where it says he stood. I'll not have it go on a
fire because it takes less room in your chests than keeping it does."

The clerk beside Ailean, who had burned four already that morning and saw no reason to stop, said that the council
had asked for the arms counted and the men sent home, and said nothing about a paper's afterlife. Fearchar did not
argue further. He simply stood, and his company stood behind him, until someone with authority to answer him could be
found.

It was Ailean, having no authority at all but a table nearer the question than most, who told him to wait, and went
himself to the master of the chests with the roll under his arm. He came back with leave: the rolls of the disbanded
companies were to be kept, not burned, boxed and sealed with the rest of the council's papers from the war. No one
troubled to explain why one captain's asking should decide it for every company after him, and Fearchar mac Raghnaill
never learned that his had been the first roll spared. He took his men home believing only that the council had, this
once, thought better of its own haste.

Ailean spent the rest of that week and the one after carrying rolls, sixty-one of them by the last count, down into
the vaulted rooms under the counting-house where the council kept what it meant to keep. It was slow, dusty work, and
no one had thought to make a record of what went into the chests beyond the chests' own numbers scratched on the
lids. Ailean, who by then had spent a whole spring learning that a thing not written down might as well not have
happened, began on his own account a list: which roll, which company, which chest, in what order laid. He filled
four sheets before he was done, and signed the last of them, though nobody had asked him to sign anything.

## V. Of the Chests Locked

On the third of an t-Ògmhios the last chest went down the stair and the mason set to sealing the door of the vault
with a course of new brick, as the council had ordered for all its war papers, meaning the seal to stand until
someone in some later year had reason to break it. Ailean stood at the bottom of the stair with his four sheets while
the brick was laid, the master of the chests beside him with a lamp that threw both their shadows long across the wet
floor.

"You've written more there than the roll asked for," the master said, nodding at the sheets.

"Someone ought to be able to say what's down here, and why, without having to break the wall to find out," Ailean
answered.

The master shrugged, took the sheets, glanced them over by the lamp, and signed his own name below Ailean's on the
last one, more from courtesy than conviction. He carried them up to be filed with the day's other business, and the
door was bricked shut behind them before evening.

Ailean walked back to his lodging that night by the long way, past the river quays where two of the council's boats lay
idle for want of anything left to carry, and took out the margin copy he still carried of the council's word from
Caol mhòr. He read again the two words he had written there in the rain, months before: *not settled — held.* He had
carried the clerk's copy so long by then, folded so many times, that the ink had begun to blur along the crease, and
he thought, not for the first time, that a word kept only in one man's pocket was not much better kept than a roll
left to burn.

He did not yet know what he would do about it, only that he meant to do something, some day, with a great many words
like it, once he had a chest of his own and no one to ask his leave. That evening he wrote nothing more. He put the
paper away, banked what fire he had, and let the town's noise carry him to sleep, the last of the Severance behind
him and the first full year of the restored country's peace not yet a fortnight old.

So closed the first summer after the Severance, with the columns settled that were never asked whether they wished
to be, a captain's roll spared by a clerk with no right to spare it, and a list of chests signed by two men neither
of whom the council had told to keep one.
