# Of the One Sheet

*SE 55 an Dùbhlachd – SE 57 an t-Iuchar*

> Duilleag na Fèithe, the sheet of the vein, held on one leaf what forty years of the Board's own files had kept apart across two towns and four presses: that the mountain had one working left in it, and that the mountain had known this a long while before the kingdom troubled to ask.
>
> — the Library's own note on the map, entered at Muileann chaol

## I. Of the Fair's Northern Day

It is told at Muileann chaol that the court of the fair at Muileann òg, in the cold week before the turn of the fifty-fifth year of the Severance, gave over one whole day of its ten to goods carried down out of the northern shires, the first day in all the fair's history set aside so, and that the wardens who cried it from the fair's own steps did so in the flat voice men use for a thing they have not yet decided whether to be proud of. The goods still went to Dia-thìreach factors, as the court of the fair had ruled thirty years before and had never troubled to unrule; a northern weaver's cloth was still weighed, priced and sold by a southern hand at a southern table. But the day was named, and the stalls it filled stood in a row of their own along the fair's eastern wall, and over each stall, for the first time in the fair's keeping, hung a maker's own mark and not only the factor's.

Daniel Frayne of Muileann leathan stood that morning at the head of the row, the Moot's chair four months old on him and sitting no more easily than a coat bought for a taller man, and watched the first cloth of the day go up on its pole: a grey twill of Doire ghlas, plain wool dyed with the shire's own bark, and along its selvedge a single thread of undyed white running the length of the bolt, so fine a stranger might have taken it for a flaw in the weave before he took it for a mark. The weavers of Doire ghlas called it An Snàthainn Geal, the white thread, and had woven it into their own cloth for three years without asking the fair's leave, selling it under a factor's stamp as they sold everything else, until the court's new rule let them sell it, for the first time, under their own. The factor who had carried Doire ghlas cloth to that fair for eleven years stood by the pole with his ledger open and his face giving away nothing either way, and when a buyer from Cathair mhòr asked him what the thread meant, he said only that it was the shire's own habit and had never lowered the price of a bolt, which was, in its way, the truest thing said at the fair that day about what the day was worth.

Frayne walked the row twice before the light went, the factors' bell being rung early against the short afternoon, and stopped longest at the Baile chiar stall, where the southern weavers, who had sold cloth at that fair since before the row existed, had begun that same season to weave a thread of their own into the selvedge of their finer bolts, a pale grey scarcely darker than the wool, the mark being spoken for already at Doire ghlas, so that a buyer who did not know to look for it would carry the cloth home none the wiser. He asked the Baile chiar weaver, an old woman who had been at the loom since before the first Còmhrag, why her house troubled to copy a northern habit it need not have noticed at all, and she told him that a mark worth having was a mark worth taking, wherever it began, and that she had woven for forty years without her own name on a bolt and did not propose to die without it. Frayne wrote nothing of the exchange in the Moot's own book that night, there being no column in it for a compliment paid sideways by a stranger's loom, but he told Mary Coyle of it the next time he rode to Caol mhòr, and she said only that a thread the south troubled to steal was worth more, in the end, than a thread the south only bought.

The frost came down hard that afternoon, as it does most years at Muileann òg once the light begins to fail, and the wardens who had cried the northern day from the steps that morning were the same men who walked the row at dusk telling stallholders to bank their braziers and mind the canvas, the ceremony of the morning having given way, as every fair's ceremony does by nightfall, to the ordinary business of getting a hundred stalls safely through a cold night. Frayne stayed later than most, walking the row a third time with a lantern of his own after the buyers had gone, and found the Doire ghlas weaver's daughter, a girl of perhaps fifteen sent to mind the stall while her mother slept in the cart, sewing by the lantern's own light a second length of the white thread into a bolt not yet sold, working it in slow and even though nobody stood by to buy it that night or to see it done. He asked her why she troubled with it in the dark and the cold when the day's trade was over, and she told him, not looking up from the needle, that her mother had taught her the thread went in whether a buyer was watching or not, since a mark sewn only for show was no mark at all, only a better sort of lie. He carried that answer home with him too, and told it to no one, judging it a thing better kept than spent.

The court's minute of that day, four lines in a clerk's small hand, records the northern day kept, the goods sold, and the dues taken, and nothing of the thread at all. It is the Library's own habit, and not the court's, to keep such things; the sheet the fair's clerk filed that winter and the roll the Library keeps of it agree in every figure and disagree, entirely, in what either thought worth the keeping.

## II. Of the One Sheet

Catrìona nic Neacail was seven-and-thirty in the year the fair kept its first northern day, a surveyor of the Board sixteen years standing, chain-hand no longer but not yet the thing the Board would shortly make her, and she spent that whole winter and the better part of the following spring doing a piece of work nobody at Cathair mhòr had asked her for in so many words, though every surveyor on the Board's roll had known for years it wanted doing and none had troubled to do it entire. She meant to draw, on one leaf if the leaf would hold it, every stretch of the vein the Board's surveys called unworked: not the search's forty-one dry holes between Dùn dhearg and Baile ruadh, which were another matter and a closed one, but the true seam that the Sloc Mòr and its daughter workings had followed for six ages, wherever any surveyor's chain had ever laid a line across ground still unopened.

She began with her own field-books, which by then ran to eleven, and worked outward through the Board's own rolls a shire at a time, riding where the rolls disagreed with each other or with the ground beneath them, which happened oftener than the Board's own clerks liked to be told. The riding took her that winter into weather no surveyor chose for the work if the choosing were left to him, frost in the saddle-leather most mornings and the short daylight gone before a chain could be properly laid twice over the same ground, and more than once she and whichever surveyor rode with her that week were glad of a shepherd's bothy or a maor's own kitchen fire to see out a night the road would not have forgiven them.

A surveyor named Griogair mac Aonghais, junior to her by six years and lent her for the summer's riding, asked her once, the two of them sat by a fire on the moor above Muileann dhearg with the day's chain-work done and the light going, what she expected the finished sheet to show that every surveyor on the roll did not already know in his own gut, and she told him that a thing known in the gut and a thing drawn on a sheet were not the same fact, whatever a tired man on a cold hill might wish to believe of them, and that she meant the Board to have the second kind whether it wanted it or not. Griogair said, not unkindly, that a sheet was still only ink, and ink had never yet mined a pound of coal nor left a pound more in the ground than was there to begin with; she agreed with him, and told him that she had not undertaken the drawing to change what was in the ground, only to change how long the Board could go on not knowing it, which she judged, whatever ink's other failings, a thing ink was entirely equal to.

The drawing, when it was finished in the early summer, fitted on one sheet the size of a door, every stretch of the true vein still unopened marked in a red the ink-maker had mixed for her specially, since no ink already on the Board's shelves would hold its colour against handling. It was smaller than she had let herself believe it would be while she was still drawing it a shire at a time, small enough that a man could stand at the Board's own long table and take in the whole of what remained with one turn of his head, where the same fact spread across forty separate rolls in forty separate hands had never once been taken in whole by anyone, herself included, until the ink was dry. She called it, in her own record and then in the Board's, Duilleag na Fèithe, the sheet of the vein, and she published it that summer in the plainest way the Board knew how to publish anything, which was to hang it on the wall of its own chamber at Cathair mhòr where every surveyor, clerk and concession-holder who came through the door could read it for himself.

Within the month of its hanging, the sheet had done more to the Board's own chamber than any minute the Board had passed in years. Concession-holders who had come in on ordinary business, a licence to renew or a boundary to dispute, took to stopping before it on their way to the clerk's own table, some counting the red stretches with a finger held an inch from the paper as though the counting might yet change the sum, others asking whichever clerk was nearest what the sheet meant and receiving, more often than not, an answer no clearer than the question. A concession-holder of the western galleries, whose family had worked the same three shafts for four generations, stood before it the better part of an hour one wet forenoon and then asked to see the surveyor who had drawn it, and when Catrìona was fetched down from the upper room where she kept her own field-books, he asked her plainly whether the red she had used for his own family's ground meant ten years or thirty, since he had daughters to settle and would rather know now than be told later that the Board had known all along and had simply never troubled to colour it in. She told him she could not put a year to it without walking his own ground again herself, which she offered to do within the season, and he thanked her for an answer that gave him nothing certain but was, he said, the first honest nothing the Board had ever offered him.

The Board named her surveyor-general before the leaves turned that same year, the office having stood empty three years since the last man who held it retired to his daughter's farm and was not troubled to fill it again until the sheet on the wall made plain that somebody ought to be answerable for the whole of what it showed. She was eight-and-thirty that autumn, and the clerk who read her the Board's own minute of the appointment, a young man new enough to his desk to still find such things worth remarking on, told her it was the youngest the office had been filled in living memory. She said only that the office had waited three years for want of anyone troubling to ask what it was for, and that she did not propose, now she held it, to let it wait so idle again.

## III. Of the Press at Cathair mhòr

A sheet the size of a door, once it is hung on a wall, wants explaining in a way a shire's own separate rolls, scattered across forty presses, never troubled anybody to explain; a fact spread thin is a fact nobody has to answer for, and a fact drawn whole is a fact somebody will shortly be asked how long it has been true. Catrìona knew this before the first concession-holder asked her the question outright, standing before the sheet with his own family's share of the vein marked in red not four miles from where his grandfather had first opened it, and asking her, in a voice that wanted an answer and not a courtesy, how long the Board had known the ground he stood on would not last his own grandson's lifetime.

She did not have the answer, and told him so plainly, which he did not thank her for; and she went down, the week following, into the Board's own files at Cathair mhòr to find it, a room in the cellar of the house the Board had taken from the house of Mac Ùisdein some years before, where every survey the Board had received since the rule that kept them there and not at the Library was pressed flat in bundles by year and by district, none of it catalogued past the bundle's own outer label, and most of the labels themselves faded past reading by a lamp that gave, on its best evening, light enough to read one line in three.

It took her the better part of a week, by that same smoking lamp, working through bundle after bundle with a clerk's boy to fetch and carry, a lad of twelve named Ruairidh who had been sent down to the cellar so often on other men's errands that he knew the presses better than any surveyor on the Board's own roll, and who told her, the second evening, that she was the first person in his own three years of fetching who had ever asked him to read a label aloud rather than simply hand the bundle across. The cellar gave up its own kind of cold that no fire lit at the top of the stair ever quite reached, damp in the stone and a smell of old paper and mouse that neither of them remarked on past the first evening, and she kept, through all five days, a tally of her own in the back of her latest field-book, a line for every bundle opened and a word for what it held, much as she had once kept a tally of shafts sunk and shafts dry on a moor two hundred miles east of that same cellar.

She found what she had expected to find in the main, surveys thinning steadily in what they reported the further east and south she read, the language of each one's own year a little more careful than the last about saying outright what the figures already said plainly, a caution she recognised, by the third day, as the particular caution of a clerk who has learned that a plain word costs him more than a careful one and a plainer fact none of his own concern. Twice that week she thought she had found the roll her instinct told her must exist somewhere in that cellar, and twice the date proved wrong or the hand too recent, and she set each aside with a small, private disappointment she did not trouble to name to the boy, until, near the bottom of a press she had all but finished with that week, her hand closed on a roll bound with a cord gone stiff with age and a knot nobody had troubled to untie in longer than the cord's own stiffness argued.

She untied it at the table, by the lamp's worst light of the whole week, and unrolled a survey of the Sloc Mòr's own faces, drawn in a hand she did not know, the vellum stiffened and the ink browned to the same tea-dark colour age gives every ink in the end, her own red not four feet away on the wall above, however brightly it still held its colour that morning. It showed the vein thinning to the east and to the south in terms that matched her own sheet stroke for stroke, in ground she herself had walked and marked red not eighteen months before, and it was dated, in the same unknown hand, thirty years before the year she sat reading it.

She read it through twice before she let herself do the arithmetic the date asked of her, and then a third time, more slowly, for the note pinned to its corner in a second hand, a Board clerk's, dry and brief: *received, filed*. Nothing more. No minute of a decision taken or a decision deferred, no record of who had read it, or whether anyone had, before it went into the press to wait thirty years for a lamp bright enough and a hand patient enough to find it again.

Ruairidh, watching her sit motionless over the roll longer than she had sat over any of the week's other finds, asked her at last what it was, and she told him only that it was proof somebody had already asked the question she had spent a year asking again, and that he need not trouble to fetch her anything further that evening, since whatever the rest of the cellar held, it would not now hold anything to match what her own two hands already did. Rather than leave the roll to be sent up with the rest, she carried it up the stair herself, and sat a long while that night at the Board's own long table, beneath her own sheet on the wall, reading the two surveys side by side by a better lamp than the cellar had given her, hers in red and the stranger's in ink gone the colour of tea, the same ground marked out in the same failing terms thirty years apart, and found, when she had finished the comparing, that she could not have said which of the two surveys troubled her more: the one that had found the truth, or the one that had been believed too late to matter.

## IV. Of Muileann chiar

She sent the Library a copy of the survey that same week, with a note of her own giving the day the Board's own bundle showed it had first been received, and then, rather than wait on the Library's answer, rode east herself, the road she had ridden a hundred times on the Board's ordinary business now carrying a different weight than any of those hundred rides had, toward Muileann chiar, where the Board's own oldest rolls still gave the surveyor of record for the district thirty years back as one Iain mac Ruairidh, called, in every roll that troubled to give him a second name at all, Iain Mapa.

She had never met him, though the name was known to her the way a chain-hand knows a predecessor's marks left on old survey pegs. He had left the Sloc Mòr's own surveying for the silver works at Muileann chiar before she came up through the vein-towns herself, and the Board's roll listed him now only as retired, on a small pension the silver guild paid out of its own funds rather than trouble the Crown for it, a fact she had not thought worth remarking on until she stood at his own door in the last week of an Giblean and saw, through the window, an old man bent over a workbench doing nothing she could name from the glass, moving small objects from one side of the bench to the other with a slowness that had nothing hurried in it and nothing idle either.

Mòr, his wife, a refiner's daughter of the same town who had kept his house near forty years, let her in and told her plainly, before Catrìona had said above three sentences of who she was, that her husband had been failing since the turn of the year and tired easily, and that whatever business had brought a Board rider so far east had best be short. The house was small and warm and smelled of the silver-work he had long since given up doing himself but had never given up keeping the tools for, fine files and a scale under glass and a tray of ore samples gone dull with handling, each one, Mòr told her while the kettle came to the boil, labelled in his own hand with the gallery it had come from and the year, a habit she said he had never once broken in forty years of marriage, whatever else about him the years had worn away.

Iain mac Ruairidh, three-and-sixty that spring, did not look, to Catrìona, like a man failing; he looked like a man who had set down, some years before, whatever weight a younger man carries without knowing he carries it, and had not troubled to pick it up again since. His hands, when he took hers in greeting, were steadier than she had expected of a man his wife called failing, though slow, and his eyes went first, before her face, to the mud still on her boots from the ride, a surveyor's own habit of reading a stranger by the ground she had lately walked. He asked her, before she had properly sat, whether the roll he had filed thirty years back still had his name on it, and when she told him it did, in another man's hand pinned to the corner, he smiled in a way she did not at first know how to read, and told her to fetch, from the second drawer of the same workbench she had watched him through the window, a folded paper he had kept there longer than his own children had been alive.

It was his own copy of the survey, the twin of the one she had found in the Board's own press, folded small and soft at the creases from thirty years of handling, and pinned to it, in ink gone no less brown than the Board's own copy, a receipt: *received of Iain mac Ruairidh, one survey of the eastern and southern faces, this day*, signed by a clerk whose name meant nothing to her and would have meant nothing to anyone who had not, as she had that same week, read it once already at the bottom of a Board minute thirty years old.

"I asked for it," he told her, "because a man who hands a fact to three other men in a room and walks out again with nothing to show for it has, by the next morning, no way of proving he ever handed it to them at all. It did not occur to me, asking for it, that I should still be showing it to anyone thirty years on. If I thought anything at all that day, it was that someone would read the survey long before the receipt for it ever wanted reading."

She told him what her own sheet showed, the red ink and the door-sized leaf and the shire's own concession-holder asking her, before the whole Board's chamber, how long the ground had been known to be failing, and he listened to the whole of it without once interrupting her, which she came to understand, before she had finished, was not patience so much as a want of surprise; he had known, in his own gut, exactly what her sheet would show, long before she had ever begun drawing it. Mòr, standing at the door with the kettle still in her hand, asked once whether he wanted to rest before hearing more, and he told her, gently enough that Catrìona judged it an old exchange between them and not a new one, that a man did not tire of hearing, after thirty years, that he had been right.

He asked her, when she had done, a little about the search for a second vein, which he had followed at a distance from Muileann chiar and judged, at the time, a search worth making even knowing it would fail, since a kingdom that has never troubled to look cannot afterward claim the looking would have made no difference; and he asked her, too, whether the school at Caol shean still kept its old question about why the vein answered some hands and none others, a thing he said he had wondered himself more than once in thirty years of watching men dig ground that answered nobody at all. When she had done he said only that it was a strange thing, to be believed at last by a woman not born when he had first gone unbelieved, and that he did not know whether the strangeness of it was better or worse than the thirty years that had come before it. He did not answer his own question, and she did not ask him to. Before she left, he walked her as far as the gate, slower than the distance asked for, and told her there, at the gatepost, that he did not propose to make old friends of thirty years' silence now that one woman had troubled to break it, and that she was to ride safe and mind the ford below Muileann chiar, which flooded most springs and had taken a surveyor's horse from under him once, years before, though not, he added, his own.

## V. Of the Bell at Caol shean

He died three weeks after her riding home, in the last days of an Cèitean, at his own workbench, and Mòr found him there in the evening in much the position Catrìona had first seen him through the window, small objects still laid out before him in an order only he had ever understood. The silver guild that had paid his small pension for the ten years since his retirement sent, as its own custom asked, a single ounce of refined silver to be laid in the ground with him, a mark of a working life spent honestly among its faces, and the guild's own master said over him, at a burial the whole street of retired surveyors and refiners turned out for, that Iain mac Ruairidh had gone down into more of the mountain's own dark than any three men living and had never once come up lying about what he found there, which the master judged, of a surveyor, the only epitaph worth the cutting.

Among his papers, going through them in the weeks that followed with a patience her own grief did not much help her to, Mòr found the folded survey where he had always kept it, the receipt still pinned to its corner exactly as Catrìona had seen it, and sent the whole of it south to the Library at Muileann chaol, with a note in her own hand saying only that her husband had asked, more than once in his last years, that it not be lost a second time, and that she did not know a safer place to send it than the one place in the kingdom that had never once told him his work was filed and nothing more. The keeper who received it, Mòrag nic Coinnich, entered it in the Library's own hand, as her practice was, with a note of where it had come from and who had sent it, so that a roll thirty years lost and a widow's own letter came, in the end, to be kept side by side on the same shelf, each answering for the other.

Catrìona resigned the office of surveyor-general before that same year was out, the Board's own minute recording it in four lines as flat as any minute the Board had ever kept, and went, within the season, to teach at the druid school at Caol shean, where the old question the schools had never stopped asking, why the vein answered some hands and not others, had begun, in the years since the search's own failure, to sound less like a riddle and more like the only honest question left to ask of a mountain that had, by every sheet the Board could now draw, very little answering left in it. She told the school's own teachers, in the letter that secured her the place, that she had spent sixteen years learning to read what the ground would give and meant now to spend what years remained her learning to teach others to read it sooner than she had, which she judged, on the evidence of one thirty-year-old roll and one dry receipt, the only lesson in her own trade worth the passing on.

Muileann dhearg, in the few years that followed, lost near a third of its households to the ports and the southern yards, its people going where work still answered a chain and a lamp, families that had cut coal at Muileann dhearg for three and four generations loading a cart with what it would hold and leaving the rest, and the maor's own count of empty houses, kept at first out of ordinary habit and then out of something closer to alarm, ran by the third year to above sixty within the shire's own walls alone. Its Fèidh hall, given back to the druid school not ten years before, was shut for want of a congregation left to keep it, the last teacher there locking its door herself and carrying the key to the maor's own house rather than leave it with no one, and its bell taken down and sold, on the shire's own vote, to the same school at Caol shean that had lately gained a surveyor for a teacher, the sale bringing the shire less coin than the bell's own casting had cost a hundred years before, which nobody at the vote troubled to remark on aloud.

At Ceann leathan the coal-ships waited at the quay for cargoes that came in half loaded where they had once come in full, their masters pacing the harbour wall through whole afternoons waiting on a hold that filled slower every season, and the harbour dues falling by half within two seasons, and the fair-wardens, whose town lived as much off the coal trade as off the Còmhrag's own purse, raised the gate-price of the melee that autumn to make up what the quay no longer earned them, a rise the fair's own crowds paid without much complaint, there being few among them, by then, who had not already heard what a door-sized sheet at Cathair mhòr was said to show.

Of the sheet the Board's own minute has nothing further to say; it hangs still, so far as the Library's last word of it goes, on the wall of the chamber at Cathair mhòr where Catrìona nic Neacail first put it up, the red ink holding its colour better than the ink-maker had dared promise her. Any concession-holder who cares to stand before it and count can read, in that one leaf, exactly how much of the mountain's one true vein remains to be worked before the counting is done.

Catrìona gave her first lecture at Caol shean on a bright morning in an t-Iuchar, the students of the school gathered in the same wood where Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain had once put the old question to a congregation that did not yet know to ask it back, and as she began to speak the bell that had rung out the last vigil at Muileann dhearg's own shut hall, hung now in the school's own modest tower, was rung for the first time to call the school in, its note carrying clear and a little strange across ground that had never before heard it, an old sound finding, at Caol shean, a use its makers at Muileann dhearg had never once intended for it.
