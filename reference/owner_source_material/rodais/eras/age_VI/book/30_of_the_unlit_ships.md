# Of the Unlit Ships

*SE 43 an Giblean – SE 45 an Dùbhlachd*

> An Dùbhlachd, night the ninth. No moon. A sail crossed under the light going north, showing no lamp fore or aft. I could not read her build. Marked her heading and the hour and nothing else, having nothing else to mark.
>
> — the log kept at Taigh-solais Baile ìseal, its first year

## I. Of the List Copied and the Grain That Came North

Tormod Pinn kept, in the front of the first book he wrote as royal chronicler, the list his predecessor had left him of what no chronicle yet held, {{date:V-0153}}, the northern towns' own accounts of themselves, the humans' old treaties in their own tongue, the Board's surveys; and it is told of him that in the second year of his keeping, riding the southern circuit Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill had ridden before him, he wrote on a leaf of its own, and not beneath her list, which he would not add to, a line of his own: that a gap named was worth more than a gap forgotten.

That same spring the dry years came again to the country about Achadh shean, {{date:V-0154}}, where the ground had failed once before in the humans' own time and been given a name for it that the shire's own people had never troubled to forget, Tiormachd Achadh shean, the drought of the old field. The rains that should have come with am Màrt held off through an Giblean and into an Cèitean, and the wells the households of the drove road had drawn from since before any living farmer's grandfather went first brackish and then, in three of them, dry to the stone, so that the maor of Siorrachd Achadh shean, a careful man who kept his own tally of every well's yield the way a miser keeps a coin-count, sent word south before the second month of the dry was out rather than wait, as an older maor might have waited, for the harvest itself to fail and prove the need past arguing. He wrote in his own petition, copied afterward into the shire's own chest, that a man could argue with a poor harvest for a season and still bring in enough at the edges to call it a harvest; a well gone to the stone argued with no one, and gave nothing back to be argued over.

The council answered faster than the north had ever known it to answer anything, and it is set down in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd, in Tormod's own quick hand, why: the grain that went to Achadh shean that year came from a store built ten years before at Cathair fhada, on the southern plain, a store of An Crùn raised great enough to feed the whole south a season and filled twice over from the south's own harvests before ever a dry year gave it reason to open its doors, and it was opened now for the first time since the masons had raised it, {{date:V-0156}}, its grain carried north not by road, which would have taken a fortnight and lost a tenth of the load to spoilage on the way, but by Slighe a' Ghuail, the same rails that carried An Sloc Mòr's coal south carrying grain north instead for the length of one season, Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn charging the council in full for the carriage and the council paying it without the argument a poorer year might have forced. The first wagons of it reached the sidings at Achadh shean on a morning wet enough at last with the season's first proper rain that the maor stood in the yard to watch them unloaded with his own boots sunk an inch in mud he had prayed six weeks for, and told the carters, half in earnest and half as a joke against himself, that the grain had plainly waited on the roof to prove it was not wanted before it troubled to come.

No one in the shire died of the drought that year, a fact Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd set down in four words and no more, and the maor had twelve wells dug to the rock along the whole length of the drove road before the following winter, {{date:V-0155}}, each one cut deep enough to answer a dry year worse than this one and each marked, at his own order, with the name of the shire and the name of the council that had paid for the digging, so that no household drawing water from them in a harder year to come would forget whose grain had answered the last one. Four of the twelve held water still, by an Leabharlann's later reckoning, long after the rest of that dry spring was a line in a chronicle and nothing more, and a fifth stood dry from the day it was dug, cut, as the well-diggers themselves owned afterward without much shame in the owning, on the word of a dowser's hazel rather than on any survey a Board man would have troubled to make first.

It is the way of a record kept at one desk to see a season's want answered and set the answering down as though the answering were the whole of the matter, and Tormod Pinn's own hand, careful as it was, did not trouble that spring to ask what a store built ten years before a want, and opened the very first time a want arose, might mean for a shire whose want came slower and less kindly than a single dry spring on Rathad na Mèinne. That question belonged, that year, to a different coast, and a different kind of keeper.

## II. Of the Keeper at the Point

Taigh-solais Baile ìseal stood on a low point of rock at the eastern edge of the island, south of the ford-town for whose salt-pans the coast was known, where the land ran out flat and treeless into a grey sea that showed nothing on a clear day but its own horizon and, on the worst nights of an Dùbhlachd, nothing at all beyond the reach of the lamp's own light. Murchadh mac Coinnich had kept it four years by the spring the dry years came to Achadh shean, a Dia-thìreach of one-and-thirty that spring, given the post by Comhairle nan Taighean-solais on the recommendation of an uncle who had kept a smaller light on the western coast and judged the boy steady enough for a post where steadiness, more than any other virtue a keeper might be asked for, was the whole of the office.

He had a wife, and a daughter not yet three, and a house of two rooms built onto the lighthouse's own base so that the stair to the lamp-room rose straight out of the kitchen, an arrangement his wife complained of on a smoky evening and was glad of on a night the wind came hard enough off the sea to make the walk from any separate dwelling a misery. The work of the post was simple to describe and endless to perform: the lamp trimmed and lit an hour before the light itself was needed and kept burning through whatever hours the season demanded, the oil accounted for to the half-pint in a book the Board's own inspector checked twice a year, the glass kept clean of salt against a wind that fouled it within a week of any cleaning, and a log kept, as every light on the coast kept one, of the weather and the hour the lamp was lit and doused and nothing more than that, a book that in Murchadh's first four years at the post held nothing a stranger reading it after him would have called worth the keeping.

The inspector who rode out from Comhairle nan Taighean-solais's own office twice a year to check the oil against the book was a dry, exact man who had kept a light himself in his own youth and judged every keeper he inspected against the standard of his own remembered diligence, and he told Murchadh, on the visit before the dry year at Achadh shean was three months old, that the log of Baile ìseal was the tidiest he read on the whole eastern round and the emptiest, both facts said in the same even voice and neither meant, so far as Murchadh could tell, as either praise or complaint. Murchadh took the remark for a compliment at the time, having nothing yet to set against an empty book but the honest fact that the coast had given him nothing to fill it with, and it was some months before he came to hear the same remark differently, as a question asked without the asking of it, whether a book kept tidy because nothing troubled it was the same thing as a book that answered for a coast truly at peace.

The coast below the light was a Dia-thìreach coast in name and a mixed one in fact, the salt-pans of Àth bheag worked by Dia-thìreach ford-keepers and the boats that ran the coast between Àth bheag and the towns north of it crewed as often by Tuathaich hands as not, since the fishing grounds off that shore paid no closer attention to a man's people than the fish themselves did. Murchadh knew most of the boats by sight before he knew half their owners by name, the way a keeper comes to know a coast, by the shape a hull makes crossing under his own light at an hour he has learned to expect it and by the absence of a shape at an hour he has learned to expect that too. He had watched, in his first four years, the ordinary traffic of an ordinary coast, herring boats out before first light and salt-barges working the tide up to Àth bheag's own quay, and had set none of it down beyond the bare fact the Board required of him, weather fair, lamp lit at the customary hour, nothing to report.

## III. Of the Burning of the Salt-Sheds

It was in an Gearran of the next year, the dry spring at Achadh shean not yet forgotten in Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd that had just finished setting it down, that ships flying no flag of any house or kingdom stood in toward the coast below Baile ìseal in the small hours of a night with cloud enough to hide a good part of the moon, {{date:V-0157}}, and put men ashore who fired the salt-sheds at Àth bheag before the shore watchers on the dunes, doubled since the raiding off Cathair gheal some years before but still no more than two men and a lantern, had done more than raise the alarm. Murchadh saw the glow from his own lamp-room before he understood what it was, a low red smear along the coast to the south where no fire had any business standing at that hour, and he woke his wife and sent her for the nearest household with a horse while he kept the light burning as the Board required of him whatever else was burning along the same coast that night, since a keeper who let his own lamp gutter to run toward another man's fire left every ship still at sea to find the rocks by guesswork.

The sheds burned through what remained of the night and into the first grey of morning, three long low buildings where the salt was dried and stored against the winter trade, and by the time the alarm had carried far enough to bring men from Àth bheag with buckets and what the tide could spare them, there was little left to save beyond the stone footings and a stack of barrels too far from the fire's own heart to catch. The ships were gone before any boat could be crewed to follow them, standing off again into the same dark they had come out of, and it was two full days before the kingdom's own patrol boats, summoned from the harbour that kept them and rowed by men who had heard of the burning only when a rider reached them, came down the coast to look the ruin over and find, as Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd set it down afterward in a line that carried more in its plainness than any longer telling would have, the sheds cold.

Murchadh rode down to Àth bheag himself the second morning, once relief had come for the light and he could leave it in another man's hands for half a day, and stood with the ford-keepers among the wet ash and the barrel-hoops the fire had left standing when the staves inside them burned away, and heard from a woman whose own family's trade the sheds had held a season's whole salt for what she thought of a coast the kingdom's own patrol thought worth two days' notice to reach. "They will build it again," she told him, a plain fact she had settled with herself before he ever rode down rather than any comfort offered him, "and it will burn again, and the boats will come the third day next time as they came the second this time, being no faster for having been asked before." Murchadh had no answer for her that satisfied him at the giving of it, and rode home along the coast road turning the plainness of her fact over in a way the ordinary business of a lighthouse had never before given him reason to.

He asked her, before he left, how she had known to look for the ships coming rather than simply to grieve the sheds once they were gone, and she told him that her own father had kept the same trade before her and had taught her, as a girl too young to carry a full basket of the dried salt, to watch the horizon at an hour no honest boat troubled to be abroad, since a coast that made its living from the sea learned the sea's dishonest hours the way a farmer learns a bad neighbour's habits, by long unhappy practice and no help from any book. It struck him, riding home, that the woman's own father had kept a kind of watch for years that no Board had ever asked of him and no chronicle had ever thought to record, and that the watch had done the shed no good at all the one night it mattered, for want of anywhere further than her own two eyes to carry what she saw.

The sheds were raised again within the year, An Crùn paying a third of the cost as it had paid a third of Cuan dhearg's relief a generation before and the shire's own households the rest, timber and stone going up through the autumn while the salt trade limped along on what the barrels spared from the fire could carry; and the patrol's own captain, called to answer for the two days before the shire's own maor, wrote a report that named the distance, the wind, and the hour the alarm had first reached his own harbour, a report the Board judged honest and the households of Àth bheag judged, when a copy of it was read to them at the market cross, an accounting that answered every question but the one that mattered, which was what a shore two days from help was worth calling a shore the kingdom kept at all.

## IV. Of the Log Begun

Murchadh went back to his light that same week no wiser than the woman among the ash had left him, but changed in a way that took him some months to put a name to, watching now, on every ordinary night the coast gave him, for a sail that carried no light where an honest boat would carry one, {{date:V-0158}}. He said nothing of it to the Board, whose own instructions asked no more of him than the weather and the hour, and he set down nothing of it in the book the Board's own inspector checked, but he kept, from the first night after the burning that the sea gave him anything at all to watch, a second book of his own, bought with his own coin from a Muileann leathan binder a carter brought up the coast that autumn, in which he wrote what the Board's book had no line for: every ship that crossed under his light showing no lamp, her heading so far as the dark let him judge it, and the hour to the nearest half of the glass.

The first entry, made on a clouded night in an t-Samhain, nine months after the burning, ran to no more than the line the epigraph of this telling carries whole, a sail crossing north under no lamp of her own and a keeper with nothing to mark of her beyond the fact of her passing; and Murchadh, closing the book that first night, judged the entry worth less than the ink it had cost him, having named nothing a stranger reading it after him could use to know one dark sail from another. It was the fishermen of Àth bheag, more than any instruction the Board had ever sent him, who taught him within the season what a keeper's own eye alone would not have found, that a coast worked as long as this one had been worked came, in time, to know its unwelcome visitors nearly as well as it knew its own boats, by the cut of a sail against a grey sky or the particular black of a hull no honest builder on the island trimmed that colour, and had names for what it knew whether any book of An Crùn's troubled to record them or not.

He heard the first of the names from an old salt-carrier at Àth bheag's own quay, a man who had watched the coast from a boat's deck longer than Murchadh had watched it from a tower, who told him, of a low black hull that had crossed twice under the light that autumn showing no lamp either time, that the coast called her An Ròn Dubh, the black seal, for the way she rode low and dark and vanished under a wave's own shadow before a watcher's eye had properly found her; and of a second, swifter and paler in her lines, that the coast called An Fhaoileag, the gull, for a habit of standing well out by day and closing only with the dark, the way a gull stands off a boat's wake until the catch is hauled and the guts go over the side. Murchadh set both names into his own book that same evening, against the two crossings he had already logged without a name to give them, and found, doing it, that a name changed what a bare heading and an hour had been until then; a sail without a name was one more unlit shape among however many the sea sent him, and a sail with one was a thing a keeper could watch for, expect, and, in time, begin to know the habits of, as surely as he knew the habits of the herring boats that crossed under his light by day with every lamp they owned burning honestly against the coming dark.

He kept the book from that season on with a care he had never given the Board's own log, ruling each page himself with a straight-edge before he ever wrote in it and setting a second lamp by the table where he wrote so that no error of a tired hand at a late hour might blur a heading he could no longer check by morning. His wife asked him once, watching him rule a fresh page by a lamp he could ill afford to burn twice in one night, why a book no one had asked him to keep should want a straighter margin than the Board's own book got, and he told her, turning the question over longer than a simple answer seemed to want, that a book nobody had asked for was the one book a keeper could be sure no one else would trouble to keep straight in his place, and that this alone made it worth more care rather than less.

By the following an Dùbhlachd, a full year into the keeping of his own book, Murchadh had entered above thirty crossings, most of them nameless still and a dozen given one or other of the two names the coast had furnished him, along with the weather of each night and, where the light let him judge it, the number of men he could make out at a hull's rail; and it was from that year's book, carried south by a clerk of an Leabharlann that winter, that Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd first drew the two names, An Ròn Dubh and An Fhaoileag, {{date:V-0159}}, setting them into the realm's own record for the first time with no better source to credit them to than a lighthouse keeper's private ledger and the salt-carriers of a coast the kingdom's patrol had answered, once, two days too late. Tormod Pinn, reading the book a clerk of an Leabharlann had carried south for his own chronicle's sake, wrote beneath the copied names that a coast kept its own record whether An Crùn troubled to ask for one or not, and that this book, more than the patrol's report or the shire's own petition, was the truest account the kingdom yet held of what sailed its eastern water unlit.

## V. Of the Champion of Àth ghlas

In that same winter, a good way south and west of the light Murchadh kept, Còmhrag Ceann leathan at Ceann leathan drew the largest crowd the fair-wardens of that town had yet built stands to hold, three sides of the field now timbered where two had answered before, Còmhrag Ceann leathan's own fame having grown, in the years since its first blood was shed, into the kind of gathering a man travelled a week to see rather than a day, {{date:V-0160}}. The fighter who took the field's own honour that year, and the crowd's, was no man at all but Ealasaid nic Coinnich, of Àth ghlas in the west, a ford-market's daughter who had learned the staff and the fist from a father who kept no sons to teach it to and saw no reason a daughter should go untaught for the lack, and who stood, at the day's ending, the last fighter left the field, to a silence the fair-wardens' own chronicler afterward called longer than any cheer the ground had heard before it.

She was given the year's wages Còmhrag Ceann leathan paid its champion and the right to bear arms the custom granted along with them, and she took both without a speech, the crowd's own telling of it afterward making more of her silence than she had ever troubled to make of it herself; and she did not fight again, in that field or any other Còmhrag Ceann leathan's own custom kept, a fact Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd set down as plainly as it had set down the dry year at Achadh shean and the cold sheds at Àth bheag, without asking, in the setting down, whether she had gone once to prove a thing needed proving only the once or had simply found, having proved it, no further reason to return to a field that asked a fighter to prove it again each year for a crowd that came, more often than not, for the blood alone.

The fair-wardens asked her, the following spring, whether she meant to defend the year's own honour when Còmhrag Ceann leathan came round again, having never before had to put the question to a champion who might answer it either way, and she told them, by the account her own kin at Àth ghlas kept afterward, that a thing won once stayed won whether or not she troubled to stand on the same field a second year to keep it, and that she had a ford-market's own trade to see to that the field had never once paid her for keeping from. The wardens made what they could of the answer, which was little, and gave the year's honour after that to whoever the field itself decided, the way it had always decided such things before her single year among them.

The two accounts reached an Leabharlann within the same season, Ealasaid's silence and Murchadh's second book, carried south by different hands and read by the same chronicler's eye, and Tormod Pinn, closing that year's chest, found himself setting one beside the other without quite meaning to, a woman who had answered a question once and would not answer it again, and a keeper who had begun answering a question every night the sea gave him leave to and meant, so far as any book of his said otherwise, to go on answering it for as long as the light stood and the coast kept sending him ships that carried no lamp. Murchadh's own book, that Dùbhlachd, stood a full year deep, its first clouded entry no longer the poorest thing in it, and he set it on the shelf above his own bed each night beside the Board's own log, the one book asking nothing of him but the hour a lamp was lit and doused, the other asking, and answering as best a keeper's single eye could answer, what manner of coast the kingdom kept and how far its own keeping truly reached along it.
