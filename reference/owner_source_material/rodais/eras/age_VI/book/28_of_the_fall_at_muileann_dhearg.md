# Of the Fall at Muileann dhearg

*SE 39 an t-Iuchar – SE 40 an t-Samhain*

> *Cha d' fhuair a' bheinn fois a-riamh, is cha d' fhuair sinne.*
> The mountain never got its rest, and neither did we.

## I. Of the First Day of an t-Iuchar

Muileann dhearg had grown fat on An Sloc Mòr's overflow for thirty years, a town of narrow streets and spoil-stone chapels five miles from the great shaft, its own pits worked to keep An Sloc Mòr's yield steady in the seasons when the greater mine's own galleries wanted rest An Crùn would not grant them. The Cill ghlas district under Muileann dhearg had stood shut from the humans' time until Comhairle nam Mèinnean reopened it, and the men who went down into it that first morning of an t-Iuchar knew its name the way every hewer on the mountain knew it, the way a house knows a grave it does not visit. Scàineadh Cill ghlas, had taken the old workings in An Aois Choigreach, and the timbers that came up afterward, what little of them came up whole, were kept for a season in the guild hall at Achadh dhomhain before anyone could bear to burn them. The new gallery ran a hundred fathoms east of where the old one had run, along a line Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own surveyors had walked twice and marked sound on the map both times. It had been working four months.

It gave at the fourth hour past midnight shift-change, when the day gang was down and the night gang not yet up, so that for one turn of the clock the gallery held more men than it would hold at any other hour of the twenty-four. Nobody afterward could say what brought it down, only that the roof let go along its whole length at once rather than falling face by face the way a bad roof usually gives a man time to run from, and that the noise of it was heard on the surface as a single long note, like a bell struck once and left to die away underground instead of in the air. Thirty-one men were in the gallery. None of them came up alive.

Word reached Muileann chiar the following evening, carried by a rider Comhairle nam Mèinnean sent without much urgency, since it was a mine matter and Iain mac Ruairidh was thirteen years at the silver works and no longer, by Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own reckoning, a mine matter himself. The rider found him at his own door with Mòr beside him, the two of them newly enough married that neighbours still called across the yard to wish them well of an evening, and he read the message once standing in the doorway and once again sitting down, and said nothing to his wife beyond that he would be a week gone, perhaps more.

He was chainman first at An Sloc Mòr before he was Comhairle nam Mèinnean surveyor anywhere else, and Cill ghlas was ground he had walked as a boy learning the chain from Lachlann mac Iain Bhàin, who had walked it as a boy under the man before him. He rode through the night rather than wait for morning, the twenty miles by Rathad na Mèinne and then the cart track up from Achadh dhomhain, and came into Muileann dhearg with the horse blown and his own legs near as unsteady under him, to find the town already at its digging, torches ringing the pit mouth and women carrying water up from the wash-house in relays that had not stopped since the fall.

They let him down on the second rope, the first having been given to the guild's own overman, and he went the whole hundred fathoms with a lamp in one hand and the guild's rope in the other, over rubble that shifted under his boots in ways rubble should not shift, until the beam of his lamp found the roof-line where it had let go and he stood a long while looking at it before he spoke to the man behind him.

"Bring me a level," he said. "And send to Muileann chiar for the old sheets I keep there. Not Comhairle nam Mèinnean's. Mine."

They brought him the level before the old maps came, since the maps had to be sent for from Muileann chiar and would not reach him before the second day. He spent the dark hours of that first night on his knees along the fallen roof-line, the level laid flat and a boy holding the lamp steady over his shoulder, and marked chalk figures on a slate the rubble dust settled over as fast as he could write them. The overman, a broad man named for no reason this book keeps to it, crouched beside him a while and asked, once, whether the fault ran under the working faces still open to the north.

"It runs under half the district," Iain Mapa said. "I don't yet know which half is the safer half."

"Then we bring up what we can and shut the rest."

"That's my own thought, aye. I'll want it in writing that it was my thought and not Comhairle nam Mèinnean's, before I put my name to closing a working that four months of Comhairle nam Mèinnean surveys called sound."

The overman said nothing to that beyond a short breath that was not quite a laugh, and went back to the rope.

## II. Of the Rules Made for the Dia-thìrich

It took four days to bring up the thirty-one, working by relays through galleries that groaned at every stroke of the pick, and on the fifth day the whole district was closed on Iain Mapa's own word before Comhairle nam Mèinnean at Cathair mhòr had so much as read his report. He had found the fault by the second day: a line of broken ground running north-east under the new gallery exactly where Scàineadh Cill ghlas's own line had run under the old one in the humans' time, so close to the surface survey's marked boundary that a man drawing the map by lamplight, in a hurry, might have set his pencil a hair's width wrong and never known it. A board that surveyed a district once and called the survey finished had made an ordinary error, the kind any board might make. The mountain itself had not finished moving simply because a map said it had stopped.

The old maps reached him on the third day, brought up from Muileann chiar by a rider who had ridden as hard coming as Iain Mapa himself had ridden going, three rolled sheets Iain had copied years before from the custodians' old rolls, older in their first drawing than any paper Comhairle nam Mèinnean kept at Cathair mhòr, drawn by hands long dead in a script Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own clerks could no longer easily read. He unrolled them on a trestle set up in the open air beside the pit mouth, weighting the corners with stones against a wind that wanted to carry them off toward the burying-ground, and laid his own new chalk figures over the old ink line by line until the two agreed to within a fathom along the whole length of the fault. The old surveyors, whoever they had been, had known exactly where the ground was broken. They had marked it, in a hand gone brown with age, with a single word he had to hold the lamp close to read: *fàgte*. Left. Let be.

The dead were buried at Muileann dhearg in a single ground broken for them at the edge of the town's own burying-yard, thirty-one graves dug in two days by every man in the town not needed underground, the custodian of the district reading over each in turn rather than once over all, since the families asked it that way and the custodian did not argue. Iain Mapa stood at the ground's edge through the whole of it, hat in hand, and was asked by more than one widow, in the flat voice grief leaves people with, whether the ground had been marked sound. He answered every one of them the same way. It had.

He did not tell the widows what the level had told him in the dark of the first night, that the ground had been marked sound by men who had walked it with a chain and a lamp and had not walked it far enough, or often enough, or with the old lists of Scàineadh Cill ghlas laid open beside their own paper while they walked. It would have told the widows nothing they could use, and he had learned enough of Comhairle nam Mèinnean by then to know that a truth spoken over a grave and a truth written into a report Comhairle nam Mèinnean would read did not need to be spoken in the same words, or even, always, by the same man. The level's figures he kept on his slate until he could copy them fair, washing the chalk dust off with his own hand rather than a boy's, the way a man washes a thing he means to use again.

Comhairle nam Mèinnean's rules followed within the month, new orders for the digging of any gallery within a mile of ground the old lists named as struck by a calamity, timber doubled, a second survey required before any face was opened, a bell-line run the whole length of a new working so that a fall anywhere along it could be heard at both ends at once. They were good rules, and Iain Mapa, reading them at Muileann dhearg where Comhairle nam Mèinnean sent them to him first as the man who had closed the district, found nothing in them he would have changed. They were written for An Sloc Mòr and its daughter workings, where Dia-thìreach hewers held the guild's own oath and the guild's own voice to ask for them. At the western pits, where Tuathaich hands worked under Dia-thìreach overseers on ground the guild had never chartered, the same order went out on the same day's post and was kept the way an order is kept by men who know no one south of them will come to check.

It is told of Àth leathan, where the overseer's own name is not given in any record an Leabharlann holds, that the new rule for timbering was posted on the pit-head door within the week, in Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own hand, and that the timber the rule called for was ordered from the yard at Ceann mhòr the same week, and paid for out of the pit's own accounts, and never came. The overseer's books, kept in the flat hand overseers kept for such things, show the order and the payment both, entered on facing pages, with no third entry anywhere to say why the carts never left the yard. The hewers at Àth leathan went on working the old way, under the old timber, because a man with a family to feed does not stop hewing coal to ask a question the pit-head door has already answered him with a posted order he cannot read.

Iain Mapa did not go to Àth leathan that autumn. He heard of it the way Comhairle nam Mèinnean heard of most things from the western pits, sideways, in a clerk's letter meant for another matter that mentioned the timber order in passing, as a thing settled, and he kept the letter. Thirteen years before, at Cathair dhearg, he had learned what a receipt was worth against a board that did not read what it filed, and he had begun, without quite deciding to, the habit of keeping every paper that crossed his own hand a second time before he let it go.

He wrote his own letter that same week, at the table Mòr kept for him by the window, naming the fault at Cill ghlas by its true line, citing the survey he had made thirteen years before of the faces of An Sloc Mòr, and setting the two beside each other on the page: a mountain that did not rest, and a board that surveyed it once and called the matter closed. He wrote until past midnight, by his own lamp, and read it through twice, and folded it, and did not seal it.

"You'll send that," Mòr said, from the door, in a voice that was not quite a question.

"I sent one before," he said. "They gave me a receipt for it and moved me to the silver."

"This one names thirty-one dead, not a thinning seam."

"It names the same Comhairle nam Mèinnean." He turned the letter over once on the table, as though weighing it in his hand might tell him something the words on it could not. "A receipt is a fine thing to have kept, thirteen years on. I've never once had cause to use it."

Mòr said nothing further that night. She had married a surveyor and not a clerk of Tional nan Tuathach, and she had learned in a year of marriage that there were silences of his best left alone, and this, she judged, was one of them. He put the letter in the drawer where he kept the receipt, unsealed, and left it there, and if he took it out again in the weeks that followed, to read over or to add a line to, no one at Muileann chiar ever saw him do it.

## III. Of the Sixty and the Forty

Muileann dhearg did not fill again the way it had filled in the first years of the concession, on wages and the promise of work. Forty families left Achadh dhomhain before the autumn was out, walking south to the fishing ports with what a cart would carry, and the town's own tally-clerk, entering their names off the roll, wrote against each one only *gone south*, since the roll kept no other word for it. Some had lost no one in the fall and went anyway, saying to whoever asked that a town could grieve thirty-one men once and go on living beside their graves, but a family did not choose to raise its own sons under a mountain that had shown, once, what it thought of a survey.

Comann nan Gualadairean, whose rolls the fall had thinned by thirty-one at a stroke, bound sixty apprentices that same season to fill them, boys sworn in at Achadh dhomhain with a hand laid on a lump of coal in the guild's own hall, the oath read out by the guild's eldest master in the same words it had been read in since the charter, each boy's father standing behind him in a line that ran the length of the hall and out its door into the yard. Not one of the sixty was Tuathaich. The guild's charter had said, since An Aois Naomh custom was taken up again, that it was for Dia-thìreach applicants, and the charter was kept, so that the pits that had just buried thirty-one Dia-thìreach hewers filled their rolls again with Dia-thìreach boys, and the western pits, where the dying was no less real for going uncounted, filled theirs with whatever hands the overseers could hire by the day and let go by the day when the work was thin.

At Caol mhòr, where the north's own minute-book had carried the fall as four lines the week it happened and nothing since, Mary Coyle read Comhairle nam Mèinnean's new rules when a copy reached Tional nan Tuathach by way of a carter who had seen them posted at Ceann mhòr, and sat with them a long while before she called for the book. She wrote to Comhairle nam Mèinnean that autumn, in her own hand, asking that the same rules be kept in fact as well as in the posting at the western pits, and named Àth leathan by name, and the timber that had not come. She sent it by the same carter's road the goods went south by, since Tional nan Tuathach had no faster road to send anything by.

The letter reached Cathair mhòr within the fortnight and was answered within the month, in three lines, thanking Tional nan Tuathach for its concern and stating that the matter had been referred to the district overseer for his report. No report was ever filed. The letter was kept, in the press where Comhairle nam Mèinnean kept what it had no further use for, and a clerk's hand noted its arrival on the cover in the same flat script that had noted the timber order at Àth leathan on facing pages a season before. Mary Coyle asked the carter, the next time he came north, whether he had heard anything more of it. He had not. She wrote nothing further that year. The book at Caol mhòr held her one letter and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's three lines, and nothing between them, and that was the whole of what the kingdom's own record held of Àth leathan's timber.

## IV. Of the Third Day of an t-Sultain

Leabharlann Muileann chaol kept its own account of that autumn apart from anything Iain Mapa saw or heard, and it is from an Leabharlann's own record, and not from any word that reached him at Muileann dhearg, that this book takes what follows.

Ailean Leabhar had kept an Leabharlann four-and-twenty years, first among the wet rolls in the cellar and then among the chests the shires sent up one by one, and he had grown old in the keeping the way the rolls themselves grew old, a little more brittle each year, the hand that had once carried a torch down a stair now needing a boy to carry the lamp for him. He was four-and-sixty that autumn, though he had stopped, some years before, letting anyone at Muileann chaol mark his birthday, saying that a keeper's business was other men's years and not his own. He kept to his desk through most of an Lùnastal and into an t-Sultain, reading, as he had read every autumn for a decade, the harvest returns the shires sent up, and he was found there on the morning of the third day of the month, at his own desk, with the returns for Siorrachd Doire chaol open under his hand and his pen laid down beside them rather than dropped, so that Mòrag nic Coinnich, who found him, said afterward that he looked to have finished the line he was writing before he stopped.

They buried him two days after, in the small ground behind an Leabharlann, where no keeper had been buried before him because no keeper before him had held the office long enough to be thought of as belonging to the place rather than merely working in it. The town of Muileann chaol turned out for it near to the last household, since there was scarcely a family in the shire an Leabharlann's own chests had not, in four-and-twenty years, asked something of, a grave-list copied or a boundary confirmed or a name set right against a parish book gone soft with damp. Mòrag nic Coinnich read nothing over him beyond his own words, the ones a clerk had once used in his hearing and that he had answered without meaning the answer to outlast him.

"An Leabharlann settles nothing," she said, at the grave's edge, in a voice that carried no further than the nearest rows of mourners. "It only keeps."

She was named keeper before the month was out, the council at Cathair dhearg confirming what Muileann chaol had already taken for settled the day Ailean Leabhar was found at his desk, and she began, within her first week in the office, a practice no keeper before her had kept: writing beside each item that came into the chests, in a small hand at the foot of its own entry, where it had come from and who had sent it and on what day. She gave no reason for the practice when she was asked, beyond that a thing a Library could not trace back to its own hand was a thing an Leabharlann could be told anything about, and she had watched, in all her years at Ailean Leabhar's own elbow, how often Comhairle nam Mèinnean's word and the shire's word and the north's word, laid side by side, did not agree.

Eight years his deputy by then, she knew which chests he had never finished sorting and which he had gone back to twice and still called unfinished, and she spent her first month as keeper doing what she told no one at the time she was doing: going through his own desk, drawer by drawer, in case some paper he had meant to file lay there still, undone. She found nothing from Muileann dhearg, and nothing from Àth leathan, since neither Comhairle nam Mèinnean's silence on the one nor its three lines on the other had ever been sent to an Leabharlann at all. What she found, at the back of the lowest drawer, was a single sheet in his own hand, headed only with a date some years old, that read, in its whole: *what we are not sent, we cannot keep. Ask the shires again.* She could not say, reading it, whether it was a note to himself or the first line of a letter he had never finished, and she kept it, unfiled, in the drawer of her own desk, where it stayed the whole of her time as keeper.

## V. Of the Fire at the Fair

Fèill Muileann òg had stood nineteen years by that Samhain, the greatest gathering the island kept, its stalls running the whole length of the river meadow below the toll-house where Oighrig nic Mhathain had kept the rules since the first year, a woman of fifty now, grey at the temple, still walking the whole length of the ground herself each morning before the gates opened, the way she had walked it the first year with the rules not yet a season old.

The fire ran on the eve of the feast, after the gates were shut and most of the stall-holders gone to their lodgings, starting, so far as anyone could afterward say, in the timber stacked behind the weavers' row for the mending of trestles, and running before the wind caught it properly along a row of canvas and dry wood that had stood in the same ground, packed close, since the gates first opened. It went from stall to stall faster than any man on the ground that night could outrun it on foot, the canvas roofs catching each in turn with a sound one witness likened to a sail taken aback in a hard squall, and by the time the bucket-chain from the river was properly formed the weavers' row and half the chandlers' beyond it stood already past saving.

Oighrig was in the ground herself when it started, having gone back for a ledger left in the toll-house, and she was seen by more than one witness beating at the canvas with her own cloak before the men who came running got the worst of it turned aside from the stalls that held the season's whole trade for a dozen towns. She did not leave the ground until the last of the flame along the weavers' row had been beaten down or burned out on its own, and it was only then, in the grey light before dawn, that anyone thought to look closely at her hands.

The court sat the next morning in the trampled field, since the toll-house had burned with the rest, Oighrig presiding from a chair a stall-holder carried out for her, her hands and one side of her face bound in linen already gone brown at the edges, and the fair went on that day and the next as the rules said it must, since a fair once opened could not, by the rules she herself had written nineteen years before, be closed before its appointed end without the toll-mistress's own word, and she gave no such word. She died of the burns before the month was out, at her own house at Muileann òg, the toll-mistress's chair going, for the first time since the fair began, to a woman who had not held it from the first year.

That same winter Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn, asked by Tional nan Tuathach to carry the north's wool south to the fair as it had carried southern goods for twenty years, refused the carriage, citing the fair's own rule that Tuathaich goods be sold only through Dia-thìreach factors and holding that a rule written for the selling of goods bound the carrying of them as well, a reading no clerk at Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn had troubled to make in any of the twenty years before. Mary Coyle wrote a second letter that winter, shorter than the first, asking only whether Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's reading of the rule was new or had always stood and simply never before been used against the north's own goods. Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn did not answer it. A clerk's hand noted its arrival on the cover, in the same flat script, and filed it beside the first.

The wool went south the old way that year, loaded onto carts at Caol mhòr in the first cold week of an Dùbhlachd, five days on the road by Cnoc ghorm and Àth fhada to Fèill Muileann òg, the carters wrapped against a wind that had not yet learned to be as hard as it would be by the time they reached the coast, the last of the north's trade for that year going south the way it had gone before there was ever a railway to refuse it. The new toll-mistress at Muileann òg, keeping the fair's rules as Oighrig had left them, sold the wool through its factors when it came, as the rule said she must, and entered the sale in the fair's own book under the makers' shire and not their names, as the book had always kept it.

Iain Mapa, at Muileann dhearg that same week, closing out the district's books before the district itself was let stand idle through the winter, wrote nothing new to the letter in his drawer, and sent nothing south beyond the district's own accounts, which Comhairle nam Mèinnean read.
