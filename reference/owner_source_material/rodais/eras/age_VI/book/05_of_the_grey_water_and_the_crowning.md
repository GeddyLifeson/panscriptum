# Of the Grey Water and the Crowning

*SE 2 an t-Sultain – SE 3 an Dùbhlachd*

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Manannan, keep the sea shut.
>
> — said at the harbour mouth of Seann Skell in the west, {{date:V-0011}}

## I. Of the Sheds at Ros dhomhain

Ailean mac Iain had been an under-clerk of the council eight days when he was sent from Cathair dhearg to Ros dhomhain with a satchel of blank paper and a letter of introduction he was told not to open, since it was not written to him.

He opened it on the road anyway, in a barn where the carter had stopped to water the horses, and found it said almost nothing: that the bearer was to assist the harbour-master in whatever the harbour-master required, and that the council trusted the harbour-master's judgement in the matter of what that might be. He folded it back along its own crease and told himself, walking the last two miles into the town at dusk with the satchel banging against his hip, that a clerk who read letters not written to him would do well to learn to fold them convincingly, and that this was perhaps the whole of the education the council meant him to have from it.

Ros dhomhain in the second autumn of the kingdom was the busiest place Ailean had yet stood in. The humans had built it for a harbour and it still worked as one: quays of dressed stone running further than a man could see in the evening mist, cranes standing idle on their gantries for want of anyone who understood how to swing them, and sheds in long rows behind the quay wall, each one stencilled in the humans' letters with a number and a word Ailean could not read. He had learned a little of the humans' hand as a boy, from a Mission reader who had stayed at his father's house one winter and taught the alphabet to any child who would sit still for it, but a little was not much, and Ros dhomhain's sheds held more of it than he had words for.

Dùghall mac Iain met him at the harbour-master's office on the first morning, a heavy, unhurried man of perhaps forty who kept his coat buttoned to the throat even indoors and spoke, when he spoke at all, as though every sentence cost him something he would rather have kept. He looked at Ailean for a long moment, looked at the letter, and said only, "Can you read their tally marks."

Ailean said he could read some of them.

"Then you'll do," said Dùghall, and took him through to a room behind the office where the humans' tariff sat in eleven bound ledgers on a shelf built to hold exactly eleven ledgers and no more, as though whoever built the shelf had known the number in advance and meant it to stay fixed. "This is what we collect by," he said. "It was theirs. It is ours now, because nobody has written another, and a harbour that collects nothing starves the shire it stands in." He took the first ledger down and opened it on the table between them, and Ailean saw page after page of the humans' close, slanting script, columns of figures, words he could sound out one letter at a time and rarely understand once sounded.

"You'll copy," Dùghall said. "Line by line, into Dia-thìris, as near as the sense will bear. Where you cannot make the sense bear it, you'll mark the line and bring it to me, and I will tell you what it says, because I have had two years now of guessing at what they meant by half of it, and I guess better than any man now living on this coast."

It was slower work than Ailean had thought any work could be. A single clause on the duty owed for salted fish landed at Ros dhomhain's own quay, no more than four lines in the humans' hand, took him the better part of a morning to render into a Dia-thìris that said the same thing and not some other thing dressed to look like it; and when he brought it to Dùghall, the older man read it twice, said "Nearly," and gave it back with two words struck through and two others written above them in a hand Ailean would come, over the weeks that followed, to know as well as his own.

"Why not write it fresh," Ailean asked him, on the third or fourth evening, when the ledgers were shut for the night and Dùghall had, unexpectedly, poured them both a measure of something the harbour-master kept for occasions he did not name. "A tariff of our own, instead of theirs turned into our words."

Dùghall considered the question longer than Ailean had expected, turning his cup on the table without drinking from it. "Because theirs is the one the boats already know," he said at last. "A skipper out of Cuan shean or Ceann mhòr has been paying this tariff, or its like, for longer than the kingdom has existed to collect it. Change the sums and he'll pay less gladly and cheat more freely, and I have neither the men nor the time to catch him at it. Turn the words and keep the sums, and he pays the same as he always has, to a different hand, and does not trouble himself over the difference." He drank, then, and set the cup down harder than he needed to. "Someday somebody will write a tariff that is ours from the first word to the last. It will not be me, and it will not, I think, be soon. In the meantime the harbour needs feeding, and I have found that a copied thing, honestly copied, feeds a harbour very nearly as well as an invented one, and costs a great deal less in error."

Ailean wrote that down, later, in the private book he had begun keeping since his first weeks at the counting-houses, a slim thing bound in undyed leather that he told no one of and showed to no one, in which he set beside the day's plain business the things said to him that he thought worth keeping and did not yet know why. He did not, that autumn, understand what use such a book would ever be to him. He kept it anyway, the way a man keeps a coin found in a field, not because he knows its worth but because it seems wrong to leave it lying.

## II. Of the Grey Water

He was still at Ros dhomhain, three weeks into the tariff and no more than a third through the eleven ledgers, when the letter came naming him tally-clerk for a voyage.

It said little more than the assignment itself: that the council was sending a ship north-about, past the north-western capes, to learn whether the crossing the humans had used still lay open to any who cared to find it, and that a clerk was wanted aboard to keep the log of what was seen, in a hand the council could read when she returned. Dùghall read the letter over Ailean's shoulder, said "So they've decided to look, then," in the flat voice he used for anything that was not the tariff, and gave Ailean three days to close his desk and be at Cuan shean by the eleventh of an t-Ògmhios.

Cuan shean lay two days' hard riding from Ros dhomhain along the coast road, and Ailean covered it in a day and a half on a Board horse he was not entirely sure he had the right to requisition, arriving with his satchel soaked through from a squall that had caught him an hour short of the town and a headache that did not leave him until well after the ship was under way. Cuan shean was the harbour where the first humans had come ashore, generations back, and it wore the fact the way an old man wears a scar he has stopped noticing: a stone laid into the quay near the harbour-master's steps, worn nearly smooth, that the town's children still touched for luck on their way to the boats, though none of them could have said, if asked, luck against what.

The ship was *An Sireadh*, the Seeking, a broad-bellied trader taken into the council's service for the voyage and fitted with stores for two months where her master judged one would likely do. Her master was Aonghas mac Mhurchaidh, a Cuan shean man near fifty who had spent the whole of his working life on the western coast and had never, by his own telling, been further from sight of land than a bad night's blow had once carried him against his will. He looked Ailean over on the quay, asked whether he had ever been to sea, and, on being told he had not, said only that he would learn quickly or he would learn nothing at all, since the sea did not trouble itself to teach a man twice.

They sailed on the morning tide of the eleventh, out past the harbour mouth and the worn stone and the priests of Manannan who stood at the water's edge in their grey and said the old prayer over her wake, though whether it was said for the ship's going or her coming back Ailean did not think to ask until it was too late to. The wind held fair for the first two days, and the coast fell away behind them, the north-western capes rising and then themselves sinking below the horizon on the third morning, and after that there was nothing.

Ailean had expected the sea to be many things. He had not expected it to be grey, in the particular, total way it proved to be: grey water under a grey sky with no line between them that a man could swear to, day after day, so that a watch on deck came, after the first week, to feel less like looking outward than like standing inside a room whose walls had been painted the one colour and left otherwise bare. He kept the log as he had been told, the wind, the heading, the soundings where soundings could be got, and found, within a fortnight, that the log's plain columns were becoming the only thing aboard that still felt like solid ground under his hand, since the deck itself no longer troubled to hold still.

There was mist for the better part of nine days running, thick enough that the bow could not always be seen from the wheel, and Aonghas kept them under short sail and a man forward with a horn the whole of it, sounding at intervals into a grey that gave nothing back. Twice the lookout swore to a shape off the starboard bow, low and dark, and twice, when they stood in toward it, it proved to be nothing: once a trick of the mist folding on itself, once, as near as anyone could afterward agree, a whale surfacing and gone before a second man could be brought to see it. Ailean logged both, in his flattest hand, as *sighted, not confirmed*, and privately thought that the phrase would do very well for the whole of the voyage, if anyone ever asked him to sum it in four words.

No land came. No second ship crossed their wake, no gull further from shore than a gull might wander on a bad day, no sign that the passage the humans had used had ever run through this water at all, though Aonghas held their heading as true as dead reckoning without a fixed star to check it by could be held, and did not, in Ailean's hearing, once suggest they had strayed from it. On the twenty-ninth day out, with the stores a third gone and the crew saying less to each other than they had said in the first week, Aonghas came to where Ailean sat with the log and said, without preamble, that they would come about at noon.

"We've not found it," Ailean said, not quite a question.

"We've not found anything," Aonghas said. "Which is not the same thing, and I'd thank you to log it as I've said it and not as you'd like it plainer. I've sailed this coast since before you were breeched, and I tell you there is a difference between a sea that has been searched and found empty and a sea that has simply refused, this once, to show itself to us. I do not know which we have here. I know we have food for three weeks if we turn now and food for a week and a scramble home if we do not, and I am not master of this ship to find out by starving which of the two guesses was the truer one." He looked out at the grey a moment longer. "Log it your way if you must. But we come about at noon."

Ailean logged it, in the end, as plainly as he could manage: that *An Sireadh* had held a north-about heading for twenty-nine days from Cuan shean without sighting land, ship, or certain sign of any passage, and that her master, judging the stores would not answer a longer search, had ordered her about. He did not write what he thought the log could not properly hold, which was that the grey water had felt, by the end, less like an empty sea than like a door that had been shut in a house whose owner no longer answered a knock, and that a man could search the doorstep as long as he liked without ever learning whether the house behind it was empty or merely silent.

## III. Of the Way Niall Came

They raised the north-western capes again on the eighteenth day of the return, and Seann Skell in the west two days after that, and it was only as the town's roofs came clear through the last of the mist that Ailean understood, counting the days on his fingers against the log, that he had missed the crowning.

It had been fixed, before he sailed, for the nineteenth of an t-Ògmhios, and he had reckoned, setting out on the eleventh, that a month's voyage would see him home with days to spare. The month had run to nine weeks. Niall had been crowned at Cathair dhearg by Macha's rite eleven days before *An Sireadh* came about, and crowned again, as far as any single fact of it reached Ailean, in every account he was given afterward, which was to say briefly, and without much that a man who had not stood in the crowd could add to.

The priests of Manannan came down to the quay at Seann Skell as she made her berth, the same grey-robed order, or near enough to be taken for the same, that had said the prayer over her going, and said the old words again at the harbour mouth as she came in: *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte*, Manannan, keep the sea shut, though whether they meant it now as thanks that the mist had let her home again or as a plainer wish that the sea should stay shut and save the council the cost of a second voyage, Ailean could not have said, and did not ask. He stood at the rail with the log under his arm, the paper gone soft and faintly salt-stiffened from nine weeks in a sea chest, and watched the town come up to meet him, and felt, more than anything else he could afterward put a name to, the particular flatness of a man returning from a thing that had cost him nine weeks of his life to a country that had, in his absence, quietly finished doing something else entirely.

He learned the fact of the crowning properly that same evening, in a tavern near the harbour where the ship's crew had gone to spend what little pay was owed them before the road home, and where a innkeeper, asked idly by one of the sailors whether anything had happened while they were away, laughed and said that the kingdom had got itself a coronation and they had missed the whole of it, and here, look, it was in his own ledger, since the debt a customer owed him for the feast night had been settled that same week and he had thought it worth a note.

He showed them the page. It was an ordinary reckoning, a name, a sum, a line struck through where the sum had been paid, and above the strike-through, in the innkeeper's own untidy hand, four words set down as casually as a man notes the weather: *mar a thàinig Niall*, the way Niall came, against a debt that had been paid, the innkeeper explained, without the usual argument over whether the feast had truly been worth what was asked for it. "It went so smooth," the man said, "the crowning, I mean, no quarrel over it that anyone south of here heard tell of, that folk have taken to saying it of anything that goes the same way. A debt paid without a fight. A door opened without a lock picked. Mar a thàinig Niall." He seemed pleased with the phrase, the way a man is pleased with a thing he half suspects he did not invent but cannot now recall where he had it from.

Ailean copied the four words into his own private book that night, under the day's date, beside nothing else, since there was nothing else about the crowning he had seen with his own eyes to set beside them. He thought, writing it, of the council chamber he had left nine weeks before, the twelve chairs, the gold-lettered minute-books, and tried to imagine the rite itself, Macha's priests, the crowd, the new king's face, and found he could summon none of it with any confidence, having been at sea the whole of the day it happened and having only, now, four words in another man's ledger and a room full of sailors who had missed it as thoroughly as he had. He wrote, under the four words, a line of his own, and then, reading it back, struck it through, because it seemed to him too much like the kind of thing a clerk ought not to set down where another clerk might one day read it and wonder what he had meant by it. The struck line does not survive; only the mark of its striking does, a single stroke of ink across a space where four more words had briefly stood.

South again, in the weeks it took to make Cathair dhearg by the coast road, he asked what he could of anyone who had been present, and pieced together, from three or four accounts that agreed in their outline and differed in every particular, that the rite had gone as such rites were said to go: the horses, the old words, a crowd larger than the capital had held in living memory, and a young man, Niall, five-and-twenty by the reckoning Ailean afterward heard given, crowned without a voice raised against him. It was, everyone agreed, entirely unremarkable, in the way that only the smoothest passages of a country's history are permitted to be unremarkable, and Ailean came, in time, to understand that he had missed nothing more dramatic than the absence of drama, though it took him the better part of that same winter to stop resenting, quietly and without ever saying so aloud, the nine weeks of grey water that had cost him the chance to judge that absence for himself.

## IV. Of the Edge of the Map

He was back at Cathair dhearg by the second week of an t-Samhain, and set, almost at once, to a table in a room he had not worked in before, with the humans' district surveys spread before him under a lamp that smoked more than it lit, and told to draw the shires.

It was, on its face, simple work. The council had decided, in Ailean's absence, that the kingdom would be divided into siorrachdan, each named for its seat, and that the humans' own district lines, being the only whole survey of the island there was, would serve for the drawing, whatever anyone privately thought of governing a restored kingdom on a stranger's paperwork. Ailean's part was to copy the lines onto a fresh sheet in ink that would hold, and set the Dia-thìris names against them, town by town, so that the finished map could be shown to the twelve without embarrassment.

He worked at it through the short days of an Dùbhlachd, the lamp burning from mid-afternoon, tracing the humans' fine black lines with a rule and a steady hand that the months at Ros dhomhain and the weeks at sea had, between them, made a good deal steadier than it had been the year before. The lines ran clean and confident across the southern half of the survey, shire after shire falling into place along roads and rivers the humans had measured to a fineness Ailean privately admired more than he was willing to say aloud in a room where such an admission might be misheard as praise of the humans generally. It was only when his rule reached the north-western quarter of the sheet that the lines began to thin, and then, a hand's breadth further on, to stop altogether, giving way to a stretch of pale, unmarked paper across which someone in the humans' own hand had written a single word Ailean had learned, by then, well enough to read without help: *unsurveyed*.

He sat with the rule in his hand for some while, looking at the blank paper and at the coastline beyond it that the map-makers, whoever they had been and whatever had stopped them, had troubled to draw in outline at least, a rough grey wash of cliff and inlet with no lines dividing it into anything a shire could be cut from. He knew the coast the blank paper covered, or knew of it, the windy country beyond the great moss where the fowlers and sealers kept to themselves and the council's writ had never, so far as Ailean had ever heard, been tested against anyone's will to resist it. He had never been there. Few at Cathair dhearg had.

He drew the shire lines as far as the survey carried them and no further, and sat some while longer with the pen dry in his hand, turning over how the gap ought to be entered, since a map that simply stopped, with nothing written to say why, seemed to him a worse thing to hand the council than a map that admitted its own edge honestly. In the end he wrote, in the small space of clear paper left beside the blank stretch, a single line, no longer than the ones he had been trained to write for every disposal that crossed his table: *the windy coast is not in the survey*. He looked at it a moment, thought of striking it and writing something fuller, some explanation of the humans' unfinished work or the council's untested writ, and did not. The line said what was true and no more than was true, and he had learned enough, in two years of clerking, to trust a short true line over a long uncertain one.

Two further gaps troubled the drawing before the map was done. Out past the eastern cape and off the northern coast of the Tuathaich country, two rocks stood that the humans' Administration had kept as customs stations of their own, each with a station-house and nothing else, no town near enough to lend either its name. The council had no rule, that Ailean could find in any minute passed down to him, for folding one small holding into the shire of its nearest neighbour, and no one troubled, that autumn, to write one. He entered both as shires in their own right, each taking the name of the island it sat on, Eilean dhìreach and Eilean ìseal, and noted beside each, in the same flat hand, that the shire held no town and its dues would need collecting by a maor who kept his own boat.

At Ceann mhòr, in the last week of the drawing, a different kind of gap wanted filling. The Tuathaich fishermen of the town, whose fathers had broken the locks of the old harbour store in the first winter of the settling and shared out what they found, had gone on holding a rough sort of market there ever since, on their own custom and nobody's grant, and the council, drawing the northern shires for the first time onto paper that might one day be shown to a court, had to decide what, if anything, to do about a market that already existed and had never been asked for leave to. Word came down, in the last days before Ailean's map was to be shown, that the council would grant it: a market right for the north-west coast, its court to sit in the same harbour store whose locks had been broken, fish and oil and the goods of the concession farms to be sold there under whatever rough rule the townsfolk themselves had already, in practice, been keeping for two years without a grant to bless it. Ailean entered the right on his map in the space reserved for it, a single clause among a hundred others, and thought, setting it down, that it was very likely the smallest piece of writing he had done all season and, of everything on the sheet, the one thing on it that the people it named had already made true for themselves before anyone at Cathair dhearg troubled to write it so.

The finished map went before the council in the last week of an Dùbhlachd, unrolled across the same table the twelve had sat their minute-books beside since the autumn before, and was received, so far as Ailean could tell from his place at the clerks' table, without any of the twelve remarking particularly on the blank stretch in its north-western quarter, or asking what lay beyond a line that read, in a clerk's plain hand, that the windy coast was not in the survey. It was approved, filed, and copied for the shire offices, and the copying, in time, carried Ailean's four words to every maor's court on the island, unremarked in every one of them as far as he ever learned, a short true line about a coast nobody at Cathair dhearg had troubled to survey, sitting now, folded into the kingdom's own map, exactly where the survey itself had stopped and gone no further.

Ailean walked back to his lodging that night by the last of the light, past the harbour where *An Sireadh* lay careened for her winter repairs, her hull scraped bare of nine weeks' weed, and thought, not for the first time that season, of the grey water beyond the north-western capes and the blank paper beyond the great moss, two edges of two different maps that had each, in their own way, told him the same thing: that a kingdom's writ, like a ship's log, ran only as far as someone had troubled to carry it, and stopped, honestly or not, exactly where the carrying stopped, whatever lay beyond.
