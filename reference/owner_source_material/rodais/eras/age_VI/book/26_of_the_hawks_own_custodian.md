# Of the Hawk's Own Custodian

*Part Five: Of the Fall and the Search*

*SE 35 an Cèitean – SE 36 an Dùbhlachd*

## I. Of the Naming at Inis ìseal

The Hawk houses of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag had kept their own hearths and their own altars for ages without asking leave of anyone south of them, and when the houses met at Inis ìseal in an Cèitean to name a custodian of their own, the asking was not for leave either. Every house sent a witness, and most sent two, so that the low stone hall by the water stood fuller than it had stood since the roof was last mended, benches carried in from three farms and set along the walls, and the smell of wet wool and peat-smoke thick enough that the door was propped from first light to last.

They named Raonaid nic Iain, whose mother's mother had kept the altar at Inis ìseal before her and whose own hands had kept it these nine years since, and they named her without a vote taken, only a standing up house by house until the hall stood, and a shout that the youngest present, a boy sent to watch the door, carried out to the road to tell whoever passed that the Hawk had its own custodian now, as the houses to the south had had theirs these thirty years.

She was not a young woman by then, past the years when a house sends its unmarried daughters to keep an altar and long past the years when anyone in the hall would have thought to ask whether she was fit for the office; she had buried a husband and raised two sons on the altar's own upkeep, gull-eggs and a widow's share of the fishing, and had sat the long vigils of the Hawk's own nights, the ones the southern Rite kept only at its greatest feasts, every year of the nine without missing one. When the shout went up she did not answer it at once, standing at the front of the hall with her hands folded into her shawl, and it was Murchadh Ruadh of Baile fhionn, the oldest man in the room, who crossed to her and set a strip of white feather into her hand, plucked, he said, from no living bird but kept in his own house three generations against a day the Hawk houses might want a custodian to give it to.

"You'll wear it or you won't," he told her, "but it's yours now either way."

She wore it, pinned at her collar, for the rest of that day and every day after that anyone in the north ever saw her.

The white billet on the shire's own arms, a plain bar the roll had given Siorrachd Cnoc bheag thirty years back for no reason any living clerk could give, hung on a board at the back of the hall that same afternoon, and more than one witness, filing out into the wet evening, looked at it and said to whoever walked beside them that a shire whose hills held the oldest hawk-altars on the island ought by rights to carry a hawk on its own seal and not a bar that meant nothing to anybody. Nobody thought, that evening, that the saying was more than an evening's grumble. The houses had said as much before, at other namings, and the roll had never once answered them.

Each house that had sent a witness sent also, as the custom of a naming asked, something of its own hearth to be set on the altar's own step before the hall broke, a handful of meal, a coil of new-spun wool, a jar of the honey the moor bees made in Cnoc bheag's own heather, so that by the time the last house had come forward the step held more than the altar would use in a season and the overflow was carried out afterward to whichever door in the town had the least behind it that winter. It was a small thing, the giving, and none of the five witnesses who later carried word of the naming south to Cathair dhearg thought it worth putting in their telling, but Raonaid, standing at the step through the whole slow giving of it, would say afterward that the shout had told her the hall's mind, but the step filling, house by house, with more than any one house could easily spare, had told her the houses' hearts, and it was the second telling she carried longer.

The rain that had held off through the naming came on hard as the last witnesses left, and those with the furthest roads home, the farms above Baile fhionn and the two lone houses out past the moor's edge, stayed the night in the hall rather than ride it, sleeping on the benches they had carried in that morning, so that Raonaid's first night as custodian was spent, whether she had planned it or not, with a full house of the Hawk asleep around her altar and the rain loud on a roof three farms had lately mended between them.

## II. Of the Letter from Seann Skell in the South

Word of the naming reached Seann Skell on the southern coast inside the month, carried, as such word always was, by a fish-buyer's factor who thought no more of the telling than he thought of the weather he reported in the same breath. The house of the Rite's high custodian sat above the harbour there, built to the design of the man who had first held the office, Dòmhnall mac Thormoid, eleven years dead by then and long since answered for by a successor the north knew only by his letters, a careful hand that gave away nothing of the man who held it.

The letter that came back to Inis ìseal that autumn was three lines long and cost, the houses reckoned afterward, more in the riding of it than the paper it was written on was worth. It named no fault in Raonaid nic Iain, gave no reason a house could argue with, and said only that the high custodian's own order of worship, written and read at every seating south of Cnoc bheag these thirty years, knew of no office called the custodian of the Hawk houses and could not, for want of knowing it, own one. It was signed with the seal the letter's own writer never troubled to explain, a seal the north had never seen before and never troubled to ask after, and it was read aloud once in the hall at Inis ìseal and then folded and put away, not burned, not answered, only kept, in the way a house keeps a thing it means to look at again.

"He doesn't own her," Murchadh Ruadh said, when the reading was done and the hall sat quiet with it, "so we'll carry on as though he had."

Nobody in the hall thought that a strange thing to say, and nobody troubled, that autumn or after, to write south again asking the high custodian to change his mind. The houses kept their own vigils as they had kept them before the letter came, brought their own disputes to Raonaid as a custodian's disputes had always been brought to whoever the altar answered to, and let the three lines from Seann Skell sit in their folded state at the back of the hall as a fact about the south rather than a judgment on the north, which was, in its own quiet way, the only answer the letter ever got.

Raonaid herself read the letter over twice more before it went into its keeping-place, once aloud to Murchadh Ruadh alone and once again silently, by herself, at the altar the night after. She was neither young enough to be stung by the refusal nor old enough, she told Murchadh, to have stopped caring what a house in the south thought of a house in the north, and she said, folding the letter the second time, that a woman could hold both those things at once without either one winning out over the other. The fish-buyer's factor who had first carried the word north was asked, the next time his cart came through Inis ìseal, whether the house at Seann Skell had said anything more of the matter to anyone in the port below it, and he answered, with the flat honesty of a man who had carried a great many messages he understood not at all, that nobody south of the harbour had spoken of the Hawk houses since the letter went, one way or the other, as though the asking and the answering of it had already used up whatever the south meant to say on the matter for a good while yet.

It was noticed, that winter and the winters after, that no house of the Hawk sent its dead south to be buried by any rite but its own, and that no house of the Hawk sent a son or daughter south to be married under any custodian's blessing but Raonaid's, a practice none of the elder witnesses could say had ever been written down as a rule and none of the younger ones thought to question, the north simply going on keeping its own as it had always kept its own, letter or no letter.

## III. Of the Order Set Down

The Hawk's own worship had never once been written in all the ages the houses had kept it, passed altar to altar by a custodian's own mouth to the ears of whoever stood the vigil beside her, each custodian carrying the whole of it in memory and handing it on the same way, a line here worn smooth by a hundred years of telling and a line there added by some long-dead keeper whose name the order no longer carried. Raonaid had it from her mother's mother the same way, sitting the long vigils as a girl and saying the words back until her tongue held them without her mind troubling over them, and she had never once, in nine years keeping the altar on her own account, thought to set any of it down.

It was a question from one of her own sons, home from the fair road that summer and asking her plainly who would carry the order whole if she died before she had passed it to whoever came after her, that turned her to the thinking of it. She had no daughter of the altar's own line grown yet to the age of learning it, only two sons neither called to the office, and she sat with the question longer than she sat with most things that were put to her, through the long evenings of an t-Sultain when the hall's business was thin and the nights long enough for a great deal of sitting.

Her son had meant the question idly, the kind of thing a man asks his mother across a hearth without expecting an answer that troubles him, and he was startled, telling it afterward to his own wife, at how long she had sat with her hands still in her lap before she answered him at all. "It would go the way the humans' own tongue went out of Ros bheag," she told him at last, "a little forgotten every year until nobody left could say what had been lost, only that something had." He had not known what to say to that, and she had not pressed him to say anything, only risen after a while and gone out to the altar alone, and it was that same night, by her own account given years later, that she first thought of the account-book with its blank pages standing unused on a shelf where her sons kept the farm's own tallies.

She began the writing in an Dàmhair of that same year, a stub of pencil and a farmer's account-book with more blank pages in it than any account had ever needed, and she wrote slowly, sounding each line to herself before she set it down to be sure the words on the page matched the words her own mother's mother had given her rather than some smoother version her own tongue might have worn them into over nine years of saying. More than once she found a line would not come to her whole until she had gone out and stood at the altar in the cold to say it, the words sitting easier in her mouth with the stone under her hand than they ever sat at a hearth with a pencil waiting, and twice that winter she woke in the dark certain she had set a word down wrong the evening before and lit a lamp to check it against the sound of her own memory rather than trust the page until morning.

It took her the better part of a season, the book filling a page at a time between the altar's ordinary business, weddings and buryings and the small disputes over grazing that a custodian was asked to settle as often as any greater matter, and there were weeks that autumn when the book saw no new line at all, set aside on its shelf while a dying man in Baile fhionn wanted sitting with or a boundary quarrel between two farms wanted a custodian's own eye on the ground. When it was done at last, in the thin light of an t-Samhain, she read it through once from the first line to the last, aloud, alone in the hall with the door shut against the wind, to hear whether it still sounded as the order had always sounded spoken rather than written, and found, she told no one but herself that morning, that it did, near enough, and that the near enough would have to serve, because a thing spoken and a thing written were never going to be the same thing entire, however careful the hand that wrote it.

She did not show the finished book to the houses, and she did not send word south of it either. She read it aloud once, on the night of an t-Samhain's full moon, to the full gathering of the Hawk houses in the hall at Inis ìseal, the only reading any of them would hear that year or any year after, and when the reading was done she closed the book, tied it with a strip of the same white feather Murchadh Ruadh had given her at her own naming, and set it into the iron box the altar had kept its lamp-oil in since before her mother's mother's time, and turned the key.

"It's heard once a year," she told the hall, when one of the younger witnesses asked why the book was not left out for any house to read at its leisure, "the way it was always heard. Writing it down was only so the hearing wouldn't die with me. It was never meant to be read the way a factor reads a ledger."

The box went back onto its shelf behind the altar, and the key went onto a cord Raonaid wore under her shawl from that night forward, alongside the white feather at her collar, and no house that came to Inis ìseal after that ever saw the book, only heard it, once a year, in Raonaid's own voice, exactly as the houses had always heard the Hawk's order before a single word of it had ever touched a page.

## IV. Of the Hawk on the Seal

The houses' own asking, the one Murchadh Ruadh had spoken at Raonaid's naming without much thought of its ever coming to anything, was carried to Cathair dhearg that same an t-Samhain by a delegation of five, Raonaid among them though she had never once before ridden further south than the fair at Muileann òg. She rode the two days south with the white feather still pinned at her collar and the iron box's key still on its cord, and found the capital, when the delegation reached it, louder and larger than any telling of it had prepared her for, the streets about the council's own hall crowded with clerks and petitioners from every shire on the roll, so that Murchadh Ruadh, riding at her side, remarked that a woman could stand a year's vigil at Inis ìseal and hear less noise in the whole of it than Cathair dhearg made in a single morning. They asked the council for what the houses of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag had asked at other namings and never been given: that the white billet the roll had carried on the shire's arms for thirty years be struck, and a hawk set in its place.

The council heard them in the same chamber where every other shire's petition was heard that season, a councillor reading the asking off the delegation's own written page in a voice that gave away no more of his own mind than the letter from Seann Skell had given of its writer's, while Raonaid stood at the front of the five with her hands folded into her shawl exactly as she had stood at her own naming, and answered, when a second councillor asked her plainly why a shire wanted a hawk rather than the bar it had always carried, only that the hills round Cnoc bheag had kept hawk-altars longer than the roll had kept its billets, and that a shire's arms ought by rights to say something true about the shire rather than nothing at all. The council gave them, against every expectation the delegation had ridden south carrying, exactly what they asked. No clerk on the council's own rolls could say what the billet had ever stood for, and a shire's arms that carried nothing anyone could read was, the council's own reasoning held, worth less to the roll than one that carried a mark the shire's own people would know at a glance for their own. The grant was written and sealed before the delegation had been a full week in the capital, the quickest business, Raonaid said afterward to the hall at Inis ìseal, that she had ever seen any council do, quicker by far than the letter from Seann Skell had taken to cross half the distance for half the news.

The maor of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag sent his own seal south with the grant to be recut, and it came back before the year was out, the plain billet gone from it and a white hawk set among the twelve crescents the shire's arms had always carried round its border, wings a little spread, the die-cutter's own touch rather than any pattern the houses had sent him. The maor himself broke the old seal's die on the flags of his own hall before the shire's assembled clerks, a small ceremony he had thought up himself and never afterward repeated for any other grant, and pressed the new one for the first time into a stick of wax held up so that every clerk in the room could see the hawk stand clear of it before the wax had properly cooled. It was the first change made to the roll at the asking of an order rather than a crown or a council's own reckoning, and the shire's clerks, entering the new seal into the rolls that autumn, wrote the fact of it plainly beside the old blazon they struck through, in the flat hand clerks kept for such things and nothing more.

Raonaid held the new seal in her own two hands the day it reached Inis ìseal, turning it once to catch the light off the wing the cutter had set spread rather than folded, and said nothing for a while that any of the witnesses standing round her troubled to write down. The book in the iron box behind the altar stayed locked as it had been locked the night of the reading, its key on its cord under her shawl, unread by any hand but her own and unheard by any ear but the houses' once a year; the seal, when it was pressed to the shire's next grant, showed a hawk to anyone who cared to look, north or south of Cnoc bheag, whether the high custodian at Seann Skell chose to own the house that had asked for it or not.

## V. Of What the North Carried That Year Besides

The order at Inis ìseal was not the only thing the north set down or gave up or fought over in those two years, and the Library's own later hand, gathering what Siorrachd Cnoc bheag had carried through them, set the rest beside it as its due.

At Ceann leathan in an Giblean of the following year the Còmhrag was fought as it was fought every year since the fair-wardens had first called it an old custom of arms, blades kept from the ring by a rule as old as the melee, bare hands and staves and the fighters' own weight against each other on ground already churned to mud by the crowd's own feet before the first pair was called. Six men died on the trampled ground before that year's melee was called at last, more than had ever died there in one year before, one of them a boy of Baile ghlas not yet twenty whose own mother had walked two days to stand at the ring's edge and see him fight, and stood there still when they carried him off it. The custodians of the southern Rite, who had never loved the Còmhrag and had said so quietly at every seating since it began, petitioned the fair-wardens that year to end it outright, sending a clerk with the petition written fair and sealed rather than trusting the asking to a rider's memory. The wardens read the petition in their own counting-room, above the very quays the melee's crowds filled every spring, and refused it inside the hour, on the plain ground, their answer said, that Ceann leathan lived off those crowds as much as it lived off anything the sea or the coal line brought it, and a town does not vote itself poor to please a custodian who does not keep its books.

The circus came to Ceann leathan that same autumn, An Siorcas Siubhail Do-thuigsinn pitching its tents on the trampled ground within a week of the six burials, its wagons painted in colours the mud had not yet had time to dull and its callers crying a show of tumblers and a caged wolf from the eastern hills to crowds that the fair-wardens had counted rightly would stay on after the melee rather than scatter home early with their pockets still part full. It was said afterward, though no one who said it could point to the hand that had written the letter, that the wardens themselves had paid the circus to come that particular year, the timing too neat for anyone who had watched a Còmhrag before to call it chance, and the boy of Baile ghlas's own mother, so the telling went, walked past the tents on her way home without once turning her head toward them.

The railway reached Ceann leathan the same season, the last miles of embankment laid through ground the coal line's engineers had put off for two summers running for the softness of it, driven pile by pile until it would bear the weight, so that coal might be carried down to a second harbour and shipped from quays that had known nothing heavier than a fishing boat's catch before that year. The first coal-ship out of the new quays loaded by lamplight the following an Dùbhlachd, her master reckoning the tide would not wait on daylight and the shore gang working the chutes through a night colder than any of them had dressed for, so that the record of it, kept by the harbour clerk more from habit than from any sense that the night wanted remembering, noted only the hour and the tonnage and the men's own complaint of the cold, nothing more. The town's own walls, built to hold the Ceann leathan the fair had made of it, held nothing of the Ceann leathan the coal line made of it after, and by the second winter the quays had spread a good half-mile beyond the old gate with no wall following them, so that a stranger riding in from the coast road could no longer say with any certainty where the old town ended and the new one began.

On the west coast that same year Ros bheag opened its own yard for the building of coal-ships, the first yard the western ports had kept for themselves rather than sending south to Ros dhomhain for every hull they needed built or mended, its first keel laid on ground that had been a drying-green for nets the summer before. The shipwrights of Ros dhomhain, whose guild had held the whole coast's building trade to itself since before any of them now living could remember a rival yard, carried their protest to the guild court at Cathair dhearg and lost it there in a single sitting, the court's own ruling holding that a guild's charter bound its members to the guild's own standard of work and not to any single yard's monopoly of the coast. The eldest of the Ros dhomhain masters, who had ridden south himself to speak the protest, was heard to say on the road home that he had lost more trade to a single morning's ruling than his own father had lost in forty years of storms, and no one who heard him say it troubled to tell him he was wrong. Ros dhomhain's shipwrights went on building what work still came to them, and Ros bheag's new yard took the rest, and within the year the western ports no longer sent so much as a rowing-boat south for mending.
