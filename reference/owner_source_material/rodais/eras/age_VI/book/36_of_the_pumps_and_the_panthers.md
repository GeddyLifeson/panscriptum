# Of the Pumps and the Panthers

*SE 61 an t-Ògmhios – SE 63 an Lùnastal*

> A crossing is a crossing, and a hungry year is a hungry year, and neither waits on the other so exactly that a man may set his harvest by it. I set mine down all the same, and struck it through when the wheat proved me wrong, since a chronicle that will not own its own bad guesses is worth less than the guessing.
>

## I. Of the Street Named for the Pit

The copper at Àth fhiadhaich drew men the way any new working draws them in a kingdom whose old one is thinning, and by the second summer of the digging the town had built, on the slope above the ford, a street of new stone houses for the miners come down from An Sloc Mòr's own failing galleries, men who had learned their trade in one pit and carried it, wage and tools and all, to another. The town's own council named the street before the last house on it had a roof, in the way a council names a thing quickly so as not to leave the naming to whoever finishes building last, and the name it chose was for the pit the miners had come from rather than the one they had come to: Sràid an t-Sluic, so that a man walking up from the ford at Àth fhiadhaich passed under a sign naming a mountain he could not see from any window on the street at all.

The house of Na Seann Spioradan at Àth fhiadhaich, which had given the new copper working no blessing when it opened, gave the naming no blessing either, and its keeper, the same woman who had told the Board's factor two summers before that copper dug for a wage was copper dug like any other stone, told the council now, by the word that reached Tormod Pinn at Muileann chaol some months after and which he judged worth the keeping, that a street should be named for the ground it stood on and not for the ground its people had left, and that a town which named its own streets after another town's mountain risked forgetting which mountain it lived under. The council heard her out, as councils do, and kept the name, there being no other ready to hand and no time, with winter coming on and roofs still unfinished, to argue one out. Tormod set the exchange down in four lines, the keeper's words nearly whole and the council's answer reduced to the one fact that mattered, that the name stood; he had learned young, from the chronicler who had trained him, that a chronicle owed a speaker her own words and owed a council only its own decision, the two debts being of a different weight entire.

The miners themselves, by every account that reached an Leabharlann that year, troubled the name less than their own council or their own priesthood did, a man newly down from An Sloc Mòr's own thinning galleries having, as Tormod judged when he set the matter down, a keener interest in the roof over his family's own new-built house than in the word painted above its door; two of the households on the street had brought with them, folded in a chest with the rest of their goods, small lumps of coal from An Sloc Mòr's own upper galleries, kept less for any use than for the plain fact of having carried something out of a mountain that had otherwise given their households nothing back for the digging. He set down, in the same entry and in fewer words still, what little came to him that season out of the north: that a man there had begun trying to draw a fuel from the waste of the mines, to stand in the coal's place, and that the trying had come to nothing. Tormod wrote the sentence once and moved on to the next matter waiting on his desk, and it is very nearly the whole of what the record of those years troubles to say of it.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, who had come to an Leabharlann three years before as a clerk of the second circuit and been kept on since at Tormod's own asking, copied both entries fair into the day-book that evening, a task she had done for him most evenings of those three years, and asked him, setting down her pen, whether a street's wrong name mattered enough to trouble a chronicle with four lines when the season had brought harder matters. Tormod told her that a chronicle worked as a net rather than a court, taking in what came its way rather than weighing each thing first against what mattered more, and that a net which let the small fish through on the grounds that they were small ended, after enough seasons, holding nothing at all worth the hauling. She said this sounded like a saying he had used on her before, and he allowed, not looking up from the letter he had begun on the desk's other corner, that a chronicler who found himself a new saying every year had probably stopped chronicling and started only talking, and that he hoped, at five-and-seventy, not yet to have crossed that particular line.

## II. Of the Galleries Let Go

The letter he had begun that evening was to the Board's own clerk at Cathair mhòr, asking, as he had asked twice before that year without an answer worth the copying, what truth lay behind the word that had reached Muileann chaol from three separate quarters that An Sloc Mòr's lowest galleries stood flooded and the Board's own pumps idle above them. The answer came at last in the new year, a single sheet signed by a clerk whose name Tormod did not know, saying that the lowest workings had been let stand under water since the turn of the season, the cost of running the pumps against so little coal as the lowest seams still gave having outrun, by the Board's own reckoning, whatever that coal was worth at the fair; the sheet called it a pause, in the Board's own hand, and gave no day by which the pause was expected to end.

Tormod rode south that spring to see the thing himself, a journey he made perhaps once in three years and only when a report troubled him past the patience of a second letter, and stood at the pit-head above Achadh dhomhain on a morning cold enough that the pump-house's own chimney, drawing no fire that day, showed no more smoke than the empty sheds beside it. The overseer who walked him down to the lip of the lowest shaft, a man Tormod had met twice before in twenty years of such visits and each time greyer, told him that the water had come up eleven fathoms in the flooded galleries and showed no sign yet of finding its own level, and that the pumps which might have held it down had stood cold since the turn of the season on the Board's own order, an order the overseer read to him from a slip of paper he kept folded in his coat as though he expected, someday, to need to prove he had not imagined it. Tormod asked him whether the Board meant the pause for a season or for good, and the overseer said that a pause a man's own coat had to carry proof of was a pause that had already outlasted whatever the word was meant to promise, and that he for his own part called it by its right name among the other overseers, though never where a Board clerk might hear him say it.

The pumps themselves, three great engines the Board's own founders had cast at Cathair mhòr thirty years before, when the lowest galleries had still paid for the coal to run them, stood drawn out of their housings and loaded onto flat wagons before the summer was old, sold, by the Board's own notice, to the harbour works at Seann Bhral in the south, where a new dry dock wanted draining faster than its own smaller engines could manage. Tormod watched the first of the three go down Slighe a' Ghuail from a siding above Achadh dhomhain, the engine lashed under canvas against the road's own dust and rain, and thought, watching it, of the wool-buyer's fact that had reached Caol mhòr some years before, that a market goes where the coin already is; a pump, it seemed to him, went where the water was, and cared no more than any bolt of cloth for the ground it had been built to serve. He wrote in his own book that evening, in words he judged too plain for the day-book's own hand and kept instead for himself, that a kingdom which sold the engines that held its own mountain dry to drain another man's harbour had answered, without troubling to say so anywhere in its own minute, the question the search of ten years before had failed outright to answer, and had answered it the way such questions are answered oftenest, by a bill of sale and not by a chronicle's own reckoning.

The overseer at Achadh dhomhain wrote to him once more that autumn, a shorter letter than the first, to say that the second pump had followed the first to Seann Bhral and the third stood ready to go, and that the men who had tended the three of them between them, six in all, had been found work on the working galleries still above water, the Board's own notice calling it no loss to any household, which the overseer, signing his own name this time rather than none, judged worth passing on without comment of his own, there being comment enough, he wrote, in the plain fact for anyone troubling to read it.

## III. Of the Dry Dock at Seann Bhral

The harbour-master of Seann Bhral in the south sent his return to Muileann chaol that winter as he sent it every winter, in the long thin hand of a man who had learned his letters counting barrels, and Tormod, who had kept Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd long enough to know which shires wrote more than they were asked and which less, read it with more attention than a fishing port's return usually drew from him, because it was the first word he had had of where An Sloc Mòr's pumps had gone to.

Seann Bhral on the southern coast had been the greatest fishing port of the kingdom for near forty years by then, since the year it first landed more fish than Ros dhomhain and let its curing-sheds run from four to eleven along the whole length of the harbour, and it had long wanted a dock in which a hull could be stood dry and worked on without waiting for a spring tide and a beach. The town had begun one some years before, cut into the rock of the harbour's inner corner behind a gate of oak and iron, and had found, as towns that cut into rock below the tide find, that the sea came into the cut faster than any engine the town owned could put it out again. The return said that the three engines of An Sloc Mòr had come down Slighe a' Ghuail one after another through the summer and autumn, the first in an t-Iuchar and the last before the herring, each on its flat wagon under canvas, and had been set on a bed of masonry at the dock's head by men the Board sent with them, and that the dock had been pumped dry for the first time on the ninth day after the third was fired.

It said as well what they burned. The harbour bought their coal at the fair's price, like any other buyer on the coast, and the harbour-master set down the cost of a day's pumping beside the cost of a day's hire of the dock, and found the second the larger by a margin he thought worth the setting down, and closed the matter there, the harbour being in the business of hulls and not of mountains.

The first hull stood into the dock was a coal-ship out of Ceann leathan with her keel sprung on the bar, which the harbour-master thought a joke good enough to enter in a return, since the pumps that had drained the galleries that filled her hold were now draining the dock that would mend her. The second was a fishing smack of the town's own. By the year's end eleven hulls had stood dry in the dock, and the shipwrights of Seann Bhral, who had been used to careening on the hard below the curing-sheds and cursing the tide, had taken the dock's head for their own and put up a shed over it. The fitters the Board sent down with the engines stayed a month to teach the harbour's own smiths the keeping of them, and then went home up the line.

Tormod set the return beside the overseer's two letters from Achadh dhomhain and read the three together at his own desk, as he had once watched the first pump go down the line from the siding above the pithead. He wrote the facts into Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd in their order: the three engines sold by the Board; the dock at Seann Bhral in the south drained by them; the first hull docked; eleven by the year's end. He added no line of his own to the entry. In the book he kept for himself he wrote a line longer, that the engines which had been cast at Cathair mhòr to keep the mountain's galleries dry had outlived, in one summer's journey, the purpose they were cast for, and had taken up another with no more trouble than a man takes up a new tool, and that a kingdom might do worse than learn from its pumps.

He noted too, because it was the kind of thing he noted, that the return from Seann Bhral named the Board's fitters by name and trade, as the Board's own notice had not, and that the harbour-master had entered the three engines under the names the fitters had told him the Achadh dhomhain men used for them among themselves, which were a woman's name, a saint's and a horse's, and which appeared in no paper the Board had ever sent to an Leabharlann. He copied the three names into the margin of his entry, and asked an Leabharlann's clerk who filed the return to file it under the harbour and not under the Board, since it was the harbour, and not the Board, that had troubled to write it.

## IV. Of Imrich nam Pantar

The word that reached Muileann chaol from the south-west that same summer came by a rounder road, a shire's own maor writing first to the Board of a stock loss he judged too large for the ordinary account of wolves and weather, and the Board's clerk, having no ledger of his own fit to hold it, passing the letter on to an Leabharlann as a thing more fit for a chronicle than a rent-roll. The maor wrote that a great host of the great cats the south-west herders called panthers, tawny beasts the size of a two-year calf that came down out of the high passes by the same road every so many years and had not been seen, by any herder's own memory then living, in longer than any of them could put a number to, had crossed the hill country near Baile dhomhain that spring by the old road the maps still called Imrich nam Pantar, and that for the better part of a month the flocks pastured along that road had lost more stock to the crossing than the shire's own dues could easily bear the loss of.

Tormod set the letter beside an older paper an Leabharlann kept, a list drawn up in the days of An Aois Naomh naming the years such a crossing had come before, a list he had first found reason to open only twice in the whole of his own chronicling, and found the crossing named there under a year whose distance from his own he worked out with a care he gave few other sums, the interval between one crossing and the last being, by the old list's own reckoning, longer than any single herder's working life, so that no man now living at Baile dhomhain had seen it before and none now living there was like to see it again. He wrote the entry longer than any other that year, setting the maor's own count of the stock lost beside the old list's count from the crossing before it, the two figures agreeing near enough that he judged the coincidence worth remarking, and noted, at the entry's foot, that the old list named the year following such a crossing a hungry one, the cats' passing having been reckoned, by whatever reasoning An Aois Naomh's own tellers had kept, to trouble the land's own bearing for a season after as much as it troubled the flocks during.

The herders themselves, by the second half of the maor's letter, had done what the old road's own name promised they would, driving what stock they could off the path beforehand and keeping Imrich nam Pantar as a season of watching rather than working, and had lost, in the end, fewer beasts than the first week's count had feared, though more than any recent year had cost them. The tanners of Baile ghorm on the coast below, hearing of the crossing before the herders had finished counting their own losses, sent buyers up into the hills within the fortnight to take the hides of whatever cats the herders' own dogs and spears had brought down defending the flocks, paying a price the herders judged fair for a skin they had no other use for and the tanners judged cheap for a hide unlike any that came to their vats in an ordinary year. Baile ghorm's own tanners cured the skins through that summer by a craft the maor's letter did not trouble to describe and Tormod did not think to ask after, and Fèill Muileann òg the following year saw, for the first time in any chronicler's own memory, saddles and belts of panther-hide sold under the Baile ghorm mark, a fashion that took the southern buyers by surprise enough that the mark's own price doubled twice before the fair's ten days were out.

Tormod wrote of the saddles too, the following spring, a shorter entry than the crossing's own, noting only that the trade in the hides had outlasted the crossing by longer than any single season and that Baile ghorm's tanners meant, by every word that reached him, to keep the mark going as long as a hide came to hand to sell under it. Beathag, copying the entry, asked him whether it was proper for Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd to note a trade that might, for all either of them could tell that year, come to nothing within a season or two, and Tormod told her that a chronicle's business was what a year had actually done and not what a later year might undo, and that a note honestly wrong in ten years' time cost an Leabharlann less than a note never set down at all for fear of being wrong.

## V. Of the Lent Lamps and the Yard at Muileann chrom

The same summer that carried the panthers south brought a smaller trouble north, one that reached Tormod first through the coal-line's own carters, who complained of it at every siding between Muileann mhin and Muileann chaol long before any shire's maor troubled to write it down. The lamp-makers of Muileann mhin, whose forges turned out the greater part of every shaft-lamp the vein-towns burned, had halved their own making that year for want of coal enough to fire the forges properly, the coal that reached Muileann mhin coming thinner and dearer with every season An Sloc Mòr's own galleries gave less of it up, and a town that made lamps by burning coal to shape the iron those same lamps then burned more of had found, that year, the two ends of its own trade closing on each other like a door.

The pits themselves, left short of new lamps and unwilling to send men below without light enough to see the roof by, took to lending shaft-lamps from gallery to gallery and pit to pit as need moved, a habit no charter had ever set down rules for and Comann nan Gualadairean, finding itself with no clause to govern it either, met by keeping a minute-book at its own hall, a plain ledger in which any overseer borrowing a dozen lamps from a neighbouring pit entered the number, the day, and his own name against repayment. Tormod sent to the Guild's hall for a copy of the book's first year, judging it a thing an Leabharlann ought to hold against the day some later chronicler asked how the pits had managed a coal so scarce it could not always light its own diggers, and found in it, when the copy came, more names entered against debts than names entered against repayments, a ledger that told, in its own dry column of borrowings, a plainer account of the vein's own thinning than any letter the Board had troubled to write him that year.

The trouble at Muileann chrom, when it came that same summer, was of a different order, and reached him first as three lines in a Board notice and only later, and at greater length, in a letter from the custodian of the yard's own small house of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, a man Tormod had corresponded with before on lesser matters and trusted more than he trusted the Board's own notices. The yard families who kept the coal trains running between An Sloc Mòr and the coast, seeing their own wages cut that spring by an order the Board judged no more than the coal's own falling price required, stopped the trains outright for three days running, the first stoppage of its kind the line had known since it was laid, and put forward as their spokesman a man of one-and-forty named Dòmhnall Ruadh mac Ailein, whose father had laid the first rail at Muileann chrom with his own hands and who had worked the yard himself since he was a boy of twelve.

Dòmhnall Ruadh, by the custodian's account, asked the Board's own factor for nothing more than the wage restored to what it had stood at the year before, and told him, in words the custodian judged worth the copying whole, that a family which had laid the rail, tended it, and kept it running through every season the line had known could not be paid less for the same work merely because the mountain the rail served had grown poorer, the mountain's own poverty being the Board's misfortune and not the yard's making. The Board's factor answered, on the third day, that the wage would stand as it had before the cut, the stoppage having cost the Board more in halted coal than the cut itself had saved it in a season, and the trains ran again on the fourth day at the old rate; on the fifth, the Board's own notice named eleven men dismissed from the yard for their part in the stopping, Dòmhnall Ruadh among them, the settlement of the wage and the dismissal of its askers arriving, by the custodian's own dry observation, in the same week and from the same hand.

The custodian who wrote to him of it kept, besides his house's own register, a small book of his own in which he set down the yard's grievances as its families brought them to him, a habit Tormod recognised at once as a chronicler's own habit worn by a man who had never called it that, and told him so in his answering letter, adding that a house which kept such a book against the day some larger matter needed it had done an Leabharlann's own work a season before an Leabharlann knew to ask for it.

Tormod set the whole of it down at greater length than the panthers had had, the wage restored and the eleven put out being, he judged, a thing later readers would want in full rather than in summary, and wrote at the entry's close, in the day-book's own plain hand and not merely his private one this time, that a Board which answered a stoppage by giving the families their wage and taking their spokesman's own place from him had settled nothing it meant to keep settled, and that a chronicle owed its readers the plain fact of both halves of such a bargain, not only the half that looked, on the Board's own notice, like a kindness.

## VI. Of the Strike-Through

The harvest that autumn, across the whole of the south and west both, came in better than any the shires had reckoned on since before Sireadh na Dàrna Fèithe had first been spoken of, the wheat standing thick enough at Cathair fhada that the maor there wrote to Muileann chaol of granaries built for a leaner year now wanting a second storey to hold what the first year's fields had actually given. Tormod read the maor's own letter at his desk in the last week of an Lùnastal, the panthers' crossing more than a year behind the kingdom by then and the old list's warning of a hungry year to follow it a full season overdue with no want anywhere the letters reached him to bear it out, and sat some while with his own book open at the entry he had written the spring before, the ink long dry and the words, read again in a good year's light, sounding to him now less like a chronicler's careful reckoning and more like an old man's fright dressed in an old list's borrowed authority.

He took up his pen, dipped it, and drew a single line through his own sentence, the one naming the year to come a hungry one by An Aois Naomh's own reckoning, leaving the words legible beneath the stroke as an Leabharlann's own practice required of every correction, a chronicle owing its readers its wrong guesses as plainly as its right ones, and wrote beneath it, in the same hand, that the harvest of the year following the crossing had proved thick rather than thin across the south and west, and that the old list, whatever true word it had kept of crossings a dozen lifetimes gone, had kept no word this time that the wheat troubled to answer to.

Beathag, coming in from the outer room with the day's letters, found him sitting with the book still open before him and the ink not yet blotted, and asked whether he meant the line to stand, a chronicler striking through his own words being a thing she had not seen him do in the three years she had kept his book. Tormod told her that he meant it to stand exactly as struck, the line and the words both, since a chronicle that quietly rewrote its own bad year earned, season on season, the trust of no one, and that he had rather be read, seven-and-seventy years old and wrong once in a season, than be believed infallible by readers who had never troubled to check him. She blotted the page for him without being asked, as she had learned to do that summer without spoiling the stroke beneath the blotting sand, and set the book back on its stand open at the struck line, where it stood, by Tormod's own wish, through the whole of that harvest month, so that any clerk who came to the desk on Library business might read, before he read anything else that year, a chronicler's own hand owning that he had once been afraid of a summer that came in kind.
