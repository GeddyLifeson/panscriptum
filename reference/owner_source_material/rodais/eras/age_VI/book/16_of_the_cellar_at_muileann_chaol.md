# Of the Cellar at Muileann chaol

*SE 16 an Cèitean – SE 17 an Lùnastal*

> *A chur san Leabharlann.*
> To put it in the Library.
> — the saying that grew up around Ailean Leabhar's cellar, within a few years of its opening

## I. Of the Cellar Under the Stair

They lit the Bealltainn fires on the hills round Muileann chaol on the first day of an Cèitean in SE 16, as they were lit on every hill on the island that had a hill to light them on, and the smoke of the whole ring hung over the town from noon until the fires were let go down at dusk, so that a man walking in from the coast road that morning might have taken the haze for weather and thought no more of it. Ailean mac Iain took it for weather himself, riding in with two carts behind him and the council's minute-books roped down under oiled sacking against a rain that had not yet come and, by the smell of the sky, meant to. He had been the better part of a week on the road out of Cathair dhearg, the carts slow on the eastern stretches where the humans' old causeway had broken up into ruts deep enough to swallow a wheel, and he had spent most of that week turning over, without managing to settle it, the single sentence in which the council had told him what he was for.

The sentence, copied into his own book from the clerk who had read it to him, ran: *Ailean mac Iain is named keeper of the record at Muileann chaol, to receive, to order, and to keep whatever is sent to him.* It did not say what a keeper was, there being no such office before it and no one living who had held one, and it did not say what "whatever is sent" might in the end amount to. The council knew, at least, where its own records were not: not burned, so far as anyone could say, and not lost past finding. No one at Cathair dhearg could name the room any of them stood in, or say when a living hand had last opened one. Ailean had asked, before he left Cathair dhearg, what the town of Muileann chaol held that made it the place chosen, and had been told only that the custodians of the district had kept rolls there since before the Severance, in cellars under the old grain-stores, and that nobody at Cathair dhearg had gone to look at them in living memory. He took the answer as a piece of the council's habit rather than as information, the council's habit being, by sixteen years' watching, to send a thing north or west or wherever its own hands did not reach and call the sending a settlement.

The town gave him a house that had stood empty since a mercer's family died of a fever six winters before, two rooms and a loft over what had been a shop, and a boy of the town, thirteen that spring, whose mother kept the house next door and who had, in the way of a boy with nothing yet fixed to do, attached himself to the carts within an hour of their arrival on the plain grounds that they were the most interesting thing to have come up the road since the tax-rider's horse went lame outside the mill two winters back. His name was Coinneach Clàrc, son of a wright who had died in the war before the Severance and left his widow a trade in nothing but the boy's own two hands, which she hired out, that spring, for whatever a household could spare, mostly carrying and fetching. Ailean, unpacking the first cart in a light that was already going by the time he reached the third sack, told the boy he could fetch and carry for a keeper as easily as for a mercer's widow, and the boy, who had not until that hour known that a keeper was a thing a town could have, said he would, and was there the next morning before Ailean himself was dressed.

It was the townsmen, not the council's letter, who led him to the cellar. Two of them came the second evening, a maltster and a man who had held some half-remembered custodian's office under his father before him, and said, in the flat courteous way of men who have carried a thing a long time and are glad to be shot of the carrying, that there was more under the old grain-store than grain, and that it had been theirs to mind since before either of them was born, and that they had minded it as well as damp stone and a low door would let two men mind anything. They took him down a stair cut into the hillside, worn hollow in the middle of each step by more feet than either of the two men now living had climbed it, and at the bottom, by the light of a single lamp one of them carried, Ailean saw what sixteen years of a kingdom too busy settling its own affairs to look backward had left of the record of everything that came before it: rolls and bound books stacked on planks laid across trestles above a floor that stood an inch deep in water after any rain heavier than a mist, the lowest tier on every stack gone soft, and in places gone to a grey pulp that held, when he touched it, the shape of a roll without any longer holding a roll's substance.

He untied the nearest and read three lines of it by the light thrown down the stair from the doorway above, standing because there was nowhere dry to sit, and then he sent the maltster's boy running for every lamp the house next door could spare, and sent Coinneach for a table, any table, and for a stool, and for whatever oil the town could sell him before its shops shut for the night. He did not go up again himself until the small hours, by which time he had four lamps burning on a borrowed table set on two trestles of its own above the wet floor, and had read, by his own later count, some forty rolls through to their ends without once finding one that told him plainly what it was, most of the custodians' own hands having written for readers who already knew.

## II. Of the Long Reading

It took him years. The council's own minute-books came east from Cathair dhearg that same summer, four sacks of them roped onto the backs of packhorses because no cart could be spared for so light and so unregarded a cargo, and behind them, more slowly, came the boxes Ailean himself had labelled and sealed in the humans' counting-houses fourteen years before, the tithe ledgers charred at their edges and written in a hand few south of the guard-line could any longer read without a Mission reader's schooling behind them, which Ailean, alone among the clerks who had boxed them, still had. He set the boy Coinneach to learning that hand within the first winter, on the plain reasoning that a keeper who read alone would die a keeper who had kept nothing for anyone after him, and by the second winter the boy could make out a tithe entry as fast as Ailean himself, faster in a bad light, his eyes being younger.

Their days, that first year and most of the second, ran the same small round with almost no variation to mark one from the next: down the worn stair before the town's own shops were open, lamps lit and trimmed, the wet floor swept as dry as a broom could make it, and then the reading, roll after roll set out on the borrowed table and read through to whatever end it had, the findings copied into a working book Ailean kept for nothing else and the roll returned, once read, to a shelf built dry above the water-line that the town's own carpenter had raised for him that first autumn on nothing more than Ailean's own promise to settle with him out of whatever the council next sent north. There were weeks together in which nothing was found worth remarking to anyone, a roll of tithes yielding tithes and no more, and Coinneach, young enough still to want a story out of every hour he gave to the work, asked more than once whether it was always to be like this. Ailean told him, more than once, that it mostly was, and that a keeper who wanted a story every day had mistaken his trade for a teller's.

What the cellar and the sacks between them held was, in the main, numbers: heads counted, grain weighed, dues paid or owed, births and deaths noted in the clipped hand of men keeping accounts rather than telling tales, and dates given in a reckoning that named no month and no season a reader south of the mountains would know at a glance, an old count that ran backward into the thousands for anything older than what the custodians called, without ever troubling to explain to whom, the sunwise turn. Ailean had disliked that count from the first roll he untied, and disliked it more with every year he spent inside it, and said so to Coinneach often enough that the boy could by the second year finish the complaint for him before he had got three words into it: that a people should not have to name every year of its own life after the day its kin went away over the water, as though nothing since had been worth a name of its own.

So he set the copyists he by then had, four of them by SE 16's end and seven by SE 17, to a plain and unglamorous labour that took the better part of two years to reach even the near edge of finished: every date the cellar or the sacks held, turned into the civil calendar the humans had themselves brought and the kingdom had kept since the Severance, month and day exact where the roll gave them exact, season and year alone where it gave no more, and set besides into the years of an age, by a table of the ages the copyists themselves were building as they went, out of the customary tellings of Clann na Ceiste laid side by side against the king-lists the custodians' rolls preserved better than any book south of the mountains had preserved them. It was in the laying of those two records side by side, in the second winter, that the reckoners under Ailean's own hand found what neither telling alone would have shown them: that very nearly half of the whole eldest age the copyists had inherited from the custodians' own reckoning lay within the span of one man's life, Ailean Mòr, crowned in the grove and king until he fell at the Ford of Blood, so long a life that no age built to hold ordinary lives could rightly be asked to hold his as well. Ailean set the matter before his copyists on an evening in an Dàmhair, and by consent of all seven the Age of Ailean was parted from the age that had held it and counted from then on as its own, the first change the Library at Muileann chaol made to any reckoning it had not itself invented, and, as Ailean noted drily in the margin of his own working copy, very possibly the last change it would ever be believed to have the standing to make.

It was in that same season, with the copyists' table only half drawn and the cellar not yet a third read, that a clerk of the town, sent up from Cathair dhearg with a query about a boundary the council wanted settled and unable, on being told the matter would want some weeks of reading before it could be answered honestly, to hide his impatience, used a phrase that neither he nor Ailean had heard used in quite that way before: that the boundary, once Ailean had looked into it, could simply be put in the Library, *a chur san Leabharlann*, and the matter would then be settled. Ailean, who had spent two years by then learning exactly how little of what the cellar held could honestly be called settled by anything, corrected him on the spot, more sharply than the clerk's small offence deserved, and the correction, repeated afterward by the clerk himself as a piece of local colour worth carrying home, was what first fixed the phrase in the town's mouth rather than loosening it: within a season half of Muileann chaol was saying *a chur san Leabharlann* of anything it wanted done with, and saying it, more often than not, precisely to see Ailean's face when he heard it. "The Library settles nothing," he told Coinneach more than once, usually with his hand still flat on some roll that had just refused, for the third or fourth time that week, to say plainly what had actually happened in the year it claimed to record. "It only keeps."

## III. Of the Two Books

The finding that turned the first year of the reading came in an Gearran of SE 17, on a roll from the camp rolls of Àth ìseal that Ailean had set aside twice already as too far gone at its lower edge to be worth the candle it would cost to read by. He came back to it a third time only because Coinneach, going through the same stack in search of a grain tally the boy had been asked for by name, noticed a second hand in the margin, cramped and later than the roll's own body, and could not himself make out more than a name before he brought it to Ailean rather than lose the thread of it to guesswork.

The name was Cailean mac Eachainn's, and what he had written, in a margin that had survived the damp better than the roll's own text because it sat higher on the sheet, was a line of descent set down, so far as either Ailean or the boy could judge from the hand and the roll it rode on, some forty years before: the Line of Aisling, name following name in the plain unbroken form a custodian's clerk gives to a thing he expects no one to doubt and no one, until that winter, to read. Ailean had it copied fair within the week, in a hand steadier than the original's had by then become, and entered into a book he had begun the summer before and had not, until that entry, known quite what to call: a book that held nothing but lines of descent, names following names with their dates where dates could be had and without them where they could not, which he settled, after turning over three or four less plain titles with Coinneach on successive evenings, into Leabhar nan Sloinnidhean, the book of lines, and set Cailean mac Eachainn's find in it directly after another line, older still, that the cellar had already yielded that same winter: the Line of the Mason, as the custodians' rolls had taken it down long before from the recital of Clann na Ceiste.

But the camp rolls of Àth ìseal held more than lines. Set apart from the tallies and the descents, in a different ink and by what looked, from its unevenness, to be a hand writing by firelight rather than by day, the same custodian's clerk had also kept a record of what was said round the camp in the long years the roll otherwise measured only in grain and heads: the tale of the Hollow Man, told, by the clerk's own note, by an old woman of the camp who claimed to have had it from her grandmother; a telling of the white stag that differed in three particulars from the version Ailean himself had grown up on in the hills; a boast, set down word for word and plainly relished by whoever had written it down, that a captain of the camp had once made about a wolf and a winter and a door he had held alone. None of it was a line of descent, and none of it, by any measure a custodian's own office would have applied to it, was business a roll ought to have carried at all; and it was that very unfitness, Ailean came to think within the week, that had let it survive at all, tucked in where no later hand thought to look for it and no later flood thought worth the reaching.

He began a second book for it that same season, this one without any argument at all over its name, for the thing wanted no name but the plain one: Leabhar nan Aithrisean, the book of tellings, and the long years of Ailean Mòr and his children went into it beside the Hollow Man and the white stag, exactly as the camp's own clerk had set them down, without correction and without comment.

It was Coinneach who asked him, copying the second entry out fair one evening by a lamp that wanted trimming, which of the two books was the true one, the lines or the tellings, since a boy who had spent a winter learning that a roll's numbers were to be trusted and its margins were not could be forgiven for wanting the question settled before he copied any further. Ailean thought about it longer than the boy expected, long enough that Coinneach had trimmed the lamp and sat back down before the answer came.

"Both," he said. "The one keeps names. The other keeps what people said." He looked at the two books a while longer, the one thin still and orderly, the other already thicker and less so, and added, in a voice Coinneach did not at the time think to write down and regretted for years after that he had not: "Neither of them keeps what happened. Nothing we do here will ever keep that. We only keep what was set down, and set down again, as carefully as two men and a lamp can manage it."

## IV. Of the First Readers

Word that there was a Library to be read at, and not merely a cellar to be minded, spread up and down the coast roads slower than either man would have guessed, the notion of a place a man might go and be shown a record concerning his own trade or his own town being, for most who heard of it that first year, too far outside anything they had reason to expect of the kingdom's offices to be believed on a single telling. Ailean had opened the reading room, such as it was, to any who could show a letter from a shire maor vouching for their errand, on the plain grounds that a Library nobody could reach was no better kept than a cellar nobody could reach, and for the better part of a year the room he had set aside for it, a dry chamber above the old cellar with two tables and a window that at least let in more light than the stair had, stood empty of anyone but his own copyists.

The first name written in the book he kept for the purpose was a shipwright of Ros dhomhain, come the length of the coast road with a maor's letter creased soft from the carrying and a question that had, by his own account, kept him up more nights than the debt he had incurred to fund the asking of it: whether the humans, in whatever they had left behind them, had ever set down the true lines of a hull, the shape of the thing before it was built and not merely the finished boats a man could measure with his own eye and his own guesswork, which was, he said, standing in the doorway with his hat still in both hands as though the room might object to a covered head, most of what a shipwright of his own generation had ever had to work from. Ailean, who had boxed the humans' papers himself fourteen years before and drawn the shires on their surveys, had never in all that time had cause to open the crates of loose sheets marked, in his own hand, *unread*, and spent the better part of a week with the man going through sheets neither of them had ever seen unrolled, and found, folded inside a survey that had nothing else to do with ships at all, three drawings of a hull's ribs and planking that the shipwright copied out, over four days, onto his own paper with a care Ailean thought, watching him, came nearer to devotion than to any trade he had a name for. On the last of the four days the man asked, not looking up from his copying, whether the Library would keep a copy of his copy too, so that the next shipwright need not spend four days over it; Ailean said it would, and set aside a shelf for it there and then, the first shelf in the room he had built for anything but the record he had brought with him. The shipwright signed the reading-book in a hand that gave the letters of his own name more trouble than the hull-lines had, and walked back to Ros dhomhain, by his own account afterward, three days faster than he had come.

The bath-house at the warm springs below Muileann ruadh, built that same year by the Guild of Hewers out of its own funds for the men the pits had made sick with the dust, sent a fair copy of its first sick-roll to the Library unasked, in the autumn, a list of four hundred men set down by name and by the shift that had broken them, with no letter of explanation beyond a note in the guild clerk's own hand that a record of who had been sick, and of which face of which pit, seemed to the guild's officers a thing worth having somewhere it would not be lost, whatever use the kingdom's own offices chose or failed to choose to make of it. Ailean entered it in a shelf of its own, unsure for some weeks what book it properly belonged in and settling at last, on Coinneach's own suggestion, that a roll which named no line of descent and told no old tale and yet meant, plainly, to be kept, had as much claim on the Library as either.

It was the guild's roll, more than the shipwright's question, that first showed Ailean what the room above the cellar might in time become, a room read not only by clerks sent on the council's own errands but by men and women who came, letter in hand, on errands the council had never thought to ask after, and who left, when they had found what they came for, entries of their own devising for a keeper too glad of the reading to turn any of them away.

## V. Of the Five Lights

The last matter the Library took in hand that summer came to it from the council, by a warrant Ailean received in an Lùnastal and read twice before he was satisfied he had understood it rightly: the five lighthouses the humans had built round the island's coasts, kept until then by whatever hands the district happened to have to spare, were to be given keepers of the kingdom's own naming, Dia-thìreach men chosen and sworn, each bound by a warrant Ailean was asked to draft in a form the council could copy five times over without further trouble to its own clerks.

He drafted four of the warrants in an afternoon, each near enough the last that a clerk copying them side by side would have struggled to say, by sundown, which words belonged to which coast. The fifth gave him more trouble than the other four together, for the fifth light stood at Baile chrom, on the Tuathaich coast, and the man the council had chosen for it, a quiet Dia-thìreach who had kept lights before on the southern capes and asked for the northern posting himself, for reasons he gave the council as nothing more than a liking for a quieter coast than his last, would be a Dia-thìreach man living alone among a people whose speech he did not have and whose town watched him, by every account Ailean had been able to gather, the way a fishing town watches any stranger the kingdom sends to stand over it.

Ailean wrote the warrant plainly, in the same clipped form the other four had taken, save for one clause he added on his own authority and did not ask the council to approve before the warrant was sealed and sent: that the keeper at Baile chrom should set down, in his season's returns, whatever he saw by land as well as whatever he saw by sea, whatever that might in time prove to be. He gave the clause no explanation in the warrant, and offered none when Coinneach, reading the fair copy before it went south for sealing, asked him plainly why a lighthouse keeper's business should reach past the tideline at all. "Because he will see things the shire's own maor will not think to ask him about," Ailean said, "and a Library that only ever hears what a maor thinks worth reporting will end its days knowing a good deal less than it might have."

The five warrants went out together at the season's turn, and the five lamps were lit within the month, the other four to no comment Ailean ever heard of, the fifth to a silence from Baile chrom that he took, that first autumn, for nothing more than a stranger settling quietly into a strange town.

He stood that evening at the window of the reading room above the cellar, the two books of the Library, the lines and the tellings, set closed on the table behind him and the guild's sick-roll beside them, and watched, though he could see none of the five lights themselves from Muileann chaol, the last of the season's light go off the hills where the Bealltainn fires had burned a year and more before. Coinneach, trimming the lamps for the night, asked him, as the boy had taken to asking most evenings by then, whether the day's reading had settled anything. Ailean did not answer at once. He thought of the cellar as he had first seen it, the lowest rolls gone soft as bread, and of the two books now standing where the cellar's ruin had once stood alone, and of a shipwright's four days over another man's plans, and of a fifth light burning that night over a coast that had never yet had reason to trust a light the kingdom sent it.

"No," he said at last. "But it's kept. That will have to do."

He blew out the last lamp himself, and left the boy to lock the door.
