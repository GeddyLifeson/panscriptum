# Of the Table of the Ages

*SE 17 an Dàmhair – SE 19 an Cèitean*

> A record kept beside the throne is kept for the throne.
>
> — Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, the chronicle's first entry, {{date:V-0079}}

## I. Of the Errand to Muileann chaol

The council sent Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill east in an Dàmhair of SE 17 for no better reason, so far as she was ever told, than that somebody had to go and see what the keeper at Muileann chaol had been doing with the year and more and the two cartloads of minute-books the council had given him, and that she was the under-clerk with the fewest causes on her own desk that month. She was four-and-forty that autumn, a council clerk's daughter who had herself clerked at Cathair dhearg for fifteen years without once being sent further than Cnoc ghorm, and she took the errand, by her own later account, as a week's dull riding and a report nobody above her would trouble to read past its first line.

She reached Muileann chaol on the fourth day, the road east of the mountain gone soft with an autumn's rain and the last stretch, past Tobar dhìreach, thick with the smell of wet paper before she had so much as seen the mill that made it. The town surprised her before she had dismounted: a place she had known on the council's own rolls as a huddle of a few hundred souls round a grain-store had grown, in the year and more since Ailean mac Iain rode in with his carts, a second street of low workshops she had not been told to expect, awl and needle over one door for the binders, a quill over another for the copyists, and at the end of the street a yard she took at first for a fair-ground until a woman selling combs out of a basket told her, when she asked, that it was market day, the second since the charter came through, and that the yard belonged to the Library, which let it be used for nothing grander than a fee paid to the keeper's boy.

Ailean mac Iain met her at the Library's own door with ink on two fingers of his right hand and no apology for it, and took her account of her errand, to see and report and nothing more, with the particular patience of a man who had given the same tour to three lesser clerks already that year and expected to give it to a fourth before winter. "You will want to see the cellar first," he told her, "since it is the part every clerk from the capital asks after, and the part least worth the asking, being empty," and led her down the same stair he had gone down a year and a half before, the racks that had once held the district's soft and ruined rolls standing bare now and swept, the water that had stood on the floor drained off through a channel he had had cut that first summer. She asked him, on the stair, whether he missed the wet cellar he described in his own reports so often, and he said only that a keeper who missed a ruin had kept his post for the wrong reason, and said nothing further on the matter the whole three days she stayed.

He showed her next the two rooms above the cellar, where the custodians' rolls of every shire lay sorted by a system of his own devising, boxes lettered by district and shelved by a rule she asked him twice to explain and did not, by the time she left, wholly master either; he told her, when she said as much, that the system wanted no mastering by a visitor, only by the two boys who ran it daily, and that a clerk from the capital judging a keeper's order by whether she herself could work it was judging the wrong thing. She let the remark stand without argument, having by then spent enough of the first day in his company to judge that he answered every question either exactly or not at all, and that a third way of answering was not a thing he kept in stock.

He showed her, last, the room where seven copyists sat that afternoon at seven desks, each with a roll unrolled before him and a fresh sheet of the Tobar dhìreach paper beside it, turning the old dates the cellar had yielded into the calendar the humans had themselves brought to the island, a labour he described to her in four plain sentences: what the rolls held, what the calendar wanted, how a date given only by season and no more was marked as such rather than guessed at, and how long, by his own reckoning, the whole labour would take to finish. She wrote it down in five, adding a line of her own about the room's quiet that he had not thought worth mentioning. He mentioned, in the same breath and no louder than the rest, that the seven had lately settled a question of reckoning among themselves that the council had never thought to ask: that the age at the root of every telling on the island held, within it, one life long enough to be counted apart, and that his copyists, by their own consent and nobody's order, had parted it that same season and begun to count it as its own, an evening's work the whole town had talked of for a week afterward and the council, so far as either of them ever heard, had not troubled to hear of at all. The keeper said it the way a man reports a roof mended, not a thing done, and Gormshuil, writing it into her own book that evening by a borrowed lamp in the room the town had found her over the mercer's old shop, found she had underlined it twice without quite deciding to, and sat a while longer than the hour wanted, turning the lamp's wick down and up again, before she blew it out.

A boy of fourteen or so, who told her his name was Coinneach Clàrc before she had thought to ask it, brought her water in the morning and asked, setting the jug down, whether the capital had sent her to take anything away with her, a question he put, she judged from his face, less out of insolence than out of a plain wish to know how much of his own morning's work might shortly be undone. She told him no, only to look and to write what she saw, and he seemed to weigh the answer for its truth a moment before he accepted it and went back to whatever he had been carrying before he stopped to ask.

## II. Of the Report Nobody Above Her Would Read

Before she rode home she walked the market yard once more on her own, without Ailean beside her to name what she was looking at, and found it a smaller and plainer thing than the word market had led her to expect from a capital desk: some thirty stalls set up on trestles between the Library's own wall and the lane, most of them selling nothing grander than thread, nails, candles and the ordinary produce of a district feeding its own new mouths, and only a handful given over to the trade the town's second street had brought it, a binder selling off-cuts of calf too small for a cover, a copyist's wife selling quills her husband had trimmed and did not need. The stall-fee, a woman told her when she stopped to ask it, was a penny a table and two on a fair-day, paid to the keeper's boy in a wooden box he carried on a strap, and the court of the fair, such as it was, amounted to the boy's own mother settling disputes over table-space in a voice loud enough to be heard the length of the yard, a form of justice Gormshuil noted down without comment, setting it beside her own knowledge of grander courts that took longer to settle less.

She wrote her report on the ride home and delivered it, as instructed, to the clerk of council who had sent her, a man named Uilleam mac Griogair who read the first page standing at his own desk, the second sitting, and the third not at all, telling her, when she pressed him on it, that a keeper's cellar and a keeper's calendar were matters for the keeper and not, in his experience, matters the council troubled to weigh twice in one generation. "You have written three pages," he said, setting the sheets down unweighted so that the draught from his own window began at once to lift the corner of the last, "where the council asked for one, and the one it asked for need only have said the cellar is dry and the man is sound." Gormshuil left the third page with him regardless, and a fair copy of it in her own hand besides, on the grounds, she told no one but her own book, that a thing worth writing was worth keeping whether or not the man it was written for meant to open it.

The town had by then, in the ordinary way of these things, already begun to settle what Ailean's cellar had made of it. The market charter Gormshuil had seen in its second week that autumn was no more than the council's usual form, a fee for every stall and a court to hear the fair's own disputes, granted after the district's maor wrote up, at the keeper's own asking, that a town which had drawn copyists, binders and paper-makers to it in a year and a half without any charter at all might as well be given one before the trade found its own rules instead. The mill at Tobar dhìreach, a few miles off along the stream, was being turned that autumn by a family of millers who, for want of grain enough to keep three wheels busy, meant to make paper instead, and the copyists already spoke of its first sheets, due the next year, as though they had them in hand. She had ridden out to see the mill herself on her second day, more from a clerk's habit of wanting to see the thing behind a claim than from any errand that required it, and had found it a low building of two wheels rather than the three the family had first raised money for, the third abandoned half-built when the grain trade that was meant to feed it never came back to the strength the millers had reckoned on; the miller's eldest daughter, showing her the new vats where the rags were to be pulped, told her plainly that paper was the family's one hope against a debt that grain alone would bury them under, and that she for one hoped the Library's keeper never ran short of old rolls to copy, since it was his copying that kept her father's wheels turning at all. Within a year of the mill's first sheets, Ailean noted in a letter, near enough every sheet the Library used came from that one stream, and the council's stationers, so far as either of them ever heard, were not told why their trade had fallen off.

Gormshuil went back to her desk at Cathair dhearg that winter and to fourteen more months of the ordinary clerking that had filled the fifteen years before it, tallies and licences and the council's own minutes fair-copied twice over for a filing system she had never once been asked whether she thought sound, and told herself, when she thought of Muileann chaol at all, that she had seen a keeper's good order and a good cellar and nothing that need trouble her further. She kept, all the same, the fair copy of her own report in the bottom of her own chest rather than the council's files, a habit she could not afterward explain to her own satisfaction and did not, that winter, try very hard to.

## III. Of the Office Made

The council came to its own decision in SE 18, and came to it, by every account Gormshuil was given afterward, for reasons nearer to the coin than to the calendar: a councillor disputing a boundary with his neighbour had sent to the keeper at Muileann chaol for a custodian's roll that settled it inside a fortnight, where the same question, put to the council's own files two years before, had gone unanswered for want of anyone able to say where the relevant paper was kept. The councillor said as much, more than once and to more than one hearing, in the tone of a man who had found a convenience rather than a lesson, and it was that plain tone, Gormshuil came to believe, that did more to move the council than any argument a keeper or a clerk might have made for the value of an ordered record on its own account. A record that could be asked a question and give back an answer was, the council found, worth more to it than one it could only add to, and in the second week of an t-Samhain it made an office to keep the kingdom's whole record as Ailean's Library had begun to keep the north's, and set the office at Muileann chaol rather than at the capital, on the plain ground that the keeper already there had shown he knew how the thing was done, a decision reached, so Uilleam mac Griogair told her afterward without any evident shame in the telling, inside a single sitting and with less debate than the same council had lately given a dispute over a fair's stall-fees.

It named Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill to the office, on the strength of a report three men had read a page of between them and one clerk of council who remembered, when the matter came up, that she had gone to Muileann chaol once and come back with more to say than he had troubled to hear; it was Uilleam mac Griogair himself, by his own later admission, who named her, less from any judgement of her fitness for the work than from a wish to be rid of a clerk who had twice now handed him more paper than he cared to read. She was sworn in a small room at Cathair dhearg on the twentieth of an t-Samhain, four-and-forty still, before a clerk who read the warrant aloud in the same flat voice he read every warrant in, naming her royal chronicler of the kingdom and bidding her keep, in whatever form she judged fit, whatever the kingdom's affairs left behind them worth the keeping. She asked him, when he had finished, whether the warrant said anything of where the record was to be kept, and he told her, looking back down at the page as though the answer might yet be found there, that it did not, the council having spent its attention on the office's naming and its powers and having left the office's own seat, as it left most such questions, for whoever held it to settle for herself. The same warrant ordered that the kingdom's years should thereafter be counted by the ages the Library's own table now held rather than by the old count of the Diosal, six ages as the table then stood, and that her own first year should be entered as the eighteenth of the Severance Era, the first year so written in the kingdom's own record rather than borrowed from an older one; the clerk read that clause too in the same flat voice, and Gormshuil, listening, thought it a strange thing to hear her whole people's way of counting set aside in a single sentence read by a man who plainly did not think the sentence worth pausing over.

She walked back to her own lodging that evening turning the day's two facts over against each other, the office made and its seat left open, and found by the time she reached her own door that the second fact had already answered itself in her mind, whatever the council had meant by its silence. Her reply went out within the week, in a hand she took some trouble over, the letter's second page rewritten twice before she judged the wording exact enough to send: that she meant to keep the record at Muileann chaol, beside the Library that already held the rolls she would need and the copyists trained to the work, and, at its foot, the one line of her own that she afterward let stand at the head of her first year's book, that a record kept beside the throne was kept for the throne, and that a kingdom wanting an honest one would do better to keep it somewhere the throne's own convenience did not reach. She showed the letter to no one before she sent it, on a plain reckoning, rather than any wish for secrecy, that a line worth writing was not, in her experience, a line improved by another clerk's opinion of it beforehand. No one at the council troubled to argue the point with her, whether from agreement or from the same weariness that had let her first report go three pages unread, and she took the silence, that winter, for consent, and moved her whole desk east before the roads closed, carrying with her four chests of her own papers, a clerk's stool she had sat at for sixteen years and saw no reason to leave behind, and a single letter of introduction to Ailean mac Iain that she judged, on the ride out, she need not have troubled to write, since the keeper had already shown her, a year before, exactly how much introducing he required.

## IV. Of the First Circuit

She spent the winter before the circuit rode out in the plainer labour the office wanted before any clerk could be sent anywhere: a warrant drafted and copied six times over, one for each pair she meant to send, asking every parish, custodian and harbour-master on the island to open whatever books it kept and let a page of each be copied into the kingdom's own record, and asking nothing be given up that its own keeper wished to hold on to, a clause she added herself, over Ailean's mild objection that a clause inviting refusal was a strange thing for a chronicler to write into her own first warrant. "A clause that only invites what a keeper would do regardless costs the warrant nothing," she told him, "and a keeper who reads it will trust the rest of the page a good deal further for it," and left the clause standing when the six copies went out.

She sent her clerks in three pairs in the spring of SE 19, one south, one to the eastern shires nearer the mountain, and one north past Cnoc ghorm to the Tuathaich coast, each pair carrying its own warrant and its own chest to fill. Among the six rode a clerk of three-and-thirty named Tormod mac Sheumais, called already, by the other copyists, Tormod Pinn for the speed of his own hand, a council under-clerk's son who had asked Gormshuil for the circuit himself rather than wait to be sent, saying, when she asked him why, only that a man who copied other men's records all winter without once seeing where they came from was, in his own judgement, only half a clerk. She sent him north rather than south, on the reasoning she gave Ailean and no one else, that a clerk keen enough to ask for the harder half of the work deserved to be given it, and kept, of that first spring's riding, a private tally of his own that he showed no one for years afterward: the number of doors that opened to him without a second knock, against the number that did not.

The south filled its share within the season, its own habits of record already near enough to the kingdom's own that the asking cost the pair sent there little beyond the road's own length. Forty chests came up to Muileann chaol before an t-Iuchar was out, grave-lists and harbour rolls and parish registers from every shire between Cathair dhearg and the southern coast, and among them, copied whole into a book of its own because no clerk on the circuit could say what else to call it, the town arms of every town that had ever cut a seal for itself: five hundred and five of them by the time the last chest was opened, a hammer over a fleece, a shuttle over a flame, a hawk among crescents, set down side by side in an order that owed nothing to rank and everything to the order the chests themselves happened to arrive in. Coinneach Clàrc, set to the copying of that book himself, asked Gormshuil once whether she meant the arms sorted by shire before the book went to the binder, and she told him no, and told him why only once he pressed her a second time: that a record which tidied its own arrival too soon forgot, in a generation, that the arrival had ever been untidy at all, and that a reader coming after them both would learn more of the season's own haste from five hundred and five seals in the order they happened to reach her than from the same five hundred and five set out by rank as though the whole kingdom's making had been a tidy thing from the first.

The north filled one chest, and filled it slowly.

## V. Of the Door at Doire ghlas

Tormod's own pair rode the northern circuit that same spring, up past Doire ghlas where Agnes Pike's schoolroom had by then taught its fifth winter of scholars, and were met at the mill agent's door, and at the mill, and at the two chapels the town kept between them, by an answer near enough the same in every house: that the town's books, such as they were, belonged to the town, and that a clerk riding up from the south with a warrant in the kingdom's name was, whatever the warrant said about nothing being asked to be given up, not a man the town saw any profit in trusting with even a page of them. He read the clause about nothing being taken aloud at the mill agent's own counter, word for word as Gormshuil had drafted it, and the agent, a Tuathaich man some ten years settled in the post, heard him out to the end before he answered that a clause a warrant carried against its own asking was, in his own experience of warrants, worth exactly as much as the hand that had signed it chose to make it worth, and that he for one had no way of knowing, standing at his own counter, whether the hand behind this one meant to keep its word a year from now or merely to get a page copied today.

Tormod's tally that week ran to eleven doors closed against one opened, the one being that same mill ledger, and the agent let him copy it only after making him read the warrant through twice aloud in the street outside, in front of witnesses enough that no one afterward could say he had been tricked into it, and only after Tormod had agreed, on his own authority and without any leave from Gormshuil to give it, that the copy would carry the agent's own name beside it as the man who had chosen to show it, rather than stand in the record as a thing simply found. He wrote to Gormshuil that night, by a poor light in the room the town had grudged him, that the town's own silence was not, so far as he could judge, unkindness so much as memory: that a people who had watched the council take their savings once already in the coin it minted saw no reason to trust it with their parish books either, whatever form the asking came dressed in, and that he had, without waiting to ask her, promised the mill agent his own name kept beside the ledger's copy, a promise he hoped she would let stand even though no clause in her warrant had asked for it.

Gormshuil, reading his letter at her own desk at Muileann chaol some days later, wrote back that the promise would stand, and stand in whatever hand of hers the record wanted to make it plain the promise was kept and not merely made; and she added his own words about memory rather than unkindness to the same book she had begun with her own line about the throne, letting them stand there unaltered, on the ground that a chronicler who mended what her own clerks reported was no better than a council that only ever heard what it wished to. She wrote nothing beneath his words to soften them, and nothing to explain to a later reader why a warrant so carefully worded against taking anything away should still have closed eleven of the town's twelve doors against it.

## VI. Of the Chest from Inis bheag

The last of the circuit's letters that spring came from Inis bheag, in the island's north-east, where the two clerks sent there had been shown, by the keeper of the Seann Spioradan house that stood at the parish's heart, a book older than any the south's forty chests had yielded between them: a parish roll that named its own dead and its own harvests back past the coming of the humans, kept, the keeper told them, by his own house's keepers before him in an unbroken line he did not trouble to number for two clerks he had known a single afternoon. He was an old man by the clerks' own account, near enough as old as the Library's own cellar had been wet, and he heard the warrant read to him the once, without asking for it twice as the mill agent at Doire ghlas had, and answered only that he had kept the book, and his father before him, against exactly this asking, and saw no reason a kingdom's own warrant should change what forty years of quieter askings had not. He let it be copied, page by page, at his own table and under his own eye, correcting the younger clerk's hand once when a stroke ran the wrong way on a name he judged too old to be misread, and would not let the book itself be carried so much as to the harbour, telling her, when she asked why a book so old should not travel under a kingdom's own seal for its own better keeping, that a book kept in one house for that many generations belonged, whatever the kingdom's seal might say, to the house that had kept it and not to the seal, and that a seal, in his own long experience of them, kept a thing only for as long as the hand behind the seal found it convenient to.

Gormshuil read that clerk's own report of the exchange twice before she filed it, and found in the keeper's answer, though she wrote nothing of the thought into her own book, an echo of a house not far in kind from her own mother's, who had likewise counted her own dead by a reckoning older than the kingdom's and had likewise never once asked a council's leave to keep it.

The copy reached her at midsummer, and she read beside it, the same week, the north's own chest, brought up at last from Caol mhòr with the season's other returns and opened at her own table with Coinneach Clàrc standing by to lift out whatever it held. It held, when she had gone through it to the bottom, the maor of Siorrachd Àth àrsaidh's own returns and nothing else: tallies of the head-due paid and unpaid, a list of the district's licensed boats, a note on a bridge wanting mending, every page in the maor's own hand or his clerk's, and not one line, in the whole chest, written by a Tuathaich hand or telling a Tuathaich fact that the maor himself had not already thought worth the kingdom's asking after.

She sat with the chest open before her a good while before she wrote anything of her own beside it, Coinneach waiting at the door for whatever instruction she meant to give him, and when she did write, it was neither the line about the throne, which she judged she had already said, nor a complaint against the maor, who had after all done exactly what his own office asked of him and no less. She wrote only that the chest from Àth àrsaidh held the maor's returns and nothing more, and left the sentence to stand by itself at the foot of the page, without the further word that would have told a later reader whether she thought the emptiness a fault in the north, a fault in the south's asking, or a plain fact neither side had yet found a way to mend. Then she closed the chest, told Coinneach to see it carried up to the shelf beside the forty, and went back to the letter Tormod had sent her from Doire ghlas, which she had not yet finished answering.
