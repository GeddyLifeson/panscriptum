# The Vein, the Boards and the Trades

The kingdom lived in this age on the vein, as Riaghaltas nan Coigreach had before it: on An Sloc Mòr and its daughter workings, on Slighe a' Ghuail that carried their yield to the ports, and on the fifth An Crùn took of every ton. It restored the guilds of An Aois Naomh in a changed form, gave its trades charters and marks, and gathered its trade once a year at Fèill Muileann òg. Through the second half of the age the vein thinned, first in a survey no one read and then in a map that fitted on one sheet. This appendix gives the mountain and its working, the Boards that ran it, the line, the guilds and their marks, the fair, the coin, the lesser trades, and the measures of An Traoghadh, as they stand in the seventy-first year.

## The Sloc Mòr

The great shaft at Achadh dhomhain was worked under the humans by A' Chompanaidh, by day and by night, and the kingdom took it over whole. In the eleventh year An Crùn granted the working of it to Uisdean mac Ùisdein of Cathair mhòr, whose house had held an overseer's post under the humans at that very shaft, on the humans' terms with the names changed: the house to work the vein and sell its coal, An Crùn to take a fifth of the yield. The same year the council refused to end the working of the vein by day and night that the humans had ordered two generations before, so that the mountain was given no season of rest; the custodians of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail entered a protest, and then blessed the shafts. The mine's yield came up to what it gave under the humans, and then passed it. The working was the same as before; only the profit went to other hands.

The pit towns filled. In the twelfth year the east went hungry and its families walked to the vein for wages paid in coin; Muileann dhearg doubled in three years, and its shut druid hall was taken for a hewers' lodging. The spoil of the shaft, a grey stone veined with black, was cut from the thirteenth year at a quarry above Achadh dhomhain and sold for building under the house's quarry-mark, a stylised wave; Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's office, the house's hall at Cathair mhòr and an Leabharlann's new wing are all of it.

Taigh Mhic Ùisdein held the concession three generations. It raised the market cross at Cathair mhòr in the twentieth year and its stone hall in the thirtieth. In the fiftieth year Alasdair mac Ùisdein borrowed against the house's share to pay for Sireadh na Dàrna Fèithe; after the search failed the house sold half its share back to An Crùn in the fifty-second year and let its hall to Comhairle nam Mèinnean. In the sixtieth year An Crùn bought the house's last working shafts and left it its share in the spoil and the quarry, and from then the Board worked the vein in An Crùn's name alone.

## The Boards

**Comhairle nam Mèinnean** was set over the vein at the concession, and sat at Cathair dhearg until the twenty-seventh year, when it moved its clerks and presses to rooms above the wool market at Cathair mhòr, to be nearer the mountain. In that year it set down in four lines, for the first time in writing, Riaghailt a' Bhùird: that its surveyors' work is kept in its own presses and not sent to an Leabharlann as every other office's reports are sent. No one on the council was asked to confirm it. From the fifty-second year it sat in the Mac Ùisdein hall. Its surveyors walk the faces with chain, level and lamp; its chainmen are taken on young. The office of surveyor-general, which answers for the whole of what the surveys show, stood empty three years before Catrìona nic Neacail was named to it in the fifty-sixth year.

**Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn** was made in the eighth year to run the mended coal line, seated at Cathair dhearg in a low building of the grey Mac Ùisdein stone, with one clerk and two engineers who had learned their trade watching the humans work it, and the humans' own timetable, copied fair and hung on a board at the Muileann chrom terminus. The engineer of the first coal train was a Tuathaich man, Colin Rudd. The Board counts the shunting families of Muileann chrom apart from the rest of the town and houses them in rows by the lines. It refused Tional nan Tuathach's goods from the fortieth year on the strength of the fair's rule on factors, until Ruairidh mac Phàdraig ruled at Seann Bhral in the fifty-ninth year that it is a carrier of An Crùn and must carry for any who pay; the Board appealed, the council let the appeal lapse, and the first Doire ghlas bale went onto the line in the sixtieth year. In the sixty-third year the yard families stopped the coal trains three days over a cut wage; the Board settled on the fourth day and dismissed eleven men on the fifth.

**Comhairle nan Taighean-solais** keeps the five lights the humans built, from Baile chrom round to Tobar dhearg, and no others; it gave them Dia-thìrich keepers by royal warrant in the seventeenth year, and inspects each twice a year, oil against book.

## The coal line

The railway the humans built from the central mountains to the sea lay broken in three places after Cogadh nam Beann. In the eighth year the council mended it as far as Muileann chrom and no further. In the twelfth the mended line was carried up the valley to the pithead at Achadh dhomhain, and the first coal train ran from An Sloc Mòr to Cathair dhearg in the kingdom's name on the humans' rails, its blessing said over the coal in the first wagon. In the thirteenth the Board laid new sidings at Muileann chrom, where the line from An Sloc Mòr meets the older line down to the ports and the trains are broken up and made up again, and carried a western branch from Cathair dhearg to the port of Seann Bhral in the west, with a spur of thirteen miles north to the Tuathaich pits at Àth leathan. In the thirty-sixth year the line was carried through soft ground, pile by pile, to Ceann leathan, so that coal might be shipped from a second harbour, and the town's quays were rebuilt for coal-ships. The line runs An Sloc Mòr, Muileann chrom, Cathair dhearg and on to Ros dhomhain.

In am Màrt of the thirty-fourth year the Abhainn ìseal flooded, took three bridges and a mile of the embankment, and shut the line; the ports on it lived on stored coal, and Mary Coyle, a month in Tional nan Tuathach's chair, had every northern harbour open its store and share it by the count of hearths until the line was mended. In the sixty-eighth year Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn shut the Àth leathan spur for the falling yield of the western pits.

## The guilds and their marks

In the thirteenth year the old custom of binding apprentices, from An Aois Naomh, was taken up again in a changed form: it is kept for the Dia-thìrich alone. The guild courts sit at Cathair dhearg.

**Comann nan Gualadairean**, the first chartered under the restored custom, at Achadh dhomhain on a cold day of an t-Samhain in the thirteenth year; its oath is sworn with a hand laid on a lump of coal, and by its charter its rolls are open to Dia-thìrich only. It built the bath-house over Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh below Muileann ruadh for miners sick with the dust in the seventeenth year, and its first sick-roll named four hundred men. After the fall at Muileann dhearg it bound sixty apprentices in one day, none Tuathaich. In the fifty-eighth year it bound none at all, and its masters voted to keep the rolls to the sons and daughters of members only. In the sixty-second year it kept a minute-book of shaft-lamps lent from pit to pit.

**The shipwrights of Ros dhomhain**, chartered in the fourteenth year, who bound thirty apprentices in a single day on the slipway. Under the charter rule the Tuathaich boys already bound to Dia-thìrich masters at Cuan shean and Seann Bhral were loosed from their indentures; some went north, most stayed on as day-labourers outside the guilds and their wages. At Cuan shean Seumas Crùbach kept Harry Marlow on at half a bound man's wage, and the other yards took up the rate. The Tuathaich shipwrights turned out of the guild yards opened their own yard at Ros mhòr in the forty-first year, building to the humans' old lines.

**The chandlers of Ceann àrsaidh**, chartered in the fourteenth year with the sole right to fit out An Crùn's patrol boats.

**The lamp-makers of Muileann mhin**, chartered in the fourteenth year, whose mark, a flame struck over a hammer, is stamped on every coal lamp of every shaft on the island. Of all the guild marks the humans' traders copied this one only. They halved their making in the sixty-second year for want of coal to fire the forges.

**The weavers of Baile chiar**, chartered in the fifteenth year under a seal of a shuttle over a fleece, with the right to set their own prices at the fair and bar any weaver who had not served seven years. Their cloth of southern wool undersold the northern at the fair until the Doire ghlas mills were built.

**Comann an Airgid of Muileann chiar**, whose seam the Dia-thìrich kept in their own hands through the humans' time, chartered anew in the thirty-seventh year when the silver was found richer at depth, its captains bound to An Crùn's tithe on every bar struck.

**The rope-makers of Seann Tarr**, chartered in the fifty-third year, the last trade the humans never touched to be given a charter of its own; their charter stays in the shire's own chest.

Beside the chartered marks stand marks no charter gave: the miller-families' mark cut into the millstone at Muileann dhomhain, kept when the mills turned from grain to bark in the fifty-third year; An Snàthainn Geal of the Doire ghlas weavers; the Baile ghorm tanners' mark on panther-hide.

## The fair at Muileann òg

Fèill Muileann òg was first held in an Dàmhair of the twenty-first year, on a field at the ford that Oighrig nic Mhathain, market clerk of Dùn chrom and its first toll-mistress, had marked out with cord and stick through the summer, and it became the greatest gathering on the island; trade came to it from every province the state holds. Oighrig wrote its three rules in her own hand: a stall-fee in coin; a court of the fair to sit each day of the gathering; and Tuathaich goods to be sold only through licensed Dia-thìrich factors, on the council's instruction. The first four factors' houses she entered under a heading of her own, Bàillidhean na Fèille. The rules are read aloud at the opening of every fair. Cùirt na Fèille's first case was a barrel of salt fish, settled within the hour by her weighing it herself. In the ninth day of the first fair she turned David Hale's wool to a factor's stall, where it sold for some fifth part less than it would have fetched at a northern market; Tional nan Tuathach's letter against the factor rule was filed. The road from Cathair dhearg was metalled for the fair's carts through the twenty-second year and called Rathad na Fèille; Dùn chrom took a toll at its gate and built a wall on it.

On the eve of Samhain in the fortieth year fire ran through the stalls; Oighrig beat at the burning canvas with her own cloak until the worst was turned from the weavers' row, and died of her burns. The next day the court sat in a field and the fair went on. From the fortieth year the north's wool came south by cart, five days by Rathad nan Cairtearan. In the fifty-fifth year the court set aside one day of the fair for goods from the northern shires, still sold through factors but under the makers' own marks, and the first Tuathaich name written on anything sold there was An Snàthainn Geal of Doire ghlas. By the fifty-eighth year the fair stood with gaps between its tents that its wardens filled with wider aisles, and the market at Baile Mòr dhomhain drew more carts two years together. In the seventieth year, when the Board cut its coal sales and the price of coal-fuel at the fair doubled, the court fixed a ceiling price by its custom and could not hold it.

## The coin

In the fifth year all the humans' coin still in hand, every silver mark and copper penny, was cried down and ceased to be money. The mint set up in the old human counting-house at Cathair dhearg under Fearchar Bonn struck Bonn na Rìoghachd in its place, the seal of each coin pressed with an ink ground from coal dust so that a clipped coin shows the colour at its edge. An t-Ionmhas reckons in purses. In the north, where the Tuathaich kept what savings they had in the humans' coin, they were left worth their weight in metal, over four hundred marks across two hundred households at Caol mhòr alone, and Tional nan Tuathach's letter asking an exchange mark for mark was received and filed. At Doire mhin Joseph Marlow melted the dead coin into ploughshares and sold them back at the cost of the charcoal. At Inis thais the merchants traded in chits on Lia Fàil's paper until the coin came. In the thirty-seventh year more silver came up from Muileann chiar than the mint could refine, and An Crùn gave the villages of the eastern hills the refining of the guild's bar, with the gold washed from the stream below Baile dhìreach, into blanks for Bonn na Rìoghachd, paid by weight; the mint strikes them.

## The lesser trades

**Salt**, from the pans dug on the flats below Àth bheag in the thirty-second year, supplied up the coast; its sheds were burned by raiders in the forty-fourth year and raised again within the year, An Crùn paying a third. **Scent**, from the twenty-seventh year, in small flasks blown by the glass-houses of the capital shore and filled by the women of Seann Vell with waters of bog-myrtle, meadowsweet and heather honey, sold within a few years in every market town. **Paper**, from the mill at Tobar dhìreach from the eighteenth year. **Cloth**, from Baile chiar's looms and, from the fifty-ninth year, from Tional nan Tuathach's mills at Doire ghlas, whose first bolt was a grey twill with a white thread at the selvedge. **Grain**, by water from Oirthir an t-Seabhaig through the dock at Ros gheal from the twenty-fourth year, and to Cnoc bheag's river quays, which by the forty-sixth took in more grain in a season than Cathair mhòr's granaries showed for a year; An Crùn's store at Cathair fhada held a season's grain for the south from the thirty-third year. **Fish**, landed above all at Seann Bhral in the south, the greatest fishing port of the kingdom from the twenty-fourth year, and sold for the capital under Ceann mhin's covered market from the fifty-fourth. From the thirtieth year the licences to fish the waters south and east of Caol mhòr were kept for Dia-thìrich boats, and the Tuathaich boats were left the northern grounds. **Copper**, from the working opened at Àth fhiadhaich in the sixtieth year with men laid off from An Sloc Mòr, which pays wages and is not the vein. **Hides**, of the panthers killed in the crossing of the sixty-second year, cured at Baile ghorm and sold at the fair under the town's mark.

## The Depletion

**The survey of the twenty-sixth year.** In the twenty-sixth year the survey reports of An Sloc Mòr began to note, without remark, that the vein yields less. The survey behind them was Iain mac Ruairidh's, called Iain Mapa: three surveys of the eastern faces, each within an inch of the last, showing the vein running narrower to the east than the old maps showed and pinching again to the south. He walked it thirty-one miles to Cathair dhearg himself and laid it before three men of the Board, none of whom unrolled it. He was given a receipt of four lines naming nothing of the finding. The survey was filed and shown to no one, and within the month he was sent to the silver works at Muileann chiar. For years the state did nothing.

**The fall of the thirty-ninth year.** On the first day of an t-Iuchar in the thirty-ninth year a gallery of the reopened Cill ghlas district gave way under Muileann dhearg, along a line the surveyors had marked as sound, and thirty-one hewers died. It is small beside Scàineadh Cill ghlas of An Aois Choigreach, and it is the same fault. The Board's new rules for digging that followed were good rules, and were made for Dia-thìrich workers; in the workings where Tuathaich labour they were kept loosely and by intent. At Àth leathan the timber the rule called for was ordered from Ceann mhòr and never came. Forty hewers' families left Achadh dhomhain for the ports. From the forty-first year Tional nan Tuathach kept Clàr nan Leòn, its register of the Tuathaich dead and maimed of the western pits, since no Dia-thìreach book counted them.

**Sireadh na Dàrna Fèithe.** In the fiftieth year the Board lent eleven surveyors to Alasdair mac Ùisdein's search for a second seam of an gual dathach elsewhere on the island, and An Crùn lent its name. Forty-one shafts were sunk from the hills above Dùn dhearg to the moors south of Baile ruadh through the fifty-first year, the moor the shepherds call am Fearann Falamh, and none struck the vein. Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain wrote to the Board that the search asked the wrong question; the Board filed the letter. Mòrag nic Coinnich found afterward in a Holy Age list at an Leabharlann the same hills named as searched and barren. Dùn dhearg won damages for its spoiled grazing; Baile ruadh fenced the open shafts at its own cost after a child of the town fell into one.

**The one sheet.** In the fifty-sixth year Catrìona nic Neacail drew every stretch of the true vein the Board's surveys called unworked onto a single leaf, Duilleag na Fèithe, and hung it on the wall of the Board's chamber at Cathair mhòr. It fitted on one sheet. The quiet note of the twenty-sixth year was now an open trouble: the last unworked stretches of the vein were smaller than any wish to own. She was named surveyor-general. In the fifty-seventh year she found Iain Mapa's survey among the Board's files, showing the same thinning thirty years before, and sent a copy to an Leabharlann with a note of when the Board received it. Within the year she gave up her post to teach at Caol shean.

**The measures of the close.** By the fifty-seventh year Muileann dhearg counted above sixty houses empty, and the coal quays at Ceann leathan stood half idle. In the sixty-first year the lowest galleries of An Sloc Mòr were let flood, the pumps costing more to run than the coal beneath was worth; the Board's minute calls it a pause. The three great pumps were sold to the harbour at Seann Bhral in the south for its new dry dock. In the sixty-eighth year the Board withdrew from Àth leathan, and the Tuathaich hewers there went on under their own pit captains, paying An Crùn's fifth under Breith Leòid; the Board enters the payments as arrears. At Cnoc thais the pits passed to their hewers the same way. In the seventieth year the Board cut its sales and the price of coal at the fair doubled. In the spring of the seventy-first the Board posted watchmen over the spoil-heaps at Cnoc fhiadhaich, where households had taken to picking coal to burn; the watchmen are men of the town, and see nothing. The capital's street lamps burn shorter hours than its lighting order asks, and the council's notice calls the shortening a saving.
