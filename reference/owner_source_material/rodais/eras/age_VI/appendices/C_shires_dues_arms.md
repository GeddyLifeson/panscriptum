# The Shires, the Dues and the Arms

The kingdom governed its land in the Age of the Kingdom through shires, *siorrachdan*, each named for its seat and each under a maor who gathered the Crown's dues and kept its peace. The shires were drawn in the third year on the only whole surveys of the island, which were the humans'; their dues were set in the council's first weeks; their seals were cut from a roll of arms made in the fourth year, much of it copied from a book the humans left behind. This appendix gives the drawing, the shires as they stand in the seventy-first year, the maoir and the circuits, the two dues and how they were gathered, and the roll of arms, as the council's minute-books and the Library's copy of the roll give them.

## The drawing

In the second year Ailean mac Iain, boxing the humans' counting-house at Seann Skell in the west, found behind a locked grating eleven district surveys of the coast, every field walked and measured and every holding numbered, to a scale marked in the corners, in a hand so exact that no survey the council owned could stand beside them. He boxed them apart and labelled them for the council. In the autumn of the third year, back at Cathair dhearg from the voyage of An Sireadh, he was set to a table with the surveys under a smoking lamp and told to draw the shires.

The council kept most of the humans' district lines under new names, and named each shire for its seat. Where it did not keep a district seat it went back to the older shire seat instead, so that the shire of Seann Tarr took its seat back from Baile Mòr ruadh, the humans' district town, to the old harbour. The humans' surveys were the only whole surveys of the island, and the kingdom used them and said nothing of it. Many of the lines they ran, and so many of the shire bounds, follow the old boundary stones of the Peace of the Threshold, which the humans' chainmen took for marks without knowing what they were.

The drawing made one hundred and twenty-two shires. One more was cut in the thirty-eighth year, when the maor of Baile chiar wrote that Doire chaol, laid out south of the capital in the first years for the families that served the royal household, had grown from a scattering of clerks' and stewards' houses into a town proper that he could not govern well from five miles off; the council granted Siorrachd Doire chaol its own arms, its own dues and a maor of its own choosing before the summer was old. It is the only shire cut new since the drawing, and the kingdom has had one hundred and twenty-three since.

**The windy coast.** The shire lines stop where the humans' survey stopped, at the edge of the great moss of the far north-west. The low shore beyond, an Oirthir Ghaothach, with its sealers and fowlers and herders on the moor's edge, was set in no shire and given no maor, and the council's minute says only that it "is not in the survey". Ailean wrote the fact onto the blank paper at the edge of his map in the same flat hand as the rest. The windy coast pays neither due and the roll carries no line for it; no census clerk has gone there.

**The island shires.** Two rocks that each kept a customs station of the humans' Administration, Eilean dhìreach off the Tuathaich coast and Eilean ìseal off the east coast, were kept as shires of their own, since the council had no rule by which one district might be folded into its neighbour. Neither has a town to name it, and each takes the name of its island; Ailean noted beside each that its dues would need gathering by a maor who kept his own boat. They are the only shires not named for a seat.

**Names that repeat.** A shire takes the name of its seat, and the island's names repeat, so the shires' names repeat too. The rolls keep apart three shires of Seann Skell, three of Seann Vell and three of Ceann mhin, and two each of several other names, by where they lie. Among towns the confusion is older than the shires: there are two Seann Bhrals, of the west and of the south, and two towns called Dùn thais, one on the west coast within a morning's ride of Ros bheag and the other seat of its own shire in the east. A clerk at Cathair dhearg filed the western town's court of the sea and the eastern town's covered market against the one name in the thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth years, and the royal record confused the two for ten years, until Tormod Pinn ruled a line through it in the forty-eighth year marking them west and east and wrote to both shires' clerks to take more care.

**Seats that are not the largest towns.** In several shires the council kept the seat the humans' district had, and did not follow the growth of the towns: Caol leathan is the seat and not Baile Mòr fhionn; Baile àrsaidh and not Seann Bhral of the west; Baile Mòr dhomhain and not Cuan bheag.

## The Tuathaich shires

Eleven shires lie wholly or mostly in the Tuathaich north and north-west: Caol mhòr, Ceann mhòr, Doire ghlas, Baile chrom, Cnoc ghorm, Cnoc thais, Àth àrsaidh, Muileann leathan, Muileann àrsaidh, Baile ìseal and the island shire of Eilean dhìreach. Their maoir are Dia-thìrich, lodged in the seat towns: at Ceann mhòr the first maor took the old human magistrate's house, and the townsfolk went on calling it by its old name. A few Tuathaich towns stand in Dia-thìrich shires, among them Àth leathan in the shire of Seann Skell of the west. The boundary stones set on the north roads in the first year mark where the Tuathaich country begins; they are the bounds of no shire. The Moot at Caol mhòr has no shire and claims none, and its minute-books, counts and registers are kept in the north outside every shire's chest.

A Dia-thìreach councillor proposed in the tenth year that the line be marked, a ditch thirty miles long, surveyed, dug and marked at the mile. The debate ran two hours; one councillor held that an unmarked boundary was one only the strong could rely on, another that the ditch would cost more than a year's whole revenue from the north for a peace that had cost nothing, a third that she had never needed telling where one country ended, the change being plain in the roofs and the smoke and the dogs. An old councillor said that in eight years no shire south of the tracks had brought him a complaint of a Tuathaich crossing where he was not wanted. No vote was taken, and the proposal was not renewed.

## The maoir

A maor was set over each shire in the fourth year to gather the Crown's dues, keep its peace, and seal the letters of its court. The first maoir's letters were ridden out by Ailean mac Iain. Some of those the record names:

**Coinneach mac Ailein**, a custodian's younger son, first maor of Siorrachd Baile Mòr mhòr, who held his court under the market roof among the fish-scales because the humans' courthouse had burned, and kept it there when the new courthouse was built; the two maoir after him did the same.

**Griogair mac Ailein**, maor of Siorrachd Ceann mhòr from the fourth year, eleven years in the magistrate's house when Eilidh nic Leòid came to hold her assize there.

**Artair mac Lachlainn**, maor of the shire about Seann Bhral in the west at the Leòid ruling.

The maor of Siorrachd Doire ghlas, whose clerks counted the Tuathaich towns from the road in the seventh year; the maor who watched Agnes Pike's children recite a psalm in the fifteenth year and sent south a report of harvest, head-due and bridge with nothing of the school in it; the maor of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag, who broke the shire's old die and pressed its new hawk in the thirty-sixth year; the maor of Siorrachd Achadh shean, who wrote south in the second month of the dry in the forty-third year and cut twelve wells along the drove road; the maor of Muileann dhearg, who counted above sixty houses empty within his walls by the fifty-seventh year; and the maor of Muileann shean, who wrote himself for the primers of his hewers' school in the fifty-ninth.

## The circuits

Justices were set to ride circuit among the shires in the seventh year, so that a man in Muileann bhàn or Inis thais or a dozen towns like them might know what law he stood under where the humans' law had gone and the kingdom's had not yet been written. The first was **Eilidh nic Leòid** of Cathair dhomhain, whose circuit took in eleven shires of the south-west in a fixed order once a year, most of a year in the riding, returning to the council's clerks a written record of every judgment. At Seann Bhral in the west in the eighth year she gave the Leòid ruling, cited in nearly every land case after; from the fifteenth year she rode the northern circuit, and at Ceann mhòr heard seventeen suits on human titles and found fifteen for the Crown. Her rulings were bound after her death as Leabhar nam Breith and a copy given to every circuit justice. Her brother's son **Uilleam mac Leòid** took the northern circuit in the sixteenth year. **Ruairidh mac Phàdraig** sat as circuit justice at Seann Bhral in the west in the fifty-ninth year and ruled the Rail Board a carrier of the Crown. The circuit court found for Dùn dhearg against the search for a second vein in the fifty-second year.

## The Crown's dues

The council set in its first weeks the two dues the maoir should gather. The **head-due**, *cìs-cinn*, is laid on every household by the number of its heads, and so falls on the whole people of the kingdom, town and country, north and south. The **market-due**, *cìs-margaidh*, is taken on every sale made by a seller of the kingdom. The humans' harbour tariff at Ros dhomhain, copied into Dia-thìris by Ailean mac Iain to say exactly the old thing in a new tongue, stood until the kingdom had coin of its own to take the market-due in, and was folded into it when the mint struck.

The head-due was gathered for the first time in the seventh year, on the households the first census had counted, at eighteen purses of the realm's coin on every five thousand heads, and taken with the harbour dues; it has stood at that rate since. The Treasury reckons in purses, *sporain*. Where the census counted a Tuathaich town from the road, the due fell on the count the clerks made and not the count the Moot kept, and the two differed by nearly a fifth.

The dues of the island are gathered in the island's own way. A treasury clerk sent to number the flocks of Eilean dhubh for the head-due in the twentieth year found the islanders unwilling to have their pastures counted, and took instead their sworn count by tally-stick, on which the island's dues have been set every year since.

**Fines and tolls.** A town's fine is taken at the maor's office. Cathair gheal was fined four purses of the realm-coin in the twentieth year for bearing arms without leave, counted out coin by coin at the maor's door. Tolls are taken where the Crown or a town sets a gate: the toll at Dùn chrom's gate on the fair's road built the town its southern wall in the twenty-second year. The fair at Muileann òg takes a stall-fee in coin; the market at Muileann chaol a penny a table.

**What the shires keep.** The shires keep what the council allows them for their own works, and a few have turned it to works the council did not think of. Cnoc leathan paid for a school in each of its six parishes from its dues in the fiftieth year, and showed the council's clerk plain accounts when he came to ask how. Baile ruadh fenced every open shaft of the search on its ground at its own cost. Cuan ruadh burned a light on its mole from the town's coal money. The first relief the kingdom granted, seed for the survivors of Achadh mhin under salt in the fourth year, came from the Treasury without a due asked, and the Crown paid a third of Cuan dhearg's relief and of the rebuilding of Àth bheag's salt-sheds.

## The roll of arms

Every maor needed a seal for his court, and in the fourth year the council's clerks made Clàr nan Suaicheantas, the roll of arms. The kingdom's arms were cut anew from the old seal of the custody, and every shire was given arms of its own. The Dia-thìrich shires took the pointed shield of the custody's seal; the Tuathaich shires kept the rounded shield of the humans' district seals, which they had sealed with before. Many of the charges were copied from a book of arms left at the Residency, as the shire lines were drawn from the humans' surveys, and so lions, parrots and crocodiles stand on the seals of shires where none was ever seen. At Seann Skell in the west the house of Òrd Mhanannain asked for its badge, the yellow scorpion of Manannan's cup, and that shire alone bears a scorpion. At Ceann mhòr the maor sealed with the wolf's head of the last human magistrate, whose house he had taken. Siorrachd Cnoc bheag was given a plain white billet for no reason any clerk could afterward give.

**The town arms.** In the spring of the nineteenth year the first chronicle circuit's clerks rode out in three pairs, and the southern chests gave up the seal of every burgh that had ever cut one: five hundred and five town arms, which Coinneach Clàrc entered in the order the chests arrived rather than by rank, on Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill's ruling that a tidied record forgets how untidy its own arrival was. In the north most town arms were copied from the humans' old town seals, since few Tuathaich would show the clerks their own. Guild marks are not arms and are not on the roll, but the council's clerks cut their seals: the weavers of Baile chiar a shuttle over a fleece, the lamp-makers of Muileann mhin a flame struck over a hammer; the house of Mac Ùisdein cut a stylised wave on every block of its quarry-stone.

**The one change.** The roll has been changed once at an order's asking. In the thirty-sixth year five witnesses of the Hawk houses, Raonaid nic Iain among them, rode to Cathair dhearg and asked that Siorrachd Cnoc bheag's white billet be struck and a hawk set in its place. The council heard them in the same chamber as every other shire's petition and granted it within the week; the maor broke the old die before his assembled clerks and pressed into wax for the first time a white hawk among twelve crescent moons, the wing set spread rather than folded.

**The coin.** The realm-coin struck at Cathair dhearg from the fifth year bears the kingdom's arms and the ruler's likeness, its seal pressed with an ink ground from coal dust, so that a clipped coin shows the colour at its edge. When Mairead was crowned in the fifty-eighth year the die of Eòghan's likeness was broken by the mint's custom, and the old coin kept good until the new was struck in number.
