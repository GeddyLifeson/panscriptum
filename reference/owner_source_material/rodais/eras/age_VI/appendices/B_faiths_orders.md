# The Faiths and Orders

The first census of the restored kingdom, taken in the seventh year, entered every town under the faith its people kept: An Creideamh Sean in its orders, Na Seann Spioradan, or An Eaglais the Tuathaich forebears brought at An t-Aiseag. It was the first roll of the rites since Seumas Bàn's clerks counted them at Dùn ìseal. On the far north-western cape beyond Baile chrom, where there is no town, the clerks found scattered families with no house of any faith, and on Eilean ghorm and the smallest islets no one to ask, and entered all of these as *gun chreideamh*, gun chreideamh. What follows gives each faith and order as an Leabharlann's rolls, Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd and the letters kept in the orders' own ledgers show it at the close of the age, in the seventy-first year.

## Òrd Bhrìde, the Coal Rite

The order that keeps Brìde's flame and Crom's stone had kept its flame through the long ages by custom and by no written creed. In the sixth year it was given a settled doctrine within An Creideamh Sean, built upon the leyline and the unbroken line of fuil-ghuail, and in the same year Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was named its first Àrd-choimheadaiche, and seated at Seann Skell on the southern coast, where the order's greatest house stands in a hall with blackened beams. He was six-and-fifty. His first charge was to write an order of worship, for there had never been one.

He read it for the first time on a grey morning near the end of am Faoilleach, in the hall, to the eleven custodians of the southern house and a scattering of others: two hearth-keepers from villages along the coast, and a priest of Òrd na Cloiche from Inis thais. He kept from that day a plain ledger of every letter Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail sent or received, and copied into its first page Òrd na Cloiche's letter wishing Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail well. Copies of the order went to every house of An Creideamh Sean that still stood, Òrd an t-Seabhaig included, to Òrd na Cloiche with thanks, and to Òrd Mhacha on the plain ground that a house might refuse to share a keeping and still be glad of a reading.

Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail blesses the shafts of the vein. When the council would not end the working of the vein by day and night in the eleventh year, the custodians entered a protest, and then blessed the shafts. Its custodians read over the dead of the mountain: over each of the thirty-one graves at Muileann dhearg in turn, at the families' asking, in the thirty-ninth year. It asked a share in the keeping of the coronation regalia in the seventh year and was refused. Dòmhnall mac Thormoid died in the twenty-fourth year at four-and-seventy, and his successors the north knew only by their letters. Each census of the age carries a line for fuil-ghuail that gives a number and nothing more, which Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's doctrine calls a settled matter.

## Òrd an t-Seabhaig, the Hawk

The houses of Òrd an t-Seabhaig about Cnoc bheag kept the old form of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, the local altar answering to no custodian above it, since before any council sat on the island. In the sixth year they wrote to Seann Skell, under the seal of a bird with wings spread flat, that the written order was handsome and not needed, and wished the southern house no ill in going its own way. They were not compelled, and here the long quarrel of Òrd an t-Seabhaig and Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail began.

One hawk-house broke the other way. The house at Muileann naomh, southernmost of Òrd an t-Seabhaig's houses and farthest from Cnoc bheag, took the written order entire in the sixth year and hung in its doorway a bell cast new for it, the first bell any hawk-house had hung, to ring at Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's measured burnings. It is counted since as a house of Òrd Bhrìde.

Òrd an t-Seabhaig's country grew rich on grain. The houses blessed the grain dock at Ros gheal in the twenty-fourth year and took a twentieth of its dues, which the clerks of that town call simply Òrd an t-Seabhaig's. Òrd an t-Seabhaig house at Inis bhàn sheltered every family the fire of the twenty-ninth year put out of a roof, sworn or not. By the forty-sixth year Cnoc bheag passed Cathair mhòr in its numbers, and Òrd an t-Seabhaig house was the richest of any order outside the capital.

In an Cèitean of the thirty-fifth year the houses met at Inis ìseal and named a custodian of their own, Raonaid nic Iain, whose mother's mother had kept the altar there, by a standing up house by house; Murchadh Ruadh set in her hand a white feather kept three generations in his house. Seann Skell answered in three lines that Àrd-choimheadaiche's order knew no office called the custodian of Òrd an t-Seabhaig and could not own one. The houses folded the letter away unanswered and went on as before. Raonaid wrote Òrd an t-Seabhaig's spoken order into a farm account-book, sounding each line against her grandmother's telling, read it aloud once at the full moon of an t-Samhain, tied it with a strip of the white feather and locked it in the iron box that had held the altar's lamp-oil. It is heard once a year and read by no one else. In the thirty-sixth year the council granted the houses' asking and the maor of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag broke the shire's die of the white billet and pressed a white hawk among twelve crescents, the first change made to Clàr nan Suaicheantas at the asking of an order.

In the forty-seventh year a council of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail at Seann Skell read Òrd an t-Seabhaig a place under Àrd-choimheadaiche with their own order of worship, terms offered six times before; the houses took the place and kept their order, and the quarrel grew quieter. It did not end. In the sixty-fifth year Òrd an t-Seabhaig of Siorrachd Ros gheal sent their birth-rolls to an Leabharlann for the first time, on the condition that they be filed under Òrd an t-Seabhaig and not under Òrd Bhrìde, and Mòrag nic Coinnich agreed. Raonaid nic Iain died in the sixty-second year.

## Òrd na Cloiche, the Stone

Òrd na Cloiche in the south-west, whose Clach, the Lia Fàil, is not Crom's stone, were bidden in the seventh year to send witnesses to the settling of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail and sent none, only a letter wishing it well and promising nothing further. They stood aside from Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's order as from An Crùn's, and dealt with both on their own paper. At Inis thais in the sixth year the merchants traded in chits written on the house of Òrd na Cloiche's paper until the new coin came, and the house redeemed them at its door and kept back a tenth. At Caol leathan the greatest house of the order was rebuilt in the twenty-first year with a tower and a bell cast from the coin of every ship that had come home to that harbour, and the town set its market and its courts by the bell. At Doire shean in the thirtieth year the house lent the town the cost of its harbour against the town's own dues, the first harbour raised since An Dealachadh without a coin of An Crùn's in it, and the mole was built to Òrd na Cloiche's measure. In the sixty-fifth year Òrd na Cloiche published their own count of the people of their shires beside the kingdom's census, and the two agreed within a hundred.

## Òrd Mhacha

The order of the capital and the crown was given back the keeping of the coronation regalia in the seventh year, which it had kept before the humans came, and refused Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's Àrd-choimheadaiche a share in it. Its bells at Cathair dhearg rang a full day and a night for Niall's death in the twenty-second year. Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail sent it a copy of the order of worship; it made no answer that Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail's ledger records.

## Òrd Mhanannain

Òrd Mhanannain keeps the harbours of the west coast, where the Dia-thìrich beside the Tuathaich towns keep it. At Seann Skell in the west the house asked the council for its badge on Clàr nan Suaicheantas, the yellow scorpion of Manannan's cup, and that shire alone bears a scorpion; the same mark is carved over the old counting-house door at Seann Bhral in the west. The priests of Manannan said the old prayer that the sea be kept shut over An Sireadh's going and over her return in the third year. In the twenty-fifth year the keeper of the house at Seann Bhral in the west, reckoned the oldest on that coast, heard Mary Coyle's asking that the north be admitted to its feast of the sea, as the Tuathaich forebears once were, and refused it: the house is kept on the tithes of families who hold the rite by blood and the custodian's book. The sea, he said, is the same; the house is not.

## The Seann Spioradan, the Old Spirits

Na Seann Spioradan keep no order and no written creed, only the hearths of their houses and the names of their dead. Their keepers are of the eldest houses in each district. The holy well of Allt an Àigh in the east, a clear spring in a fold of pale stones with a cairn beside it that every walker adds a stone to, draws walkers from every quarter of the island; the village of Achadh gharbh raised a long hall for them in the eleventh year, and its ledger held the names of walkers from fifty shires by the end of that decade. At Seann Dunn in the twenty-second year forty households of the east coast raised a common hall with two hearths and no image, where each year the living heads of the families name their ancestors aloud; from the next year the hall sent an Leabharlann a copy of its naming, a little longer every year. At Inis bheag in the north-east Na Seann Spioradan house keeps a parish roll older than the coming of the humans, which its keeper let be copied in the nineteenth year and would not let travel. At Làrach an Teampaill in the east the elders of the district asked in the thirty-first year that an Leabharlann measure the ruin rather than open it, and Ailean Leabhar entered it as measured, and let be. At Àth fhiadhaich, where the oldest folk rite on the island is kept by a ford whose fishers still bless their nets, the house gave Comhairle nam Mèinnean's copper working no blessing in the sixtieth year, and spoke against the naming of Sràid an t-Sluic.

Na Seann Spioradan keep also the fear of certain ground. Above Doire uaine the herders will not graze the one shoulder of hill where old writings tell of the Marbh-bheò, a thing that is dead and does not rest; a shepherd new to the country was lost there in the thirty-ninth year, and the magistrate entered wolves. At the old ground of Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, closed to new graves in the ninth year, families still tend the graves, and some say the place is not empty after dark.

## Scoiltean nan Draoidhean, the druid schools

Scoiltean nan Draoidhean of Flidais, silent since the humans ruled, opened again in the forty-sixth year. Their oldest question, why the vein answers some hands and not others, pressed hard once more, for the vein itself was failing. Their first teacher, Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain, lectured in the sacred wood of Flidais near Muileann chiar in the east, where the druids last met under the humans, and put the question plainly; she told an old cutter's son that she did not mean to answer it before she finished teaching it. Villagers mended the long-shut druid house by the wood with their own hands before the first lecture. Within half a year the houses of Siorrachd Baile gharbh in the north-east opened at Baile gharbh and Àth fhada, and their teachers walked south each winter to hear her. In the wet months the school kept a house at Caol shean, where its students copied the census line on fuil-ghuail and disputed it openly; Comann nan Gualadairean gave the school its hall at Muileann dhearg in the forty-seventh year on condition that nothing taught there named a living captain or gallery. In the forty-ninth year the school's students laid copies of Colbh Cnoc chiar, Carragh Cnoc bheag and Calbh Cnoc ghorm side by side and found signs in common and no meaning, and Seonaid wrote that Seann-Dhaoine were here and said so, and that none living can hear it. In the fifty-first year she wrote to Comhairle nam Mèinnean that Sireadh na Dàrna Fèithe asked the wrong question, that the school had always asked why the vein answers some hands and no one had asked why there is one vein; Comhairle nam Mèinnean filed the letter. Catrìona nic Neacail, who had drawn the one sheet, came to teach at Caol shean in the fifty-seventh year, and the bell of Muileann dhearg's shut hall rang in the school's tower for her first lecture.

## An Eaglais, the Church of the Tuathaich

Most Tuathaich towns keep An Eaglais their forebears brought at An t-Aiseag, answering to bishops of their own. In the first year the council shut the humans' chapel at Ros bheag for good and carted its bell north. In the ninth year the north raised at Cnoc ghorm the greatest church it had built since the crossing closed, of stone hauled on carts Tional nan Tuathach paid for by subscription, and hung in its tower the Ros bheag bell, crated in a barn since the first spring. In the tenth year the church opened a school for its priests, with a master from Seann Skell, teaching in the humans' tongue and reading am Bìoball in the Dia-thìris text the preachers use on the west coast; in all the north both were taught there only. The Tuathaich kept quietly the telling of A' Chiad Churach, the voyage of Naomh Breandan to Inis nam Beannaichte, which the Dia-thìrich priesthood calls superstition still.

The north kept a thin blessing of the boats at Cnoc ghorm each spring. After Seann Bhral turned the north away in the twenty-fifth year, Mary Coyle made of it Fleadh na Mara, the feast of the sea: on the second Sunday of an Gearran in the twenty-sixth year more than fifty boats were drawn up at Cnoc ghorm and the parish priest blessed each hull with heather dipped in harbour water, and David Hale counted them for Tional nan Tuathach's day-book. The north has kept it there every year since, and within a generation it was greater than the western feast. An Eaglais's registers, like Tional nan Tuathach's books, stayed in the north when the shire chests were called in.

## Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise, the Mystery of the Grey Night

Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise is an office within An Eaglais and not quite of it, older than the buildings it was kept in and answerable to no one south of Muileann fhionn. Its keepers hold one night of silence in the year under Sìne, an Gèadh Sìtheil, the sect's name for an Gèadh Fiadhaich, and outsiders are not told which night it is. In the ninth year the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane kept the vigil in the open on the headland below the old light at Baile chrom, for the first time since An Dealachadh; the bishops called it irregular in a letter that stayed folded behind the altar-cloth, and the vigil was kept in the open again the next year and the year after. In the twenty-seventh year a Dia-thìreach magistrate at Baile chrom forbade the gatherings as disorderly and fined Hannah Crane; Tional nan Tuathach paid the balance of her fine, and the vigil went indoors, into a byre lent by a family of the rite, and went on. In the thirty-second year the Dia-thìreach keeper of the light at Baile chrom reported the vigil kept silent in the tower's own shadow and made no move against it, and no order came back. The vigil-books of Baile chrom stay in the north in private keeping.
