# The Reckoning and the Record

The kingdom changed how it counted its years in this age, and made the offices that keep its record: Leabharlann Muileann chaol in the sixteenth year, Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd in the eighteenth. It counted its own people three times, and kept the count it liked least locked in a keeping-room. The north kept its own books and did not send them. This appendix gives the count of years as the council's warrant fixed it, Clàr nan Aoisean as it stands at the close of the age, the calendar the kingdom keeps, an Leabharlann's books, Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd and its foot-notes, the three censuses, the calling-in of the shire chests, and Tional nan Tuathach's own records, all as they stand in the seventy-first year.

## The count of the Diosal given up

For the first years of the kingdom the island dated its public writings as it had through An Aois Choigreach, by the civil day and month the humans brought and by the year of An Diosal, the count that runs from An Diosal of a' Scaradh fleet in the river pool below Seann Skell of the west. The keeper's proclamation at the founding of an Leabharlann in the sixteenth year was dated the year one thousand nine hundred and forty-five of An Diosal, and it is among the last public writings so dated.

The count had served the scribes of the rolls. It served an Leabharlann ill. Everything older than An Diosal had to be counted backwards in years that ran into the thousands, An Ceangal nearly three thousand years before An Diosal and Teine a' Chlachair nearly thirteen thousand, numbers no keeper could write with a steady hand, since no roll and no telling measured them. Ailean Leabhar disliked it besides for running from an absence, saying that a people ought not to have to name every year of its life after the day its kin went away. He set his copyists instead to turning every date the cellar and the tithe ledgers held into the civil calendar, month and day exact where the roll gave them exact, season and year alone where it gave no more, and to setting each into the years of an age.

In the eighteenth year the council's warrant that made the office of royal chronicler ordered that the kingdom's records should count their years no longer by An Diosal but by the ages of an Leabharlann's table, each age from its own first event: six ages as the table then stood. The chronicler's first year was entered as the eighteenth of Linn an Dealachaidh, the first year so written in the kingdom's own record rather than borrowed from an older count. Thereafter An Diosal was kept only in old books. Its name lives on in Là an Diosail, and in the quay at Seann Skell of the west.

## The table of the ages

The copyists of the cellar had cut the past into ages before an Leabharlann had a keeper, out of the customary tellings of Clann na Ceiste laid against Clàr nan Rìghrean the custodians' rolls preserved. In laying the two side by side in the seventeenth year, Ailean Leabhar's reckoners found that nearly half of the copyists' eldest age fell within the single life of Ailean Mòr, and by the consent of all seven copyists that life was counted an age of its own. It was the first change an Leabharlann made to any reckoning it had not itself invented. Each age is counted from the first thing remembered in it; its first year is year one, and there is no year nought; an age's last year is also the first year of the next.

An Aois Choigreach closes with An Dealachadh on the first day of its hundred and fifty-first year, and the first events of An Aois Rìoghachd follow within that same year, which is therefore also the first of Linn an Dealachaidh. Linn an Dealachaidh is counted from the fixing of Tìr-dhùthchais nan Tuathach, read at Caol mhòr on the twenty-second of am Màrt. An Aois Naomh is counted from the fall of Ailean Mòr at Àth na Fala, three weeks before An Ceangal, so that no age should outlast the man it was named for. In running prose Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd is content to write "the fortieth year of Linn an Dealachaidh", or, among Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd's own clerks, simply "the fortieth year".

## The civil calendar

The kingdom keeps the civil calendar the humans brought, since a century and a half of contracts, ledgers and letters already stood on it and no one on the council wished to return the kingdom's debts to the moon. Its year begins in am Faoilleach. Its twelve months have the Dia-thìris names the clerks gave the humans' months: am Faoilleach, an Gearran, am Màrt, an Giblean, an Cèitean, an t-Ògmhios, an t-Iuchar, an Lùnastal, an t-Sultain, an Dàmhair, an t-Samhain and an Dùbhlachd, thirty or thirty-one days each, an Gearran twenty-eight with a twenty-ninth day every fourth year. An Creideamh Sean keeps the reckoning of the moon in its shrines, and the feasts of Brìde, Bealltainn, Lùnastal and Samhain fall on the firsts of their months. Tional nan Tuathach and the Tuathaich parish books give the civil day and month and, in their dealings with the kingdom, the kingdom's era; what count they keep among themselves an Leabharlann cannot say.

## The Library's books

Leabharlann Muileann chaol was founded in the sixteenth year on the custodians' rolls and bound books found in a wet cellar under the old grain-store, where they had stood on planks above standing water since before An Dealachadh, the lowest tier gone soft as bread. The council's minute-books came east to it that summer in four sacks, and the humans' tithe ledgers Ailean mac Iain had boxed unread fourteen years before came after them. Its keeper's warrant bade him receive, order and keep whatever was sent to him. An Leabharlann's books of this age are these.

**Leabhar nan Sloinnidhean**, Leabhar nan Sloinnidhean, begun in the seventeenth year for nothing but lines of descent: Sloinneadh a' Chlachair, found wedged behind a shifted course of brick in the cellar, and after it Sloinneadh Aisling, Cailean mac Eachainn's line set down in a later hand in the margin of a camp roll of Àth ìseal, which Coinneach Clàrc noticed on copying the roll a third time.

**Leabhar nan Aithrisean**, Leabhar nan Aithrisean, begun the same year for the tales the Àth ìseal roll's margin also kept, set down by firelight rather than by day: Marbh-bheò, the white stag, a captain's boast of a wolf and a door held alone. Na Bliadhnachan Fada of Ailean Mòr and his children are entered in it beside them without correction.

**The reading-book**, in which every reader is entered who comes with a letter from a shire maor. Its first name is a shipwright of Ros dhomhain, who copied three drawings of the humans' hulls over four days in the seventeenth year and asked that an Leabharlann keep a copy of his copy too.

**The register of finds**, which enters an Leabharlann's own diggings: the wall at Làrach an Dùin of the thirty-first year, entered as of Seann-Dhaoine, purpose unknown; Làrach an Teampaill, measured and let be; and Carragh Cnoc bheag, copied stroke by stroke by Una and fenced.

**Leabhar nam Breith**, Eilidh nic Leòid's gathered rulings on Tuathaich land, bound in plain calf in the sixteenth year and given in copy to every circuit justice.

An Leabharlann keeps as well the lists the orders and towns send it: the Seann Dunn hall's naming of the dead each year from the twenty-third; Comann nan Gualadairean' sick-roll of four hundred men sent unasked from the bath-house at Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh; Òrd an t-Seabhaig' birth-rolls of Siorrachd Ros gheal, filed under Òrd an t-Seabhaig at their asking; the copy of Inis bheag's parish roll, the book itself kept at home. It holds Iain Mapa's survey of the twenty-sixth year twice over: the copy of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own that Catrìona nic Neacail sent in the fifty-seventh year with a note of when Comhairle nam Mèinnean received it, and his own copy, sent by his widow with Comhairle nam Mèinnean's receipt still pinned to it; and a Holy Age survey that named the hills above Dùn dhearg and the moors south of Baile ruadh searched once already and barren, which Mòrag nic Coinnich found in the lower room in the fifty-first year, the same week the forty-first shaft of the second search was closed. The phrase *a chur san Leabharlann*, to put a thing in an Leabharlann, was first said at Muileann chaol by a council clerk who thought a boundary query could be left there settled; Ailean corrected him sharply, and the correction fixed the phrase.

An Leabharlann's paper, and nearly every page of Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd in this age, came from the mill at Tobar dhìreach on the stream below the village.

## The royal chronicle

The office of royal chronicler was made in the eighteenth year, and its first holder, Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, wrote within the week of her swearing that she would keep the record at Muileann chaol beside an Leabharlann and not at the capital, a record kept beside the throne being kept for the throne. In the spring of the nineteenth year she sent the first chronicle circuit out in three pairs of clerks: the southern chests yielded the arms of five hundred and five burghs; the eastern the returns of the shires nearer the mountain; the northern chest, from Àth àrsaidh, the maor's returns and not a line in a Tuathaich hand, which she entered plainly with no further word. She let one idiom into the record, writing that the succession of the twenty-second year came *mar a thàinig Niall*, and resolved to admit no other while the office was hers. She wrote the succession of the twenty-eighth year as a bare entry of three facts. Her last entry, in the forty-second year, was a plain list of what Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd under her hand had never held: the northern towns' own accounts, the humans' old treaties, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's surveys left unrolled.

Tormod Pinn opened his own first book by copying her list whole, and added beneath it on the southern circuit a line of his own, that a gap named is worth more than a gap forgotten. He struck through his own note of a hungry year to follow the panthers' crossing when the harvest of the sixty-third year proved thick, leaving the words legible beneath the line, holding that a chronicle owes its readers its wrong guesses as plainly as its right ones.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, chronicler from the sixty-fifth year, keeps the list at the front of each year's book unchanged and adds her foot-notes beneath it: in her first year, that the census clerks had relied on Tional nan Tuathach's own figures; in the sixty-eighth, that an Leabharlann's fourth floor stands ready and no cart has come; in the seventy-first, that the record cannot learn how many grates in the capital's poorer wards went unlit for want of coal, a count no census asked for and no shire kept.

## The censuses

**The first census**, of the seventh year, counted the kingdom's people for the first time since it took back the island. It entered every town under the faith its people keep, and entered the families of the far north-western cape and the smallest islets as *gun chreideamh*. Its roll noted, and said no more of it, how few fuil-ghuail are. In Siorrachd Doire ghlas the Dia-thìrich clerks counted the Tuathaich towns from the road and entered few of the houses; Tional nan Tuathach kept its own tally that year, and the two totals differed by nearly a fifth. Each side holds to its own. The head-due was first gathered on the households this census counted.

**The second census**, of the thirty-third year, set a new column beside the first, what a Dia-thìreach household holds and what its Tuathach neighbour holds, and so measured for the first time how far the Tuathaich live below the Dia-thìrich. The sums went onto a stack in the council's keeping-room and no further; the twelve gave out only that the figures wanted study. Seonag nic Ille-Mhoire, who had ridden her own shire's tally in, asked a council clerk to see the island's whole sum and was refused, and wrote the asking and the refusal the same evening into a private book. It is the only mention of the report outside the council.

**The third census**, of the sixty-fourth year, was the first whose figures were published openly. Two census clerks rode to Caol mhòr, the first southern office to ask Tional nan Tuathach for its own count, and Daniel Frayne gave them Tional nan Tuathach's figures, kept every third year since the room opened, with the school and vigil registers. The published figures, read aloud at Tional nan Tuathach in the sixty-fifth year, showed the northern towns at about half the Dia-thìrich measure in every column the clerks chose to take. Tional nan Tuathach's minute says only: *Now written.* The census, like the first, has a line for fuil-ghuail that gives a number and nothing more, and Tormod Pinn copied it without comment. In the shires of Lia Fàil the houses published their own count beside the kingdom's, and the two agreed within a hundred.

## The shire chests called in

In the sixty-sixth year Mòrag nic Coinnich sent her clerks along the roll to bring every shire's record-chest to Muileann chaol, judging a history kept in forty scattered chests no history at all. Most shires sent what they held, some gladly and some because a council notice told them to. Cathair naomh sent two carts with a town chronicle kept by one family through four generations, ridden south by the family's eldest daughter. Seann Toll sent its chest willingly and asked for a copy of all it held; the copy, bound and set beside the original before either left the shire's hall, became the pattern for every later copy. At Seann Tarr the old keeper burned the chest rather than send it, and met the clerks at the gate the next morning with the smoke on his coat; its record is the only one of the calling-in an Leabharlann never read. An Leabharlann doubled its holdings and took the lesser archives of the provinces into one record, and from that time the history of the kingdom is kept in one place and not in many.

The new wing at Muileann chaol, built of the grey spoil-stone of An Sloc Mòr, was finished in the sixty-eighth year. The provinces' returns, town chronicles and vigil-books the shires sent fill three floors. The fourth, raised to the same measure, is left empty for what was not sent.

## The records that stayed north

No Tuathaich record was called in. Tional nan Tuathach's day-books and minute-books, kept from the second year in a fishmonger's ruled ledger bought at Caol mhòr and then in Walter Hale's small upright hand, David Hale's after him; Tional nan Tuathach's counts of its people every third year; the school registers, the first opened by Agnes Pike in the fifteenth year with eleven scholars; Clàr nan Leòn, the register of the dead and maimed of the western pits, begun in the forty-first year on the families' own word, its second copy kept apart at Muileann leathan against fire or flood; the churches' registers; and the vigil-books of Baile chrom all stay in the north, in private keeping, as they always have. When the council's notice of the calling-in reached Caol mhòr in the sixty-seventh year, Daniel Frayne put it to the room whether Tional nan Tuathach should offer its books south. After an evening's debate the minute records no vote by count and two words at the close of the sitting: *kept north.* At Cnoc thais in the sixty-ninth year Sam Varley opened a book of the pit's own accounts headed only with the day and the pit's name, the first page of that town's register of its pits. Of what these books hold, an Leabharlann knows only what their keepers have chosen to tell it.
