# The Crown and its Offices

The kingdom proclaimed from the counting-house steps at Cathair dhearg in the second year of Linn an Dealachaidh named itself Rìoghachd Dia-thìr and was ruled, for most purposes, as the council had ruled before it. A crown was set on a head in the third year, and Comhairle na Rìoghachd sat on beneath it; An Leabharlann, Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd, Britheamhan na Cuairte and Comhairle nam Mèinneans were made in the years that followed, each by the council's own sentence and each with less said of its purpose than its holders afterward needed. This appendix gives the offices as the council's minute-books, Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd and an Leabharlann's rolls give them at the close of the age, in the seventy-first year, with the ages of their holders where the rolls allow them. Ages are given in whole years, at the taking of an office and at its end. The rulers' years are those of An Crùn's own roll, and nothing is set down of them here that Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd does not itself say.

## The rulers of the restored line

**Niall** was crowned at Cathair dhearg in the third year, at five-and-twenty, being born in the hundred and twenty-eighth year of An Aois Choigreach. The crowning passed without quarrel, so smoothly that a debt paid without a fight was said thereafter to have gone *mar a thàinig Niall*, the way Niall came; an innkeeper's ledger at Cathair dhearg holds the first written instance of the saying, and Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd the first in its own hand, at the next crowning. The mint struck his likeness on Bonn na Rìoghachd from the fifth year. He reigned nineteen years and died at Cathair dhearg in the twenty-second year, at four-and-forty. The bells of Òrd Mhacha rang a day and a night for him, and the capital kept no market.

**Brìghde**, Niall's daughter, born in the first year of the age, was crowned in the twenty-second year at one-and-twenty, within the week of her father's death, and the council swore its oath of service afresh before her. The chronicler wrote that the succession came *mar a thàinig Niall*, and admitted no other idiom to the record in all her years. Brìghde reigned six years and died in the twenty-eighth year at seven-and-twenty. No cause of her death is made known, and Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd enters none.

**Eòghan**, Brìghde's cousin, born in the hundred and forty-sixth year of An Aois Choigreach, was crowned in the twenty-eighth year at two-and-thirty. The council's sitting on the succession did not pass as quietly as the crowning: its own minute names Tormod mac Iain Òig and Sìleas nic Artair as the two who stood against seating him, gives no reason either offered, and records that the two did not sit again. He reigned thirty years, the longest reign of the age. It was in his reign that An Traoghadh, first noted without remark in the survey reports of the twenty-sixth year, became known to all. His reign ended in the fifty-eighth year, when he was two-and-sixty. Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd gives no death. The die that struck his likeness on the coin was broken at Cathair dhearg by the mint's custom, the old coin kept good until the new was struck in number.

**Mairead**, born in the twentieth year of the age, was crowned in the fifty-eighth year at eight-and-thirty, and inherited a kingdom whose founding wealth was plainly failing for the first time since the restoration. She reigns at the close of the age, in her fifty-second year.

## The council of twelve

The council was seated at Cathair dhearg on the afternoon of the twenty-third of an t-Sultain in the second year, twelve chairs set round a table too large for the room, drawn by the proclamation from the custodian families that had kept the island's old law through the humans' years and from the captains who had led the companies of Cogadh nam Beann. Its minute-books are the oldest unbroken record of the state in this age. It sat on a fixed day, heard petitions read by its under-clerks, and wrote on most of what came before it the two words the north learned by heart: *received; filed*. The councillors the minute-books name in this age are these.

**Fearchar mac Raghnaill**, captain of Cogadh nam Beann, whose plea that his company's roll not be burned at the standing-down spared every company roll after it. He was four-and-forty when the council was seated and had alone among the twelve walked the north roads. He died in the twenty-sixth year at eight-and-sixty.

**Beathag nic Artair**, of the old custodian houses, seven-and-forty at the seating, who asked whether Tional nan Tuathach's first letter required an answer and was told that nothing in it asked for one. She sat until her death in the fortieth year.

**Griogair mac Tormoid**, a custodian of the western shore, eight-and-forty at the seating; he died in the thirty-fifth year.

**Cailean mac Sheumais**, who commanded a company at the fords of the western hills, four-and-forty at the seating, and who asked, when Tional nan Tuathach's letter was read, what a Moot was. He died in the thirtieth year.

**Seumas mac Coinnich Ghlais**, of Ceann àrsaidh, for the custodian houses of the east, seven-and-forty at the seating. In the tenth year, in the eighth year of his seat, he brought a map to the table and proposed a marked border between the kingdom and Tìr-dhùthchais nan Tuathach; the debate ran two hours, no vote was taken, and the proposal was not renewed. He died in the thirtieth year at five-and-seventy.

**Tormod mac Iain Òig** and **Sìleas nic Artair**, who stood against the seating of Eòghan in the twenty-eighth year, Tormod at six-and-fifty and Sìleas at three-and-fifty. Neither sat again. Tormod went home to his lands in the western shires and died in the forty-fourth year; Sìleas gave her Na Seann Spioradan kin's house the time council business had taken and died in the forty-ninth. Their seats were filled before the year turned by a landed man's son of the western shires and a widow of a coastal house.

The council's own clerks are named where their work touches the record: **Uilleam mac Griogair**, clerk of council, who sent the under-clerk Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill east to Muileann chaol in the seventeenth year and named her to the new office on the strength of a report three men had read a page of between them; **Tòmas**, a mason's clerk before the war, who drew Clàr nan Suaicheantas; and **Ruairidh**, master of the counting-house room at Cathair dhearg, who told Ailean mac Iain to box the humans' ledgers unread.

## The masters of the mint

The mint was set up in the old human counting-house at Cathair dhearg in the fifth year under **Fearchar mac Dhòmhnaill, Fearchar Bonn**, then three-and-forty, who pressed the seal of each coin with an ink ground from coal dust so that a clipped coin shows the colour at its edge. He kept the mint until his death in the thirty-first year at nine-and-sixty. The rolls name no master after him; the mint was kept by its own clerks under the council's warrant.

## The keepers of the Library

An Leabharlann was founded at Muileann chaol by the council's sentence in the sixteenth year, its keeper to receive, to order and to keep whatever was sent to him, and nothing more said.

**Ailean mac Iain**, called Ailean Leabhar, first keeper, from the sixteenth year to the fortieth. He was born in the hundred and twenty-sixth year of An Aois Choigreach, a hill-born man of the council's service who had ridden as tally-clerk to the guard at the heads of the north roads at five-and-twenty, boxed the humans' counting-houses, kept the log of An Sireadh and drawn the shires. He was forty when named keeper. He parted An Aois Ailein from the eldest age on the consent of his seven copyists, opened Leabhar nan Sloinnidhean and Leabhar nan Aithrisean, and drafted the warrants of the five lights. He was found dead at his desk on the third of an t-Sultain in the fortieth year, at four-and-sixty, and buried in the small ground behind an Leabharlann, where no keeper had been buried before him.

**Mòrag nic Coinnich**, his deputy from the thirty-second year, on the condition that the register name her deputy and never keeper. She took the keepership in the fortieth year at two-and-forty and held it thirty years. She called the shire chests in to Muileann chaol in the sixty-sixth year and built the new wing, three floors filled and a fourth left empty for what was not sent. She laid the keepership down in the seventieth year at two-and-seventy.

**Raghnall mac Mhuirich**, her deputy, keeper from the seventieth year at fifty. In his first year he changed nothing of an Leabharlann's hours or custom.

## The royal chroniclers

The office of royal chronicler was made with Clàr nan Aoisean, and its first year entered as the eighteenth of Linn an Dealachaidh. Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd was kept at Muileann chaol, beside an Leabharlann, and not at the capital.

**Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill**, a council clerk's daughter of the capital, sworn under-clerk beside Ailean mac Iain in the second year, sent east to report on an Leabharlann at four-and-forty, and sworn royal chronicler on the twentieth of an t-Samhain. She rode the first chronicle circuit's returns into the book, let one idiom into the record and no other, and wrote the succession of the twenty-eighth year as a bare entry. She laid the office down in the forty-second year at nine-and-sixty, her last entry a plain list of what Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd had never held, and died in the forty-eighth year at five-and-seventy.

**Tormod mac Sheumais**, called Tormod Pinn for the speed of his hand, clerk of the first circuit at three-and-thirty, royal chronicler from the forty-second year at six-and-fifty. He opened his book by copying Gormshuil's list of gaps whole, ruled the two towns of Dùn thais apart, drew the raiders' names from a keeper's log, and struck through his own guess of a hungry year when the harvest proved thick. He died in the sixty-fifth year at nine-and-seventy and was buried behind an Leabharlann.

**Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh**, of an Leabharlann's senior clerks, royal chronicler from the sixty-fifth year at three-and-fifty. She keeps Tormod Pinn's list at the front of each year's book and sets her own foot-notes beneath it.

## The high custodians of Òrd Bhrìde

**Dòmhnall mac Thormoid** was named Àrd-choimheadaiche of Òrd Bhrìde and seated at Seann Skell on the southern coast in the sixth year, at six-and-fifty, the first Àrd-choimheadaiche Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail ever had. His first charge was to write its order of worship, and he read it first near the end of am Faoilleach, in the hall with the blackened beams. He asked a share in Òrd Mhacha's keeping of the coronation regalia and was refused. He died in the twenty-fourth year at four-and-seventy. His successors the north knew only by their letters, and Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd does not name them.

## The circuit justices

**Eilidh nic Leòid** of Cathair dhomhain was named first circuit justice in the seventh year, her circuit eleven shires of the south-west and most of a year in the riding. She was one-and-fifty when she gave Breith Leòid at Seann Bhral in the west in the eighth year, that land held under human title passes to An Crùn and the working of it stays with the holders; she rode the northern circuit from the fifteenth year, heard the seventeen suits at Ceann mhòr, and gathered her rulings into Leabhar nam Breith. She died at Cathair dhomhain in the sixteenth year at nine-and-fifty. Her clerk on every circuit was **Murchadh Clàrc**.

**Uilleam mac Leòid**, her brother's son, named to the northern circuit in the sixteenth year at six-and-twenty, riding with Leabhar nam Breith behind his clerk's saddle. He died in the sixtieth year.

**Ruairidh mac Phàdraig**, circuit justice at Seann Bhral in the west, who ruled in the fifty-ninth year, at four-and-forty, that Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn is a carrier of An Crùn and must carry for any who pay. Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn appealed, and the council let the appeal lie.

## The Boards

The **Comhairle nam Mèinnean** sat at Cathair dhearg from Cead an t-Sluic Mhòir until the twenty-seventh year, when it moved its clerks to Cathair mhòr and set down that its surveys should be kept in its own presses and not sent to an Leabharlann. **Ruairidh mac Ailein** of Comhairle nam Mèinnean signed the receipt for Iain Mapa's survey of the twenty-sixth year. From the fifty-second year Comhairle nam Mèinnean sat in the stone hall of Taigh Mhic Ùisdein, let to it for the house's debts. The office of **surveyor-general**, left empty three years, was given in the fifty-sixth year to **Catrìona nic Neacail**, then eight-and-thirty, the youngest in living memory to hold it; she resigned it in the fifty-seventh year at nine-and-thirty to teach at Caol shean.

The **Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn** was made to run the mended coal line in the eighth year, one clerk and two engineers at first, with its office at Cathair dhearg. The **Comhairle nan Taighean-solais**, Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, keeps the five lights the humans built and no others, its charter naming five.

## The speakers of the Moot

Tional nan Tuathach has no standing in An Crùn's law, and An Crùn's roll does not name it; its own day-books name its speakers.

**Walter Hale**, once a harbour clerk of Ros bheag, born in the hundred and seventh year of An Aois Choigreach, speaker from the opening of Tional nan Tuathach's book in the second year, at five-and-forty, until his death at Caol mhòr on the twenty-eighth of an Gearran in the thirty-third year, at six-and-seventy. He held the chair one-and-thirty years.

**Mary Coyle**, a fisherman's widow of Caol mhòr, the first woman in the chair, speaker from the thirty-third year at two-and-fifty until she laid it down in the fifty-fifth year at four-and-seventy. She died in the sixtieth year at nine-and-seventy.

**Daniel Frayne** of Muileann leathan, bookbinder and Tional nan Tuathach's clerk, the first speaker born after An Dealachadh closed, chosen in the fifty-fifth year at fifty. He holds the chair at the close of the age.

## The house of Mac Ùisdein

**Uisdean mac Ùisdein** of Cathair mhòr, whose family held an overseer's post under the humans at An Sloc Mòr, took the concession in the eleventh year at five-and-forty, on the humans' terms with the names changed and a fifth to An Crùn. The house raised the market cross at Cathair mhòr in the twentieth year and its stone hall in the thirtieth. Uisdean died in the thirty-eighth year at two-and-seventy. His grandson **Alasdair mac Ùisdein** borrowed against the house's share in the fiftieth year, at four-and-thirty, to pay for Sireadh na Dàrna Fèithe; after its forty-one dry shafts the house sold half its share back to An Crùn and let its hall to Comhairle nam Mèinnean in the fifty-second year, and in the sixtieth An Crùn bought its last working shafts, leaving it its share in the spoil and the quarry.

## The Hales and the Pikes

**Ellen Hale**, Walter's wife, kept the family's first tallies of the shared catch and taught David his letters; she died in the twentieth year. **David Hale**, born in the hundred and forty-third year of An Aois Choigreach, kept Tional nan Tuathach's minute-books in his father's time and after, counted the boats at the first Fleadh na Mara, and was turned away at the gate of the first fair with his father's wool; he died in the sixty-seventh year. **Alice Hale** opened Tional nan Tuathach's second school, in a net-store at Caol mhòr, in the fifteenth year.

**Agnes Pike**, a miller's daughter of the Pike mill at Doire mhin, opened the byre school at Doire ghlas on the fourteenth of am Faoilleach in the fifteenth year, at four-and-thirty, and kept it until her death in the fifty-eighth year at seven-and-seventy.
