# Roads, Houses, Bridges and Measures

The Holy Age was carried on people's backs. Until the last four centuries of the age there was no cart on the island. The coal came down from the galleries in baskets, the salt went inland in baskets, and the fish went up-country dried and bundled. Heavy stones went on ox-sledges when they had to go at all. Everything the rìghrean ruled, they ruled along the carriers' roads. This appendix tells what those roads were, the houses and bridges on them, and the measures by which the island reckoned what went along them.

## I. The Carriers' Roads

The first roads were the mining paths, worn by carriers taking the Hall's baskets down from the galleries under the mountain to the households of every share. They were footpaths, and for most of the age most of them stayed footpaths, widened where the traffic wore them and mended where a household cared to. The oldest named road work was the path to Ceann chaol, where carriers going down a steep mile of bare rock had turned more ankles than the Hall had troubled to count. In FE 149 Ailean Clachair of the fourth hearth took on the cutting of steps there, and they were finished two years later. In FE 154 the Abhainn dhomhain rose in the night and took eleven carriers and their baskets at a ford. The Hall mourned the coal with the carriers, and the island began to think about bridges.

By the time of Goraidh Mòr the island had four roads that everyone named. The mountain road ran west to the potters' harbour of Doire shean. The western road ran to the harbours of the west coast and the bridge-foot of the Abhainn dhomhain. The southern road ran across the panthers' crossing to the gulf. The northern road ran over the high pass to the northern river. The eastern road ran north from Seann Dunn to the stag's country. Mòrag nan Rathad, in the first years of her reign, walked every carriers' road on the island and had the bad stretches made anew, the first ruler to walk the whole circuit. No ruler walked it again until Anna Ruadh, who walked it twice in the twenty-ninth century.

The great cats' crossing ran straight over the southern road between the southern hills and the fens. From FE 421, by Eilidh nic Ìomhair's ruling, no panther might be killed on that road while the crossing lasted, and carriers caught there at the wrong hour sat and waited for the last of them to clear it.

Rivers carried as well as roads. In FE 1,665 a landing for river-boats was built on the Abhainn uaine at Cidhe Beag, over the carriers' protest, so that lowland grain could go upriver by water. The flat boats of hide over withy were built at Muileann chrom, whose boatmen swore their fellowship on their own paddles. Carts came onto the lowland roads in numbers only from about FE 2,571. A cart asked for a level bed and a bridge wide enough to take it, and the fellowship of masons spent the next two centuries widening the bridges.

## II. The Waypoint Houses

The first waypoint houses were raised along the mining roads from FE 166, a day's carrying apart, so that a carrier with a basket could sleep under a roof. They were kept by families, not lords, and the island's circuit of houses grew out of them.

- **Mòr nic Dhùghaill's house** at Doire shean (FE 169), where a widow with more buffalo than grass boiled a haunch for three carriers caught in the rain and asked nothing for it. She served jugged buffalo in a wooden bowl, and her line kept the house through the whole age. It is Am Buabhall Fortanach, the Auspicious Buffalo.
- **The house at the middle of the road** (FE 172), where the carriers' road from the mountain meets the road to the western harbours.
- **The far-off house**, so called because a traveller was never nearer to it than halfway. The keepers of the waypoint houses first set down the road-peace there. It was rebuilt in stone in the twenty-seventh century and kept its name.
- **The sunny house** (FE 604), a turf longhouse on the eastern road where the carriers from Cnoc leathan meet the southern coast path, named for the way its south wall catches the morning. Its keepers mended the worst of the road out of their own stores for three generations. Anna Ruadh granted it a toll of its own, Slighe Cnoc leathan, the only charter any waypoint house holds, and had it cut in stone.
- **The golden house** (FE 781) on the road north of Seann Dunn, the first waypoint house raised by a ruler and not a family. Mòrag nan Rathad opened it with a night's feast for every carrier who could be reached in time, none charged the toll, and they called it golden before its timber had weathered.
- **The house on the northern road** above Inis mhòr, built for Catrìona Ghlic. In the twenty-seventh century a new keeper there began serving a spirit distilled from barley that the carriers called fire-water. The Keeper ruled that a fire in a man's own belly was no burning the Law had ever been written to answer for.

**The road-peace.** About FE 175 the keepers of the waypoint houses agreed that no feud might be pursued under an inn roof or within a spear's throw of its door. It was the oldest law on the island that neither Keeper nor lord made, and it held through the long strife of the heirs. The hearth-sign token let a carrier on the rìgh's or the Hall's business sleep free at any house on the road.

The houses became the frame the rìghrean ruled through. Their keepers gathered the road toll and kept a share of it. Dòmhnall Crùbach, who could not walk the roads as his mother had, ruled through them, and they grew mighty in his reign. In FE 1,735 the counters of the census of hands made their stations at the waypoint houses, and the keepers fed the counters at the quarter-days.

## III. The Road-Guards and the Tolls

**The toll.** In FE 719 Goraidh Mòr set the first road toll: one fish in twenty, or its worth in whatever a carrier's basket held, to pay the road-guards and mend the bridges. The waypoint keepers gathered it and kept a share, and the rolls say so. The keepers of Ceann mhin took the gathering into their own hands in FE 863 and paid the rìgh what they judged fair, and their judgement was generous to themselves. Sìleas Dhonn found their tallies did not add, bade every keeper render account at her hall once a year, and, once there were letters, had the account cut in them and checked against the road-guard's own cutting at the gate. When the lords of Caol leathan took the western road's toll for themselves and sent none to Dùn dhearg, the result was the War of the Roads.

**Toll-houses and charters.** The toll-house at Àth ghlas (FE 1,661), where the southern road crosses toward Ceann leathan, was the first whose keeper was sent from the rìgh's hall and not chosen in the place. Cathair dhomhain won a charter of its own tolls from Beathag Mhòr in FE 1,699. The masons' fellowship set the toll on its bridge at Dùn chrom at half of what the capital's crossing asked, and no reeve of Cathair dhearg ever took up its offer to hand the bridge to the crown.

**The road-guards.** The toll paid the guards of the roads, first raised under Goraidh Mòr from Dùn dhearg. They walked for twenty-six centuries and rode the small tough horses of the southern grass country from FE 2,583. The riders of that first company are the first whose names their roll keeps. From FE 1,654 they were paid in stamped silver rings. The guards of the southern ford held a yearly meeting of staves at Ceann leathan, which Mòrag Dhubh of Cnoc mhòr won in FE 1,665, and which Anna Ruadh gave a stone-walled field. The guards of Caol leathan swore their watch on the white wolf-stone.

## IV. The Bridges

Stone crossings began to join the mining paths to the coast from FE 157. The first great one was Ailean Clachair's single span of fitted stone over the river above Ros fhionn (FE 160). Forty cutters and masons of the nine hearths built it, none of whom had ever cut anything meant to hold a river's weight. Ailean cut the small mark of the fourth hearth into its keystone. From that crossing the bridge-masons formed a fellowship bound by an oath sworn on the stone cup at Dùn ìseal: no span was to be closed in the dark, and none was to be paid for in coal. Their lodge was kept near the western crossing.

| Bridge | Year | What is told of it |
|---|---|---|
| Ros fhionn | FE 160 | Ailean Clachair's span; the eastern approach mended on deeper footings after a flood in the twenty-sixth century, with Ailean's sign left untouched |
| Cathair dhearg, the western bridge-foot | before FE 754; rebuilt FE 794 | widened by Mòrag nan Rathad so two laden carriers could pass, then widened again for two carts abreast; the old keystone set in the parapet |
| Dùn chrom | FE 2,578 | joined the southern road to the western; half toll |
| Ceann àrsaidh | FE 2,591 | the fourth stone bridge of the lowlands, built by forty fisher-kin of Sliochd Raghnaill, all named on the fellowship's roll |
| Doire mhin | FE 2,643 | a small span over a stream that flooded every spring; Beathag Clachair cut her name beneath the hearth's sign |

Two customs of the fellowship came out of the last centuries. A mason mending an older crossing left the old signs standing wherever the stone beneath them still stood, and cut his own work a course apart. After Beathag Clachair cut *Beathag Clachair a chuir seo* on the bridge at Doire mhin, masons of every hearth cut their own names beside the fourth hearth's sign, and they call that Beathag's mark.

## V. The Measures

**Of the coal.** The lidded basket of plaited heather, one to a household and filled at Samhain (FE 48). The stone cup, cut by Fionnlagh Dall to hold what a cupped pair of hands will hold (FE 139). The handful, which the Small-Burning Law made the season's whole measure (FE 142).

**Of the silver.** The seam at Muileann chiar was found about FE 71, and Fearchar Airgeadach hammered its silver into rings. The first ring was traded at Ros fhionn in FE 77 against a whole boatload of salted fish and sealskin, and after that a boatman naming a price said "a ring's worth". Silver for salt at Ros fhionn, one ring to a boatload, was the island's first trade in anything but food (FE 84). The rings were the only money of the Holy Age. There was never a coin.

**The weights.** In FE 542 the silversmiths of Muileann chiar cast a set of weights, the least the weight of a barley-corn and the greatest that of a newborn lamb, and every honest trader came to use them. After the Silver Feud (FE 1,745 – 1,750), in which Ruairidh Liath demanded one ring in five of the silver and settled for one in ten, the Muileann chiar weights were declared the only lawful weights on the island's roads. Bronze copies went to every waypoint house, and the house of Fearchar was paid for each. Beitris nic Aonghais cast them at Muileann dhearg with Sliochd Fhearchair's hearth-sign cut on the underside.

**The stamped rings.** From FE 1,654 Fearchar Òg's clerks paid the road-guards in small silver rings of the Muileann chiar weight, stamped with the hall's mark. It was the first payment in metal on the island, and the rings travelled further than the guards did. Ceann leathan's traders had the season's finest ring struck with the ford's own mark beside the hall's.

**The fleece.** On the western plain the lords of Baile Mòr fhionn made the fleece of a grown ewe the measure of their market (FE 1,699). Grain, wine, cattle and horses were priced in fleeces there, and silver rings were taken but reckoned in wool. The lords of the deep river took one fleece in forty at the fords of Baile Mòr dhomhain.

**Of grain.** The miller's due was one measure in sixteen, and the roll calls the miller of Muileann dhomhain fair for taking no more. The common granaries of the provinces lent by the measure and were repaid by it, harvest by harvest.

**The tallies.** Before letters and long after them, every one of these measures was counted on notched sticks: a notch for a basket, a notch for a toll in fish and another for a toll in wool.

## VI. The Lights on the Coast

No coal might feed a beacon (FE 148). The fire on the headland at Tobar dhearg was kept with driftwood, and every sea-light raised after it was kept the same way. After a boat was lost in the calm, the fishers of Baile chrom relit their old beacon (FE 409), and the coast's households took turns carrying driftwood to it. Three beacons were rebuilt in the census year of FE 1,735, one on the eastern cape, one on the northern headland at Muileann bheag and one on the north-eastern cape at Àth dhearg. From the twenty-seventh century the beacons of the western harbours burned seal-oil from Ceann mhòr, the brightest light on Dia-thìr. At the end of the age Tobar dhearg's beacon was built up in stone into a tower, the first coast fire on the island kept above the ground.
