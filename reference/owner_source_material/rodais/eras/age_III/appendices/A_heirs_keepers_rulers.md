# The Heirs, the Keepers and the Rulers

Three rolls carry the Holy Age: the cord of the heirs, knotted at the gathering-house door in the first autumn; the Keepers' roll of Dùn ìseal, recited for thirteen centuries and set down in stag-letters in FE 1,320; and the king-list of the lowlands, first written by Tormod Scrìobhaiche for Ealasaid Chiar in FE 1,299 and kept after that year by year at Dùn dhearg and then at Cathair dhearg. The three do not agree in every place, and the Hall's count of burnings agrees with none of them exactly. What follows gives each office as the rolls give it at the close of the age, with the years of those who held it and their ages, taken at the taking of the office and at its end.

The ages need a word before the names. Ailean Mòr, slain at Àth na Fala on the first morning of the age, was four thousand six hundred and twenty-six years old. His children had long years from him, and every generation after them had fewer. The longest life in any generation was never longer than the longest in the one before it, and no child of the kin outlived a long-lived parent's span. So the rolls show the long years running out of the blood over some twelve centuries. The nine great heirs lived two and three thousand years. Their grandsons lived near a thousand, and Goraidh Mòr, seven generations on, counted himself old at two hundred and fifty. The last of the ruling line whose years were counted long died at a hundred in FE 1,190. No one born in FE 1,200 or after, of the kin or of any other house, is known to have lived past ninety-five. By the close of the age a rìgh at Cathair dhearg could expect the same years as a potter of Baile ghorm.

## I. The Heirs at the Roinn

The cord of the heirs held seven hundred and twelve knots when Sìne nan Snaidhm laid it on the floor of the gathering-house. On the night of the handful in FE 2 the heirs swore Roinn na Rìoghachd, the parting of the kingdom, by all four of the rules they had argued through the winter. There were nine great shares for the nine eldest lines with living heirs. The Red Share, which Ìomhar Dearg had taken with a sword, was parted around rather than given. Cuid nam Marbh, the share of the dead, went to the children of heirs who had died before the parting. Within two years the great shares were parted again into countries, provinces, counties and holdings, until the knot-keepers gave up counting at a hundred and thirty-one provinces.

The holders of the great shares whose seats the tellers keep are these:

- **Murchadh Mòr**, eldest living son of the king, held the north. He was near two thousand nine hundred at the ford, argued the eldest's rule through the whole winter of the sitting, and died in FE 64 at two thousand nine hundred and sixty-one. His share was cut among eleven sons.
- **Eithne nic Ailein** held the lowland plain from her hall at Cathair mhòr. She was nineteen hundred at the ford, and her knife cut through a knot on the cord in the second year. She outlived all her brothers and sisters and died in FE 1,122, three thousand and twenty-one years old, the last of the nine.
- **Fionnghal nic Ailein** held the western shore from Ros dhomhain and died there in FE 262. Her line, Sliochd Fhionnghail, carried the quarrel of the unforgotten wedding.
- **Artair mac Ailein** held the salt shore from Baile ghorm and died in FE 298. His line, Sliochd Artair, was the other side of that quarrel.
- **Cormac mac Ailein** held the east from Caol shean and died in FE 331.
- **Ùna nic Ailein** held the north-west from Muileann àrsaidh.
- **Coll mac Ailein** held the hills east of the Red Hill along the Abhainn fhionn, from Ceann àrsaidh.
- **Gormlaith nic Ailein** held the south-east from Tobar dhearg.
- **Tadhg mac Ailein** held the Abhainn ìseal.

**Niall Òg**, a grandson who held no great share, was some nine hundred at the Roinn. He was the youngest voice at the sitting that anyone remembered, and he walked the roads of the island for eleven centuries after it, calling peaces that did not hold. He died at Cathair mhòr in FE 1,109.

## II. The Red Share

The Red Share was the only share won with a sword before the parting, and its holders at Dùn dhearg were the line from which the rìghrean came.

| Holder | Held | Age at taking | Age at death |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ìomhar Dearg | FE 1 – 121 | 1,399 | 1,519, killed at a ford by Eachann Ruadh |
| Dùghall Dearg mac Ìomhair | FE 121 – 360 | 823 | 1,062 |
| Ruairidh Glas | FE 360 – 612 | 658 | 910 |
| his son, unnamed in the rolls | FE 612 – 650 | 582 | 620 |
| Goraidh mac Ìomhair | from FE 650 | 156 | 259 |

Ruairidh Glas was some three hundred at the first blood at Dùn thais in FE 3 and had never killed a man. Goraidh's father, whom the rolls also leave unnamed, was heir to the share but died in FE 620 at four hundred, before his own father. Goraidh gathered three counties of the east to the Red Share by the deaths of two cousins without heirs and a marriage to a third, Muirne nic Dhonnchaidh. In FE 674 the lowland households who ate from Sliochd Ìomhair's granaries began to call him their rìgh.

## III. The Keepers of the Flame

Coimheadaiche na Lasrach was chosen by the nine hearths from FE 10. From FE 385 the choice lay with the Four Houses of custody: Sliochd Mhuirich of Dùn ìseal, Sliochd Ìomhair of Dùn dhearg, Sliochd Thormoid of Seann Dunn and Sliochd Raghnaill of Seann Tarr. The Keeper kept the flame on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach, the stone cup and the galleries, and could hold no share and no crown. At the close of the age the roll held one hundred and forty names from Beathag Bhàn to Catrìona nic Mhuirich. Seumas Bàn counted that more than half of them were of Sliochd Mhuirich, and nearer two in three counting marriage and fosterage. The Keepers were of no long-lived line, apart from the late, short-lived branch of Ìomhar that Eilidh came from, and their years were always a common household's years. That was the point of them.

**Beathag Bhàn** kept the first flame and was laid on the slope facing the vein with her back to the sea. **Muireach Liath** fixed the lidded basket of heather as the first measure, and ruled that Allt an Àigh belonged to the ancestors and not to the Hall and that silver was not the mountain's. **Gormshuil** shut the galleries to the stag's people. **Fionnlagh Dall** had the stone cup cut anew to hold what a cupped pair of hands will hold and made the Small-Burning Law. He was carried eleven days to Tuam Cnoc dhìreach. **Eilidh nic Ìomhair**, the first Keeper chosen outside the house of Muireach, bound the office to the Four Houses and let the panthers pass on the southern road. **Coinneach Mòr** kept the flame longer than any before him and changed nothing. **Seonaid Chiar** let the stag's cutters back into the galleries and had oak props set after the fall in the high gallery. **Calum Liath** sent cutters with knotted cords to count the vein and died at the Hall's door. **Oighrig Bhàn** bound the first twelve apprentices, and four of them held the flame after her. **Dùghall mac Raghnaill** heard the Silver Feud in the rain-shut Hall. **Raonaid Dhubh** made the shared-meal law with Lachlann Bàn, the first law made by rìgh and Keeper together. **Seumas Bàn** counted his own house on the roll, had the count scraped from its preface, and saw the council part into eight companies. **Catrìona nic Mhuirich** set the stone cup in Mòr nic Coinnich's hands.

The rìgh kept the roads, the granaries and the tolls, and the Keeper kept the flame and the galleries, by the division agreed between Goraidh mac Ìomhair and the council in FE 694. **Goraidh Mòr** raised the timber hall with a granary at each corner and set the first road toll of one fish in twenty. **Mòrag nan Rathad** walked the whole circuit of the roads, the last ruler to do so before Anna Ruadh, and opened the golden house free of toll. **Dòmhnall Crùbach** sent grain to Seann Chwen after the storm. **Sìleas Dhonn** found the waypoint tallies that did not add and died at her ledger. **Iain Garbh** fought the War of the Roads, the first war since the Peace, and left his spear on the road at Baile chiar. **Catrìona Ghlic** was crowned at Dùn dhearg and outlived Eithne nic Ailein by two years. The long years ran out in her grandchildren.

The eleven who followed her are only names in Tormod's roll, some from the reciters and some from living memory. The first of them sat with the flame for a season as a boy. **Ealasaid Chiar** brought Tormod Scrìobhaiche to the hall as her clerk and bade him write the doubt down beside the names. The list keeps no ruler between her and Fearchar Òg; the Keepers' roll carries those years. **Fearchar Òg** came to the hall a boy of seven, paid the road-guards in stamped silver rings, and is remembered chiefly for dying old. **Beathag Mhòr**, called the first ban-rìgh by the clerks of Cathair dhearg, moved the hall to the red stone of the plain and ordered the census of hands. Her body was carried to Dùn dhearg on the longest royal journey the age made. **Ruairidh Liath** asked one ring in five of the silversmiths and settled for one in ten. **Seonag Chrom** gave the clerks a room, and from her reign the list was kept year by year. **Lachlann Bàn** built granaries in every province seat that would have one. **Oighrig nic Lachlainn** was present at the first famine turned back and had no child. After her death the red hall was shut five years. **Gilleasbuig Mòr**, a clerk's son of Seann Dunn, was the first ruler made at Dùn ìseal, with the stone cup set in his hands. He drew a line under Oighrig's name. **Anna Ruadh** walked the circuit twice. **Coinneach Bàn** had the laws copied for every province. **Mòr nic Coinnich** won the hall against her uncle Tormod Donn with the Keeper's word, and was the second and last ruler of the age to hold the cup.

Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill, Mòr's elder daughter, was crowned at Cathair dhearg within the month of her mother's death, by her own house's word and without the cup, at fifteen. The clerk Ruairidh Clèireach found the old roll full and began a new skin with her name at its head. Mòr's name is the last on the roll of the Holy Age.
