# The Shares, the Provinces and the Stones

At the fall of Ailean Mòr the island below the mountain had one king and no divisions but the ones its households kept among themselves. Within two years it had nine great shares, a Red Share, a portion of the dead, forty-four countries and a hundred and thirty-one provinces. The counties under those could not be counted at all. This appendix tells how the land was parted at Roinn na Rìoghachd, how the parting went wrong, how Sìth an Stairsnich fixed it, and how the rìghrean gathered it again into the provinces of An Talla Dearg.

## I. The Four Rules

Through the winter of FE 1 and the summer after it, the heirs in the valley below Dùn ìseal argued four rules for Roinn na Rìoghachd. The first was the eldest's rule: the eldest living heir holds the whole and feeds the rest. Murchadh Mòr argued it hardest. The second was the rule of the living: each living child of the king takes by nearness of blood. The third was the rule of the dead: the children of the king's dead children stand in their parents' places. The fourth was the lot: the shares to be thrown for. No rule could be sworn alone, since each left out a line strong enough to fight. Aodh Crom, eldest of the keepers of the slab, who held no share, set out a settlement that gave each rule something, and the nine strongest lines agreed to it in the second summer.

In the same months the strongest lines swore, fire by fire, that none of the nine should be called rìgh or take the word for a share, since the kingdom had ended with Ailean Mòr. Murchadh Mòr swore it hardest, having watched what the word had cost his father. The clause held for six hundred and seventy years, until the lowland households who ate from Sliochd Ìomhair's granaries began to call Goraidh mac Ìomhair their rìgh. No council ever gave him the word.

## II. The Night of the Handful

On Oidhche an Dòrlaich in FE 2 the keepers brought Sreang nan Oighrean down from its chest and laid it twice round the gathering-house floor, from the hearth to the door and back, with lamps along its length. Nine plain knives were laid out for the nine lines. Murchadh Mòr cut first, in one stroke, exactly where he had long meant to, and took the north from Baile Mòr mhòr to the high passes. Eithne nic Ailein knelt a long while over her place and cut through a knot rather than between two, so that one heir's line lay divided between her share and the next. That was Ciorstaidh of Tràigh an t-Salainn. When the two lords quarrelled over her, she answered, "I'll take both", and it was let stand. Where the nine knives crossed the cord's thickest stretches, eleven knots in all were cut through, and eleven households found themselves sworn to two lords at once. Most paid both oaths rather than contest them. At dawn the nine lords went out through the door one after another, each with a length of cord over his arm, and the valley emptied along nine roads.

Roinn na Rìoghachd as sworn that night stood so:

- **Na h-Earrannan Mòra**, for the nine eldest lines with living heirs: the north (Murchadh Mòr), the lowland plain (Eithne nic Ailein), the western shore (Fionnghal nic Ailein, at Ros dhomhain), Tràigh an t-Salainn (Artair mac Ailein, at Baile ghorm), the east (Cormac mac Ailein, at Caol shean), the north-west (Ùna nic Ailein, at Muileann àrsaidh), the hills of the Abhainn fhionn (Coll mac Ailein, at Ceann àrsaidh), the south-east (Gormlaith nic Ailein, at Tobar dhearg) and the Abhainn ìseal (Tadhg mac Ailein).
- **An Earrann Dhearg**, an Earrann Dhearg, at Dùn dhearg, which Ìomhar Dearg had taken with a sword after the ford. Four lines protested it. Eithne answered that no rule written after the fact could unmake a thing done before it, and it was let stand as his share.
- **Cuid nam Marbh**, cuid nam marbh, carved out of all nine for the lines of the king's dead children.
- **Talla na Lasrach's ground**: the hill of the slab, the gathering-house and the valley about it as the keepers walked it lay in no share, as sworn at An Ceangal. No spear might come onto it.
- **An Cnoc Ruadh**, whose holder had sworn to Ailean Mòr alone, declared itself that night sworn to nobody. Murchadh Mòr and two other lords claimed it at once, and none could make the claim stand.

## III. The Stones and the First Blood

Through the winter after the handful the stewards of the nine lines rode the boundaries with the reciters. Masons cut and set the clachan-crìche, Clachan-crìche, each bearing the sign of the share it faced. Some were moved in the night and set up again. At Dùn thais neither the north nor an Earrann Dhearg would yield the old fort, so the masons set one stone on each bank of the burn below it, one cut with Murchadh's sign and one with the sign of an Earrann Dhearg. In FE 3 Ìomhar Dearg told his grandson Ruairidh Glas that the grazing above Dùn thais was an Earrann Dhearg's, stone or no stone. The men of Ruairidh Glas and of Aonghas Bàn mac Mhurchaidh came to blows there, and Aonghas Bàn was killed. His brother Eachann Ruadh swore over his body that the blood would be answered, and a hundred and eighteen years later Ìomhar Dearg was killed at a ford by Eachann Ruadh. So began Aimhreit nan Oighrean.

## IV. Shares of Shares

Under each great share the households were parted again into countries, the countries into provinces, the provinces into counties, and the counties into holdings no larger than a steading. At each level the lesser blood of Cuid nam Marbh had to be fitted in among the living heirs' claims. Luchd nan Snaidhm of Dùn ìseal rode the boundaries with their own cords and counted forty-four countries, a number that held for a season, and a hundred and thirty-one provinces, which held the better part of a year. They gave up at the counties. Cairistìona, Sìne's eldest daughter, told Aodh Crom that the counties changed their boundaries faster than any cord could be tied to hold them.

A stream that shifted its bed in a wet winter could leave a holding claimed by two counties, and many households swore to both and paid both. At a bend of the Abhainn dhomhain three counties claimed the eel-catch of an old mill's pond, and the miller, who had built his wheel before Roinn na Rìoghachd was thought of, took to paying each lord a third of the autumn catch unasked. The quarrel went no further. By FE 4 many holdings owed oaths to two lords and some to three.

## V. The Double-Sworn and the Long Strife

The double oaths of Oidhche an Dòrlaich, and the many that came after, were the weak place of Roinn na Rìoghachd. A household sworn to two lords could be called out by both. For five and a half centuries Aimhreit nan Oighrean went on in raids, burnings and killings, argued at a hundred hearths and fought in fits. It was never one war. The quarrel of the western shore and Tràigh an t-Salainn began with a drinking-horn at a mason's wedding at Ros dhomhain in FE 214, when Griogair mac Lachlainn of Sliochd Fhionnghail struck Tormod mac Ruairidh of Sliochd Artair across the face. It ran for generations. Niall Òg set a long table on neutral ground at the crossing of Ros fhionn in FE 352 and brought the heads of both lines to it unarmed, and each recited a longer account of wrongs than he had come with.

It ended at Achadh mhòr. Fearghas Dà-Mhionn, lord of the granary fields there, was sworn both to the line of Ìomhar and to the lords of Cathair mhòr, by the double oath his grandmother Ciorstaidh had taken on Oidhche an Dòrlaich. Her stone stood in his hall. When each lord called him out against the other, both came to take his fields, and in FE 556 the fields and the hall were burned with him in it. That was Cogadh an Dà Mhionn. The last sheaf of Achadh mhòr was carried clear of the fire.

## VI. The Peace of the Threshold

In the hungry winter after the burning the lords of the shares were called to Dùn ìseal, and Eithne nic Ailein, then near two and a half thousand years old, was carried there. On the threshold of Talla na Lasrach, beside the stone cup, with Coimheadaiche na Lasrach as witness, they swore Sìth an Stairsnich. It had three clauses:

1. No share might be parted again at a death. A holding passes whole to one heir.
2. No man might swear to two lords. The double-sworn households each gave up one oath.
3. Clachan-crìche were set where they stood that day, and moved no more.

The market under Talla na Lasrach's walls was held for the first time at the next quarter-day. There was no war on the island for four hundred and forty-three years after Sìth an Stairsnich, until Cogadh nan Rathad in FE 999.

## VII. The Gathering of the Shares

Because a holding now passed whole, the shares no longer broke up at each death, and they could only grow together. Two cousins dying without heirs, and a marriage to a third, put three old counties of the east round Dùn dhearg into Goraidh mac Ìomhair's hand. With an Earrann Dhearg they made four. The lowland households who ate from the stone granaries Sliochd Ìomhair had built at Dùn dhearg in FE 434 called him rìgh from FE 674. In FE 694 he and the council at Dùn ìseal agreed Roinn a' Ghleidhidh: Coimheadaiche na Lasrach kept the flame and the galleries, the rìgh kept the roads, the granaries and the peace of the lowlands, the rìgh might not enter the galleries, and Coimheadaiche na Lasrach might not raise spears. The island was governed on that division for the rest of the age.

Under Goraidh's line the old shares became provinces of the rìgh, each with a seat and a reeve, and na h-Earrannan Mòra survived as names on the stones. When Eithne nic Ailein died in FE 1,122 no holder of a great share was left alive, and the lords of the deep river set their market at the fords of Baile Mòr dhomhain that same year. Some places stayed outside. The shepherds of Achadh chaol were refused a seat for want of a hearth-right cut in stone at Roinn na Rìoghachd. Cathair dhomhain won a charter of its own tolls from Beathag Mhòr. Caol mhòr, at the edge of the north-west, answered to its own headman.

Na Ceithir Taighean of custody, bound under Eilidh nic Ìomhair in FE 385, stood beside the provinces: Sliochd Mhuirich at Dùn ìseal, Sliochd Ìomhair at Dùn dhearg, Sliochd Thormoid at Seann Dunn and Sliochd Raghnaill at Seann Tarr. They chose Coimheadaiche na Lasrach, and when An Talla Dearg stood empty after Oighrig nic Lachlainn they chose the rìgh. Late in the age two towns won seats on the council beside them. Baile Mòr mhòr got one at the foot of the hall, and Cuan ruadh got one beside Sliochd Raghnaill's in return for its boats.

## VIII. The Provinces of the Red Hall

In the last five centuries of the age the provinces were counted by their seats. Cunntas nan Làmh in FE 1,735 counted every household by the waypoint house nearest it, since every household on the island lay within a day of one. From FE 2,406 Lachlann Bàn built a common granary in every province seat that would have one, and Cnoc mhòr's was first. Under Lagh a' Bhìdh Roinnte of FE 2,411 a household with grain above its winter's need gave the surplus to its district's granary before the storm season, and the granary fed any household that ran short. The common granaries of the mountain round Muileann chiar, where the households lived by cutting and carrying, were filled from the lowlands, and the lowlands grumbled.

The law was tested in FE 2,472, when late snow closed the high passes and the mountain households ate their seed. The granaries were opened at Muileann gheal, the reeves of Dùn dhearg sent grain without waiting to be asked, and no one died of hunger. The rolls count it the first famine on the island, and it was turned back. It was tested again at Achadh shean in FE 2,900, and the grain Baile chaol lent was repaid measure by measure to the last. By the close of the age the provinces sent their rolls up to Cathair dhearg each year with little in them a teller would trouble to keep, which the tellers took as a sign that the island was doing well.
