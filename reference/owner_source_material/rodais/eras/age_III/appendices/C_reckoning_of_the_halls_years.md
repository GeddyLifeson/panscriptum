# The Reckoning of the Hall's Years

The Holy Age did not number its years from a single day. When the island said how long ago a thing had happened, it said it by a reign, by a burning, by a moon or by a knot. For nine hundred years none of these could be written, and the keeping of time was a matter of cords, notches and recitation. This appendix tells how the Hall and the hall of the rìghrean counted, how they kept the count before there were letters and after, and why the two rolls did not agree at the close of the age.

## I. By Nights and by Moons

The Dia-thìrich counted by the moon, and by nights rather than days. The night went before its day, as the dark half of the year went before the bright. A feast began at dusk on its eve, and a debt of "ten nights" fell due at the tenth dusk. The year began at Samhain, with the dark half, and held twelve moons of thirty and twenty-nine nights in turn: Samhnas, Dubhlas, Faolach, Gearras, Sìolach, Bearnach, Bealltach, Ògach, Teasach, Lùghnas, Pailteach and Damhrach. Each moon began on the night of the full, waned through its dark half, turned at the dark of the moon and waxed through its bright half to the next full. Below the moon the island had the half-moon of fourteen or fifteen nights, and no week. Debts, sentences and turns in the galleries were set by the half-moon, and markets met at the full. The night of the full and the night of the dark were rest-nights, on which the Hall let no coal be cut and the fishing households set no nets.

Twelve moons fall some eleven nights short of the sun's year, and without help the feasts would have walked backwards through the seasons. So about every thirtieth moon a thirteenth of thirty nights was put in at the year's end, after Damhrach and before Samhnas, so that Samhain should not come before the summer had ended. It was called Eadrach, the between-moon. Through the whole of the Holy Age the Hall at Dùn ìseal declared it. The Keeper watched the harvest and the stags, and when Damhrach came too early for the season she sent word down every road that the year would have its between-moon. The Hall's reckoning of the years in which Eadrach was put in was kept first in knots and later in letters. It is the thread on which the island's older dates hang, and it is broken in places.

The four fire-feasts fell on the first night of their moons: Samhain at the opening of Samhnas, Là Fhèill Brìde of Gearras, Bealltainn of Bealltach and Lùnastal of Lùghnas. From the Peace in FE 556 the market under the Hall's walls met on those four quarter-days, and the Lùnastal fair at Muileann òg took its name from the last.

## II. By Burnings

The Hall's own count ran from the Small-Burning Law of FE 142. Each season a household might burn its measured handful, and the Hall numbered the seasons from the Law: the first burning, the second, and so on through the centuries. A burning ran from one filling of the baskets to the next, and the Hall filled them at Samhain, when the galleries closed and the hearths began to need the coal. The first sentence, on a household of Seann Tarr, took away its right "for the season after" and was entered by the burning in which it fell. Of all the island's counts the count of burnings came nearest to a count from a fixed beginning, since it ran from one act and was meant never to stop. Nobody outside the Hall used it. A carrier asked the year would give a reign, and a fisher would give a storm.

## III. By Reigns

The second count was by reigns. The Keepers were remembered in a recited roll beginning with Beathag Bhàn, and from FE 674, when Goraidh mac Ìomhair was first called rìgh, the lowlands had their own list. A year was named by whoever held it: "the ninth year of Iain Garbh", "the third winter of the Keeper Seumas Bàn". That was good enough for a debt within living memory. For the distance between two reigns it was no use, since the recited lists gave names and no lengths. The long years made it worse. A reign in the tenth century might run eighty years, one in the twenty-fifth barely thirty, and a teller who counted "six reigns ago" might mean four centuries or two.

The Hall's tellers also counted by the Hall's own centuries, the hundreds of years since the Binding, for things too far back for any reign to reach. It is from them that the island's talk of "the Hall's years" comes: "in the fourth century of the Hall" is how the reciters of Seann Dunn placed the raising of their house's shrine. The Hall's centuries were reckoned from the Keepers' roll, taking so many Keepers to a hundred years. That served until someone counted the Keepers and found that no two tellers gave the same number.

## IV. The Knots and the Notches

For nine hundred years no one on the island could write. The records of the Holy Age before FE 914 were kept in three ways.

**The cord.** Sìne nan Snaidhm, knot-keeper of Dùn ìseal, tied the cord of the heirs at the gathering-house door in the first autumn of the age, one knot to an heir, with a colour for each line: green for Eithne's, grey for Fionnghal's, brown for Artair's, yellow for Cormac's. She tied near two hundred knots in four days, and Eithne's own knot-keeper Fionn Beag counted after her and found no mistake. The finished cord held seven hundred and twelve knots. The knot-keepers of Sìne's line kept it, and could read from it who had stood where at the Roinn. In FE 1,320 Cairistìona nan Snaidhm, last of that line who could read a knot without letters, untied it knot by knot over two days, naming each heir aloud for a young scribe to set down. Then she coiled it in the old way in the chest the Hall had made for it and said that after her no hand living would be able to make it speak.

**The notched sticks.** Tallies were cut on sticks of ash or hazel: a notch for a basket carried, a toll paid, a season's handful drawn. The waypoint keepers notched the road toll from Goraidh Mòr's day, a notch for a toll paid in fish and another for one paid in wool. A household of the fourth hearth at Cnoc leathan, sworn to neither side in the long strife, kept a stick notched for whichever steward called first each season, and by FE 400 it carried better than a hundred and fifty notches a face. The harbour-master of Ceann leathan kept tallies on notched sticks, and the oldest counted things on the island are some of those. When the households of Muileann àrsaidh and Ceann àrsaidh quarrelled over a meadow, Gilleasbuig Mòr found their two sticks agreeing exactly until a season forty years back.

**Recitation.** Everything else was said aloud and remembered: the Keepers' roll, the lists of the lowland rulers, the terms of the Peace of the Threshold, the sentences of the Law. A reciter was trained from childhood to give a roll the same way every time. The rolls still drifted. By the thirteenth century the hall's reciters gave a mother's name two ways and a reign as nine years in one mouth and eleven in another.

## V. The Stag-Letters

The first letters on Dia-thìr came from the cutters' tally-notches. At the stag's school in the far east, about FE 902, a teacher named Rònat, who had come to the school as a boy with a talent for losing count past thirty, cut his own name onto a length of ash in small strokes, one to a sound, and read it back three days later. He had watched his old teacher Aonghas Cam grope after the names on the teaching-thread in his last fever. Teachers of the western school came east to learn the sound-marks. Rònat carried them five days west to the grey stone near Cnoc bheag and set them stroke for stroke beside the old marks cut there. They matched nothing, and the scholars cut the first thing written of the old marks: that the stone's makers had not taught them. In FE 912 Rònat cut Niall Òg's name and put the stick in the old heir's hand, and Niall read it back. By FE 914 the teachers of the stag had made the tally-notches into signs for sounds, so that a teaching might be kept without a teacher. These were the stag-letters.

Sìleas Dhonn took them into the rìgh's hall at once. Rònat spent a winter at Dùn dhearg teaching her clerks. In FE 916 the keeper of the Ceann mhin toll was called to account in the new marks and checked against the road-guard's own cutting at his gate, and was found short. Rònat's record of it was the first whole sentence cut in the letters.

## VI. The Written Rolls

**The king-list.** Tormod mac Iain, a younger son of the house of Tormod, was sent east to Muileann chiar in FE 1,255 at fifteen to learn the letters, the first of the royal kin to read. He was called Tormod Scrìobhaiche for the rest of his life. In FE 1,299 Ealasaid Chiar, out of patience with hearing her own house's roll given a different order at every telling, set him at fifty-nine to write down every ruler the reciters could give him. His roll ran from Goraidh Mòr to Ealasaid Chiar. He marked which names came from the reciters and which from living memory, and at the queen's bidding he wrote the doubt down beside the sure thing where he had it. It was the first written king-list, and every later roll was kept on its pattern.

**The Keepers' roll.** Shamed, in the Hall's own words, by the rìgh's clerk, the Keepers had their spoken roll written in stag-letters at Dùn ìseal in FE 1,320. It began with Beathag Bhàn and ran several centuries longer than Tormod's. In FE 1,380 the Hall set two of its own clerks to learn the letters, so that it need not borrow another order's hand to read its own roll.

**Year by year.** From Seonag Chrom's reign (FE 1,781), when she gave the clerks a room of their own in the red hall and a wage in silver, the king-list was kept year by year. Peigi nic Tormoid, clerk of the hall from FE 1,926, was the first whose own hand can be told from the others. By the twenty-third century the roll ran to more chests than the room could hold, and the clerks worked from summaries of summaries. Coinneach Bàn had the rolls copied for every province seat, and several of those copies outlasted the originals.

## VII. The Count at the Close

By the close of the age the Keepers' roll held one hundred and forty names and the king-list more than ninety. Mòr nic Coinnich had the count made at her own asking. The two rolls did not agree in every place, and the Hall's count of burnings agreed with neither. Where the king-list is empty, as between Ealasaid Chiar and Fearchar Òg, the Keepers' roll carries the years. Where the Keepers' roll is thin, as in the silent years when a Keeper is named with only the length of her keeping, the rìgh's clerks carry them. Where both fail, the Hall's reckoning of the between-moons still counts the years.

The last entry of the Holy Age roll is Mòr's own name. When the skin was full Ruairidh Clèireach began a new one, headed with the name of Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill. The count went on, but not on the same skin.
