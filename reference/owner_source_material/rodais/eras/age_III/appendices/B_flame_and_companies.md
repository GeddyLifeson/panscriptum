# The Flame and the Companies

The Holy Age began with one flame and one measure and ended with eight companies on eight benches, each certain that it alone kept the flame truly. This appendix tells what the Hall kept and how it kept it, then the rites that grew up beside the Hall and apart from it, then the parting of FE 2,858, and last the two things no company claimed.

## I. The Binding

On the slope above the gathering-house at Dùn ìseal, three weeks after the fall of Ailean Mòr, the nine keepers of the slab brought down the fire of their hut from Tobar dhìreach in a covered pot. The heirs of the king, who could not part the vein among them, swore it to no one of the blood. The flame was bound there: the undying flame of Brìde, kept over the stone of Crom Cruaich, for the island and not for any heir. The eldest keeper, Aodh Crom, had asked for it on the reasoning that a thing no heir could have was a thing no heir would fight for. The tellers of Tobar dhìreach say the fire was theirs before it was the Hall's. The reciters of the Mason's line say it came down from the mason's own fire. The Hall has never troubled to deny either. Gille-Brìde carried a coal of the first flame home from the Binding, and his line kept it.

Beathag Bhàn, of no heir's blood, was chosen in FE 4 to hold the flame. From FE 10 nine households about the mountain's foot, one for each district of the old keepers, took its tending in turn, and these were the nine hearths. In FE 15 a long hall of dry stone and turf was raised over it, Talla na Lasrach, with one door toward the vein and none toward the sea. It was rebuilt many times on the same footings. The flame burned on its threshold, beside the stone cup, through the whole of the age.

## II. The Measure

Everything the Hall did came back to how much of the coal a household might burn.

**The basket.** In FE 48 Muireach Liath fixed the first common measure for coal carried down from the galleries: a lidded basket of plaited heather, one to a household, filled at Samhain.

**The stone cup.** In FE 139 Fionnlagh Dall had the old keepers' stone cup cut anew in grey stone to hold just what a cupped pair of hands will hold, the fill of two palms. Every household's dues were measured by it after, and every oath of the Four Houses, the masons and the apprentices was sworn on it.

**The Small-Burning Law.** After the smoke at Cnoc dhubh in FE 132 killed five people, the custom of the handful was made law in FE 142: no household might burn more than a measured handful of the coal in a season. It became the holy law of the Keepers and was never changed in the Holy Age. The Law governed what a hearth or a forge sent up into the air and nothing else. It did not govern wood, peat, or driftwood, and it did not govern what anyone swallowed, as the Keeper ruled of fire-water in FE 2,673.

**The sentences.** A household that burned more than its measure lost its right to the coal for the season after. The first sentenced was a household of Seann Tarr in FE 145, and its name stood on the roll with the offence beside it for centuries. A third offence took the right away altogether. Murchadh Ruadh's household of Baile chrom went down to the strand in FE 398 rather than beg, and some stripped households took to boats in the north-western waters and lived by raiding the fishing strands. The Keeper also sentenced the diggers of Baile ghorm in FE 2,386 as though they had broken the Law, though no law covered what they had done.

**What the coal did not feed.** No coal might feed a beacon, since a beacon burns for strangers (FE 148). The Tobar dhearg light and every sea-light after it was kept with driftwood, and in the last century with seal-oil. The only payment in coal the Law ever allowed was a handful each season to the grave-keepers of Tuam Cnoc dhìreach.

**The cutters and the trial.** The Keeper's cutters went out each season to try the measure of every lamp and shrine. They tried the fullest lamp at Seann Dunn, the mill-shrine at Muileann naomh, and the hawk-keepers' dawn-burning. They found the measure kept almost everywhere. In FE 1,735 the census of hands counted every household that claimed the right: eleven thousand four hundred proved it and some two thousand were refused.

**The galleries.** The Keeper kept the galleries, where cutters took the coal under the mountain. After the fall in the high gallery in FE 614, Seonaid Chiar had oak props set everywhere. Calum Liath sent cutters with knotted cords to count the vein (FE 1,602) and cut the sum on a slab, which was heard in silence. After two youths died in the lower gallery, apprentices were bound for seven years to a master cutter, and from FE 1,634 they swore on the stone cup.

## III. The Old Spirits

Older than the Hall's rite, and never set under it, was the reverence of the hearths, Na Seann Spioradan: reverence for the ancestors, for the vein, and for the weather that rules the mining seasons. It took its shape at the hearths in the first generation of the age (FE 21), and it kept no priests because it needed none. The Hall let it be, and several times said plainly that a place belonged to it and not to the Hall:

- **Làrach an Teampaill.** Beathag Bhàn found its altar already turned toward the mountain and declared the place not the Hall's to keep (FE 34). The Keepers do not enter it.
- **The hills of Doire uaine.** In obedience to the old inscriptions on the standing stones, the Keepers forbade any fire to be lit in sight of those hills (FE 33).
- **Allt an Àigh.** The stream was held to bring luck to any who drank of it in a certain quarter of the moon. Muireach Liath ruled in FE 59 that it belonged to the ancestors and the weather and not to the vein. That was the first plain parting of the Keepers' rite from the folk reverence, and it was done without a quarrel.
- **The ancestor-cairns.** The greatest was the cairn at Cathair naomh, which grew by a stone at every burial from FE 417. At the end of the age the people of Cnoc naomh still kept a shrine to the ancestors and the sìth of their hill with no hawk, wolf, sting or stag in it.
- **The drowned.** At Inis thais the keepers burned their handful on the shore so that the drowned, gone west over the sea, might see it. At Baile chrom the strand set out a bowl each year for the five lost in the calm.

## IV. The Rites Beside the Hall

**The stag's people.** Sìleas nic Coinnich, a cutter who had watched the coal show its colours for some hands and not others, began to teach in the sacred wood of Muileann chiar in the far east about FE 110. Her hearers named themselves for the stag said to have stood in the grove through a whole lesson. Theirs was the first of the schools of the druids, gathered round one question: why does the vein answer some hands and not others? Gormshuil shut the galleries of Dùn ìseal to them, holding that a question asked of the vein was a demand made of it. A house of the stag founded at Cnoc bhàn in FE 565 took cutters again, and Seonaid Chiar let them back into the galleries sworn to ask nothing of the coal while they cut it. From the stag's school came the stag-letters, and from Cnoc bhàn the strike-books, an apprentice's account of every stroke. In FE 2,290 Donnchadh mac Iain read the strike-books of many generations and found the coal broke brighter every year for hands of every birth. He left Cnoc bhàn with twenty-two hearers for Muileann chaol, whose books keep no parentage. The two schools of the stag, the elders' and his, traded letters for the rest of the age. Raonaid Dhubh heard both and gave no ruling, saying that the vein had not asked the Hall to decide.

**The wood-watchers.** From FE 1,569 a small company kept watch at the edge of Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, the wood never cleared, for a black horned beast they said walked in it. They cut a black-barked tree in the stag's grove, the stag's teachers answered by teaching in the dark wood, and the two woods were claimed by both sides. It was the first strife of holy claims the island remembered. In FE 1,595 the woods were divided before Fearchar Òg and Calum Liath, with boundary stones cut in stag-letters. The wood-watchers read the fall of a dropped stick and the turn of a bird's flight, and held Macha for their goddess, of sovereignty and of horses both. Anna Ruadh took one of them into the red hall in FE 2,822, and the Keeper protested. The watchers held that the black beast had two natures, one that keeps and one that devours, and that the vein had the same two. They told of two horses, the black Dubh Sainglenn and the grey Liath Macha, pulling one chariot. From FE 2,852 they judged their own quarrels under the trees.

**The wolf-sworn.** After the War of the Roads, in FE 1,009, the men of Caol leathan's siege became its road-guards and swore their watch on a white stone carved as a wolf at the town gate. The road-guards of the island took up the custom. In FE 2,181 they dragged a second white stone to the shrine of Inis thais, and its keepers came to pray before it as well as to the drowned. In FE 2,843 the wolf-sworn raised a hall about the stone at Caol leathan and swore oaths there that were no longer a guard's oaths of the road. They said the stone watched the whole island and would cry out under a rightful king. Men called it Lia Fàil, and its keepers An Fhianna.

**The dawn-hawk.** At Seann Skell on the northern coast the fowler families kept a hawk in every household and fed it before they ate, and from about FE 1,999 they burned their season's handful at dawn with the hawk on the wrist, holding that the flame is a hunger and should be fed as one. The rite spread up the Abhainn ìseal to Inis ìseal and Cnoc bheag. The Keeper wrote that it kept the measure exactly and was no concern of the Hall's, and never again answered any rite so simply. In FE 2,840 Ealasaid Bhàn turned the Hall's cutter away at her door and sent him home with the refusal on a strip of hide, saying that the measure was the hawk's and not the Keeper's.

**The sting-oath.** At Baile àrsaidh on the western heath, where the yellow scorpions are thick, a woman stung during a trade finished the bargain with her hand swelling, and the town came to swear on a scorpion's sting kept in a box (FE 862 – 879). The oath went north with the merchants to Ros bheag, whose harbour court took it for every trade from FE 2,145. At the western Seann Skell in FE 2,846 the court's keepers made it a creed: one power alone, Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea, stings the liar wherever he stands, and the scorpion is his sign. Oaths there were sworn over a cup marked with the scorpion, Manannan's Cup of Truth, said to break in three at three lies and mend at three truths. It was the first teaching on the island to claim that it alone was true. Seann Vell carved its scorpion in yellow stone in FE 2,849, the first image of it cut for worship.

## V. The Parting of FE 2,858

In the winter of FE 2,838 Seumas Bàn had the clerks set down every rite on the island that burned a handful at dawn or laid any claim to the vein. The list ran longer than he expected. Within twenty years the reports of hawk, wolf, sting and wood had shown that the one practice kept since the Binding had come apart. From FE 2,855 men called the whole scattering of rites An Creideamh Sean, the Old Faith, and set the older reverence of the hearths beside it under its own name. In FE 2,858 the council at Dùn ìseal, which had sat by hearth and house since the Roinn, began to sit in companies by rite on benches of its own. These were the eight companies:

| Company | Sign | Seat |
|---|---|---|
| The Hall's rite | the flame and the stone cup | Dùn ìseal |
| The keeping of the ancestors | the cairn | every hearth |
| The dawn-hawk | the hawk fed first | the northern Seann Skell |
| The wood-watchers | the black beast and the two horses | Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag |
| The wolf-sworn | the white stone | Caol leathan, Inis thais |
| The sting-oath | the scorpion | the western Seann Skell, Ros bheag, Seann Vell |
| The elders of the stag | the stag | Cnoc bhàn |
| Donnchadh's school of the stag | the stag, and no parentage in the books | Muileann chaol |

The carpenters set the hawk-keepers' bench nearest the door for no reason any clerk wrote down. Seumas Bàn said at the door on the first morning that he kept the one flame now for eight peoples, and each of the eight was certain it was the only one that kept it truly. None of the eight sat together again by hearth and house in the Holy Age.

## VI. What Was Held in Common

Two things stayed outside the companies. **Allt an Àigh** kept its pilgrims of every rite, walking by night from the landing at Caol shean to reach the water in the right quarter of the moon. The Hall's list found no place to set the stream and left it out of the reckoning. In FE 2,860 pilgrims of four rites met on the path by chance and mended its stones together. **The springs at Muileann ruadh**, where the sick had been carried up in litters and bathed at dawn since FE 66, had a bath-house and a keeper appointed by the Hall in Dian Cècht's name. In FE 2,863 the keeper Beathag Fhionn ruled that the sick of every rite be bathed without being asked which company they kept. Every company accepted it, since it came from the one office in the Hall's keeping that had never had anything to do with the coal.

The flame on the threshold at Dùn ìseal burned on through all of it, keeping the one measure no company had yet found cause to dispute.
