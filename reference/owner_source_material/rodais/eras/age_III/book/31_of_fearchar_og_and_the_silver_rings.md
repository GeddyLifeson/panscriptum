# Of Fearchar Òg and the Silver Rings

*FE 1,642 – 1,685*

> *Cha b'e an aois a rinn an rìgh; ach dh'fhàg i sinn ga chuimhneachadh.*
> It was not his age that made the king; but it left us remembering him by it.
>
> — a saying of the road-guards of Dùn dhearg, said of no other rìgh before or since

## I. Of the King Who Went On

In the year the first strike-book entry was set down at Cnoc bhàn, three words scratched by a novice's stiff new hand, Fearchar Òg had sat at Dùn dhearg for nine-and-forty years, and was still called Òg by everyone who had known him as the boy of seven Coimheadaiche na Lasrach had entered on her roll. He had stopped correcting them some twenty years before. A name given at seven, he told his own steward once, is a debt a man pays the rest of his life whether he likes the price or not, and Fearchar Òg, six-and-fifty years old that year, greying at the temples but not yet stooped, had long since made his peace with it.

He was not a builder of walls, as Cathair mhòr's rìghrean before him had been, nor a maker of laws remembered by their sentences, as Fionnlagh Dall was remembered for the stone cup and Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig two centuries before him. What he built, in the main, he built out of the plain fact that he did not die. Ealasaid Chiar's own line had run thin in the years before him, a ruler here and a long gap there that the reciters could not close, and every household on the roads between Dùn dhearg and Dùn ìseal had grown used to hearing, every few years, that the hall was empty again and the succession in question. Fearchar Òg ended that simply by staying. He crowned no wars, quelled no strife Sìth an Stairsnich had not already quelled a hundred years before him, and is remembered by the roll, in the end, for very little beyond the plain number of his years and a handful of small things his clerks did in his name that outlasted every one of them.

The first of these small things began twelve years after, in the sixty-first year of his reign. His steward, an old man named Griogal who had served Fearchar's mother's house before him, came to the hall one evening with a complaint he had carried three days from the southern road: that the road-guards of the southern ford, who kept the crossings and the waypoint houses against thieves and floods both, had gone two seasons without their due from the hall, because the granary-clerks who paid them in grain and cloth could not always find carriers spare to send south when the grain was wanted nearer home.

"Pay them in coal, then," said Fearchar Òg, who had heard the same complaint in different words every winter of his reign and had grown tired of answering it the same way each time.

"The hall has no coal to spare either, lord. The mountain gives what the mountain gives, and the guards do not sit near enough Talla na Lasrach's galleries to be paid from them even if it had."

Fearchar Òg sat a long while with that, turning a ring on his own hand, a plain band of silver his mother had worn before him and her mother before her, the kind every household of any standing kept a few of against a daughter's marrying or a debt too proud to pay in grain. It is told that he looked at it longer than a man looks at a thing he has worn every day of his life without once considering it, and that when he spoke again it was to ask Griogal how much silver the hall's own smiths could spare from a season's ordinary work without troubling any household for more.

Griogal did not understand him at first, and said so plainly, being an old man too tired of the road to pretend to a cleverness he did not feel. Fearchar Òg explained it slowly, as a man explains a thing he is still working out himself as he speaks it: that a guard paid in grain must carry his own pay home on his own back, and a guard paid in cloth must find a buyer for what he does not need. A small stamped ring of silver, known at a glance to every smith on the island from the Muileann chiar trade, could be carried in a fist, spent at any market that knew silver at all, and would travel, once spent, far past wherever the guard himself ever walked.

"It is not grain," said Griogal, who did not think much of the idea and said so.

"No," said Fearchar Òg. "It will keep longer."

## II. Of the First Rings and Aonghas Ruadh

The first rings were struck that winter, small and plain, no thicker than a woman's finger and stamped on one face with the hearth-sign of Dùn dhearg, and there were not many of them: forty, by the steward's own count, enough for one season's pay to the road-guards of the southern crossings and no more. Among the men who took one that winter was Aonghas Ruadh of Cnoc mhòr, a guard of the ford below Ceann leathan these eleven years, a broad, quiet man who had buried one wife of the coughing sickness and married a second, Sìne, the year before the rings were struck, and who had, that same year, a daughter he and Sìne named Mòrag, for no one in either house could afterward agree whose grandmother it was for.

Aonghas Ruadh did not think much of the ring when it was first put into his hand at the ford, a small cold weight he turned over twice before he understood it was meant to be his whole winter's due and not some token toward it. He had carried grain home from the hall's granary every winter of his service and had grown used to the weight of a full sack on his shoulder as the shape his labour took. A ring the size of a barleycorn's shadow felt, at first handling, like being paid in nothing at all.

He carried it home anyway, because a guard does not argue with the hand that pays him, and it was Sìne who saw the good of it before he did. She walked it to the market at Ceann leathan the next week, where a cloth-seller from the eastern road, who had never in his life had reason to travel as far west as Dùn dhearg and would not have known a grain-measure of that hall's stamp from any other, weighed the ring on his own scale, found it good silver of the Muileann chiar weight he already dealt in, and gave her cloth for it without a question asked beyond the weighing. Sìne came home with more cloth for the ring than Aonghas could have carried home in grain in the same day's walk, and told him so at the hearth that night while Mòrag slept in the basket by the fire, not yet a year old and already, though neither of them could have said it then, growing up in a household that had begun, without quite meaning to, to think in silver.

Word of the southern crossings' new pay went east before the spring was out, carried the way all such things were carried, by carriers who had nothing better to talk of on a long road and travellers who liked a curious thing to tell at the next hearth. By the time Fearchar Òg's clerks struck a second batch of rings, the following winter, waypoint-keepers from three roads he had never troubled to pay in silver before sent word asking whether the hall meant to go on with it, and whether their own guards might be included. Fearchar Òg, who had meant the rings as no more than a fix for one season's short grain, found within five years that every road-guard on the roads out of Dùn dhearg was paid, in whole or in part, in the small stamped silver, and that the rings themselves had begun to turn up in markets the guards had never walked to, carried on by hands the hall's clerks would never trouble to count.

"They travel further than the men do," Griogal said to him once, some years into it, in the tone of a man admitting he had been wrong without quite saying so.

"That was the whole of the thought," said Fearchar Òg, and did not say more, because there was not much more to say of a thing that had, by then, said itself.

## III. Of the Toll-House at Àth ghlas

The southern road crossed the river at Àth ghlas on its way toward Ceann leathan, a shallow ford between two low banks where a laden beast sank to the hock in a wet spring and a child could wade it dry-shod in a dry summer, and it was there, within five years of the first rings, that the hall built its first toll-house.

There had been a toll at Àth ghlas long before the house was built, kept as most such tolls were kept, by whichever household of the ford's own bank had the standing and the patience to sit by the crossing and collect it, a portion going to the household and a portion carried up to Dùn dhearg twice a year with the rest of the road's due. It had worked well enough for longer than anyone at the ford could say, and the household that kept it, a family called the Griogaraich after some ancestor no one living remembered, had grown used to thinking of the toll as theirs by a kind of standing that felt, to the family, near enough a right to be worth defending.

Fearchar Òg's clerks judged, reasonably enough by the hall's own reckoning, that a toll paid in the new silver wanted a keeper who could be trusted to weigh a ring honestly and not merely to eye a purse and guess, and sent a man of their own from Dùn dhearg to keep the new toll-house: a clerk named Dòmhnall Beag, careful with a scale and careless, by his own later admission, of the ford's own feelings, who arrived at Àth ghlas in the spring with the hall's own commission in his hand and no kin at the ford at all.

The Griogaraich took it hard. It is told, and told still at Cnoc mhòr, where Aonghas Ruadh's own household heard it from the ford folk themselves, that the family's eldest stood in the toll-house door the morning Dòmhnall Beag arrived and asked him, plainly enough that the whole crossing heard it, what right a man with no kin at the ford had to weigh a ford-family's own toll. Dòmhnall Beag, who had been told to expect trouble and had, in his own quiet way, prepared an answer for it, said only that the commission in his hand was the rìgh's, and that he had not asked to come, and would go again the day the rìgh sent for him and not a day before, and that in the meantime he meant to weigh every ring honestly, the Griogaraich's tolls along with everyone else's, and would be glad of their help if they cared to give it and would manage without it if they did not.

They did not help him, not for the better part of a year. But a toll-house wants firewood in winter and a hand at the gate when the spring floods run high, and Dòmhnall Beag, being alone at it and no fool, hired what help he needed from whoever at the ford would take his silver, most of the Griogaraich in time, one at a time and never all admitting it to one another. By the year the rings had been struck for a decade, the toll-house at Àth ghlas stood as the first on any road of Dia-thìr whose keeper had been sent from a hall and not chosen by the ford, and the Griogaraich had made their peace with the fact of it, because the toll ran honester under Dòmhnall Beag's scale than under any of their own.

Aonghas Ruadh crossed that ford twice a season for the rest of his working years, and it was there, waiting his turn at Dòmhnall Beag's scale with his daughter riding his shoulders one summer when she was too small yet to walk the whole way, that Mòrag Dhubh first saw the games.

## IV. Of Mòrag Dhubh

She was called Dhubh from the day she could stand, for hair black enough that her mother's own people, fair to a woman for three generations, took to saying she must have got it from the ford's own water. She grew up at Cnoc mhòr in a household that kept sheep on the low ground and sent its men, when the roads wanted guarding, down to the crossings her father had walked all his life, and she grew up, too, in the particular way a road-guard's daughter grows, hearing more of tolls and thieves and the weight of a season's silver at her own hearth than most children of Cnoc mhòr heard of anything.

Her father taught her the staff before he taught her much else. It was no wish of his to make a fighter of her; a road-guard's household simply kept staves the way a fisher's kept nets, hung by the door and taken down as often for mending a gap in a fence as for anything else, and a girl underfoot at a hearth full of staves will sooner or later be given one to stop her taking someone else's. Aonghas Ruadh, whose own father had taught him the ford-guards' old bout-play with a stick no thicker than a thumb, more a way of keeping a man's balance and his temper both than any true fighting art, taught it to Mòrag the same way, in the yard behind the house, first for the game of it and then, as she grew and would not be turned from it, because she asked him to and would not be put off.

She was quick rather than strong, small for her years until she was not, and it is told of her, by those who trained beside her, that she never once in all her years at it struck the first blow of a bout, preferring to let an opponent spend himself against her guard before she answered, a patience her father said came from her mother's side and Sìne always swore came from his. By the time she was fifteen she had outgrown every staff her father owned and cut one of her own from a young ash at the wood's edge, straight-grained and a hand's breadth taller than she was, and carried it everywhere a girl of that country could carry a staff without remark, which in a road-guard's household was nearly everywhere.

It was at Àth ghlas, riding her father's shoulders as a small child and, later, walking beside him as a girl grown tall enough to carry her own bundle, that she first heard the talk that would shape the rest of her young life: that the guards of the southern crossings, paid now in silver they had somewhere to spend and nowhere in particular to spend it on any given day, had begun meeting once a year at Ceann leathan, where the ford-market already drew traders from three roads, to fight bout after bout with blunted staves for the newest and best-struck ring the hall's smiths had sent south that season.

## V. Of the Meeting at Ceann leathan

The meeting had begun small, as such things do, a handful of guards from the nearest crossings settling an old argument over whose bout-play was the surer with a match fought in the market square on the day of the autumn trading, the loser buying the winner a cup and the whole thing forgotten by the next year's trading, except that it was not forgotten. The next year there were more of them, guards from crossings two days' walk distant who had heard the tale and wanted a bout of their own, and the year after that the market began setting aside a corner of the square for it, because a crowd that gathers to watch two men trade blows with staves is a crowd that buys cloth and cups and cooked meat while it waits its turn to watch, and Ceann leathan's traders were not slow to see the good of that.

By the time Mòrag Dhubh was old enough to be let compete, some ten years after the meeting first began, it had grown into a full day's contest, guards from every southern crossing entering by turns through the morning and the afternoon both, the losers of each bout dropping away and the winners meeting again until, by evening, two stood who had beaten every man put in front of them, and fought the last bout by torchlight for the season's finest ring, struck that year and never worn by anyone until it was won.

Her father did not want her to enter, the first year she was old enough by the meeting's own loose custom, which asked only that a fighter be grown and be a guard or a guard's kin trained to it, and said so plainly at the hearth the week before the autumn trading. It was not, he told her, that he doubted her staff-work, which he had watched grow past his own teaching some years before and had stopped correcting, out of a kind of pride he did not often show her, when she was seventeen. No woman had ever entered the meeting in all its years, and he did not want his daughter's first bout to be one fought, as he put it, against the whole square's opinion before ever a staff was raised.

"Then I'll fight the square's opinion first," said Mòrag Dhubh, "and my bouts after, and see which tires me more."

She lost that first year, in the second round, to a guard of the eastern ford twice her age and near twice her size, who beat her guard down by main strength rather than by any skill she could not have answered in a longer bout, and who told her afterward, not unkindly, that she had the surest staff of anyone he had fought that day and the worst luck of drawing him for it. The second year took her further and still ended in a loss, to a younger man whose reach she had not yet learned to read. The third year and the fourth she did not lose, and by the fifth, the year she turned twenty-five, she had beaten every man put in front of her through a long grey morning and a longer afternoon, standing at dusk across the torchlit ground from the last guard left to beat, a broad-shouldered man of Muileann chrom named Ruairidh Fada who had won the meeting twice before and meant, by every sign he gave before the bout began, to win it a third time without much trouble from a woman half his size.

## VI. Of the Last Bout

It is told at Cnoc mhòr, in the telling the household kept for three generations after her and no doubt shaped a little in the keeping, that Mòrag Dhubh said nothing at all before that bout, where every other fighter of the day had traded some word or some grin with his opponent across the torchlight, and that Ruairidh Fada, finding no word offered him to answer, said his own anyway, loud enough for the square to hear, that he would go easy on her for her father's sake, who he knew by the crossing where they had both served a season together some years before.

She did not answer that either. She let him come first, as she let every man come first, and took the weight of his opening strike on a guard raised so quietly that half the square, watching for a woman's nerve to fail her, missed the moment it did not. He was strong and he was quick for a man his size, and for the better part of the bout he pressed her back a pace at a time across the torchlit ground, striking hard and often, spending, as her father had taught her every man eventually spends, more strength than the bout wanted from him because he had not yet learned, three strikes in, that she meant to let him.

She turned him on the ninth strike, catching his staff high and driving it wide with a twist of her own that owed more, by her own telling, to a morning spent freeing a gate-post from a stuck hinge than to any bout-play her father had ever named for her, and in the gap that twist opened she struck once, low and short, across the back of his knee, and he went down in the dust of the square with the crowd's noise breaking over both of them at once, a noise, it is told, louder than the square had made for any bout in the meeting's history, because no one there, Ruairidh Fada least of all, had thought to see a woman standing at the end of it.

The ring given her that night was the finest struck that season, heavier than most, cut with the hearth-sign of Dùn dhearg on one face and, new that year, a small mark of Ceann leathan's own on the other, the market having asked the hall's smiths, now that the meeting had grown into something the whole square profited by, for a ring that carried the ford's name along with the crown's. Mòrag Dhubh is the first winner of that meeting whose name the reciters of the southern crossings kept, and it is not lost on any who tell it that the first name kept was a woman's, in a contest no woman had fought before her.

Her father wept at it, which he had not done at either of her losses nor, so far as the household ever knew, at very much else in his life, and told her afterward, walking home in the dark with the new ring cold in her own fist for the first time, that he had been wrong to try to keep her from the square, and that he would not try again.

## VII. Of Muileann òg and the Lùnastal Fair

The ring Mòrag Dhubh won that autumn stayed in her own keeping the rest of her life and passed, after her, into her household's, worn on high days and shown to travellers who asked after the mark on it and told the tale, in shorter and shorter tellings as the generations went, of the woman of Cnoc mhòr who had beaten every man in the meeting the year the ring was cut. But the silver itself, the rings struck each winter in their dozens and scores now, went on doing what Fearchar Òg had first hoped a small hard thing might do, travelling further than any guard who ever carried one, into markets and hands the hall's own clerks would never trouble to trace.

Nowhere did that travelling show plainer than at Muileann òg, a market town on the ford where the old Rìghrean na Cloiche and the holders of An Cnoc Ruadh had once agreed salt for wool, burned in Cogadh nan Rathad six centuries before and left, since, little more than a scattering of rebuilt hearths around the old ford-stones. In the same years the meeting at Ceann leathan was growing from a handful of guards to a whole day's contest, traders drifting west along the southern road with silver rings in their purses had begun stopping at Muileann òg's ford rather than pressing on, finding there, in the ruin of a town too poor to charge them anything for the privilege, goods and prices neither Ceann leathan's crowded market nor Dùn dhearg's own could match.

It was traders' own custom, at first, and not the hall's doing, that fixed the meeting at Muileann òg to the feast of Lùnastal, when the first of the harvest came in and the roads were driest and best for travelling; but within a few years of the custom taking hold, the households resettling the town, seeing what a season's fair might bring them, asked Fearchar Òg's clerks for a charter of their own, and were given it gladly, the hall by then having long since learned that a market well fed brought more silver home in tolls than a market starved of custom ever would. The fair of Muileann òg, held that first chartered year and every year after at the turn from summer into harvest, grew within Mòrag Dhubh's own lifetime into the largest gathering of goods and craft on the southern half of the island, its ford lined three days running with stalls that sold, by the end of her years, near everything the roads of Dia-thìr could carry to it.

She went there herself, once, some years after her own win. Muileann òg kept no meeting of staves; she went there only to trade a season's wool for cloth her own household could not weave as fine, and it is told, though the household's own telling grows uncertain on this point with every generation that passes it on, that a trader of the eastern road, seeing the mark on the ring she still wore, asked her outright whether the tale of the woman of Ceann leathan was true, and that she answered only that it was, and let him tell the rest of it to whoever asked him after, because a tale told by a stranger travels further, she said, than one told by the person it happened to, and she had already seen how far a small hard thing could travel once it left a person's own hand.

## VIII. Of Cidhe Beag and the River-Men

Not every road the silver opened ran so smoothly. Some twenty miles north-east of Muileann òg, where the Abhainn uaine ran broad and slow enough for a laden boat to sit steady in it, an old landing had stood since Ailean's own age, a place called Cidhe Beag, the little quay, where a fisherman or a ferryman had put a boat into the river time out of mind without ever troubling to make more of it than a scramble of flat stones at the water's edge.

The carriers of the eastern road, who had walked grain and salt fish overland from the lowland granaries to the market towns for longer than any of them could count generations, had never had reason to think much of Cidhe Beag at all, until the year Fearchar Òg's clerks, weighing the cost of paying carriers' wages against the cost of building a proper landing there, judged that a river running near empty of traffic beside a road worn to ruts under sack after sack of the same grain was a waste neither the hall's granaries nor its silver could afford to go on making. They built the landing that same year, timbered piers driven into the old scramble of stones and a shed raised above it for the goods waiting passage, and put out word to every boatbuilder on the lower river that the hall would pay well for flat-bottomed craft of hide over withy frame, built to sit shallow enough for the Abhainn uaine's own shifting bars.

The carriers of the eastern road sent word to Dùn dhearg within the month, three of their eldest walking the whole distance to say to Fearchar Òg's own face what they had already said to every clerk who would listen, that the granaries' grain had gone on their backs for generations and ought to go on going there, and that a river-boat, once it took up the trade, would leave half their households without the wages that had fed them since before the hall was built.

Fearchar Òg heard them out fully, in the same unhurried way he had heard every complaint of his reign, and answered them, in the end, much as he had answered Griogal over the rings some years before: that a sack of grain carried on a man's back for two days' walk cost the hall, in wages and in spoiled grain both, more than the same sack floated down in an afternoon cost it in the wear of a boat and the pay of two boatmen, and that he could not, weighing the granaries' own good against the carriers' own comfort, choose the dearer road when a cheaper one lay open beside it.

"Then what becomes of us?" asked the eldest of the three, a woman named Ciorstaidh who had carried grain on that road herself for sixty years before her knees gave out and left her to the walking rather than the carrying.

"The road does not close," said Fearchar Òg. "Only the river opens beside it. There is grain enough on this island, and will be more each year the granaries fill, for a road and a river both to carry, and neither to starve of work while the other eats." It was not, by the carriers' own reckoning, an answer that mended what they had walked so far to mend, and they went home saying so plainly to anyone who would hear it; but it proved, in the years that followed, truer than they had believed it could be, for the river carried the heaviest and least urgent of the grain, the loads that could wait a boat's slow pace, while the roads kept the carrying trade in everything that could not wait, cloth and tools and the small urgent parcels no boatman would trouble himself over, and both trades, in time, grew rather than starved.

## IX. Of Muileann chrom and the Paddle-Oath

The boats themselves, once the landing was built and the first of them launched, drew their builders and their crews from Muileann chrom, a small town up the valley from Cidhe Beag whose men had fished the river's slower reaches for longer than anyone at the landing could say, and whose skill with hide over withy, learned first for coracles no bigger than a single man could carry, proved, once it was set to the larger work, exactly what the hall's clerks had hoped to find.

The first crews to take up the trade were young men of Muileann chrom with more nerve than standing, sons of fishing households too many to divide a single boat's catch among, and it was among themselves, not at any hall's asking, that they settled the custom that came, in time, to mark every boatman of that river after them: that no man might call himself one of their fellowship, nor be trusted at another boatman's back on a laden crossing, until he had sworn on his own paddle, held flat across both palms before the others of his crew, to answer any fellow boatman's need on the water before his own household's, a river being no place, as the eldest of them put it, for a man who would let another drown to save his own cargo.

The swearing was a small thing, no priest's rite and no hall's law, a custom made among working men who wanted a way to know, before they trusted a stranger's hand on a crossing, that the stranger had sworn the same oath they had. But it held, year on year, more surely than many a law the hall itself had made with far greater ceremony, and by the time Mòrag Dhubh was grown old enough to have grandchildren of her own at Cnoc mhòr, the boatmen's fellowship of Muileann chrom was building flat river-boats not only for the Abhainn uaine but for every river of the island wide enough to float one, each boat leaving the yards with a paddle laid across its bow until the crew that took it had sworn their own oath upon it.

Her own younger brother, Dòmhnall, who had never taken to the staff the way she had and had spent his boyhood down at Cidhe Beag watching the first boats loaded rather than at Àth ghlas watching the toll weighed, was among the second crews to take up the river trade, and swore his paddle-oath the same year Mòrag Dhubh's own daughter was born, so that the household at Cnoc mhòr came, within one generation, to hold both a silver ring won on land and a paddle sworn on water, and told both tales at the same hearth without ever quite agreeing which of the two mattered more.

## X. Of the King Who Died Old

Fearchar Òg lived to see all of it: the rings spreading past every road his clerks had first meant them for, the meeting at Ceann leathan grown from a handful of guards to a whole day's contest, Muileann òg's fair drawing traders from three roads at once, the river opened beside the roads and neither one starved by it. He did not live to see much beyond it. His reign, remembered by the roll for very little else, is remembered above all for its length, and in his last years, when he had sat at Dùn dhearg longer than any ruler the reciters could name before him, it became a kind of custom among the households of the southern roads to reckon their own years by his, a child born in the year of the toll-house at Àth ghlas, a marriage made in the year the fair was chartered, a death in the year the river-boats first sailed, so that Fearchar Òg's long sitting became, without his ever asking for it, the measure other lives were told against.

He died in the winter of his ninety-fourth year, at Dùn dhearg, in the same hall his clerks had struck the first rings in more than fifty years before, and it is told that Griogal's own grandson, steward by then in his grandfather's place, found him in his chair by the hearth one morning, gone as quietly as a man goes who has simply, after so very long, stopped going on. He was buried among Sliochd Ìomhair with the state a reign of eighty-seven years was owed, and the roll that recorded him, which had made much of other rulers for their wars or their laws or their buildings, could find little to say of him beyond the plain fact the whole island had already learned to measure by: that he had come to the hall a boy of seven and left it, eighty-seven years later, an old man who had simply gone on, season after season, longer than anyone then living had thought to expect of him.

Word of it reached Cnoc mhòr the way all such word travelled, carried down the southern road by guards who had themselves been paid, most of their working lives, in the dead king's silver, and it found Mòrag Dhubh in the winter after her own win, sitting by her own hearth with the ring still bright from the smith, kept where her household could see it on high days. She is said to have turned it once in her fingers when she heard the news, the same slow turning her father had given it the winter he first carried it home, and to have said only that the king had done one thing right in a long reign of small quiet things, which was to make a thing worth keeping that did not need his own hand on it to go on travelling after he was gone.

She lived on herself many years more at Cnoc mhòr, and the ring lived longer than she did, passed down through a household that never afterward forgot, whatever else it forgot with the passing of the generations that first won it, whose hand had held it first and why.
