# Of the Red Hall and the Census of Hands

*FE 1,685 – 1,735*

## I. Of the Twenty-Year-Old Queen

Beathag Mhòr took the hall at Dùn dhearg five years after Fearchar Òg died at ninety-four, and she took it at twenty, which the reeve Lachlann Dubh, who had kept the granary accounts under three rulers of her line and meant to keep them under a fourth, thought a poor age for anyone to begin anything. He said so to no one but his wife, and even to her only once, for Beathag Mhòr's great-grandfather had died old and easy and his own father before him the same, and a house that buried its rulers gently was not a house a careful man complained of.

She was brought up through the granary yard on the morning of her crowning, past the sacks that had fed the Red Share since Ìomhar Dearg burned the old halls within the double bank, and Lachlann Dubh walked at her stirrup with the tallies under his arm because it was his office to walk there, whoever rode above him. He had expected a girl's questions, the kind Fearchar Òg's own heir had once asked him, about the weight of a full sack and how many the granary held. Beathag Mhòr asked him instead how many of the sacks were owed elsewhere before the winter was out, and when he told her, she asked him to whom, by name, and wrote nothing down, because she had not yet learned her letters past her own name and the count to a hundred, but she remembered every name he gave her, and asked after three of them again a year later, by which time he had stopped thinking her age a fault in her.

It was the list-keepers at Dùn ìseal, and not Beathag Mhòr herself, who first wrote her down as ban-rìgh, queen, rather than rìgh with a woman's name set after it as the rolls had always done for Eithne and Catrìona and the rest. Nobody asked her leave for the change, and she gave none, for it seemed to her, when Lachlann Dubh told her of it, a small matter beside the sacks, though she noticed, in the years after, that the word travelled ahead of her wherever she rode, spoken first by strangers who had never called any ruler anything but rìgh in their lives and found the new word came easier to them than she would have guessed.

Her crowning was held at Dùn dhearg in the last cold week of am Faoilleach, {{date:II-0134}}, with snow lying thin on the double bank and the wind coming down off the granary roofs in a way that made the household's fires gutter every time a door was opened. Fearchar Òg's own line filled the hall three deep, cousins she had met perhaps twice in her life come to stand for a great-grandfather most of them remembered only as an old man too tired at the end to be much afraid of, and Lachlann Dubh, watching from his usual place by the grain-stores' door, thought the girl looked smaller under the household's old crown than any ruler he could remember crowning, and said as much to his wife, who told him that every crown looked too large on whoever first wore it and grew no smaller for the wearing.

There was a moment, before the crown was set, when the eldest of the cousins, a woman near six hundred years old who had outlived two rulers of her own generation and buried a granddaughter besides, stood up unasked and said to the hall at large that a twenty-year-old girl who had never sat a council nor ridden a boundary was no fit head for a line that had held its ground unbroken since the ford. Beathag Mhòr had no leave yet to speak from the chair, and held her tongue; she looked at the woman long enough that the hall grew quiet waiting to see what would come of the look, and what came of it, in the end, was nothing at all: the crown was set, the woman sat down, and no one in that hall ever heard Beathag Mhòr mention the challenge again, though Lachlann Dubh's own accounts show that the woman's household received, every winter after, its full measure of grain and never a sack more.

## II. Of the Fleece at Baile Mòr fhionn

In her third year she rode west to the fair at Baile Mòr fhionn, where the lords of the plain held their market each autumn and had lately taken to pricing everything in fleeces, a grown ewe's fleece the measure against which grain and wine and the plain's own horses were weighed. Beathag Mhòr had never seen a market run on wool before and stood a long while at the edge of it watching a horse-trader and a grain-factor argue a bargain down to the quarter-fleece, each of them naming a different ewe in his head and neither troubling to say so.

The silver rings of Muileann chiar were sold there too, though the plain's traders would not call a price in rings outright and reckoned every ring against its fleece-worth first, as if the silver were a foreign thing that needed a native measure to stand beside it before an honest man would touch it. Beathag Mhòr bought nothing at Baile Mòr fhionn that year but a length of the plain's own wool cloth, dyed the red the western dye-houses were known for, and had it made into a cloak she wore at her own crowning-feast the following spring, so that the two colours of her reign, the red of the Share she was born to and the wool-red of the plain she had gone to see, were the same colour before anyone thought to remark on it.

She rode home by the northern road rather than the way she had come, three days longer, because Lachlann Dubh's own accounts had shown her that the northern waypoint houses saw less of the queen's custom than the western ones did, and she wished, she told her household, to know what a keeper's face looked like when a queen's own party filled his hearth for the first time in a generation. It looked, at the house called An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, like a man who had killed his last fowl for a stranger's supper and was already wondering how to feed his own children through the week that followed, and Beathag Mhòr, seeing it, sent a cart of grain back to him from Dùn dhearg before the month was out, though she never told him it was hers.

The keeper of that house was a thin, quick-spoken man called Fearchar Iar, no kin to the line of kings that shared his first name, and he served the queen's party the same steamed carp and green brandy his house had served the ox-teams every winter since the road was first cut, apologising for it twice over supper as though a queen's own stomach might want better than a carrier's. Beathag Mhòr told him, the second time he apologised, that she had eaten worse at her own hearth as a girl and would eat worse again before she died. It was a small enough lie, and her own household knew it for one, yet Fearchar Iar took it for kindness rather than flattery and repeated it to every traveller who lodged under his roof for years afterward, so that the saying outlived the visit that made it by a good deal longer than the visit deserved.

She asked him, before she rode on, how many households the house served in a hard winter and how many turned away for want of room, and he answered her plainly, having nothing to hide and no reason yet to think a queen's questions worth measuring his answers against. It was that plain answering, more than anything seen at Baile Mòr fhionn, that stayed with her longest of everything from that journey, and she said once, years later, to Dùghall mac Raghnaill, that she had learned more of her own island from one night at a road-house's hearth than from every steward's letter that had ever reached her at Dùn dhearg.

## III. Of the Bridge-Foot

The hall at Dùn dhearg had stood on its hill since before the shares were cut, and Beathag Mhòr's own line had held it whole and unquestioned longer than any other line on the island had held anything. It was Lachlann Dubh, oddly, who first said aloud what she had already begun to think: that a hall five days' ride from the western harbours and eleven from Tuam Cnoc dhìreach in the far south-east could rule a boundary quarrel at either end only at second hand, through a steward's letter that was a season old by the time it was answered, and that a queen who wished to see her own island rather than hear it reported to her would do better to sit where the roads and the river and the western harbours all came together.

He meant it, he said afterward, as an old man's grumble and nothing more, having no wish himself to leave the granary he had counted for forty years, but Beathag Mhòr rode down to the bridge-foot town the winter after he said it and stood on the rise above the harbour a long while in a wind that had her household's cloaks snapping about them, and said only that the ground was good building ground, and asked no one's leave before she began.

Dùn dhearg's people took the news as they took most things touching their own pride, at first with a flat refusal to believe it, and then, when the masons' carts began to pass through on the western road, with a silence Lachlann Dubh recognised from the winter of the counting slab two generations before, the silence a household keeps when it has decided a thing cannot be argued and will not therefore be forgiven either. He himself said nothing against the move in the queen's hearing, but he kept, from that year on, a second set of accounts beside the Hall's own, a private tally of every sack that left Dùn dhearg's granaries for the new hall at the bridge-foot, and showed it to no one, not even his son, who found it among his papers after he died and could not say what his father had meant to do with a count kept for no eye but his own.

The new hall was built of the red stone the town was already named for, on foundations laid deep enough that no age after would need to dig them again, and the masons cut the hearth-sign into its lintel as if the whole building were no more than a threshold, which in a sense, Beathag Mhòr said once to the master mason, it was. The building took four years, through two wet winters that flooded the harbour road and a third summer so dry the masons carried water up from the river in relays of carts to keep their mortar workable, and Beathag Mhòr rode down from Dùn dhearg to stand on the scaffolding herself more often than her own household thought fitting for a ruler, saying, when Lachlann Dubh's own steward asked her why, that a hall built without its ruler watching was a hall built for someone else's reign, not hers.

The master mason, a broad-shouldered woman of Cathair dhomhain named Sìleas nan Clach, had never before been asked to raise a building meant to outlast every hall on the island, and she told the queen plainly, at the laying of the last course, that she could promise the stone would hold and nothing else, for a hall's worth was made by whoever sat in it after the masons had gone home, and that was no mason's business to answer for. Beathag Mhòr took the answer for the honest one it was and paid Sìleas nan Clach in full before the hearth-sign was even cut, against the custom of paying a mason only on the building's finishing, which Sìleas nan Clach remembered, she told her own apprentices in her old age, as the first time in her working life anyone had trusted her hand before they had seen the whole of what it made.

From that year the hall at the bridge-foot was where a ruler of Dia-thìr kept court, whatever else about the reign might change, though Dùn dhearg kept its granaries and its graves and went on calling itself, in every reeve's own letters home, the rìgh's hill, long after the rìgh had stopped living on it.

## IV. Of the Harbour's Grudge

Cathair dhomhain lay near enough to the new hall to smell the masons' dust, and its people, who had built their own harbour into the largest on that coast while Dùn dhearg's rulers were still five days' ride away and easily flattered by a gift of fish, did not care for a queen's toll-gatherers appearing at their own quays as though the harbour had been waiting all along for someone to come and own it.

The harbour-master, a broad woman named Iseabail nic Fhearchair who had run the quays longer than Beathag Mhòr had been alive, brought the matter to the new hall herself rather than send a steward, and stood in front of the queen's own chair with her arms folded and said that Cathair dhomhain had weighed its own fish, mended its own nets and buried its own drowned for longer than the Red Share had held a single stone of Dùn dhearg, and had no memory of asking the Red Share's leave to do any of it. Beathag Mhòr heard her out to the end, which Iseabail nic Fhearchair said afterward she had not expected, having come ready to be interrupted, and answered that she asked no toll of a harbour older than her own hall's foundations, and that Cathair dhomhain might keep its own tolls under its own charter if it kept its own peace with the new town beside it.

The charter was written that same season, in the clerks' own hand and not the knot-keepers', for Beathag Mhòr had by then set two of the Hall's young stag-scholars to write down what had once only been recited, and Iseabail nic Fhearchair carried it home rolled inside her own cloak rather than trust it to any steward's saddlebag. It settled nothing between the two towns beyond the letter of the tolls themselves. Cathair dhomhain's harbour-masters went on for the rest of the age finding some new law of their own to keep more strictly than the new town beside them kept it, a rivalry that cost neither town anything worse than pride, and pride, as Iseabail nic Fhearchair told her own successor before she died, is a cheap enough thing for two towns a few miles apart to spend on each other instead of spears.

The rivalry came out first, and most plainly, in the matter of the winter storm the third year after the charter, when a grain-ship out of the western islands broke its mast off Cathair dhomhain's own harbour mouth and drifted, helpless, toward the shoals below the new hall's rise. Cathair dhomhain's boats reached it first, being the nearer and the more practised at a lee shore in weather, and towed it in to their own quays rather than the new town's, and though the grain itself went, in the end, to whichever granary the queen's own clerks directed it, Cathair dhomhain's harbour-master let it be known, in every telling of the rescue after, that the ship had been saved by an older skill than any charter could grant a younger town, and the new town's own boatmen, stung, spent the better part of that winter refitting two hulls of their own against the day a ship might drift the other way instead.

None ever did, in Beathag Mhòr's reign or after it, but the two towns kept their boats the sharper for the waiting, and a visitor to either quay had only to ask after the weather to hear some version of the same story, told from whichever side he happened to be standing on.

## V. Of the Quarrels over the Handful

At Dùn ìseal, in those same years, the council that judged the small disputes the Small-Burning Law had always settled found itself judging fewer boundary quarrels and more of a kind it had barely troubled with before: households whose right to the handful was disputed outright, a new hearth claiming descent from one of the nine without proof, two households sworn to the same waypoint-keeper each saying the other had no standing at all.

The Keeper who held the flame in those years, an old man who had come to it late from a hearth in the far north and left it again after a short season none of the reciters of Dùn ìseal cared to dwell on, had let the disputes pile against each other without settling more than the loudest, and it fell to his successor, a man named Dùghall mac Raghnaill, chosen by the nine hearths in his twenty-ninth year, to find the council's floor at his own first sitting so crowded with claimants that he could not walk from the door to the cup without a hand catching at his sleeve.

Dùghall mac Raghnaill was of Sliochd Raghnaill, one of the Four Houses sworn at Seann Dunn, though he had left his own house's hall as a boy to learn the vein's reckoning and had not gone back to it since, and he judged, in that first crowded season, with a plainness his own house was not much known for, ruling three claims for and eleven against inside a single afternoon, until an old woman of the eastern road stood up before him and asked, not unkindly, how a Keeper meant to judge eleven claims a season for the rest of his life without ever once knowing, for certain, which of the eleven he had judged rightly.

He had no answer ready for her, and he told her so, which the reciters of Dùn ìseal count the beginning of what came after, for a lesser Keeper, they say, would have found some answer rather than admit he lacked one, and Dùghall mac Raghnaill rode to Cathair dhearg within the month to ask the queen for a count.

The old woman who had asked him the question, a widow of the eastern road named Beitris nic Ailein, lived only long enough to see the first counters ride out from Dùn ìseal in the autumn of that same year, and she told her own grandchildren, more than once before she died, that she had put the question to the Keeper meaning only to shame him a little for the crowded council floor, and had never once expected an island's worth of households to be walked and written down because of it. Dùghall mac Raghnaill himself, when the reciters asked him in his own old age whether he remembered her question word for word, said only that he remembered her face better than her words, and that a Keeper who forgot the faces that had unsettled him was no Keeper worth the flame he kept.

## VI. Of the Count Agreed

Beathag Mhòr received him in the new hall's smaller chamber, the one that still smelled of fresh-cut stone, and heard him through without once interrupting, a habit Lachlann Dubh, watching from the doorway, recognised from the granary yard eleven years before and thought, not for the first time, that a queen who listened whole before she answered was rarer than her own age's reeves ever gave her credit for.

What Dùghall mac Raghnaill asked was simple to say and hard to do: that every household on the island claiming the old right to the handful be counted once, in its own place, its right proved or refused before a clerk who wrote it down, so that no council afterward need judge the same claim twice from memory alone. Beathag Mhòr agreed to it before he had finished asking, and asked only where the counting should be done, since neither the Hall's own clerks nor the queen's own stewards could reach every household on the island inside a single lifetime without some fixed ground to work from.

It was Dùghall mac Raghnaill who thought of the waypoint houses, for every household on Dia-thìr lay within a day's walk of one, and the queen agreed to feed the counters at her own cost out of each house's own stores, which the waypoint-keepers, more used to feeding travellers who never paid enough to cover a night's fire, remembered afterward as the best year their own hearths had ever had. At the house called An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, the same house Beathag Mhòr had ridden three days out of her way to see filled once, a counter named Sorcha nic Ruairidh sat six months with a household's own grandmother going back four generations by name and by story until the old woman's claim, thin as it had looked on the council's own doubtful roll, stood proved beyond any argument the council could have raised against it, and Sorcha nic Ruairidh wrote the four names down in a hand the old woman could not read and would not have trusted if she could, and told her, when she asked what was to become of the paper, that it would outlast them both, which was the plain truth and no more comfort to the old woman than the plain truth generally is.

## VII. Of the Refused Roll

The count ran the length of the island and took the better part of ten years, and when the last waypoint house had sent its tally in to the clerks at Cathair dhearg, the rolls gave eleven thousand four hundred households holding the right by clear descent and near two thousand whose claims the counters had been unable to prove by any account, old or new, that the council would accept.

The refused were written on a roll of their own, kept separate from the rest, and it was that separate roll, more than the larger one, that the island remembered afterward, for a family with a clear right had nothing to think about it and a family without one had a paper to search for the rest of its life. Most of the refused lay along the north-eastern coast about Seann Tarr, fisher-families who had never burned coal at all in living memory and now found, when the counters came to their doors expecting to write down a right already held, that no such right had ever been theirs to begin with. A fisherman there named Ruairidh Cam told the counter plainly, when she asked whether his house meant to appeal the ruling, that his house had burned peat before the Small-Burning Law was thought of and would go on burning it after, and that peat, in his own experience of both, was the warmer fire besides, a saying the counters carried back to Cathair dhearg and that spread, in the years after, further along that coast than the census roll itself ever did.

Sliochd Raghnaill, Dùghall mac Raghnaill's own house, took up the fisher-families' cause at the council the following spring, arguing that a right so long unclaimed ought to be granted rather than refused, since a household that had never burned coal could hardly be said to have forfeited a habit it had never had. The council heard the argument through and ruled against it, on the plain ground that the Small-Burning Law had never promised a right to a household that had not held it, only protected the right of a household that had, and Dùghall mac Raghnaill, sitting the ruling out from the Keeper's own place beside the cup, did not speak for his own house's argument nor against it, a silence the reciters of Dùn ìseal note without troubling to explain, since a Keeper's silence in his own house's cause was, by then, the kind of thing the Hall had learned to take for something, though never for the same thing twice.

Three of the old sea-lights, fallen to ash and unkept along stretches of coast the census had newly walked from end to end, were rebuilt in those same years, at Baile ìseal on the eastern cape, at Muileann bheag on the northern point, and at Àth dhearg on the north-eastern headland that marked the passage in toward Seann Tarr, each kept afterward with driftwood and seal fat as the Law required, by households who had counted their own coasts, in the walking of the census, for the first time in longer than any of them could say.

Ruairidh Cam himself never troubled the council again after the ruling, and lived to see his own grandson keep the driftwood fire at Baile ìseal's rebuilt light two winters running, a duty owed to no coal-right at all but to the plain fact that his family had always lived nearest the point and knew its rocks in the dark better than any counter's roll could teach a stranger. He was asked once, by a traveller who had heard the saying about peat repeated as far south as Cathair dhearg, whether he still held to it, and he answered that he did, and that a fire a man had tended with his own hands since boyhood was always the warmer one, whatever its ash was made of, which the traveller wrote down as a fisherman's stubbornness and the fisher-families of that coast kept, afterward, as something nearer the truth.

## VIII. Of the Long Road Home

Beathag Mhòr's son Ruairidh was born at the new hall in the census's second year, grey-headed almost from his cradle, which his own household took for an ill sign and his mother, when she was told of it, took for nothing at all, having by then buried enough of her own line's short-lived cousins to know that a child's colouring foretold no more than a child's colouring ever does.

She lived thirty years past the count's finishing, long enough to see the refused roll consulted so often by families proving an old claim through a cousin's cousin that the clerks kept it in a chest of its own, worn soft at the corners, and long enough to see Dùghall mac Raghnaill grow grey in his own turn at the cup, still Keeper, still hearing the same handful of claims each season that the census had not quite managed to end.

In her last years she sat less at the harbour rise and more at the small chamber's own window, which looked east, back the way the bridge-foot road ran toward Dùn dhearg, and her household came to know that a queen sitting at that window in the last hour of daylight was a queen who wished to be left with her own thoughts rather than her own council. Her son Ruairidh, grown into a tall, grey-headed man who had long since stopped minding the colour that had once been called an ill sign, sat with her there more often than his own stewards thought fitting for an heir with a hall's business waiting on him, and it was to him alone, in the last winter, that she gave the command about her carrying, speaking it plainly and only once, as though a thing worth doing at all was not worth the saying twice.

She died at Cathair dhearg in her seventieth year, in the same small chamber where she had first heard Dùghall mac Raghnaill ask her for a count, and by her own command, given years before to her son and never once changed, she was carried back to Dùn dhearg to lie among the house of Ìomhar, the granaries she had grown up counting and the hill her own great-grandfather had died old and easy upon.

No royal journey before it had been so long. The road ran east the whole width of the island, five days at a walking bier's pace, and it was carried by relays of bearers drawn from every waypoint house along the way, twelve to a stage, so that the same road that had once counted the island's households now carried its queen home along the ground her own counters had walked. Ruairidh rode at the bier's head the whole distance and would let no one take his place, though his own stewards offered more than once to spell him on the harder stretches, and at each waypoint house where the bearers changed he stood by while the new twelve took up the poles and said nothing, having, his own household remembered, very little left to say by the second day.

Dùghall mac Raghnaill rode with it the last stretch from Dùn ìseal, an old man himself by then, remembering, as the bier passed the waypoint house at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr where a counter had once sat six months over one grandmother's claim, that the queen he was burying had asked him, the very first time he stood before her, only where the counting should be done, and had never once, in all the years after, asked him whether it had been worth the doing. He thought, riding behind her bier into the granary yard at Dùn dhearg where Lachlann Dubh's grandson stood waiting with the household's own tallies under his arm, that he might have told her, had she asked: that the roll of the refused, thin and worn as it already was, would go on being searched long after that, when every hand that had written it was dust, and that this, of everything a reign could leave behind it, was no small thing to have made.

They laid her among the house of Ìomhar on the slope above the double bank, within sight of the granaries Lachlann Dubh had walked her through as a girl of twenty who had asked a steward the name of every household a sack was owed to, and no sack's weight besides. The roll of the refused was carried up from Cathair dhearg in its own worn chest the following season, kept for the living rather than laid with the dead, and read aloud once at Dùn dhearg before the household of Ìomhar, so that the hill that had given up its own hall might hear, at least once, what the giving had bought.
