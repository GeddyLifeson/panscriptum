# Of the Knots

*FE 1*

## I. Of the Flax and the Door

*At Dùn ìseal the knot-keepers tell this, and they tell it with a cord across their knees.*

Sìne nan Snaidhm sat down at the door of the long gathering-house below Dùn ìseal on the second day of an Dàmhair, in the morning, with a basket of flax beside her and a stool under her and a new cord in her lap.

She had spent the days since the old keeper's knock making the cord. It had taken the flax of three households, bought with the keepers' coal, and she and her two daughters had twisted it on the long walk behind her house, the elder girl turning the hook and the younger walking backward down the walk with the strands, until they had a rope as thick as a thumb and longer than the gathering-house, coiled in the basket like a sleeping snake. It was pale and new and smelled of the retting pond, and she had never had one so long in her hands.

She had worked out the knots at night, by the fire, on a spare length. The cord of the households had one knot to a hearth, and small knots beside it for the mouths the hearth fed. That would not do for the heirs. The heirs had no hearths that mattered; they had lines. So she had taken threads of dyed wool, one colour for each child of the king who had living heirs in the valley, as near as the keepers could tell her how many that was, and she meant to tie each heir's knot with a turn of his line's thread caught in it, so that a knot could be traced back by its colour to the child of the king it came down from. It was not a new thing. The households did something like it for marriages. But nobody had ever done it with so many colours, and she had had to send to the dyers of the lowlands for some of them; and by the first morning she had twenty-three colours of wool in a bag at her belt, and knew she would want more.

The keepers had chosen the door because every heir had to come through it. Aodh Crom had said to the heads of the lines that no one would be heard in the gathering-house on the matter of the shares who had not first been knotted at the door. The heads of the lines had agreed, each thinking that it would keep out somebody else. So when the doors were opened that morning there was a crowd outside them that went back down the path and round the side of the house and out onto the grass, all in their good cloaks, all watching the woman on the stool.

"Say your line," said Sìne nan Snaidhm to the first of them, "and I'll tie your knot."

## II. Of Murchadh Mòr

The eldest came first. That had been agreed without argument, because none of the others could think how to argue it, and because nobody wanted to stand in front of him.

Murchadh Mòr stood over her in the doorway so that his shadow fell across the cord, and he gave his line in two words.

"Ailean's son."

Then he looked down the valley to see who would argue. Nobody did.

Sìne took the thread of his colour, which was a dark blue, and caught it into the cord, and tied the knot. She tied it slowly and hard, because she could feel him watching her fingers, and she did not look up. He stood over her the whole time she tied it, as if she might tie it loose. When it was done she ran her thumb over it and said, "That is you," and he bent down, a very long way, and felt the knot himself between his great fingers, and then straightened up and went in without another word.

After him came his sons, eleven of them, all old men, and each gave his line in three words, "Murchadh's son, Ailean's grandson," or near enough; and after the sons their sons, and theirs. The blue went into the cord at every knot, and the cord began to come out of the basket and lie across Sìne's lap and over her knees and down onto the step, and the elder daughter, who sat behind her, began to coil it into a second basket as it came.

It took the whole of the first day to knot the line of Murchadh.

## III. Of Eithne's Knot-Keeper

Eithne nic Ailein came on the second morning, and she did not come alone.

She had brought a knot-keeper of her own from the lowlands, a thin young man with long clever fingers and a face like a heron, named Fionn Beag. Why she had brought him she did not say, and had no need to. She set him on the step beside Sìne, close enough that their elbows touched, and told him to count the count after her.

Sìne said nothing. She gave Eithne her knot, with green for her line, and Eithne gave her line crisply, "Ailean's daughter," and stood back and watched Fionn Beag bend over the cord and run the knot through his fingers and nod.

After that Fionn Beag counted every knot. He counted Eithne's children and grandchildren, and he counted the lines that came after them, the grey of the line of Fionnghal of the western shore, the brown of Artair's line of the salt shore, the yellow of Cormac's in the east. He counted them all day. When Sìne tied a knot he reached across and felt it; when she caught a colour into it he checked it against the bag; when an heir gave a line he listened with his head on one side, and more than once he stopped an heir and asked him to say it again. Sìne let him. She did not hurry and she did not slow down.

On the evening of the fourth day he went to Eithne at her fire and told her that the knot-keeper of Dùn ìseal had made no mistake. He said it had been four days and near two hundred knots, and that he had never in his life seen fingers like hers, and that he did not think he could have kept up with her if she had not been letting him.

Eithne sent him home in the morning.

Before he went he came to the door of the gathering-house and stood a moment on the step, looking at the cord coiled in its baskets, and then at Sìne.

"How do you do it?" he said. "Four days, and your hands aren't tired."

"They are tired," said Sìne. "It isn't my hands that are doing it."

He did not understand her, and she did not explain, and he went back down to the lowlands, where he kept the cord of Eithne's households for the rest of his life and was reckoned the best knot-keeper in the south. But he told his own children that there was a woman at Dùn ìseal who could hold seven hundred people in her head at once and tie them in the right order, and that the heirs of the king did not know what they had sitting on their doorstep.

## IV. Of Niall Òg and the Knot Tied Once

Niall Òg came on the fifth day, laughing.

He was a grandson of the king, nine hundred years old, and at nine hundred he was a boy beside his uncles and his aunts. So they had called him the Young since before he could remember, and he had never got rid of it and had long since stopped trying. He had a round face and quick brown eyes and a mouth that was always about to smile, and he had been in the valley all summer at a small fire of his own on the edge of the upper camps, where he argued for nothing in particular and listened to everyone.

He gave his line through his mother, who was a daughter of the king, dead these two thousand years; and Sìne took the colour of that line, which was a pale red, and began to catch it in. Then he gave his line again through his father, who was a son of a son of the king, of another line altogether, the grey line of Fionnghal; and when Sìne looked up at the second he grinned at her and said, "I'm owed twice."

She tied the knot once.

She tied it with the pale red of his mother's line, since he had given that first, and left the grey out; and when it was done she ran her thumb over it and said, "That is you."

"And my father?" said Niall.

"Your father has a knot of his own," said Sìne. "Two, if you like. His own, and his line's. You are one man."

Niall Òg looked at her. For a moment she thought he would argue, and she saw the men of the lines standing behind him lean in to hear it. Then he laughed again, differently, and put his hand on the cord for a moment, over his knot, and said, "So I am," and went in.

He told it for the rest of his life. He told it at inn tables and by hearths and in the halls of three great shares, and he always told it against himself. His saying was that he had been nine hundred years in the world and had never been told so plainly what he was, and that it had been done by a woman on a stool who had nothing she wanted. And once, when he was very old and had had a good deal of heather ale, he said that he had gone into the gathering-house that morning and sat on his bench and cried for his grandfather for the first time since the ford, and that he did not know why it was the knot that had done it.

## V. Of the Cow That Was Not Taken

By the sixth day the heirs had learned that a knot on the cord was worth something, and had begun to think about what it was worth.

It was then that the stewards began coming to Sìne's house at night.

The first was a steward of one of the lines of the dead, a line that came down from a son of the king who had died in the second millennium of the reign and had left a great many grandchildren. He came to her door after dark with a boy leading a cow behind him, a good red cow in milk, and he said that his lord's line had some great-grandchildren who might be hard to place, and some cousins by marriage whose claim was not quite clear, and that it would be a kindness if the knot-keeper could see her way to tying them close in to the line, and tightly, so that they could not be picked out afterward; and that the cow was hers in any case, for her trouble, since he could see she had only a goat.

Sìne looked at the cow, and at the steward, and at the boy.

"Take it home," she said. "I have a goat."

The steward said that he did not think she understood him.

"I understand you," said Sìne. "Tell your lord's great-grandchildren to come to the door and say their lines. If they are his, I'll tie them to him. If they aren't, I won't. And if you come to my house again with a cow I'll tell Aodh Crom, and he'll tell the gathering-house, and then every line in the valley will know that yours has cousins it needs to buy knots for."

The steward went away with the cow. Two more came in the nights after that, one with a pin of carved bone and one with the promise of a stretch of summer grazing for her husband's sheep, which he did not have. She sent them both away. Word went round the fires that the knot-woman could not be bought, and after that the stewards left her alone, and began instead to watch each other's lines at the door, very closely, for anyone the knot-woman might be tying close in who had no business being tied at all.

Sìne said to her daughters that this was the best thing that had happened. She said that a cord that no one could buy was a cord everyone would watch; and that a cord everyone was watching was a cord that would be believed.

## VI. Of the Knots of the Dead

On the eighth day the lines of the dead began to come to the door, and Sìne had to decide what to do with them.

The king had been having children for four thousand years, and a great many of them had died before him: some in the long wars of the reign, some of accidents, some of the slow thinning that came even to the long-lived in the end, and a few, the eldest, simply of years, having been born before the long years were in the king's blood at all. They were more than the living. Their children and grandchildren were in the valley in hundreds, and every one of them came to the door and gave a line that began with a name nobody living had seen.

Sìne could not tie a knot for a dead man in the heirs' cord. The dead were not heirs. But she could not tie his grandchildren to nothing, either, and have them hang loose in the cord between the living lines with no colour to say whose they were. The lines of the dead watched her at the door that morning to see what she would do, and so did the lines of the living, more closely.

She tied a knot of the dead.

She took a length of black wool, and tied it round nothing, in the old way the households tied a knot for a child who had died at birth, so that there was a small hard ring in the cord with a hole through the middle where a knot should be. Then she tied the grandchildren's knots after it, each with a turn of the black in it and a turn of the dead child's own colour beside, so that anyone running the cord through his fingers could feel the hollow and then the knots that hung from it.

There was a murmur in the crowd at that. A grandson of a dead daughter of the king, an old man with a stick, came up and felt the hollow knot for a long time.

"That's my mother," he said.

"That's where she would be," said Sìne.

He nodded, and gave his line, and she tied him after the hollow knot. After him came the rest of that line, eleven of them, and each of them stopped before giving the line and put a finger on the hollow knot first, as one touches a door-post on coming home; and the custom stayed, so that for the rest of the gathering nobody of the lines of the dead was knotted without first touching the black ring of the parent they came down from. When he went in he said to the lines of the dead who were waiting that the knot-woman had put their fathers and mothers in the cord, and after that they gave her no trouble. But the lines of the living went in muttering that the dead were being given places in the cord; and Aodh Crom, when he heard of it, went and looked at the hollow knots a long while and said nothing, and Sìne thought afterward that he had seen before any of the heirs did that the dead would have to be given a place in the shares as well.

By the eleventh day there were more hollow knots in the cord than there were knots for living children of the king. The heirs walking the cord on the last night felt them under their fingers in the lamplight and counted them too.

## VII. Of Ìomhar's Knot and the Child in Arms

Ìomhar Dearg came on the seventh day. He came at the head of his line, which was the line of a living son of the king, and gave it plainly, "Ailean's son, and lord of Dùn dhearg." Sìne tied the knot with his colour, a rust red, and did not tie in the lordship, because there was no colour for it.

Behind him in the crowd four men of four different lines began to mutter, and one of them said out loud that the cord ought to have a knot for what Ìomhar had taken as well as for what he was owed. Ìomhar turned round and looked at him. The man stopped. But the muttering did not stop; it went back through the crowd and round the side of the house, and the next day four lines sent their stewards to Aodh Crom to say that when the shares came to be cut, they would not agree to Dùn dhearg being counted as one.

Most of the knots were not like that. Most of them were people Sìne had never seen before and would never see again: sons and daughters of the king's great-grandchildren, come in from the far coasts in the last days before the gathering closed, who had never been in the valley and stood blinking at the door in their travelling cloaks and gave their lines slowly, as if they were not sure of them. Many of them were not sure. Sìne helped them. She knew the lines by then better than most of the people in them.

There were old men and women, grandchildren of the king, who had been born in his second thousand years and looked it. There were young ones, great-great-grandchildren, who were the age of her own daughters and had the long years in them so faintly that they might live no longer than anyone. And on the ninth day there was a child in arms.

The child was the youngest of all of them: a great-great-grandchild of the king, some months old, of the salt-shore line. The mother came to the door with the child wrapped in a shawl and held it up in both hands for Sìne to see, and gave its line for it, one name after another, back to the king. The crowd behind her went quiet to hear it. It was the longest line anybody gave at the door, and the child slept through all of it.

Sìne tied the smallest knot she tied in those eleven days, with a turn of the brown in it. Then she put out her finger, and the child woke and took hold of it, and the mother laughed, and some of the crowd laughed with her; and for the only time in eleven days Sìne found that she had to stop, and wait, before she could tie the next.

## VIII. Of the Cord on the Floor

The cord took eleven days. On the evening of the eleventh the last heir came through the door, a great-grandson of the king who had walked from the far north-west and arrived with his shoes in his hands; and Sìne tied his knot, and put her hands flat on the cord in her lap, and sat a while without moving.

Then she told Aodh Crom the count. She had not counted as she went. She had not needed to. The cord went through her fingers from the beginning, knot by knot, from Murchadh Mòr's dark blue to the tired man of the north-west, while the old keeper stood over her, and the count came to seven hundred and twelve.

That night the keepers had the cord laid out on the floor of the gathering-house. It ran from the hearth at the upper end all the way down the length of the floor to the door, and turned there, and came back up the other side to the hearth again, and still there was some left over, coiled at the hearthstone. Lamps were set along it. The heirs were let in.

They walked along it in their good cloaks, slowly, bending down with the lamps. Each of them was looking for his own knot, and when he found it he stopped, and knelt, and felt it with his fingers, as Murchadh had done on the first day; and then he stood up and looked at the knots on either side of it, and counted them.

Sìne watched from the door. She watched them all evening. She saw an old grandson of the king find his knot and kneel by it for a long time, and then get up and walk three knots down to where his brother's knot was, and kneel by that, and look back at his own, and frown. Eithne nic Ailein walked the whole length of the cord twice without stopping at her own knot at all, and Sìne saw it. She saw two cousins of the western shore stand on either side of the cord at the turn by the door and argue across it about a knot that lay between them. She saw Ìomhar Dearg stand over the rust-red knots of his line with his arms folded, and the four lines who had muttered at him standing at the far wall, watching him.

She saw Niall Òg find his own knot and sit down on the floor beside it and look at it for a long time, and then look up and catch her eye across the house, and nod.

She said to her daughters afterward that the heirs had not come to see their own knots. They had come to see everybody else's. She said that she had tied seven hundred and twelve people to a rope, and that every one of them had walked the length of it that night to see who was tied too near.

On the next day, the thirteenth of an Dàmhair, the keepers of the slab declared to the whole valley that the heirs of Ailean Mòr were counted, and that there were seven hundred and twelve of them; and the heirs agreed, because every one of them had watched every knot being tied, and none of them could say otherwise. It was the first thing they had agreed on since the Binding, and the last for a long while.

That evening Aodh Crom came down to Sìne's house for the second time, and this time he came in and sat by the fire. He had brought her a basket of the keepers' coal, a season's handful and more, and he set it down by the hearth without a word. She kept it. It was the only thing she took for the cord, and she said afterward that she took it because it came after the work and asked nothing of her. The old man sat a while and watched her daughters coiling the spare flax. Then he asked her whether she thought the heirs would keep to what the cord said, when it came to the cutting.

"They'll keep to the knots," said Sìne. "It's what lies between the knots that they'll fight over."

Aodh Crom laughed, which she had not heard him do before, and said that she should have been a keeper of the slab, and she said that she had enough to keep.

That gathering is called *Cruinneachadh nan Oighrean*, the Gathering of the Heirs; and the cord Sìne tied at the door of it was kept in the gathering-house in a chest of its own, and was called *Sreang nan Oighrean*, the cord of the heirs, and was not cut until the night of the handful.
