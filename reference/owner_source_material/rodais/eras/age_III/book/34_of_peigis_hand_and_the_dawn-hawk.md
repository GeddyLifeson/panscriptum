# Of Peigi's Hand and the Dawn-Hawk

*FE 1,782 – 2,217*

> *Chan eil fuil aig an fhìrinn; tha i mar a chithear i.*
> Truth has no blood in it; it is as it is seen.
>
> — a saying of the clerks' room at Cathair dhearg

## I. Of Fish that Do Not Need Reading

At Inis bheag, where the Abhainn bheag comes down brown with peat-water into the sea and the fisher-houses stand along the one street with their backs to the wind, a teacher of the stag's people took a boat north in the first years after the Silver Feud was settled and stayed. He had been a cutter's son himself before he was a teacher, and had come to letters the way the stag's people came to most of what they taught, sideways, from a lesson meant for something else; but he found at Inis bheag a village of children who would never in their lives cut a handful of coal, whose fathers measured their year by the herring and not by the Hall's roll, and he taught them the marks anyway, on slate and on the smoothed bark of driftwood, because it seemed to him a poor thing that a mark should belong only to the vein.

The Hall's roll took note of it, as the Hall's roll took note of most things done anywhere on the island that year, and set down beside the notice, in the dry hand of whichever clerk kept the roll that season, a line to the effect that fish had no need of reading. Most who copied the line took it, in the years after, for nothing crueller than a clerk's small joke, the kind a tired hand makes at the foot of a long entry, and for two generations at Inis bheag it was told against the reading fisher-children themselves, at the boat-mending in winter, a jest the letters had as yet done nothing to earn: they had lightened no net and stilled no wave. The children the teacher taught grew old and taught their own in turn, and the marks he had begun on driftwood were still, by the time his grandchildren's grandchildren were grown, marks for counting a catch and naming a boat, no stranger in the eyes of the rest of the island than a knot on a cord, only harder on the eye to read at a distance.

The teacher himself did not live to see the marks travel further than his own street. He died an old man at Inis bheag, having taught three generations of the same dozen families and never once been asked by the Hall what use the teaching served, and his own grave-marker, cut by a pupil's hand, carries his name in the fisher-marks rather than in the Hall's own hand, the first stone on the island so cut, though no teller thought, at the time it was raised, to set the day down.

What the fisher-houses did not know, because no one troubled to tell them and they had no way of finding it out on their own, was that a boy of their own street, sent inland in his youth to serve at Cathair dhearg because his mother's cousin kept horses in the red hall's own stables, carried the marks with him into the red hall's own household, where no one before him had thought to teach them to anyone who was not already a clerk. He taught them badly and by accident, scratching a fisher's tally on a granary door for his own amusement while he waited on a groom who was slow to his work, and a clerk's daughter watching him at it asked what the marks meant and would not be put off with an answer that they were nothing, being a child who had already, by nine years old, formed the habit of believing that a thing written down meant something, or it would not have been written at all.

## II. Of Peigi's Hand

The clerk's daughter was Peigi nic Tormoid, of the line of Tormod Scrìobhaiche, who had first set a ruler's name on a leaf of oiled hide more than six hundred years before her, and by the time she came to the clerks' room herself, grown, the room Seonag Chrom had given her grandmother's generation had four benches in it instead of one, and a chest for the rolls that would not shut for the fullness of it. She learned the fisher-marks from the stable-boy before she learned the Hall's own hand from her mother, and kept both all her life, though she used the second far oftener. Clerks two generations after her own said that a document in Peigi nic Tormoid's hand could be told from any other in the chest without a name beside it at all: the letters were set close and upright, and never once trailed off at the foot of a page the way a tired hand trails, whoever holds the pen.

She was given the clerk's stool in her twenty-sixth year for a plainer reason than her own family cared to admit afterward: the elder cousin who had held it before her had died of a fever the winter past, and no one else in the room wanted the work. Peigi herself, told the plain truth of it years later by the reeve who had made the choice, thought it a better reason to be given anything than most reasons rulers give. She kept the roll of the red hall for four-and-forty years from that day, through a reign and the half of another, and left behind her, when she died in her own bed at seventy, entries enough that the clerks who came after her learned to date a leaf by nothing more than whether her hand lay on it. More than one of them called it a truer clock than the reign it was written under.

Her days ran, for most of those four-and-forty years, on a pattern so unbroken that later clerks who read her own account of an ordinary week found it stranger, in its way, than any single ruling she made. She rose before the household woke, kept her own small fire until the granary bell rang, and walked the length of the clerks' room twice before touching a single leaf, to see, she told a young clerk once who asked her the use of it, whether anything had been moved in the night that oughtn't have been. Nothing ever had been, in all the years she kept the habit, but she never gave it up, on the reasoning that a habit which cost nothing and caught nothing was still worth keeping against the one year it might catch something.

She never gave a reason where a fact would serve, and it was this above her hand that the clerks who followed her remembered longest. A lord of the plain came to her once, in her middle years, to have a boundary dispute set down in his own favour, and pressed her at length for her opinion of the case's justice before she would write a word of it. Peigi told him she kept no opinions in the roll, only what was said and by whom, and that if he wanted an opinion set down he should say his own aloud and she would write that too, word for word, with his name beside it. He did not ask her again. What she wrote instead, plainly and at his own asking once he saw there was no other way to have it set down at all, was the boundary as both sides had described it, the disagreement between the two descriptions, and the day the two sides had last agreed on anything, which turned out, when a Keeper's clerk went looking for it two centuries later in a dispute grown far larger than the first, to be the one fact in the whole roll that settled the matter.

## III. Of the Grey Hawk of the Northern Skell

While Peigi kept her roll at Cathair dhearg, far to the north at Seann Skell on the cold coast the fowler families had begun a custom the Hall's roll would not hear of for years yet, being a custom kept entirely among themselves, on a shore few clerks ever troubled to visit. Every household there kept a hawk, as fowlers along that coast had always kept hawks, for the wild geese and the duck that came down onto the flats in their season; but somewhere in the years after the letters reached Inis bheag, without any teacher coming north to bring them, the fowler households of Seann Skell took to feeding the hawk before the household ate, on the reasoning, as one of them put it to a trader who asked, that a hunger fed first was a hunger that did not turn on the house that kept it waiting.

Raghnaid nic Chaluim was born into one of those households in the same generation that Peigi's roll, far south, was already growing thick with her successor's hand, and her own household came to say of her, in the tellings that followed, that she was the first of her line to carry the custom the whole distance it would go. Her hawk was a grey bird her father had flown before her and called Mòd, as the old households of that coast called their hawks Mòd, after the grey hawk of time that had lived down every age as a salmon and an eagle and a hawk by turns and remembered, between its shapes, more than any single creature could remember alone. At every dawn of the season when the handful was due she stood at her own threshold, unhooded the bird herself rather than leave it to a servant, and burned her household's measure with Mòd on her wrist, so that its eye was on the flame the whole time it burned, the smoke going up past its head into a sky still more grey than light.

A clerk sent up from Cathair dhearg to write the custom down for the Hall's own roll found her at that threshold one such dawn, in the last winter of her middle years, and asked her plainly, once the burning was done, why the bird had to watch it rather than wait fed in its mews until the handful was ash. She did not answer him at once. She looked at the hawk a while, working a loose feather straight with two fingers, and then said only that the flame was a hunger too, as the hawk on her wrist was a hunger, and ought to be fed the way a hunger is fed, in the sight of the thing that hungers; a household that burned its handful with the bird shut away would be doing the whole business by halves, the way a man does a kindness by halves when he does it where no eye can find him out. The clerk wrote the answer down as she gave it, without a word of his own beside it, for the Hall's roll had learned, at some cost, what came of setting a joke beside a keeping it did not yet understand, and meant, this time, to make no such mistake twice.

## IV. Of "No Concern of Ours"

The custom spread from Seann Skell up the Abhainn ìseal within Raghnaid's own lifetime, carried by traders who had seen it kept and thought it a fair thing to keep, until households at Inis ìseal and at Cnoc bheag were feeding a hawk before themselves and burning their dawn handful on the wrist as the Skell fowlers did, though none of them fowled for a living and more than one household, in the first years, kept a bird they had bought at no small cost from a Skell trader and fed better than they fed their own children, which their neighbours who kept no hawk at all were not slow to remark on.

Word of it reached Dùn ìseal in its own season, as word of every rite kept anywhere on the island reached Dùn ìseal sooner or later, and the Keeper who held the cup that year, whose name the Hall's roll set down plainly enough but whose face no teller troubled afterward to remember, was pressed by a household of the lowland plain to say whether the Hall meant to rule on the hawk-rite as Fionnlagh Dall had once ruled on a household's burning of its own measure. The household was uneasy less at the burning itself than at a keeping it did not understand spreading past its own coast into ground it thought its own to watch. The Keeper sent a clerk north to Cnoc bheag to see the dawn kept before answering rather than trust a report carried three times over by mouths that had not seen it, and the clerk came back and said plainly that the hawk-households burned no more of their handful than any other household on the island, to the grain, no later in the day and no earlier, only in the sight of a bird where other hearths kept no such sight at all.

On the strength of that report the Keeper wrote to the lowland household, and to every household after it that raised the same question in the years that followed, four words that were copied afterward into more than one household's own chest as a ruling in their own right: the rite kept the measure, and was no concern of the Hall's. It was, as it turned out, the last time in the whole age that the Hall answered any rite so simply. No one at Dùn ìseal that season, occupied wholly with the hawk-households and nothing beyond them, reckoned how many other keepings the island held that a household might one day put the same question to, nor how much harder each answer after the first would grow, once the first had been given away so freely and for so little in return.

## V. Of the Sting and the Western Harbours

Down the western coast, at Ros bheag where the harbour court had kept its own customs of trade since before any hawk was fed on that shore, a different swearing had begun to travel up from Baile àrsaidh among the yellow scorpions of the dry hill country, where a sting shut living in a box had been sworn on for longer than any teller could say why. A merchant of Ros bheag named Aonghas Ruadh, trading salt fish for the dry country's dyed wool, came home from his first journey south with the box and the custom both, and swore his next bargain on the sting before the harbour court had ever seen such a thing done, more from habit picked up on the road, he said afterward, than from any belief that a sting in a box bound a man tighter than his own word.

It bound the western harbours tighter than anything else had, within a few short years, for reasons Aonghas Ruadh himself was the first to admit he had not foreseen. A trader of the plain came to Ros bheag the following season, meaning to buy a season's fish on credit against a debt he swore would be paid by midwinter, and would not swear it on the sting when the harbour court, having by then grown fond of the custom, asked him to; he gave his reasons plainly enough, that a box of scorpions was no fit thing to swear a grown man's debt on and he would swear it on his own name or not at all. The harbour court let him swear on his own name, as he asked, and sold him no fish, on the reasoning, given him by the court's own eldest without much softening, that a man who would not swear on the sting when every other trader on that coast did was a man telling the court something about his debt that his own name alone did not say.

He came back within the season, paid double what the fish would have cost him at midwinter, and swore on the sting the second time without being asked, and it was remembered afterward at Ros bheag, by those who told the tale against traders of the plain generally, that he had learned in one dear lesson what the harbour had known for a generation: a custom trusted by everyone costs a stranger far more to refuse than to keep. He was not the last to learn it the hard way. A wool-factor of Cathair mhòr, a few years later, made the same refusal on the same grounds and got the same answer, and left Ros bheag that season, the harbour's own tellers say, with a cart still full of the fleeces he had meant to sell against fish he never bought.

By the time Aonghas Ruadh himself died, an old man still keeping his own scorpion in its box behind the harbour-master's door, no merchant travelled the western coast without learning the swearing before he learned the prices, and the harbour courts from Ros bheag to Baile àrsaidh had come to hold, without any of them writing it down as a law, that a bargain sworn any other way was a bargain a careful man watched closely. His own box, kept after him by his daughter and her daughter after her, outlived three generations of the family that first brought it north, the scorpion within it renewed from the dry hill country each time the old one died, so that the box itself, the harbour-master's clerks noted once in an idle entry, had by then held more scorpions than any single trader who swore upon it would live to count.

## VI. Of the Second Wolf-Stone

Inland and to the south, where the roads Ailean Clachair's masons had first bridged now carried carts as well as carriers, the wolf-sworn road-guards of Caol leathan kept a watch the island's peace had come, by slow degrees, to lean on more than it once needed to, feuds being rarer on the roads with every generation that forgot what the stones at Dùn thais had once marked. The guards swore their own oath on a white stone at their founding, and it had stood alone for so long that most travellers who passed it took it for no more than a boundary marker of the guild's own ground, until the shrine-keepers of Inis thais, hearing of the guard's stone and its keeping, asked whether a second might be set at their own shrine, where the drowned were already prayed to and a guard's oath, they thought, would sit well beside an older prayer.

The guards agreed, being flattered more than they let on to be asked, and set about the dragging of it themselves rather than trust the work to any mason who had not sworn their own oath. The stone they chose was white quartz from high on the mountain, heavier by half than the guard's own first stone, and it cost them, by the account their captain Calum Mòr gave afterward to a teller who asked him plainly what a shrine-stone was worth, more oxen than the guild could easily spare and the better part of a season's toll besides, the sledge breaking twice on the lower slopes and one ox dying under the strain of the second breaking before it ever reached the road proper.

Calum Mòr stood at Inis thais himself when the stone was finally raised beside the shrine, and it was noted by more than one of the shrine-keepers present, and set down afterward in their own thin roll, that the road-guard's captain, asked by the eldest keeper whether he wished to pray before the stone himself now that it stood, answered only that he had sworn on it once at his own founding and saw no cause to swear on it twice in one lifetime, but that his guards were welcome to pray there as often as the drowned welcomed a stranger's prayer, which was a longer answer, the keeper thought, than the question had strictly asked for, and she let it stand in the roll unaltered, since a captain's own words, once given, were no clerk's business to shorten.

The eldest keeper herself, who had asked leave to set the second stone in the first place, lived to see it stand thirty years and more, long enough to watch travellers who had never known the shrine without it take the second stone for as old as the first, and older pilgrims correct them without much patience. She said once, to a younger keeper who asked her why the guard's white quartz sat so close beside the shrine's own darker stone rather than at a more respectful distance, that a shrine which could not stand a guard's oath at its elbow had no business calling itself a shrine to the drowned, who had never in any teller's memory asked to be prayed to alone.

## VII. Of the Roll Filed Without Comment

None of these keepings, taken alone, seemed to the Hall at Cathair dhearg a thing worth troubling a ruler's own attention with, and for a long while none of them troubled it much. The clerks who kept Peigi nic Tormoid's roll after her, and their own successors after them, entered each report as it came up from the coasts and the western harbours and the road-guards' ground, in the same dry hand the roll had always been kept in, without a word beside any of them to say which, if any, mattered more than the rest. A trader passing through Cathair dhearg in the last years of the age, asked by a clerk what he had seen kept on his travels that season, might name the hawk fed before its household at Seann Skell, the sting sworn on at Ros bheag, the wolf-stone prayed to at Inis thais, and the fisher-marks still taught at Inis bheag though no fisher's child who learned them any longer remembered the joke once made about them, all in the one breath, as though they were pieces of the same custom rather than four keepings that had grown up a hundred miles apart from one another without ever once being planned together.

The Hall's own roll, by the year the last of these reports was entered, ran to more chests than the room Seonag Chrom had first given the clerks could hold, and a later generation of them had long since given up trying to read every leaf of it at once. They worked instead from summaries their own predecessors had made of it, summaries of summaries by then in places, the original hands beneath them known to the clerks who copied them only by name and reputation, never by sight. What no clerk at Cathair dhearg thought to write, because it was not the kind of thing the roll had ever been kept to say, was that the Hall which had once answered a hawk-household's rite with four words and no more had found, in all the years since, no occasion to answer any other rite so simply again. Each of the coasts and the harbours and the road-guards' ground had, by slow and separate degrees, grown a keeping too much its own to be dismissed any longer in a clerk's single line, the way the first of them had once been dismissed and never troubled again.

The report on the second wolf-stone, the last of that season's news to reach Cathair dhearg, was filed in its turn behind the sting-oath and the hawk-rite and the fisher-marks, in a hand none of the four keepings would have known from any other. No ruler of the red hall that year, and no clerk who filed the report, thought to ask what it might mean that the island now held so many ways of keeping faith with what it could not fully explain, each sworn and fed and prayed to in its own corner, none of them counted among the rest, nor asked to be.
