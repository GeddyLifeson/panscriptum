# Of the Knotted Cords in the Galleries

*FE 1,596 – 1,641*

## I. Of the Keeper's Cutters

Calum Liath had kept the flame nine years by the time the last stone was set between the two woods, and in all that time, the reciters of Dùn ìseal say, he had asked for nothing that a Keeper had not already asked for before him. He fed the flame, he weighed the coloured coal against no man's claim, he judged the small quarrels the Small-Burning Law was made to settle, and beyond that he wanted little, a want so plain that people remarked on it, for the Keepers before him had mostly wanted a great deal in one direction or another. What he wanted, when he came at last to want something for himself, was to know how much coal the mountain held.

He put the question first to old Aonghas nan Uaimh, a cutter grown grey in the lower galleries who had followed the vein by touch since before Calum Liath was born, and Aonghas laughed at him, not unkindly, and said that a man might as well ask how deep the sea ran under a boat. The vein went where it went. It thickened under one gallery and pinched to a thread under the next, and no cutter living had ever cut to the end of it, because no cutter had ever needed to. You cut your measure, you filled your basket, you went home. Calum Liath heard him out and said that he had not asked whether the thing could be known, only whether anyone had tried to know it, and when Aonghas allowed that nobody had, the Keeper said that was answer enough for him.

He sent for cutters from every gallery at Dùn ìseal in the turn of that year, {{date:II-0119}}, and gave each of them a cord knotted at the length of a man's forearm and a lamp that burned longer than the Law allowed a household's, being the Hall's own oil and not a hearth's handful, and told them to walk every gallery the mountain had, counting the knots as they went, and to mark on a slate where the coal ran thick under their feet and where it ran thin, and where it ran out altogether into blind rock. He did not tell them why, beyond what he had told Aonghas, and most of them did not ask, because a Keeper's asking passed for an answer in the Hall, and had done since Beathag Bhàn's silence was first counted a virtue.

It was slower work than any of them had reckoned. A cutter's hand knows a rich vein from a poor one at a touch and does not trouble to measure the poor one; Calum Liath's cutters had to measure both alike, poor and rich, dead rock and running coal, cord-length by cord-length, in galleries that had never been walked from end to end by anyone living, because no one had ever had reason to walk them so. Aonghas, who took the lowest galleries for his own share of the work, being too old by his own reckoning to be sent to the high cold ones, came up some evenings with his hands shaking from more than the cold, and said to anyone who would listen that a man's whole life passed differently when he was counting instead of cutting; that a cutter felt the mountain giving to him, handful by handful, the way a household feels a harvest coming in, but a counter only felt how much of the mountain he had not yet counted, which was, every evening, nearly all of it.

## II. Of the Three Lean Seasons

The counting cost what counting costs. A cutter set to measuring the vein was a cutter not filling a basket, and the baskets of Dùn ìseal's own galleries, which fed the nine hearths and, through them, more households than any other seam on the island, ran short through three seasons running while the cords were walked. The Small-Burning Law did not bend for it. The measure stayed the measure, a household's handful was still a household's handful, but there were fewer hands filling the store the handfuls were drawn from, and by the second of the three lean seasons the waypoint-keepers who kept the Hall's own tallies were sending less than the Law's full measure to households a full day's carry from the mountain, with a mark against each short delivery so that it might be made good later, though nobody could say when later would come.

At the household of Beitris nic Ruairidh, three days' carry north on the road to Baile Mòr mhòr, the winter of the second lean season was cold enough that her husband's father, an old man who had outlived two Keepers and three of his own sons, sat by a fire built half of driftwood and half of the coal they had, and said, more than once and to anyone in the house who would sit still for it, that he did not remember the mountain ever asking a household to wait on it before. Beitris, who had six mouths at her own hearth besides his, told him each time that the mountain was not asking anything, the Hall was asking, and the Hall had told them plainly enough why, if he cared to have the reason read to him again from the waypoint-keeper's mark. He did not care to. He had lived, he said, under the Binding his whole life and under no other law, and the Binding had never before told him to burn driftwood in his own hearth while the mountain kept its coal for counting.

It is told, and the reciters of Dùn ìseal tell it against themselves as often as for themselves, that in that second lean season three households of the northern road sent a steward to Dùn ìseal to ask the Keeper directly whether the counting would end before the grain in their own stores did, and that Calum Liath received the steward himself, at the door of the Hall and not within it, and answered only that he could not yet say, because he did not yet know the answer himself, which was true and satisfied nobody. The steward, a stooped man called Uisdean who had walked three days to ask a question and would walk three days home with the same one unanswered, said before he turned to go that a household could live on a true answer no better than on a false one if neither filled a basket, and Calum Liath, the reciters say, had no reply to that either, and let him go with only the one truth to carry. The households of the northern road burned what driftwood and dried dung they could find that winter, and kept their grudge, as households do, longer than the Keeper who had given them cause for it.

Even at the mountain's own foot the short seasons were felt, though less sharply, for the nine hearths ate first from what the galleries gave and felt the pinch last. The waypoint-keepers, whose own tally sticks recorded every short delivery against the day it might be made good, found that the marks piled faster than any later plenty could clear them, and more than one keeper of a northern house, going over her sticks by firelight, wondered aloud whether a debt owed by the mountain to a household was a debt at all, or only a Keeper's word that a household must take on trust, the way it had always been made to take the weather on trust, and the vein's own moods, and everything else that no household had ever been asked to agree to and every household had always had to live inside.

## III. Of the Cords in the Dark

Through all three seasons the cutters went on knotting their way through the mountain's galleries, and by the second summer even Aonghas, who had begun the work half in scorn of it, had come round to a kind of pride in the doing, though he would not have called it that himself. He had walked, by his own count, further under the mountain than any cutter before him, further than any hunter had walked above it, and he had begun, without meaning to, to know the mountain's shape as a blind man knows a familiar room, by the cords and the echo of his own lamp and the way the air moved or did not move in a gallery that ran true or one that had been abandoned for a reason nobody living remembered.

The young cutters set to the high galleries fared worse. Two of them were lost a full day in a working that had been cut and abandoned in Fionnlagh Dall's time and forgotten since, and came out half starved and swearing they had heard something moving in the dark beyond their lamp's reach that was not the mountain settling, though neither of them would say more of it afterward, and the tellers of Dùn ìseal, who love a mystery no better than they love a plain answer, leave it exactly as the two cutters left it: unfinished.

Calum Liath walked the counted galleries himself when the cords came back to him. He had no skill to check a cutter's work and did not pretend to it; he went because he had said, from the first, that he would not ask a cutter to walk ground he had not walked, and he kept to it even in the last winter of the counting, when his own knees had begun to trouble him on the steep pitches and Aonghas, twenty years his elder, went ahead of him with a hand held back to steady him over the worst of the rubble, an old cutter steadying a Keeper, which the reciters say pleased Aonghas more than anything else that happened in those three years.

## IV. Of the Slab and the Silence

When the last cord came back and the last slate was read against the others, it fell to a clerk of the Hall, a woman named Mór nic Aonghais who had learned her letters from a wanderer of the stag's school and kept the Hall's small store of writing ever since, to set the sum down where any who could read the stag-letters might see it. At Calum Liath's own word she cut it on a slab of the grey stone the Hall's masons kept for its own building, a slate being too easily lost or broken for what it would carry, and set the slab upright inside the Hall's door, where the light from the doorway fell on it in the morning.

The sum said that the mountain, giving out no more than the Small-Burning Law already allowed and no less, could give every household on the island its handful for more generations of households than there were grains of barley in the granaries of Dùn dhearg at full harvest, a comparison Mór nic Aonghais had chosen herself, having counted grain for the Hall before she counted anything else and knowing no larger number that a household could hold in its own hand and believe.

It was read out at the council that autumn, {{date:II-0121}}, with the great shares' stewards and the waypoint-keepers and as many of the nine hearths as could be spared from the flame all crowded into the Hall to hear it, and Calum Liath read it himself, plainly, without adding to it, and when he had finished there was a silence in the Hall long enough that the reciters who were there, and told it after to their own daughters, remembered the silence better than they remembered the sum. Nobody stood to speak against it, and nobody stood to praise it either. The stewards of the northern road, who had burned driftwood three winters running to pay for the counting, said nothing at all, and it was that silence above every other in the Hall that the Keeper, and the Hall after him, chose to take for assent, though Mór nic Aonghais herself said once, late in her life, to a niece who pressed her on it, that a silence could mean a great many things besides yes, and that she had never been sure, cutting the sum into that slab, whether she was cutting the Hall's proof or the Hall's excuse.

## V. Of the Lower Gallery

The lean seasons ended with the counting, and the galleries filled again, and it might have been no more than a hard three years remembered afterward the way households remember any hard three years, if it had not been for what came in the sixth year after the slab was set, when two youths of the nine hearths, neither of them a full season in the galleries, were sent down into the lower working at Dùn ìseal, the same working the two cutters had once lost a day in, to bring up a basket the evening's cutters had left behind.

Their names were Griogair mac Dhonnchaidh and a girl called Sìne Bheag, cousins of the same hearth, sent together because the lower gallery frightened Griogair on his own and Sìne Bheag had gone down it twice before and told him there was nothing in it worse than the dark. They went without a Keeper's cutter to walk ahead of them, because none was to spare that evening and the errand seemed a small one, a basket and back, and in the dark beyond the last lamp-mark they broke, by whatever mischance the tellers can no longer reconstruct, a face of rock that Aonghas's own cords had marked as unsound two years before, a mark that had gone into the Hall's slates but never, it was found after, into any warning a cutter carried down with him in his own head. The rock came down across them both. It was Aonghas himself, roused from his own hearth near midnight when the two did not come up, who went down with three others and dug them free, and found Griogair already gone and Sìne Bheag alive but not for long past the digging.

The Hall keened them as it keened its own, which it had never before had cause to do for two so young, and buried them on the slope above the vein where the Keepers' own dead were laid. Aonghas, who had marked the unsound face himself two years before and lived to dig the two of them out of what it had done, kept his own silence about that winter for the rest of his life, and spoke of it only once, in his own old age, to Oighrig Bhàn, telling her that he had gone down into the lower gallery that night thinking of nothing but the digging, and had only understood, climbing back up into the grey of the morning with Sìne Bheag's weight failing in his arms before he reached the air, that his own mark on his own slate had done this as surely as the rock had, a mark he had cut and trusted a slate to carry when it should have been carried, he said, in the head of every cutter who went down after him.

It might have ended there, in grief alone, as such things mostly did, but Griogair's mother, a woman named Ealasaid Rìoghach of no particular note before that winter, stood up at the council that followed and asked the Hall, before every steward and hearth-holder gathered there, what it owed her son.

## VI. Of What the Hall Owed

It was not a question the council had an answer ready for, and the reciters of Dùn ìseal, who are fond of Calum Liath and slow to fault him, admit plainly that he had none either, not that day. The Small-Burning Law set the measure a household might burn and the peace of the Hall's own ground; it had never set down what a household was owed for a child sent into the dark on the Hall's own errand and lost there. Ealasaid Rìoghach did not ask for silver, having none to spend and no custom to spend it in; she asked only, and asked it twice more before the council found an answer, why a boy of one season in the galleries had been sent down a working two cutters had already lost a day in and a slate had already marked unsound, and why no one had told him so before he went.

The council sat three days on it, which was longer than it had sat on anything since the Small-Burning Law itself, and the first day was spent, by the reciters' account, on little more than stewards of the nine great shares each saying, in their own words, that the fault lay elsewhere: with the errand's giver, who had not thought a basket worth a Keeper's cutter; with the slates, which had kept a warning no one had thought to carry down in memory; with the two youths themselves, who ought, said one steward and was shouted down for it, to have known a lower gallery's dangers without being told. Ealasaid Rìoghach sat through all of it on the floor by the door, and it was only on the second day, when she stood a second time and asked again, in fewer words than before, what the Hall owed her son, that the council turned from fault to custom, which the reciters count the true turning of those three days, more than anything said in them.

The Hall had no payment to offer that would not have looked like a coal-price set on a life, and offered none. What came out of the three days was a custom instead: that no young Dia-thìrich would go into any gallery of the vein's custody again, on any errand however small, until they had spent seven years bound to a cutter of proven years, learning the mountain's marks and its unsound faces and its moods before they were trusted alone in its dark. It was not called a payment, and Ealasaid Rìoghach, when it was told to her, said only that it came seven years too late for her own son, which was true and which nobody in the council disputed.

Calum Liath himself did not live to see the custom much beyond its first winter. He had failed through the last of the counting years, a failing the reciters put down more to age than to any single grief, though they note, as reciters will, that he was never quite the same Keeper after Griogair and Sìne Bheag as he had been before, readier to sit long over a small dispute than he had once been, as if he had learned, late, that a Keeper's silence could cost more than his own dignity. He died in the ninth year after the slab was set, {{date:II-0123}}, in the Hall itself, by the door he had had the slab set beside, and was carried up to lie among the Keepers' own dead near the two he had buried before him, a resting he had asked for himself and been given without argument.

## VII. Of Oighrig Bhàn

The nine hearths chose Oighrig Bhàn to follow him, a woman of six and thirty years who had come to the Hall as a girl of the northern road, one of the very households that had burned driftwood through the lean seasons, though she never once, in all her years at the flame, spoke of that against the Keeper who had caused it. She had sat with Calum Liath in his last winter more than any other of the nine hearths, she had no wish to be chosen after him; she had a gift the reciters call patience and a plainer name calls stubbornness, and she had stayed at his side through the failing when younger hands found reasons to be elsewhere.

She took the flame in a season thin on ceremony, the Hall still close in its grief for the old Keeper, and one of her first acts, before she had worn the office a full year, was to send for the custom the council had made after Griogair and Sìne Bheag and ask why, three years on, it still stood only as a thing agreed and not a thing done. Nobody in the Hall could give her a good reason, beyond the plain one that grief cools and custom is easier sworn than kept, and Oighrig Bhàn, who had buried nobody of her own in the galleries but had grown up on short seasons all the same, said that a custom not kept was worse than none, because it let a household believe itself answered when it was not.

## VIII. Of the Twelve on the Stone Cup

She bound the first twelve herself, at Dùn ìseal, in the Hall's own doorway where the light fell on the reckoning-slab still, each of the twelve sworn by name to a master cutter of her own choosing, most of them the very cutters who had walked Calum Liath's cords through the lean seasons and knew the mountain's unsound faces better than any slate could tell it. Aonghas nan Uaimh, old now past any gallery-work of his own, took the youngest of the twelve, a boy called Tormod Beag who had been born the same winter Griogair and Sìne Bheag died, and taught him, in his first season, more of where not to cut than of where to cut, which Aonghas said, to anyone who asked, was the whole of the teaching that mattered.

The twelve swore on the stone cup, each in turn, to serve seven years at a master's side before the coal was theirs to touch alone, {{date:II-0124}}, and Oighrig Bhàn stood by the cup for every one of the twelve oaths and did not sit down until the last was sworn, a small stubbornness the reciters note because it cost her a long morning on her feet in a doorway open to the winter air, and because it was, in its way, the same stubbornness that had first brought her to Calum Liath's bedside and kept her there. Four of the twelve, grown old in their own turn, came in time to hold the flame themselves, which the reciters of Dùn ìseal count the best proof the custom ever had, better than any words the council could have found for Ealasaid Rìoghach, though none of it, they are careful to say, ever answered her question in full, for no custom made after a death answers the death that made it, only the deaths that might have followed and did not.

## IX. Of Cnoc bhàn and the First Entry

Word of the binding travelled east before the first winter of the twelve's service was out, carried by the same carriers who carried most things between the mountain and the far side of the island, and came at last to the stag's house at Cnoc bhàn, where the teachers who kept their own long watch over their own grove heard it and judged, after the manner of that school, that a custom worth a Keeper's keeping was worth their own testing. They took up the seven years for their own cutters, binding each apprentice to a master of the school as the Hall bound its own, and added to it a rule the Hall itself had never thought to make: that every apprentice keep, in their own hand once the stag-letters were learned, a written account of every stroke made in a season's cutting, so that a master might read back, at the season's turn, exactly what the apprentice's hand had done and where it had gone wrong.

It was a small rule when it was made, meant, by the teacher who first set it down, only to give a master something firmer than memory to correct an apprentice by, but the accounts grew, season on season, into a store the school kept and consulted the way the Hall kept its slates, and in time the teachers of Cnoc bhàn came to call them by a plainer name than any the stag-letters gave them: the strike-books, for the strokes they recorded, one stroke and one line at a time.

The first of them was begun by a girl called Rìoghnach, no kin that any reciter can trace to Ealasaid Rìoghach of the northern road though the two names are near enough that more than one teller has made the mistake, a novice of that first bound company at Cnoc bhàn, in the winter after her binding, at her master's own bidding. She wrote her first line by lamp-light in a hand still stiff from the letters' learning, three words and no more, set down as plainly as a stag-letter allows: *chuir mi buille*, I struck a stroke, and under it the day, and under the day nothing else, because there was, in that first season, nothing else yet to say. It was the least of anything written on the island in that age, and it is kept still among the strike-books of Cnoc bhàn, the school's own reciters say, because a beginning ought to be kept exactly as small as it was.
