# Of the Plunderers and the Thing under the Boat

*FE 398 – 421*

> *Cha do dh'fhaighnich a' mhuir cead.*
> The sea did not ask leave.
>
> — said still at the wake of Baile chrom, by whoever pours the first cup

## I. Of the Households Put Out to Sea

Anndra Cìobair was eighteen the year his uncle's household lost its right for the third time, and he remembered it less as a shame than as a leaving: three men going down to the strand at Baile chrom with what they could carry on their backs, a woman weeping without troubling to hide it, and his uncle Murchadh Ruadh saying nothing at all, which those who knew him thought the worse sign of the two. The Law took no pleasure in a third offence and no more trouble over it than over a first; it set the household's name on the roll that the waypoint-keepers cried at Samhain, and after that no hearth on the coast would give the household coal, nor grumble, nor discuss it, for the discussing had all been done at the first offence and the second. Anndra's father stood at his own door and watched his brother's household go down the path to the strand with their bundles on their backs, and said only, when Anndra asked him why he did not call out some word of parting, that a word said across a threshold the Law had shut could do the leaving household no good and the speaker a good deal of harm, and that he had five sons of his own still under that roof to think of.

What the Law did not do, and had never troubled to do, was ask where a household went once its name was on that roll. Some went inland to kin who would have them on sufferance and cold porridge. Some starved slowly and were counted among the reverent centuries' quiet dead, a household here and a household there that the rolls simply stopped naming, no cause given because none was asked. And some, that year and in the years after it, went down to the strand instead and put out in whatever hull would still float, and lived off the north-western waters the way a household with no coal and no grain and no roof lives: by raiding the strands of households luckier than themselves.

They were not the first to try it. Baile chrom's own grandfathers told of a leaner year, generations back, when two brothers had done the same thing for a single winter and come home again once their own cousins forgave them, and no one had troubled to give it a name. This time the name stuck, because there were more of them, and because most did not come home, and because within three years of the first household's leaving there were boats enough on those waters, out of half a dozen strands and not one household among them, to make a raid worth calling a raid rather than a theft worth calling a shame. Spùinneadairean, the strand called them, plunderers, and by the time Anndra was a man grown the word had settled onto the north-western waters as a whole, the whole grey width of them from the sea-monster's rocks to the point where no boat of Baile chrom cared to go unescorted, as if the sea out past the headland had always belonged to thieves and always would.

Anndra's own household kept its right. His father fished the near grounds and paid the Law its handful without complaint or love, and taught his sons that a man who lost his right had lost it fairly, whatever pity a body might feel watching the boats go out, and that pity spent on a man already gone to sea was pity wasted on the one man who could no longer use it. But Anndra had grown up beside boys whose cousins were now spùinneadairean, and he had sat at more than one hearth where a mother would not say the word for her own son, only "my boy that's on the water now," as if he had merely taken up fishing a little further out than most, and Anndra never once heard any of those mothers say where the fish he brought her came from, or ask.

## II. Of the Night the Southern Strand Burned

He was one-and-twenty, and two years wed, when the raid came near enough to his own coast that he stood in the smoke of it. It fell on a strand half a day's walk south along the shore, six households that kept goats on the machair above the tideline and dried more fish than they ate themselves, and it fell in the small hours of a night with no moon, which every teller after agreed was no accident, for a boat coming in dark and going out dark leaves little for a strand to swear to in the morning beyond smoke and empty racks.

Anndra was not there when the boats came in, only when the word came up the coast at first light, carried by a boy who had run the whole way and could barely stand by the time he reached Baile chrom's own hearths, and he went south with a dozen others of the fitter men, arriving to find two of the six houses burned to their stone footings, the drying-racks stripped bare to the last strip of fish, and a woman of that strand sitting on the ground before her own ruined door with a burned hand wrapped in her skirt, saying to whoever would listen and to no one in particular that she had known two of the men in the boats by their voices, though she would not, then or ever after, say the names aloud where the Law's own ears might carry them home to kin who still had a right to lose.

It was the first raid Anndra had seen the marks of rather than merely heard of, and it taught him a thing the tellings had never quite made plain, that the spùinneadairean did not raid as strangers raid, taking what they could carry and gone before a strand woke, but as men who knew exactly which houses kept the fullest racks and which dogs barked loudest and which men slept furthest from the door, because most of them had grown up on those same strands themselves and had cousins sleeping in half the houses they burned. He said as much to his father that same evening, and his father told him only that a man does not choose his kin. He chooses, every day of his life, which of his kin's roofs he will burn and which he will not, and a household on the wrong side of the Law's third offence had made that choice already, whatever pity it earned it afterward.

## III. Of the Calm Water

The year Anndra was five-and-twenty he crewed the boat of Cailean Buidhe, a broad-beamed hull that had brought in more herring than any other keel out of Baile chrom in a decade, with four men aboard besides himself: Cailean at the steering-oar, his two sons Murchadh Òg and Eachann, and Anndra's own second cousin, a quiet man named Torcall who talked to the nets more than to the crew and was thought lucky for it, though luck, as it turned out that year, is not a thing any man can lend another.

They went out past the headland on a morning so still that Anndra remembered afterward being able to hear, a mile from shore, a dog barking on the strand behind them as clearly as if it stood at his elbow. The water lay like poured metal, no swell in it at all, which the older men said was a thing to mistrust more than any storm, for a boat in a gale at least knows what it is fighting. Cailean himself said so that morning, easing the boat out past the point with the oar barely stirring, and Murchadh Òg laughed at him for it and said a calm sea had never yet drowned a careful man, to which Cailean answered nothing, only watched the water a while longer before he gave the word to shoot the nets.

They shot the nets in the flat calm and sat waiting on oars gone slack in their locks, the sea so smooth under the hull that a man could watch his own face in it if he leaned over the gunwale, which Torcall did, once, leaning far enough that Eachann told him to mind he didn't fall in and save the fish the trouble of being caught, and Torcall straightened without answering and said nothing about what he had seen there, if he had seen anything at all beyond his own reflection.

It came up without a wave to announce it. Anndra was watching the net-floats for the first tug of fish when the whole boat lifted: a slow, deliberate lift, the lift of something enormous passing beneath, as if the sea had drawn one long breath and held it. The floats went under. The boat tipped, righted, tipped again, and for three or four heartbeats every man aboard stood or crouched exactly where the lift had caught him, not one of them making a sound, for there was, in that silence and that stillness, nothing yet to cry out against: no shape breaking the surface, no fin, no eye, only the water gone taut and strange beneath the keel, as a drumskin goes taut under a hand laid flat on it before the hand strikes. Cailean said one word only, low, not a command and not quite a prayer either, and no man aboard remembered afterward what the word had been.

Then the boat went over.

Anndra never afterward could say, and would not for the rest of his life be made to say, whether the capsizing came from below or from the men's own scramble to the one side, which is the ordinary way boats are lost in a calm as much as in a storm, five men throwing their weight the same way at the same instant out of the same fear. He went into water colder than the still morning had promised, came up under the hull's shadow, and swam clear of it without deciding to, the way a man's body decides such things for him when the deciding mind has nothing yet to decide with. Close by, once, he heard a great slow displacement of water that was not a wave and was not any fish he had ever heard broach, a sound with weight in it, the sound a hillside makes settling rather than a sound the sea usually makes, and he did not look toward it. He swam and did not look, and has never in any telling since said which of the two took more from him.

Of the five who had gone out on Cailean Buidhe's boat that morning, two came in: Anndra himself, and Torcall, who had somehow kept hold of an oar the whole way and gave it up to no one on the strand, not even when his hands had to be prised from it finger by finger once he was ashore and could no longer feel them. Cailean and his two sons were never found, not that day nor after, though the coast was walked for a week and the near rocks searched at every low tide, and the boat came ashore three days later a mile down the coast, upside down, its planking sound, not a mark on it that any man could point to and call a cause.

## IV. Of What Was Not Said

The strand asked Anndra what he had seen, in the way a strand always asks, gently at first and then less gently as the days passed and no bodies came ashore to answer for themselves, and Anndra told them what he had heard and what the boat had done, and no more. He did not say that the lift beneath the keel had felt, for the length of one held breath, like a hand cupping something too small for it. He did not say what size he had judged the displacement to be, out of the corner of an eye that had refused, in the moment, to turn and look directly at it, nor did he ever, in the forty years he lived after, describe the sound of it to anyone in words closer than "a hillside settling," which satisfied no one who asked and was, he always maintained, the truest account he had to give. Torcall said even less, and within a year had stopped fishing the near grounds at all and taken instead to mending nets ashore, a trade no one begrudged him and which he followed, in near total silence, for the rest of a long life.

It was not the first such loss the north-western waters had given the coast, nor, the old women said, would it be the last, and there were tellers enough at Baile chrom's own hearths who called the thing by a name before the year was out, the Sea-Watcher, and swore it had been seen off that same headland since before any hearth now standing had been raised, some thirty feet of it by the tellings that kept closest to modesty and a good deal more by the tellings that did not. Whether it was one beast or many, or the sea's own way of answering a calm too still to trust, no one at Baile chrom claimed to know, and those who claimed to know were, as a rule, men who had not been in the boat.

What the coast did agree on, without needing to discuss it the way the household's third offence had needed no discussing, was that the loss should be kept, and kept every year, on the day nearest the one it had happened, with a bowl set out for the three who did not come home and the boat's name spoken aloud once by whoever poured the first cup. Anndra poured it the first year, because he was the one who had come back with a story to tell and would not tell it, and by unspoken agreement he went on pouring it every year after, growing old at the task the way another man grows old at a trade he never chose and could not now set down.

## V. Of the Fire Relit

Four years after the loss, in a season when three more boats had gone missing off the same stretch of water, though none so strangely as Cailean Buidhe's, the fishers of Baile chrom climbed the headland and lit the old beacon on the point again, the one their grandfathers had let go dark once the Law's own coal-galleries made every hearth's fire a counted and measured thing and a fire kept for no household's own handful had come to seem, to a generation grown careful of the Law, a kind of waste no one wished to answer for. They fed it with driftwood, as the Law required of any fire kept outside a share's own handful, and a beacon fed with driftwood burns unevenly, now high and now guttering, so that a boat far out watching it learns to read its own danger in how steadily the point holds its light, and learns, too, that a fire which cannot be measured cannot be rationed, and will burn on whatever the tide happens to leave it, generous some nights and mean on others, exactly as the sea it watches over is generous or mean.

Anndra carried wood up that headland himself more nights than he later cared to count, in the years the beacon was young and its keeping still fell to whoever happened to be ashore and willing rather than to any household by right. It became, before long, a charge some households took in turn the way other coasts took turns at a granary door or a toll-stick, a season each, and the fire on the point came to be called, by fishers from other harbours who steered by it once Baile chrom's own people had stopped thinking of it as anything but the fire, the light of Baile chrom, kept for no coal the Law could measure and asked for by no one but the sea.

He stood the watch there the night word came up the coast, carried by a trader's boat out of the east that had put in for water and stayed the night for the company, that other households far off had found other uses for driftwood and silt and old stone, and Anndra, who by then thought of little beyond his own headland and his own three names in his own bowl, listened to the news the way a man listens to weather from a country he will never sail to: with interest, and no great sense that it touched him, and asked the trader, more from courtesy than curiosity, whether the coast he spoke of kept any beacon of its own, and was told that it did not yet, having found, in the stone it had dug up, a better inheritance than any fire a household could feed.

## VI. Of Seann Bhrenn and the Stone Quay

Far to the north-east, on a coast Anndra never saw and never expected to, fishers from the mountain's northern side had grown too many for the harbours they held and gone looking, as households on every coast of the island had gone looking before them, for a shore with room enough and water deep enough and no one already standing on it to say otherwise. They found such a shore at the old harbour the herders of that coast still called Seann Bhrenn, empty since some earlier age had emptied it, and settled there, and kept the name they found it under rather than giving it one of their own, the way the western settlers before them had kept Seann Warr's name and the road-builders before that had kept Seann Mhorn's, the whole island by then grown used to the notion that a place's old name outlasted whoever had first worn it out.

They found more than an empty harbour. Digging back the silt that had swallowed the old shoreline, the first families to break ground there struck stone: a quay, cut and laid by hands none of them could account for, buried deep enough that only the lowest tides had ever shown a hint of it, and sound enough, once cleared, to take a hull heavier than any their own boats yet built. They used it without asking who had laid it, the way a household uses a well it finds already dug, and the harbour prospered faster for the finding than it would have prospered digging its own quay from nothing, which is the kind of luck a coast does not question closely once it has taken hold of it, though more than one household there was heard to wonder, at its own hearth and no further, what manner of hands had cut stone so true before any hand now living had learned the trade.

Word of the stone quay travelled the coast the way word of any strange find travels, mouth to mouth and boat to boat, gathering a little in the telling at every hearth it passed through, so that by the time it reached Baile chrom, three seasons and the width of the island removed from the digging, the quay had grown in some tellings to a whole buried town and shrunk in others to a single worked stone no bigger than a door-lintel. Anndra heard it told both ways at his own hearth in the same winter, from two travellers who had never met each other and neither of whom had seen the quay themselves, and believed neither telling more than the other, which struck him, he said once to Torcall over the mending of a net, as very much how his own boat's story must sound by the time it reached a hearth as far off as Seann Bhrenn, grown to a whole drowned town in one mouth and shrunk to a man's own fright in another, and neither telling any truer than his own silence had been.

## VII. Of the Cairn at Cathair naomh

Further north still, at Cathair naomh, a smaller thing was begun in the same years that would grow, in its own way, larger than any stone quay: a cairn, raised on the hill above the town for its own dead, with a single stone brought to it at every burial by whichever household had lost the body being laid down. It was not the island's first ancestor-cairn, nor even the first at Cathair naomh, whose people had marked their dead with stones since before any hearth there could say why, but it was the one that took hold and did not stop growing, so that within Anndra's own lifetime travellers who had seen it once and passed that way again years later swore the hill looked changed, heavier at the crown, as if the mountain had grown a second, smaller mountain out of nothing but grief carried up one stone at a time, each stone no bigger than a fist and the whole of it, by the time word of it had travelled the length of the island twice over, taller than any hall the Hall had raised.

Anndra never climbed that hill. He knew of the cairn the way he knew of the quay at Seann Bhrenn, as a thing told at a hearth by someone who had it from someone else, and it would not have occurred to him, hearing it, that a coast so far off from his own headland kept its dead in much the way Baile chrom kept its own three names in a bowl once a year, a stone against a cup, the two of them sharing one refusal: that a loss should go unmarked merely because the sea, or the years, offered no better answer to it than silence. He thought of it once, hearing the tale a second time from a different mouth some years on, and said to his own wife by the fire that a coast a man had never seen and would never see kept its grief the same as his own coast kept its, which she took, rightly, for as close to a kind thought as her husband ever spoke aloud about a stranger.

## VIII. Of the Great Cats and the Southern Road

It was in the one-and-twentieth year after the boat went down that news came to Baile chrom of a ruling far to the south, carried up the coast the slow way such things travelled before any road ran the whole length of the island: that the carriers of the southern road had found the great cats' own crossing running straight over their own path, between the southern hills and the fens beyond them, at the same season every year without fail, the beasts coming down off the high passes in numbers no herder had troubled to count and crossing the road in a body rather than singly, so that a carrier caught on that stretch at the wrong hour might sit an hour or more waiting for the last of them to clear the road. Eilidh nic Ìomhair, Keeper of the flame these forty years and more, had ruled, on being asked what the Law made of it, that no panther might be killed on that road while the crossing lasted, whatever beast it cost a herd or a household to let pass, and that the carriers were to wait it out in the waypoint houses built along that stretch for exactly such waiting.

The carriers, the telling had it, had grumbled at first, as carriers grumble at any delay the Law lays on a basket already late, one waypoint-keeper of that road being quoted, by the time the tale had travelled as far as Baile chrom, as having asked Eilidh outright what a beast's road was worth set against a household's coal, to which she was said to have answered only that the beast's road had run there longer than the carriers' own, and that a Law which measured every handful to the grain could measure a few days' patience too, if it chose. They kept the rule, once it was made, the way the island had come to keep most rulings it did not much like: without gladness, and without the will to test what the Hall would do if they broke it, and it cost them, in the end, exactly what she had said it would, and no more.

Anndra, hearing the tale at his own hearth from a trader who had it third or fourth hand from the south, thought it a strange kind of law to trouble a ruling over, a beast's own road left alone for a season, and said so to Torcall, who by then mended more nets than he spoke words, and who answered, after turning the thought over a good while with his hands still busy at the mesh, that a coast which had learned to let its own dead go unnamed for the sea's sake could hardly grudge a road a few days for a beast's sake. Anndra had no answer to that, and turned the tale over again himself that night on the headland with the beacon guttering and flaring above him in its usual driftwood way, and it seemed to him, by the time the fire burned low, that a rule which cost carriers only time, to let something older than any road pass unhindered across it, was cheaper wisdom than most rulings he had heard of in his life, and dearer bought than most too, if a man reckoned rightly what it had taken to learn it.

## IX. Of the Bowl at the Wake

For near forty years after the boat went down, Anndra kept the beacon and poured the wake's first cup, growing from the young man who had swum clear of a lift in the water he could not name into an old fisher whom the younger households asked, when a strange calm came over the bay, whether it was the kind of stillness that wanted watching. He told them honestly what the water had done and what he had heard, the lift and the sound like a hillside settling, and never once, in all those years, what he had seen out of the corner of an eye he had refused to turn. The younger households learned, in time, not to press him further than that, the way a strand learns which questions a tide will answer and which it will only ever wash back unanswered.

He died in his sixtieth year, in his own bed above the strand, with Torcall's grandson keeping the beacon by then and the wake's bowl already passed to other hands some seasons before, Anndra having grown too stiff at the last to carry the cup up from his own door to the shore without a boy's arm to lean on. The coast buried him within sight of the headland, as he had asked, and the fire he had carried wood to on so many nights burned that same night as it always burned, unevenly, fed with whatever driftwood the tide had left, watched by no one who had been in the boat with him, and answering, as it had always answered, to no measure the Law had ever troubled to keep.
