# Of the Shut Hall and the Choosing of Gilleasbuig

*FE 2,475 – 2,558*

> *Cha do rugadh rìgh; chaidh e a shuidhe air an stòl a bh' ann.*
> No king was born; he came and sat on the stool that was there.
>
> — a saying of the clerks of Seann Dunn, kept in no other house

## I. Of the Boy at the Rolls

At Seann Dunn the hall and the shrine stood on the near bank of the Abhainn chaol, and the mountain stood across the water, and between them the ford ran shallow enough in summer that a boy of five could wade it holding his father's hand. Gilleasbuig's father was Tormod Beag, a copying clerk of Sliochd Thormoid, and his trade was the same trade Peigi nic Tormoid's line had kept since her own hand was first proved worth telling from another's: he sat at a bench under the shrine's north window with a knife and a scraped hide and put down, in the small square hand the house had taught for six generations, whatever An Talla Dearg sent him to copy. Gilleasbuig grew up under that bench before he grew up beside it. He knew the smell of the scraping-knife's grease before he knew the sound of his own name in any mouth but his mother's, and by the time he was old enough to be sent for water he could tell, without being told, which of his father's rolls was Clàr nan Rìghrean and which was only a province's tithe.

He asked once, small enough still to be answered rather than sent off, why Clàr nan Rìghrean needed six copies where the tithe needed one. His father said the tithe would be true for a year and then thrown out, and a king-list had to be true for longer than any man who wrote it, so it went into six hands at once, that if fire took one house or flood another, the rest would still agree. Gilleasbuig turned that over for the length of a whole winter and came back to it in the spring with a second question, whether a king-list could be wrong if all six copies agreed and were wrong the same way. His father put down the knife at that one and looked at him a while before he answered, and what he answered the boy did not think worth remembering until he was an old man himself and had cause to remember it very well.

The temple across the river was Sliochd Thormoid's own, raised in Eilidh nic Ìomhair's day when Na Ceithir Taighean were first bound, and its lamp burned Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig's measure like every other lamp on the island, or was supposed to; the other three houses said, in the flat voice houses use for an old grievance they no longer trouble to prove, that Seann Dunn's lamp burned fuller than its neighbours'. Gilleasbuig heard that said at his own hearth more than once before he was ten, always by a stranger and always denied at once by whoever in his own house was nearest, and he learned early that a thing repeated and denied in the same breath, in every house on the island, was probably not worth losing sleep over either way.

He learned his letters at that bench, and then the shapes of Roinn na Rìoghachd's old shares, and then, because his father's hand was wanted at Cathair dhearg two summers out of three, the road between Seann Dunn and An Talla Dearg, first riding behind Tormod Beag and later, at fourteen, riding alone with the rolls in a satchel across his back and a hearth-sign token that let him sleep free at any waypoint house on the way. The road ran a day and a half in fair weather, down through the mountain districts and onto the lowland plain, and he came to know every waypoint keeper on it by name before he knew half the great families whose shares those waypoints stood in, which his father said was the proper order for a clerk to know a road in, whatever order a lord might prefer.

He was set once, at eleven, to copy out a short roll of a province's tithe under his father's eye, and made in it a single error, a stroke too many in a number that turned a modest year into a plentiful one, and did not himself catch it. His father caught it before it left the bench, scraped the hide clean to the one line, and made him write the whole roll again from the beginning rather than mend the single stroke, though the mending would have taken a tenth of the time. Gilleasbuig asked, aggrieved as a boy of eleven is entitled to be, why the whole roll needed doing over for one wrong stroke. His father told him that a roll with one mended stroke in it taught a boy that a mistake could be quietly put right, and a roll done over from the beginning taught him it could not, and that of the two lessons only one was worth having in a clerk who might one day be trusted with a roll no one stood over to check. Gilleasbuig did the roll over, and never again, by his own later account, let a wrong stroke stand unscraped in anything that left his own bench.

He was, by every account that troubles to say anything of him at all before his fortieth year, an unremarkable clerk's son doing an unremarkable clerk's work, and there is no sign that he thought of himself as anything else, or that anyone else thought of him as anything else, until the year the line of Goraidh Mòr ran out.

## II. Of the Failing of a Line

The failing had been long in coming and was no secret to anyone who read the rolls Gilleasbuig's own house kept. Lachlann Bàn had built his granaries and set his shared-meal law with Coimheadaiche na Lasrach Raonaid Dhubh, and had a daughter, Oighrig nic Lachlainn, who took An Talla Dearg at one-and-twenty and held it the better part of fifty years without ever bearing a child of her own body. The houses had begun their counting early, before her reign was even half spent; Gilleasbuig heard it done at his own father's bench, in the careful unhurried voices men use when they are reckoning something they hope not to need. Sliochd Ìomhair reckoned its blood, which ran thin by then but ran; Sliochd Thormoid reckoned the clerks who had kept every roll An Talla Dearg owned since Tormod Scrìobhaiche's own day; Sliochd Raghnaill reckoned its boats and its silver and the seat at Seann Tarr Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig had first named. Only Sliochd Mhuirich, which held Coimheadaiche na Lasrach's own office and answered to no share at all, took no part in the reckoning, and said, when asked, that the vein had not asked it to give judgement on the matter.

Oighrig herself, whom Gilleasbuig saw only once in his life, on a summer he came to Cathair dhearg at sixteen with his father's rolls, was by then a spare grey woman who signed what was put before her and asked few questions of it; the famine that was never allowed to become a famine had happened under her reign and under her name, though it was Lachlann Bàn's law and the reeves of Dùn dhearg's own quick hands that had turned it back, and she is remembered, where she is remembered at all, for having been present at a good thing rather than for having done it. She asked him, that one summer, whose son he was, and when he told her, said only that his father's hand was known to her and left it there; it was the whole of the speech any ruler of the island ever made to him before he came to sit where she had sat, and he remembered it, he said later, chiefly for how little either of them had thought to make of it at the time.

She died at Cathair dhearg in her one-and-seventieth year, without an heir of her body and with the line of Goraidh Mòr ending in the same breath as her own life did, and An Talla Dearg was shut behind her, its doors barred and its clerks — Gilleasbuig's own house among them — set to keep it, there being no other hand to give it to.

Gilleasbuig was one-and-forty that year, a clerk of thirty years' standing and no more remarkable than he had ever been. He was at Seann Dunn when the word came up the river road, and what he remembered of the day, when he was old and told it, was the small practical scramble that followed it at his own hearth: which rolls to send down to Cathair dhearg and which to keep, and whether the shrine's own lamp should be let burn on as before or banked against a season nobody yet knew the shape of. He said the news of it, oddly, went out of his head almost at once, crowded out by the tally of rolls he had to sort before nightfall.

## III. Of the Shut Hall

The clerks kept the shut hall the better part of two years before Na Ceithir Taighean so much as agreed to sit in the same room together, and Gilleasbuig's own hand was among the hands that kept it: he rode down to Cathair dhearg the winter after Oighrig's death and stayed the better part of a season, sealing rolls and answering, as best a clerk could answer for a hall with no one in it, the reeves who came asking whom they were to render their accounts to now. He told them, truthfully, that he did not know, and that the tallies were to be kept all the same, on the plain reasoning that whoever came to An Talla Dearg next would want them true and would not thank a clerk for having let a good year go unrecorded for want of a name to put at its head.

The queen's own chambers were shut with the rest of the hall, the fires in them let go cold, and Gilleasbuig slept that whole season in the rolls-chamber among the boxes of hide, with the other clerks of Sliochd Thormoid turning the lock in the great door themselves each night, since no steward remained whose office it was to do it for them. He wrote to his father once that winter that a red hall with no one in it was, for all that anyone outside it could tell, exactly the same red hall it had always been, the rolls being kept, the rents being reckoned, the granaries opening and shutting to the same seasons; only the men who kept it knew how strange it felt to hand a reeve his own tally and have no name to put after the word *sworn to*. His father's answer, kept among the house's own letters, said that a clerk who found that stranger than he found the years before it, when the tallies were kept for a queen he never once saw sign her own name to them, had not yet understood what a clerk's work had always been.

Na Ceithir Taighean met at last at Dùn ìseal, in the second year of the shut hall, because no custom on the island covered a red hall with no one to fill it and Talla na Lasrach's own ground was the one place none of the four could be told they had no right to stand. Sliochd Ìomhair claimed by blood, thin as that blood now ran; Sliochd Thormoid claimed through the clerks who alone among Na Ceithir Taighean had never once let the rolls fall silent in fifty years of an ageing queen; Sliochd Raghnaill claimed through its boats and its silver, which by then paid for more of the island's carrying than any two of the other shares together. Sliochd Mhuirich, whose own house held Coimheadaiche na Lasrach's office and so stood outside every reckoning of blood or trade, made no claim at all, and said again what it had said at Oighrig's death, that the vein had not asked it to give judgement on the matter of a stool it had never sat in and had no wish to.

How long the three houses argued, and whether they argued in good faith or only long enough to be seen to have argued, is not a thing the rolls of any of them agree on; Sliochd Ìomhair's own account, kept at Dùn dhearg, makes more of the sitting than Sliochd Raghnaill's does, and Sliochd Thormoid's account, kept by the same clerks' bench Gilleasbuig had grown up under, says least of all, which a man might read either as modesty or as the plain fact that the clerks had the least to gain from making the quarrel sound weighty. What is agreed by all three is that no blow was struck and no word was said that could not be unsaid over the same table the next morning, and that the sitting dragged three years past the meeting itself before any name was put forward that all four houses could be brought to sit still for.

Gilleasbuig himself sat through most of that sitting at the edge of the room rather than in it, called down from Cathair dhearg twice a year to lay the hall's own rolls before whichever house asked to see them, and so he heard more of the three years' arguing, from the doorway and the side bench, than any of Na Ceithir Taighean' own principals ever troubled to say to his face. He heard Sliochd Ìomhair's eldest ask, more than once, what blood was worth if it were passed over for a boat or a bench; he heard Sliochd Raghnaill answer that the island's silver did not care whose grandfather had held a hill, only whose hand could be trusted to reckon it truly; and he heard, oftenest of all, the long patient silences of Sliochd Mhuirich's own man, who came to every sitting, said the same few words at the start of it and the end of it, and otherwise let the other three talk themselves hoarse over a stool his own house had no wish to sit in. Of himself, in all that time, nobody asked anything at all, beyond whether the year's tallies balanced, and he answered that question, each time it was put, exactly as truly as he had ever answered it for Oighrig.

## IV. Of the Cup Set in His Hands

The name that was put forward, in the end, was Gilleasbuig's own, and by every account it was Sliochd Raghnaill that first said it aloud, on the reasoning that a clerk who had kept the shut hall's rolls straight for three years without once putting his own house's claim ahead of the work had shown, whether he meant to or not, exactly the kind of hand a red hall wanted at its head. Sliochd Ìomhair, whose blood claim would have cost the other three houses more to refuse outright than any of them wished to pay, consented on one condition only, which it had been readying since the first year of the shut hall and did not trouble to soften in the asking: that whoever took An Talla Dearg, the graves at Dùn dhearg would remain the kings' own burying-place, as they had been since Ìomhar Dearg first washed his hands at the ford. Gilleasbuig, when the condition was put to him, is said to have answered that he had never in his life expected to be buried anywhere else, which was true in a plainer sense than the house that heard it understood, and the condition was sworn to on the spot.

He was summoned to Dùn ìseal in the sixth year of the shut hall, in his six-and-fortieth year, and told nothing of why beyond that his own presence was wanted before the council; he rode down from Cathair dhearg expecting, by his own account, to be asked some further question about the tallies, and packed no better cloak than he wore for any ordinary sitting. He stood before the council of Na Ceithir Taighean in that same plain cloak while Sliochd Raghnaill's own elder named him, and Sliochd Ìomhair, having already had its condition of the graves sworn to beforehand, consented without argument, and Sliochd Mhuirich's man said the same few words he had said at every sitting for three years, which this time were enough.

Coimheadaiche na Lasrach set the stone cup in his hands, filled to the fill of two palms as it had been filled for every household's dues since Fionnlagh Dall's own day, and for the first time in the whole of the age a ruler was made under the mountain rather than crowned or chosen anywhere else on the island. Gilleasbuig held the cup a long moment before he drank from it, and what struck him then, by his own later telling, was the plain foolishness of a clerk's son from Seann Dunn holding in his two hands the very measure his own father had spent a lifetime copying onto hide for other men to swear by. He asked Coimheadaiche na Lasrach, before he drank, whether a clerk's hands were the right shape for it; Coimheadaiche na Lasrach answered that the cup had no opinion of the hands that held it, only of whether they spilled it, and Gilleasbuig did not spill it. It looked, more than one chronicler has since said dryly, very much as though Talla na Lasrach had made the king, there being no blood in the choosing and no boat and no old grievance settled, only a hand Talla na Lasrach had come to trust with its own rolls.

He rode to Cathair dhearg that same season to take An Talla Dearg, by the lowland road his father had ridden twice a year for thirty years, and slept his first night in it, by his own account, in the small room off the rolls-chamber where the clerks kept their own beds, because the queen's own chambers had stood empty five years and nobody had thought yet to air them.

## V. Of the Line Drawn and the Temple Enlarged

His first act of any weight, the year after the cup was set in his hands, was to have Clàr nan Rìghrean copied again from its beginning, in his own house's careful square hand, with a single line drawn beneath the name of Oighrig nic Lachlainn. He drew it, or had it drawn, not to mark her death, which every copy of the list already marked well enough in its own way, but to mark the ending of a whole line of names running back to Goraidh Mòr, so that no clerk after him would waste an afternoon hunting a kinship that no longer existed. The line is in every copy of Clàr nan Rìghrean made since, and it is the first mark in the whole record that a clerk's own hand put there for a reason the clerk himself troubled to explain in the margin, which some later readers have taken as the surest sign of all that a clerk, and not a warrior or a boat-captain, had at last come to sit in An Talla Dearg.

He kept the clerks' office in his own house's hands for the whole of his reign, which cost him nothing, since no other house had ever much wanted the work of it, and he sent for his son Tormod, a boy of sixteen already taught the bench at Seann Dunn as Gilleasbuig himself had been taught it, to sit beside him at Cathair dhearg and learn An Talla Dearg's own rolls before he was taught anything else of ruling, on the same reasoning his own father had given him and never troubled to improve upon. He enlarged his house's shrine at Seann Dunn into a full temple in the one-and-twentieth year of his reign, with a court where pilgrims from as far as Cnoc bhàn and Muileann chiar could stand under a roof while they waited their turn at the lamp, and the other three houses said, as they had always said, that its measure burned fuller than Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig allowed; a Keeper's cutter was sent every season after to try it by the same weights used everywhere else on the island, and every season the cutter found the measure kept, which satisfied nobody who had not already believed it and changed the mind of nobody who had not already doubted it, and so the saying outlived the doubt by a good many years, as sayings will.

By the plain evidence of the rolls his own house kept of him, he was neither a great king nor a poor one. He raised no new law that outlasted him and lost no old right that had not already been half lost before he came to the cup; the granaries Lachlann Bàn had built stood full or empty by the same seasons as before him, and the roads his mason predecessors had cut were mended by the same waypoint-keepers as before him. The island under his hand was, by every account, neither better nor worse governed than it had been under Oighrig's, only governed by a man who had grown up knowing exactly how the rolls that governed it were kept, which is not nothing, though it is not much to set beside the deeds of the men the earlier tellings of this age remember.

He heard, in his eighth year, the one dispute of his reign the rolls trouble to set down whole: a household of Muileann àrsaidh and a household of Ceann àrsaidh both claiming a single water-meadow their grandfathers had shared without a stone between them, each holding a notched stick that named the whole meadow theirs and neither stick agreeing with the other. Gilleasbuig sent a clerk of his own house, rather than a steward, to copy both sticks stroke for stroke before he judged anything, and found that the two tallies agreed exactly up to a season forty years back and disagreed only after it; this told him less about the meadow than about the year a notch had been cut wrong by someone long dead on one side or the other. He gave the meadow to neither household outright and to both together, with a boundary stone cut down its centre for the first time in living memory, on the plain reasoning that a stick a man could not check against another man's stick was worth exactly as much as the two men trusted each other, which by then was not much. The households of Muileann àrsaidh and Ceann àrsaidh have shared the meadow by that stone ever since, and grumbled at it about equally, which a clerk of Seann Dunn once told his own son was the surest sign a judgement had been fair.

## VI. Of His Death and the Son Who Followed

He died at Cathair dhearg in his one-and-seventieth year, in the small room off the rolls-chamber he had never troubled to leave for the queen's own empty chambers, having kept, to the end, the habit of rising before his own household to check the night's tally against the day's before either was sent on to a steward. He was carried to Dùn dhearg to be buried among Sliochd Ìomhair, as had been sworn at his choosing, over five days on the lowland road his own father had ridden twice a year for thirty years, the bier stopping each night at a waypoint house whose keeper had known him first as a boy with a satchel of rolls and last as the man whose seal was on every tally in the house. Sliochd Ìomhair kept its own oath to the letter: the grave was dug beside Ìomhar Dearg's own kin, and no voice was raised against a clerk's son lying there, the condition having been the graves' and not the blood's.

His son Tormod succeeded him at Cathair dhearg in his one-and-fortieth year without any meeting of Na Ceithir Taighean at all, and none of the four asked for one; whatever quarrel over An Talla Dearg the island had once thought worth three years of sitting at Dùn ìseal for, it had by then settled itself so thoroughly into a single house's keeping that a son following a father seemed to every house the plainer course, and the plainer course, once taken, was never afterward questioned. Between that quiet handing-on and the coming of a queen from the same house long years later, Clàr nan Rìghrean at Cathair dhearg gives nine rulers their names and their years and very little else, the clerks of Gilleasbuig's own line having proved, in the reigns that followed his son's, as diligent in the keeping of the rolls as they were brief in the writing of anything beyond a harvest, a toll and the mending of a bridge. It is a sign, the tellers of Seann Dunn still say, of an island doing tolerably well; a shut hall had been filled without a blow struck over it, and a clerk's own careful hand had proved, in the end, as sound a measure of a king as blood or a boat or an old grievance nursed three centuries long.
