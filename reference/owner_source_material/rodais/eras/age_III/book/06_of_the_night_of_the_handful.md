# Of the Night of the Handful

*FE 2*

> *Aon oidhche, aon teine, aon scian.*
> One night, one fire, one knife.
>
> — said afterward of Oidhche an Dòrlaich at Dùn ìseal

## I. Of the Telling

*Niall Òg told this in his old age to whoever would sit and hear it, and by then he had told it so many times that the households of Tràigh an t-Salainn could say the ending with him. He always began the same way.*

There is one night in the valley below Dùn ìseal, Niall Òg would say, that every hearth on the island still keeps some memory of, though not one hearth in a hundred could tell you why. It is kept without being understood, the way a child keeps a game its grandmother taught it and never asks what the game was for. On Oidhche an Dòrlaich every household burns its whole season's measure at once, though Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig was not yet made and would not be made for six generations, and no household then owed Talla na Lasrach a handful of anything. They did it because the heirs did it. The heirs did it because there was a night in the second year of An Aois Naomh when every fire in the gathering-house below Dùn ìseal burned at once, and by morning the kingdom of Ailean Mòr had nine lords and one that would not be sworn to any of them.

Niall was there. He was some nine hundred years old, a grandson of the king in one of the lesser lines, and he had spent the year since the ford carrying words between his aunt Eithne and his uncle Murchadh because he was the only one of the family either of them would let into a room with the other. He used to say that this made him, of all the king's grandchildren, the one who least wanted the cutting of the cord to come, because a peace was a thing he could carry messages about, and a division was a thing that was simply done, after which there would be nothing left for him to do at all. He was wrong about that, as it turned out, but on that night he did not know he was wrong, and he watched the whole of it the way a boy watches a house being pulled down that he has played in all his life.

The year between the agreement of the four rules and the cutting had been a long, watchful one. The grass of the valley, trodden by seven hundred and twelve heirs and their households and their stewards' households besides, had not come back green that spring, and the summer after it had been worse, so that a man riding down from the passes that autumn said the valley looked less like the floor of a kingdom than the yard of a fair that had gone on three years too long. The nine who would cut the cord spent that year each in his own tent or hall, sending stewards to argue small points that had been thought settled and were not, and Niall carried more words in those twelve months than he had carried in the nine centuries before them. He grew, he said, very tired of being trusted by everyone and believed by no one, which he came in time to think was most of what it meant to be neither eldest nor first.

## II. Of the Waiting Out the Year

The stewards of the nine lines spent that year haggling over things the four rules had left unsaid, and most of what they haggled over came down, in the end, to boundaries: whether a line drawn "from the ford to the hill" meant the near hill or the far one, whether a wood belonged with the pasture beside it or the stream beyond it, whether a fishing-weir built by a household now claimed by two lines paid its catch to one or split it. None of it could be settled before the cutting, because none of the nine would agree to a line on the ground before they had agreed to a line on the cord, and so the small quarrels piled up outside the gathering-house all that year like unswept snow, waiting for the one night that would let them all be argued at once, by daylight, with a boundary-stone instead of a steward's word.

Aodh Crom aged visibly in that year, more than a man of his hundred and eighty-odd years by rights should. He had carried the asking of An Ceangal and the counting of the knots and now the setting of this last night, and he told Sìne, when she asked him once at her own door whether he slept, that he did not think a man his age was owed sleep on top of everything else the valley wanted of him. The eight other keepers of the slab watched the hill and kept the flame and let the old man carry the heirs' business, because none of them wanted it and he had never once refused it.

By the time autumn came round again and the heirs began riding back down into the valley for the second time in two years, the grass under their feet had given up trying to grow back at all, and the gathering-house, which had stood through the knotting with its thatch still new, wore the black smudge of eleven days' smoke over every door. Niall rode down with his uncle's household, as he had the year before, and found the valley smaller than he remembered it, though nothing in it had shrunk; only the crowd in it had grown used to being crowded, the way a room grows smaller the longer a family sits in it.

## III. Of the Cord Laid Twice Round the Floor

They chose Oidhche an Dòrlaich for it, Oidhche an Dòrlaich, because it was already the one night of the year on which no household in the hills held anything back. Whatever coal a family had put by against the winter it burned that single night, all together, so that the dark half of the year should begin with every hearth in the valley alight at once and none of them alone in the cold. It was an old custom and older than the king, and Aodh Crom said, when the heirs asked him to set the night, that it was fitting the kingdom should be cut on the one night when nobody kept anything back.

The keepers had Sreang nan Oighrean brought down from its chest and laid along the floor of the gathering-house as it had been laid on the last night of the knotting, from the hearth to the door and back to the hearth again, with the length that was left over coiled at the hearthstone. Lamps stood along it the whole way, one every few paces, so that a man walking beside it in the smoke could see his own feet and not much else. There were more people in the house that night than there had been on any night of the eleven days of knotting: every heir who had given a line to Sìne nan Snaidhm was there, or had sent someone in his place, and the nine who would cut the cord stood each at the place along it where his own colour began, with a household's worth of kin crowded behind him against the wall.

The hearths outside were doing what the gathering-house's own hearth was doing, burning the whole valley's measure at once, and the smoke of it lay over the roof and came in under the eaves and hung along the beams until a man's eyes stung with it before the night was an hour old. Niall said afterward that he had never in his life, before or since, been in a house so full of smoke and lamplight and people that he could not see across it, and that the cord itself, when he first looked for it on the floor, seemed to swim.

Nine knives were laid out on a cloth by the hearth, plain knives, none of them marked, because no one wanted it remembered afterward which knife had been whose. Aodh Crom stood over them. He had grown old fast that year, faster than a man of his years should, and he leaned on a stick he had not needed at An Ceangal. He said only one thing before the cutting began, and Niall remembered it word for word because it was so short: that the cord had been laid once for the whole kingdom, and would now be laid nine times for nine lines of it, and that whoever cut wrong would have to live with his own hand and no other's to blame.

Each of the nine walked the cord first with a lamp, bent low, before he was let touch a knife. Murchadh Mòr went first because he was eldest, and the house went quiet enough to hear the cord's fibres creak under his hand as he ran it through his fingers, counting. Then Eithne went, and after her the rest in the order they had agreed among themselves that summer, an order none of the tellers give the same way twice, because none of the nine were there afterward to be asked and cared to be remembered as having gone last. Each of them walked the whole length twice: once toward the door, once back. By the time the last of the nine had walked it the lamps had burned low enough that servants came along the wall refilling them, and the night was not young any longer.

## IV. Of Murchadh's Cut

Murchadh Mòr was near two thousand nine hundred years old that night, the eldest living son of the king by more than seven hundred years over the next, and he had known for a summer exactly where along the cord he meant to cut. He did not walk it twice out of doubt. He walked it twice because the keepers had told the nine to walk it twice, and Murchadh did what he was told when he had already decided to do it.

Murchadh knelt at the place, took up his knife, and cut. It went through clean, one stroke, and the two ends of the cord lay apart on the floor with the lamp between them, and he stood up without looking at either end again. A murmur went along the wall, the first sound the house had made since the cutting began, because everyone there had been waiting to see whether the eldest of them would flinch at doing with a knife what he had spent a summer arguing for with his tongue, and he had not flinched at all.

"There," he said, to no one, and went to stand by the hearth with his length of the cord already gathered over his arm, and did not speak again that night, though he watched everything.

His length ran from the hearth almost to the turn by the door, the longest of the nine, for it held more knots than any other line: Earrann Mhurchaidh, from Baile Mòr mhòr to the high passes, with eleven sons of his own body already knotted into it and their children's children besides. He had asked for nothing more than the rule of the eldest gave him and had got exactly that, and Niall, watching him stand at the hearth with the whole length of the north over his arm, thought that this was the last time in his life he would see Murchadh want for nothing.

## V. Of Eithne and the Woman Who Would Take Both

Eithne nic Ailein was the youngest of the nine, not yet two thousand years old, and she had argued harder for the lowland plain than anyone had argued for anything else that summer, on the plain grounds that it was flat, it was rich, and it was hers by right of being loudest about it longest. She knelt at her place a long while before she cut, longer than Murchadh had, running her thumb back and forth over a single knot as if she were counting it more than once.

When she cut, her knife went through the knot itself.

The house went silent in a different way than it had for Murchadh, a silence with an edge on it. Aodh Crom, who had been standing by the hearth with his stick, came forward at once and looked at the cut end, and asked, in a voice that carried the length of the house because every other voice in it had stopped, whose knot that had been.

A woman came out from the wall with her hand over her mouth. She was a cousin of Niall's own generation, of the line of Tràigh an t-Salainn, and her name was Ciorstaidh; Niall had known her since they were children together at the spring below Dùn ìseal, before either of them had learned to count the years in their blood, and he said afterward that he had never seen anyone go so white so fast in a room already full of firelight.

"That is mine," said Eithne, looking at the cut end in her own hand.

"That is mine," said the man who held the length beyond it, the head of the next share down the cord, and he was not smiling either.

Ciorstaidh stood between the two ends with the whole house looking at her, and for a moment she said nothing at all. Then she took her hand away from her mouth.

"I'll take both," she said.

Nobody had thought to ask her whether she wanted to be part of the argument at all, and it was the first time in the whole of Roinn na Rìoghachd that anyone had answered a question no one had put to her. Eithne looked at her for a long moment, and then laughed, short and without much warmth in it, and said that the girl had more sense than either of the two lords quarrelling over her knot, which was not a kind thing to say in front of both of them, and was also true. The man beside Eithne did not laugh. But neither of them wanted, on the first night of Roinn na Rìoghachd, to be seen arguing over one knot in front of seven hundred witnesses, and so it was let stand: Ciorstaidh of Tràigh an t-Salainn swore to both lines that night, the first of the double oaths, and carried both colours in the wool she kept afterward, and her children after her carried both, until the lands under each grew so far apart that no one family could ride between them in a season and the two oaths quietly went their own ways.

## VI. Of the Rest of the Nine

After Eithne the cutting went on, and it did not go cleanly again. Some of the nine had chosen their places with as much care as Murchadh, and their knives went through the cord and nothing else; some had not, and where the cord ran thick with the knots of Cuid nam Marbh, thick enough that no eye could be sure which knot lay exactly under a knife in that light and that smoke, more than one blade went through wool that had a name tied into it. By the time the ninth of them had cut, eleven knots along the whole length of the cord had been cut through and not between, and ten households besides Ciorstaidh's found themselves sworn that night to two lords at once, though none of the others made so short and plain an answer to it as she had. Some quarrelled for a season over which oath should stand and which should lapse; most simply paid both, the way a man pays two tolls on a road he cannot avoid, and grumbled about it at both ends.

Fionnghal nic Ailein cut her length clean, and it ran west to Ros dhomhain and its harbour, and she was some twenty-five hundred years old that night and had wanted the sea more than she had wanted the plain or the hills, and got it. Artair mac Ailein took Tràigh an t-Salainn, with its seat at the western Baile ghorm, a little more than twenty-three hundred years old and the quietest of the nine, who had said less in the whole summer of argument than any of his brothers and sisters and had ended, all the same, with a coast the length of two days' sail. Cormac mac Ailein cut through to Earrann Caol shean, seated at Caol shean, a little over two thousand years old, and did not yet know, and could not have known, that a seam of silver lay under a hill in his own share that no one then living had found.

The last four cuts went quicker, because the smoke was thick by then and the lamps were burning down and everyone in the house wanted the night over. Ùna nic Ailein took the north-west, with her seat at Muileann àrsaidh; Coll mac Ailein took the hills east of An Cnoc Ruadh, along the Abhainn fhionn, with his seat at Ceann àrsaidh; Gormlaith nic Ailein took the south-east, seated at Tobar dhearg; and Tadhg mac Ailein took the Abhainn ìseal and the bay the hawks fished, with his seat at Cnoc bheag. The tellers who came after did not agree on the order in which these four cut, and Niall himself, asked about it in his old age, said only that he had been watching Ciorstaidh at the time and could not swear to it either, which the tellers took as leave to put the four in whatever order suited the telling.

When the ninth cut was made Sreang nan Oighrean no longer ran the length of the floor. It lay in nine pieces, each gathered over the arm of the lord who had cut it, with the loose ends of the eleven double knots hanging free where two lords held the same knot between them; and an Earrann Dhearg of Ìomhar Dearg, which no knife had cut that night because it had never been tied into the cord with any colour of its own, stood as it had stood since the summer after the ford, needing no cutting because it had already been taken.

## VII. Of the Red Hill Sworn to Nobody

There was one length of the old kingdom that no knife touched that night, because no line of the nine could say it was theirs to cut.

An Cnoc Ruadh was not an Earrann Dhearg. An Earrann Dhearg was Dùn dhearg and the ground about it, which Ìomhar Dearg had taken with his own hand at the ford and no one had found a way to take back. Far to the west, above the lake shore, lay An Cnoc Ruadh, another place entirely, where a settlement had stood since before the king's grandfather's time, and its holders had sworn to Ailean Mòr and to no one else in all the years of his reign; not to Dùn ìseal, not to any lord of a share, only to the king himself, man to man, in a way that no other holding on the island had sworn.

On Oidhche an Dòrlaich An Cnoc Ruadh's holder stood up in the smoke and the lamplight, when the nine had finished their cutting and the house had begun to think itself done, and said what everyone in the house had known he would have to say sooner or later: that his oath had been to Ailean Mòr, and Ailean Mòr was in the ground beside his stone, and there was no man now living he had ever sworn to. He did not ask to be released. He said only that he was already free, and that An Cnoc Ruadh was, as it stood that night, sworn to nobody at all.

It was the quietest claim made in the whole gathering, and it caused the loudest argument. Before he had well finished speaking three of the nine had crossed the floor toward him at once, Murchadh from the hearth end and two others from nearer the door, each saying that a holding sworn to nobody could not simply remain so, and each meaning, though none of them said it so plainly that first night, that it ought to be sworn to him. An Cnoc Ruadh's holder said nothing further. He had said his piece, and the three lords could argue over an empty answer as long as they liked; it changed nothing that he had said. They argued a good while, there on the floor among the cut ends of the cord, with the fires burning low outside and the smoke thinning at last through the roof, and came to no settlement that night or for a long while after. An Cnoc Ruadh stayed as its holder had left it, claimed by three and kept by none, a small stubborn place that grew no faster than a place claimed by three lords and served by not one of them ever does.

Aodh Crom, who had watched the argument from the hearth without joining it, said one more thing before the house began to empty. He reminded the nine, in the same short way he had reminded them of everything else that night, that the ground on which they stood, the whole hill of the slab and the gathering-house and the valley below it as far as the boundary the keepers themselves had walked, was in no share and never would be; that this had been sworn at An Ceangal and was not to be argued afresh on a night when everything else in the kingdom was being cut apart. No one answered him. No one, that night, had the appetite left to argue with the one thing in the valley that nobody wanted for himself.

## VIII. Of the Grey Light and the Lengths of Cord

Dawn found the gathering-house half empty and the fires outside burned to grey ash, the whole valley's measure spent in one night as it was spent every year at the turning, though this year it had lit a division that would run for centuries where every other year it had only lit the dark. The nine lords went out through the door one after another, each with his length of the cord folded or coiled over his arm, and their people went with them, carrying baskets and rolled hides and children still asleep against a shoulder, so that the road out of the valley by full light looked less like the end of a gathering than the start of nine journeys that all happened to begin at the same door.

Niall stood by the door a long while and watched them go. He had no length of cord of his own to carry; his knot hung in Cuid nam Marbh, after his mother's hollow knot, for Niall held no share and would hold none, being neither eldest nor first among the living children of the eldest line, only "owed twice," as he put it himself, by two different accountings that had never yet paid him anything. He watched Ciorstaidh go last of all, with two colours of wool wound together over her wrist where the others each carried one, and she caught his eye at the door the way she had caught it across the house the night before, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing yet to say that the whole valley had not already seen.

He told it afterward, in his old age, always ending the same way: that he had stood at that door and thought the division was finished, the cord cut, the kingdom parted, the worst of the year's work done, and that he had been a young fool of nine hundred to think so, because a cord, once cut, does not stay cut in the same nine pieces forever, any more than a family, once parted, stays parted along the lines its parting first drew. An Cnoc Ruadh alone, he would say, sworn to nobody on that grey morning, outlived every one of the nine who had argued over it, and was still sworn to nobody when he himself was an old man; and of all the things cut and given and sworn on Oidhche an Dòrlaich, it was the one piece of the old kingdom that belonged, in the end, to no one at all.
