# Of Beathag Bhàn

*FE 4–15*

*Part Two: The Flame without a Hand*

> *Cha do dh'iarr sinn fear an fhuil aice; dh'iarr sinn làmh a bha saor dhith.*
> We did not ask for one of her blood; we asked for a hand free of it.
>
> — said still at the choosing of every Keeper after the first

## I. Of the Rush on the Slope

The mud was cold before it was anything else. Beathag Bhàn stood at the back of the crowd on the slope below the slab, and the wet of the path had climbed past her ankles and found her shins, and she thought, before she thought of anything grander, that she should have worn the other shoes. It was the twenty-eighth day of an t-Ògmhios, the fourth year since the fall of the old king, and half the valley had come up the hill to watch nine old men and women decide who would hold a fire that no one else was allowed to touch.

She had come for the barley, if she was honest with herself after, not for the choosing. Her mother's household sent her up with two others to trade a basket of it for salt, and the trading had not gone well, and she had stayed to watch because there was nothing better to do on the walk home. The keepers of the slab sat on their stones in a half-ring below the shoulder of the mountain, old people in undyed wool, and behind them stood the households who had sworn the Binding in the first weeks after the fall, smelling of wet fleece and peat-smoke and the black dust that does not wash out of a hill-woman's hands even after she has stopped mining. Further up the slope, apart from the rest and from each other both, stood the heirs who had sworn it first: men and women in good cloaks who did not look at the crowd, and did not much look at each other either.

A wind came down off the high passes and worried at every cloak on the hill, hers included, and she pulled it tighter and thought about her wet feet and did not hear her own name the first time it was said.

The second time she heard it because the woman beside her turned and stared, and then the man on the other side, and then the whole crowd around her seemed to draw its breath in together and step back, the way a field of standing barley parts when something runs low and fast through it. She had seen a hare do that once, seen the grain lean away on either side of a path the hare made and had not asked to make, closing again behind it as though nothing had passed. She understood, standing in the gap the crowd had opened for her, that she was the hare.

The walk down the lane the crowd had made left no mark on her memory at all. What she kept was the arriving: the bottom of it, in front of the nine, with mud on her shins and salt still owed to her mother's household, and the eldest of the keepers holding out a rush already lit, cupped in both his hands the way a man holds water he is afraid to spill.

His name was Aodh Crom, and she had seen him before at a distance, at the swearing that bound the flame to no one's blood, though she had not known his face up close until that morning: an old man bent sideways from some hurt long healed crooked, with eyes gone the pale grey of woodsmoke.

"Hold it for Brìde," he said, in a voice pitched to carry to the heirs on the slope above as much as to her. "And answer to Crom for what is taken from the vein."

She took the rush. It stank of tallow and the little flame bent sideways in the wind, away from her and toward the mountain, as though it too would rather be somewhere else. She found she had nothing to say. The silence went on long enough that she heard, behind her, one of the heirs shift his weight and someone's dog bark twice down in the valley, and still she said nothing, because nothing she could think to say seemed large enough for the size of what had just been put in her hands, and in the end she only stood there holding the flame steady against the wind with her whole body turned to block it, the way she would have sheltered a lamp carried across a dark yard.

The keepers counted that silence in her favour. She learned this only afterward, from one of them, an old woman named Beitris who had sat nearest the fire and had watched her rather than the flame. "A mouth that has nothing ready is a mouth that has not been planning," Beitris told her, months later, when the two of them knew each other well enough for the old woman to say such things plainly. "We had had enough, that spring, of mouths with something ready."

Of what Beathag Bhàn had been before that morning, little enough was thought worth keeping even by those who had known her. She was of no heir's line: her mother's household held no share and traced its blood to none of the seven hundred and twelve who had stood up to claim the old king's leavings, and no lord in the valley, however the shares fell out in the months after, could call her his by right of birth. That was the whole of why she had been chosen, and the keepers, whenever asked, gave no other reason and seemed to feel that none was wanting. She was six-and-twenty that summer, by her mother's counting of the winters, which made her younger than most of the keepers' own grandchildren and younger by some centuries than the least of the heirs standing above her on the slope, several of whom had outlived kingdoms she had never heard named.

The heirs did not come down from their place on the hill to speak to her. She stood at the bottom of the slope a long while after the keepers had risen and gone, still holding the rush, until someone from the households below came up and told her, not unkindly, that a fire held that long in the wind would want feeding soon, and showed her the way down to where they had made her a bed of dry heather in a turf hut at the mountain's foot, the nearest shelter anyone had thought to build before the choosing was done. She carried the flame in with her, set it on a flat stone in the hut's one room, and sat up with it through the first night telling herself, without much success, that she would think of the right words in the morning. The right words never came, that morning or any other. In time she came to believe that the keepers had been right, and that no words would have served her better than the silence had.

## II. Of the Slab and the Nine Households

The keepers of the slab were an old order and their own, older than the fall of the king and older, by their own telling, than the coming of Ailean's people to the mountain at all, and they kept a reverence that ran alongside the new Binding rather than under it: the ancestors, the sìth of the hollow hills, the vein itself, and the weather that ruled when a gallery might safely be opened. They had no wish to keep the flame themselves, and said so plainly to anyone who asked why they had chosen a girl of no line to do it rather than one of their own: a thing given to no one's blood, Aodh Crom said, could not afterward be claimed back into any blood, theirs included.

They taught her what they judged she needed and no more. In the first weeks it was the shape of the mountain from outside: which slope held snow longest and which shed it, where the galleries ran under which fields, which of the cairns above Cnoc ghlas the watchers climbed to read the cloud on the high passes before a gallery was let open for the season, for a coal cut when the weather above was wrong could bring the roof down on the cutters, and the watchers had died more than once proving it. Beitris took her up to the nearest cairn herself, on a morning cold enough to whiten their breath, and taught her to tell a cloud that meant rain from a cloud that meant only wind, a distinction Beathag never afterward managed with the old woman's certainty, though she learned to fake it well enough that no one below the cairn could tell the difference.

Of the galleries themselves she was told less, and told it slowly, a little at a time, as though the keepers were feeling out how much a stranger's mind could safely hold before it began to want too much. She went down once, in her second month, on Aodh Crom's arm, into a low black gallery lit by nothing but the lamp he carried, and heard the coal answer his pick with a sound she never found the word for afterward, a note somewhere between a knock and a sigh. He did not let her cut it herself. "The hand that holds the flame does not also cut the vein," he told her on the climb back up into daylight, sounding, for the only time she ever heard him, almost apologetic. "That was decided before either of us was born, and it will not be undecided for us."

He named the galleries for her one by one as they went, in a low even voice pitched for the dark rather than for teaching, as though he had said the names so many times to himself that saying them to another person cost him nothing extra: the first gallery, the low gallery, the gallery under the stone that fell, the gallery of the cold spring. She asked him once why none of them bore a person's name, as she had heard was the custom in the eastern hills, and he was a long while answering, long enough that she thought he had not heard the question over the drip of water somewhere ahead of them in the dark. "A gallery outlives the man who cut it," he said at last. "Name it for him and you've promised the mountain will remember a debt it never agreed to. Better it owes nothing to anybody, the same as the flame." She did not fully understand him that day. She came, over the years that followed, to think it the truest thing he ever told her, and to repeat it, in her own turn, to keepers who asked her the same question in the same dark.

That first winter taught her, too, what it was to be looked at rather than to look. Word of her went down the valley faster than she herself ever travelled, so that she came, in households she had never visited and among people she had never met, to be a figure fixed and finished in other people's mouths before she had finished becoming anything in her own eyes: the girl of no blood who had said nothing at the choosing, who fed the flame Brìde's fire and answered to Crom for the coal, who was already, by the second winter, being credited with a wisdom she did not feel she owned and a coldness she did not feel she deserved. She minded it less as the years went on, since a face seen only from a distance will always be simplified into whatever the distance requires, and she came to understand that the valley's Beathag Bhàn and her own were never going to be quite the same woman, and stopped trying to correct the gap between them.

Nine households stood about the mountain's foot, one for each of the old slab's districts, and it was settled among the keepers, in a summer of arguing she was not asked to sit through, that the tending of the flame through each season would pass among the nine in turn, a son or a daughter of each house sent up to sit with her and learn what she herself was only just learning. There was more argument in the settling of it than the keepers ever told her outright, though she pieced enough together over the years from things half said: two of the nine districts had wanted the office kept wholly among themselves, on the grounds that their people had sat nearest the old slab the longest, and had been outvoted by the other seven, who pointed out, not gently, that the whole reason for choosing a girl of no blood at all had been to keep exactly that kind of claim from ever taking root again. The two districts did not forget the vote, and their grudge over it outlasted every one of the keepers who had cast it, though it never once, in Beathag's own years, rose to more than a cold word at a gathering.

The first of the nine to come up the path was a broad quiet boy named Cailean, from the northern district, on the twenty-seventh of an t-Iuchar in the tenth year, who arrived expecting, he admitted to her much later, a shrine rather than a cold hut with one girl in it barely older than himself, and adjusted his expectations within the first evening. He turned out a poor hand at the fire and a good one at silence, which she came, over the season, to value rather more. After him came a sharp-tongued girl of the western district, then a widower's son who cried the first three nights and never once afterward, then in their turns the rest of the nine, each carrying up whatever their own household's gossip had made of the Hall before they arrived, and each, by the season's end, carrying something truer back down.

More households claimed some thread of blood back to one or another of the nine than nine hearths could ever truly have bred, and the keepers learned to let such claims stand unchallenged, since nothing was gained by disputing a thing that cost nothing to grant. In her own years the nine were still few enough, and near enough, that she knew every face that came up the path by name before it reached the top.

## III. Of Niall Òg's Gift

He came up alone, without the household guard that most heirs, even the least of them, thought beneath their dignity to travel without, in the twelfth year, on a morning when the mist had not yet lifted off the lower slope and the mountain above the mist looked, from where she stood, like an island in a grey sea. She knew him before he reached her only because Beitris, standing beside her, went very still and said his name under her breath as though it might carry uphill ahead of him and give warning.

Niall Òg was old even among the old. He had stood among the heirs at the fall of the king and again at the cutting of the cord, though he held no share of his own and never would, and he carried his years the way some carried a limp, lightly enough that a stranger might not notice it until he moved wrong, and then would notice nothing else. What she noticed first, coming up out of the mist toward her, was that he was smiling, and that the smile did not look practised, which she had been told to expect of any heir who troubled himself to climb this far.

He had brought a cup of worked silver, small enough to sit in one palm, its rim beaten into a pattern of running deer. She had never in her life held a worked thing so fine. "For the hut," he said, holding it out, "since a girl who feeds a fire for the whole valley ought to have one cup that is only hers."

She looked at it a long moment before she answered him, long enough that Beitris shifted beside her, and she understood, looking at the cup and at the man holding it, that whatever he meant by it, and she believed afterward that he meant it kindly and simply, the valley below would read it another way entirely: an heir's gift to the Keeper of the flame, silver into the hand that was sworn to hold nothing of any blood but its own, and by evening every household on the mountain would have heard some version of it, most of them wrong.

"I have no cup that is only mine," she said, "and I am not to have one. Whatever I hold, I hold for all of them." She nodded down toward the households scattered below the slope, and did not name the heirs standing further off in their good cloaks, though both of them understood she meant them as much as the crofters. "If I take a gift from your hand, I must take one from every hand in the valley before winter, or answer for why yours was different. I'd rather answer for nothing."

He did not press it, which she had also been warned an heir would do, at length and with wounded dignity. He turned the cup over once in his own hand, looking at the running deer as though seeing them for the first time, and put it back inside his cloak. "That's a harder rule than the one I swore," he said, "and I swore it standing in the mud with everyone watching, and you're keeping yours alone on a hill with nobody watching at all." He looked at her a moment longer, not unkindly. "I think you'll keep it longer than I keep mine."

He left the way he had come, down into the mist, and she never heard that he tried the like again, with her or with any other Keeper. The keepers of the slab, when the story reached them, said it was the first proof they had that the choosing had been rightly made, since a girl of no line, offered silver by one of the oldest blood on the island, had known without being told what the silver was for. Beathag herself gave it less weight than they did, whenever the tale was told back to her. "I was afraid of the talk," she told Beitris once, "more than I was strong. A stronger woman might have kept the cup and answered the talk down. I only wanted the talk not to start."

## IV. Of the Raising of Talla na Lasrach

The turf hut held the flame for eleven years, and it was never meant to hold it longer, but a hall is not raised quickly by nine households who must also mine their vein and mind their sheep and pay their measure of coal at the season's end, and the work went forward the way such work goes, in a week here and a week there between the harvest and the first snow, so that it was the fifteenth year before the last stone went into the wall.

They built it of dry stone below and turf above, on ground a little higher than the hut, where the wind could not so easily find the door. Aodh Crom, older now and slower on the slope than he had been at the choosing, walked the ground with Beathag before the first stone was set and marked with his stick where the one door should stand.

"Toward the vein," he said, "and none toward the sea." He would not say more when she asked him why, only that the sea had no claim on the coal and ought not to be given one by a door left open to it, and she let the matter rest there, as she had learned to let most things rest that the old keeper judged better left unexplained. In the years after, other keepers gave other reasons for the same rule, none of them the same as each other's, and none of them, so far as anyone could later discover, the reason Aodh Crom had actually meant.

The masons were the nine households' own sons and daughters, taught as they built, and the walls show it: straight enough where the ninth hearth's people laid them, and less straight where the second hearth's did, and the keepers who came after Beathag Bhàn learned to tell one household's work from another's by the set of a stone as surely as by a face. She herself carried water up the slope for the mortar through two whole summers, since the flame could not be left untended long enough for her to do heavier work, and her arms, she said once to Beitris, learned more of the Hall's building than her hands ever did.

It stood finished by the last month of the fifteenth year: one long room of dry stone under a turf roof, a hearth-pit at its centre lined with the same grey stone the old slab was cut from, and the one door facing east along the line the vein ran under the mountain. No window looked toward the sea, or toward anything else; what light the hall had came in at the door, or from the flame itself.

## V. Of the Two Who Did Not Speak

Before the roof was finished the valley kept its Samhain at the foot of the new walls, since the ground below the Hall, unlike every other ground on the island by then, belonged to no lord and could be walked on by every lord without any of them owing thanks to another for the standing room. Beathag watched the fires lit that night from the door of her hut, the last Samhain she would keep there, and saw among the crowds below two men she had not seen stand near each other since the choosing.

One was Ruairidh Glas, of the line of Ìomhar, who two years before had put a spear through Aonghas Bàn mac Mhurchaidh at the stones of Dùn thais over a boundary neither of them, in the end, had been sure which side of the line it stood, and had not gone back to that ground since. The other was Aonghas Bàn's brother, Eachann Ruadh, who had sworn over the body that the debt would be paid, and had spent the two years since finding no ground on which he was willing to be the first to speak and no ground on which Ruairidh Glas was willing to be found. Between the households sworn to each of them lay two years of raids and reprisals that the tellers had already begun to call Aimhreit nan Oighrean, the strife of the heirs, and would go on calling it for five centuries more before either side found reason to stop.

They stood, that Samhain, on opposite sides of the same fire below the unfinished Hall, close enough that either could have crossed to the other in a dozen steps, and neither did, and neither, so far as anyone standing near enough to hear could report, said one word to the other across the flames the whole night through. But they stood. Every other ground on the island that year would have put a spear between them before the fire had burned an hour, and this one did not, and both of them, whatever they felt about it, seemed to understand that this was the one ground on which they could not afford to be the man who broke it first.

Beathag watched them from the hut door a long while, and told Beitris afterward that it was the first time she understood what the flame was actually for. The mountain would have gone on giving its coal whether anyone tended a hearth over it or not; the ground the Hall stood on was another matter, ground that could hold two men in the same firelight who would have killed each other anywhere else on the island, and ask nothing of either of them but that they stand there and not draw.

## VI. Of the Flame Carried In

The carrying was done at midwinter, on the shortest and coldest day of the fifteenth year, with the last frost of the old hut's floor still white on the heather when Beathag lifted the flame from the hearth-stone where it had burned, unmoved, for eleven years, and carried it out into the grey morning in a covered lamp that Cailean's own hands had made for the crossing.

The nine households came up for it, and the keepers of the slab, Aodh Crom leaning now on two sticks where one had served him at the choosing, and a scattering of the valley's crofting families who had no particular right to be there and came anyway, since a flame does not move house every day and a person likes to have seen a thing once that she can tell of afterward. Sìne nan Snaidhm came up from the gathering-house below with her cords, as she came to every gathering that seemed to her worth a knot, and she tied one that morning into the long record she kept of such days, a plain unmarked length beside the tangled record of the Roinn, and told anyone who asked that it was the first knot she had ever tied for good news alone. No heir came. None had been asked, and none, that morning, seemed to feel the lack.

Beathag walked the short distance from the old hut to the new door slowly, shielding the lamp with her body against a wind that had dropped, for once, to nothing worth shielding against, out of what she admitted afterward was pure habit and a little vanity, since a Keeper who let her flame gutter on the one morning everyone was watching earned no other name for the rest of her days. She set the lamp down inside the door, on the grey stone hearth cut to match the old slab, and opened it, and the flame that had burned eleven years in a turf hut at the mountain's foot burned that evening for the first time under a roof built to hold it, with its one door facing east along the vein, and no door at all toward the sea.

Aodh Crom stood at the door through it all, leaning on his two sticks, and said nothing while the flame was set, though Beathag, glancing once at his face across the new hearth, saw a look there she had not seen at the choosing eleven years before, on the slope in the mud and the wind: the particular stillness of a man watching a thing he had built with his own hands begin to stand on its own. He had never told her the reason for the door, and that evening, watching the flame settle into its new stone as though it had always meant to end up there, she found she no longer much minded not knowing it.
