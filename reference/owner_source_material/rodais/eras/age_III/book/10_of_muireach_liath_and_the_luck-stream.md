# Of Muireach Liath and the Luck-Stream

*FE 45 – 68*

> *Bheir an fhèith an gual; bheir an t-sruth an t-àgh.*
> The vein gives the coal; the stream gives the luck.
>
> — said of Muireach Liath's ruling on Allt an Àigh

## I. Of the Basket Once a Season

Muireach Liath had held the flame a year before he asked the nine hearths for anything, and what he asked for, when he asked it, was so small a thing that Beitris laughed when she heard it, and then stopped laughing, because he had not meant it as a jest.

"A basket," he said. "One to a household. Filled once a season, and not before."

He had watched, in that first year, what his aunt Beathag had watched for forty and never fixed: that a household near the mouth of a gallery sent its baskets down for coal whenever the weather turned, and a household two valleys off sent a boy with a creel only when the cold had already got into the old people's hands, and that the households near the mouth thought themselves no more favoured than anyone, because they had never once had cause to think about it at all. Muireach had grown up the son of no great house, in the fourth hearth, and he had carried a creel himself as a boy on more than one bad morning when the family nearer the gallery had already had theirs filled twice. He did not put it to the hearths as a grievance. He put it to them as a measure, the way Catrìona Mhòr's people had once measured a knot to a hearth, and let them argue over the size of the basket until they had argued themselves into agreeing on one, a lidded thing of plaited heather that a strong arm could carry full and an old woman could carry a quarter full, so that no basket asked more of a household than the household had to give, and none took more than its neighbour's.

There was grumbling. A household with six mouths said the same basket that fed six ought not to feed a widow and her one grandson at the same measure, and Muireach heard that out and answered that the basket measured coal, and a widow's hearth burned the same whether one sat by it or six. The grumbling thinned within the season and did not return. The weavers of the third hearth took the making of the baskets on themselves, since the weave wanted a particular closeness of plait to hold its shape under a full load, and it became, before Muireach had held the flame five years, a thing a household was almost proud to be known for, that its basket had lasted three winters unmended while a neighbour's had gone soft at the rim after one. Baskets of that weave are made at Dùn ìseal still, long after the cup took the measure from them, and are made now for no purpose the weavers themselves could tell you but the making.

Beitris, who had kept the slab since before the Binding and buried a Keeper, said of Muireach in those years that he ruled the way water finds a channel: patient, going round whatever stood in the way so quietly that the thing in the way did not notice it had been gone round until the water was already past. It was not entirely meant as praise. She had known keepers who pushed, in her time, and one or two who had needed to. But she said it to his face once, in the third year, sitting by the hut's fire with her hands gone stiff from thirty winters of carrying water up the slope, and Muireach only turned the question back on her, saying little, as he did most things.

"Would you rather I pushed?"

"I'd rather you knew when the water can't go round," she said, "because there'll come a stone in your channel one day that won't be gone round, however patient you are with it. I only hope you know the stone when you meet it."

He said nothing to that. In the third year of his keeping he did not yet think himself a man who had met such a stone, and Beitris, watching him carry the rush-lamp in through the door that faced the vein that evening, let the matter rest, being by then an old woman who had learned there was little use pressing a young one before the world had done it for her.

## II. Of the Stream Named for Luck

There was a stream that came down off the mountain's northern shoulder, no more than a stride wide at its narrowest and never dry even in the driest summers the valley had known, and it had no particular name in the years before Muireach's keeping beyond the plain one the households gave any small water, until a woman of Caol shean, whose boat had gone down twice in one season and come up both times with every man in it, said openly at a gathering that she had drunk of that stream on her way inland the week before each sinking, in what she swore was the right quarter of the moon, and had thought no more of it until the second boat came up whole as the first had. She was not believed at first, or not much, and the tellers say the households of the eastern share laughed at her more than they credited her, until a second man, and then a third, came forward with tales enough alike that the laughing thinned out.

By the time Muireach had held the flame ten years the stream had a name of its own, Allt an Àigh, the stream of the luck, and a season had not gone by without someone walking the long way up from the coast to drink of it under a moon they judged right, though no two households agreed entirely on which quarter that was, and the tellers of a later age would say only that the pilgrims never wholly ceased to come, without ever once agreeing among themselves on the reason.

The path they wore was walked mostly by night, since the right quarter more often than not fell in hours when a fisherman or a herder had no other business being anywhere but asleep, and it ran up from the river landing at Caol shean along the valley's eastern flank, worn deeper each year by feet that trusted the dark better than they trusted daylight questions about what exactly they were doing out in it. A boy of that landing, sent with his grandmother on the walk in his ninth year because she would not make it alone and would not be talked out of going, told Muireach himself, years later, that he had asked her on the way up what the stream would do for them, and she had told him she did not rightly know, only that her own mother had walked it and lost fewer nets in a lifetime of fishing than any other household on that stretch of coast, and that a woman did not need to know why a thing worked to know that it did.

Muireach heard a great many such tellings in those years, sitting outside the Hall on evenings when the pilgrims passed below on their way up, and he listened to all of them the same way, without correcting any and without endorsing any, a habit that more than one of the nine hearths took, in time, for wisdom, and that Beitris, who knew him better, took for what it more plainly was: a man keeping his own counsel until he had heard enough tellings to trust his own judgment over any single one of them.

## III. Of the Hall's Ruling

The stone under the mountain had never wanted for people willing to claim more of it than the Binding allowed them, and it was perhaps only a matter of years before someone thought to ask whether a stream that brought luck to those who drank of it might not, in some fashion, belong to the same reckoning as the vein it ran down from.

It was a keeper of the older reverence, one of the hearth-elders who had kept the ancestors' watch and the sìth's courtesy since before Beathag Bhàn had ever climbed the slope with a lit rush in her hand, who put the question to Muireach directly, on a morning when the mist still lay in the valley and the Hall's own hearth had not long been fed. She was old enough to remember Aodh Crom, or said she was, and she had walked up from the third hearth with a question she had turned over, by her own account, for the better part of a winter.

"The stream runs off the same mountain the vein runs under," she said. "If the vein is the Hall's to answer for, why not the stream?"

Muireach was quiet long enough that she thought, for a moment, that he meant not to answer her at all, which was a thing he had a name for among the nine hearths by then, though not always a kind one.

"Because the vein is stone that doesn't move," he said at last, "and coal that only comes out when a hand goes in and takes it. The stream moves on its own, the way weather moves on its own, and it was giving luck, if it gives it, long before any keeper stood on this mountain to answer for anything. I won't have the Hall claim what it never gave and can't take back."

"Then who does answer for it?"

"The same as answers for the wind, and for the dead under the ground it crosses. Not the vein. Not us."

It was said plainly, in a few sentences, on a morning no teller thought to mark as anything unusual while it was happening, and it was only afterward, looking back on it from years further on, that the households came to see it as the first time the Hall's own reckoning and the older reverence of the nine hearths, which up to then had run alongside each other without much need to define where one left off and the other began, had been made to stand apart and be named separately. The hearth-elders who kept the ancestors' watch took Muireach's ruling as the Hall admitting, plainly, that there were things on the mountain older than the vein and outside its claim, and said so with some satisfaction to anyone who would listen. The keepers of the slab, for their part, took it as proof that the Hall's flame answered only for what it had been sworn to answer for and reached for nothing beyond it, which they judged, on the whole, the more honest position, and said so with satisfaction of their own. Both sides have cited the same handful of sentences ever since as proof that Muireach Liath had been right, though they have never agreed on what, exactly, he had been right about.

Muireach himself, asked once late in his years whether he had known, standing in the mist that morning, that he was drawing a line the whole valley kept walking along in the years after, said only that he had not been thinking about lines at all, only about a stream and a question that deserved a true answer rather than a convenient one, and that anyone who found more in it than that was welcome to, so long as they did not ask him to agree with them.

## IV. Of the Carrying to Muileann ruadh

West of the mountain, where the ground broke into a shoulder of grey rock above a wooded glen, the warm springs of Muileann ruadh had run since before any teller could set a beginning to them, water that came up from some depth in the mountain's roots already heated, without fire and without any hand breaking the stone to make it so, and pooled in hollows a man could sit in to his chest.

The sick had been carried there in litters for longer than the Roinn itself, but it was in Muireach's years, when the roads down from the high valleys had worn a little smoother under the traffic of pilgrims going the other way to Allt an Àigh, that the carrying became a thing the households did as a matter of course rather than a last resort. A fevered child, a shoulder that would not set right, an old man whose joints had stiffened past what any winter fire could ease: the litters went up before dawn, so that whoever was carried could be bathed in the springs at first light, which the households held, without much argument among themselves, to be the hour the water worked best, though no one could say why that hour and not another.

Muireach let it stand without ruling on it at all, which was itself a kind of ruling, since more than one of the keepers of the slab thought the practice wanted the Hall's blessing, or its restraint, one or the other, and got neither. "The heat comes up from the mountain on its own," he told them, when they pressed him on it, "the same as the vein's coal sits in the mountain on its own until a hand goes in for it. No one breaks the stone for this water. I'll not break anything by speaking on it."

He went up himself once, in his fourth year, carrying nothing more than a walking-stick and a mind, by his own later account, to see what the households saw in the place before he let it run past him unquestioned as he had let the stream run past him. He found an old woman of the second hearth sitting waist-deep in the nearer pool with her granddaughter beside her, the girl's arm bound from wrist to elbow in a splint that had not set true the first time and was being given, the grandmother said, one more season in the warm water before anyone spoke of breaking it again to reset it. Muireach asked her, as he had asked no one about Allt an Àigh, whether she believed the water did the mending on its own, or only the rest that came with sitting in it a while each dawn.

"I believe my granddaughter's arm hurts her less after than before," the old woman said, "and I've stopped asking the water to explain itself, the same as I've stopped asking most things that help me to explain themselves. You might try it yourself, Keeper. It's restful, not knowing everything."

He did not answer that either, though Beitris, hearing the tale from him afterward, said it was the nearest thing to a joke she had ever heard him repeat against himself, and that she took it as a good sign in a man who did not, as a rule, find much in his own work to laugh at.

## V. Of Gille-Brìde in the High Valleys

Word came down from the high valleys in the ninth month of Muireach's ninth year that Gille-Brìde, of Somhairle's line, who had carried a coal of the First Flame home from the Binding and asked for nothing since, had died in his own bed among his own people, two hundred and twenty-four years old, in a winter that had come early to the passes west of Tobar dhìreach and stayed hard on the hill-folk who wintered there.

He had never come down to claim a share of anything in all the years since the Roinn, and the hearths at Dùn ìseal, who had grown used to seeing his sons and grandsons pass through on their way to trade wool for salt at the coast, had grown used also to thinking of Somhairle's line as a thing apart from the quarrelling of the nine great shares, a people who lived on the mountain's shoulders the way the sìth were said to live under its roots, wanting nothing from the valley below but leave to go on as they had always gone on.

It fell to his son Ceallach to keep the coal after him, on the same hearth it had burned on since the year it was carried up, and the tellers of the high valleys say that Ceallach did nothing on the day of his father's death that he had not already been doing for years, since Gille-Brìde had grown too frail, in his last seasons, to tend the fire himself, and Ceallach had been feeding it quietly on his behalf for longer than either of them had thought fit to remark on aloud. What changed, the day his father died, was only that Ceallach fed it now in his own name and not another's, and the hill-folk who came to sit by that hearth in the months after, as they had always come to sit by it, found the fire no different for the change, which was, Ceallach himself said once, the whole point of a coal kept so long: that it should not know, or seem to know, whose hand fed it.

Niall Òg, who by then had outlived more of his father's kin than he cared to count on any cord, heard the tidings at Dùn ìseal from a wool-trader come down off the passes, and went up himself, alone, without asking leave of anyone, to sit an evening by that hearth before the year turned. He had known Gille-Brìde only once, briefly, on the morning the Binding was sworn, a still man among heirs who none of them were still, and he had thought little of him since, in the way a man thinks little of a hill he has only once climbed. The fire he found there was small and steady, the household around it unhurried, Ceallach himself a grey-eyed man not unlike the father Niall dimly remembered, and Niall said afterward, to Muireach, that it was the first death of anyone of Ailean's whole scattered kin, in the fifty-odd years since the ford, that had left him with nothing at all to feel bitter about, only a stillness he did not much know what to do with.

"He never asked for anything," Niall said. "Not the flame, not a share, not a knot on Sìne's cord. I keep waiting to hear what it cost him, and nobody up there can tell me, because as far as they're concerned it cost him nothing at all."

Muireach, who had by then buried enough people of his own to have a settled answer for most things, offered none for that one, and Niall, who had not come up the mountain looking for an answer so much as somewhere to say the thing aloud, did not ask him for one twice.

## VI. Of the North Cut in Eleven

Murchadh Mòr, eldest of Ailean's living sons at the Roinn and the first of the seven hundred and twelve to be knotted on Sìne's cord, died at Baile Mòr mhòr in the eleventh year after Gille-Brìde, close on three thousand years old, the eldest of Ailean's children to the last, with a score of his brothers and sisters still living to hear of it.

Niall Òg went north for it, as he had gone north for little else in the years of calm that had settled over the great shares since the shares of shares were first counted, because Murchadh had been the first man's voice he remembered from the gathering-house floor, the door on a cold hinge that had given the line "Ailean's son" and dared anyone to argue it, and because Niall, whatever else he had become in nine hundred years of watching people die, had never entirely stopped being the boy who had grinned at Sìne nan Snaidhm and said he was owed twice.

He found Baile Mòr mhòr crowded with a different quarrel than the one he remembered from the Night of the Handful, though it wore the same shape. Murchadh had left eleven sons, none of them by the same mother, each with a household and a following and a grievance old enough to have outlasted the grievance's original cause, and none of them willing to let the north pass whole to any one of the eleven the way the old man himself had let no cousin near it in his lifetime. There was no cord for it this time, and no knot-keeper sent for, since the households of the north had long since stopped trusting a cord to settle anything a knife could settle faster; there were eleven knives instead, one to each hall of the north's chief holdings, and eleven nights of arguing over which hall went with which knife before a single cut was agreed on.

Niall stood at the edge of that hall on the night the cutting was finally done, an old man himself by the reckoning of anyone but the line he had been born into, and watched Murchadh's sons divide their father's great share the way he had once watched the nine eldest divide their father's kingdom, with lamps and cords and the same brittle courtesy men use when a wrong word might cost more than either side can afford. Lachlann Mòr, the eldest of the eleven, took the seat at Baile Mòr mhòr itself and the name that went with it, and was given it, in the end, with less argument than Niall had expected, since none of his ten brothers much wanted the burden of a hall their father had held so long that half the households under it remembered no other lord. Donnchadh Iar, the fourth of the eleven and the sharpest-tongued, took the western glens after an argument that ran two nights longer than any other, and made no secret, in the years after, that he judged the division unjust and Lachlann's seat too easily won.

"Eleven knives for one man's share," Niall said to Muireach, when he came back down to the mountain some weeks after, "where Ailean's whole kingdom took nine and a cord besides. I watched my grandfather's kingdom cut, and I've now watched my uncle's, and I'll tell you the only thing that's changed: nobody troubles now to call it holy."

"Does it need to be holy to hold?"

"I don't know yet," Niall said. "Ask me again once I've seen what eleven small lords do with a share too small to feed the quarrels their father used to feed on his own."

The north that came out of Baile Mòr mhòr's last division was a floor of counties smaller than any that had stood on the mountain's other flanks since the Roinn, each with its own hall and its own small lord and none of them, singly, able to raise so much as twenty spears, a fact the tellers already read as the plainest proof the long-lived blood was fading from that line faster than from any other, though at the time no one troubled to say it in so many words, being too occupied with the counting of glens to count anything so far off as a generation not yet born.

## VII. Of the Cattle Taken at the Ford

It was in the third year after Murchadh's dividing that Eachann Ruadh, of the line that had shed the first blood at Dùn thais and never wholly laid the feud down since, rode with a dozen men across the boundary stones into the Red Share's northern grazing and drove off near forty head of cattle before the herders of that ground had done more than raise the alarm.

No one on either side called it the opening of a new quarrel, since the old one between Eachann's line and Ìomhar's had never closed enough to need reopening; it was, by every account the tellers give, simply the feud finding, after some fallow years, a fresh grievance to spend itself on, the boundary stones at Dùn thais having stood long enough by then that a generation had grown up on each side of them who knew the stones only as a line to be tested rather than a wound to be remembered. The Red Share's steward sent word to the Hall asking whether the cattle, once recovered, might be driven onto the Hall's own ground for safekeeping until the matter was settled, on the reasoning that no feud had ever been permitted to follow a man onto that ground since the days of Aonghas Bàn's death, and Muireach refused him as plainly as he had refused the hearth-elder over the stream.

"The Hall's ground keeps a man safe from a knife," he said. "It was never sworn to keep cattle safe from a drover. Settle it the way the shares of shares are settled, or don't settle it at all, but don't ask me to make holy ground of every quarrel that finds its way to my door."

The cattle were never fully recovered, and the two lines went on, in the years after, much as they had gone on before, trading raids and grievances across a boundary that had cost one man his life already and would, before the long years finally ran out of that blood, cost more than one more. Niall Òg, hearing of it at Dùn ìseal, said only that he had stopped being surprised, some centuries back, by how little either side needed to remember why they were fighting so long as they still remembered that they were, and went back to whatever quieter business had occupied him before the news came, being by then a man who had learned, as Muireach had learned on his own mountain, which stones in the channel could be gone round and which could not, and who no longer troubled himself overmuch trying to move the ones that could not.
