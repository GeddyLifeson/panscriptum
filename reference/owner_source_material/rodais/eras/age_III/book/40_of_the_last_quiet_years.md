# Of the Last Quiet Years

*FE 2,864 – 5 am Faoilleach FE 2,960*

> *Chan eil bliadhna aig aon nach eil aig càch.*
> No one has years that the rest do not.
>
> — said at Dùn ìseal, of the roll's last count

## I. Of the Provincial Rolls

The clerks of the red hall kept, in these last generations of the Holy Age, a roll of the provinces that no single teller has ever set down whole, for the plain reason that no single year of it held anything a teller would trouble to carry from house to house; and yet it is out of that same roll, dry province by dry province, that the shape of these last quiet years is best read, since the red hall has, for most of that stretch, next to nothing else to say.

At Cnoc dhìreach in the east, on the carriers' road south of the springs, the weavers turned out in these years a heavy undyed cloth that the carriers came to wear on the high passes and nowhere else, a cloth coarse enough to turn a wind that finer weaving let through, {{date:II-0225}}. It is not, the roll is careful to say, the same Cnoc dhìreach that keeps the old tombs and the grave-keepers' silence; the two towns share a name and nothing further, and the roll tells them apart, where a stranger could not, by that one cloth. The dyers of Muileann bhàn, in the eastern hills not far off, learned in the same years to fix a pale blue out of a hill plant that grew nowhere lower, and the mountain country's cloth was dyed there ever after, {{date:II-0226}}; the Keepers alone, of everyone who wore the cloth, kept theirs undyed, and said so to any who asked, holding, as they had always held, that a flame kept for no one's blood needed no colour to mark whose it was.

A water-mill was raised in those same years at Muileann dhomhain, further into the hills, to grind the grain of the common granaries that the mountain districts had kept since Lachlann Bàn's own day, {{date:II-0227}}. The miller took one measure in sixteen for his own trade and no more, and the roll calls it fair, a word the roll uses of few enough millers in the whole of this book. It was at Àth chiar, not two valleys off, that the same rains which had watered Muileann bhàn's blue plant and Cnoc dhìreach's cloth-wool that generation came down instead as a flood, carrying the river clean over its ford and taking with it the carriers' shelter and six of the town's own people, {{date:II-0228}}. The common granary of that district stood on a rise the flood never reached, and it fed the town through the winter that followed on nothing but its own stores, so that the roll, having told the drowning plainly, tells the granary's answer just as plainly and leaves the two to stand together without further comment, as the roll's own custom was.

On the south-eastern coast the people of Seann Bhral began, in this same stretch, to lay oyster-beds in the shallows below their own town, a craft they held had come with their town's own name, though the clerks who keep the provincial roll set the claim down and mark it doubtful, {{date:II-0229}}, the roll's own custom being to doubt a claim it had not itself walked out and seen made good. Further down that coast Seann Vell grew, on the strength of its own fishing and the silver of Muileann chiar carried down the eastern road, into a town large enough to raise the first stone quay any harbour on the island had ever built, {{date:II-0230}}, its timber jetties having rotted out from under one too many seasons of the tide. On the southern cape the old beacon at Tobar dhearg was built up in stone in the same years, a tower now rather than a heap of driftwood, with its fire kept burning above the ground for the first time on any coast of Dia-thìr, {{date:II-0231}}; it burned seal-oil carried the whole length of the island from the northern rocks, the same trade that had made Ceann mhòr's name a generation before, and every sailor of that southern water learned to look for its light before he learned much else his trade required of him.

At Muileann naomh, on the same southern coast, a mill and a shrine came in these years to share one roof, the miller keeping the shrine's own handful of grain beside his millstones as faithfully as he kept his sixteenth measure, {{date:II-0232}}; the Keeper's cutter who came out to try it, as the Hall's cutters tried every rite's measure in its turn, found the handful kept and the flour good, and wrote as much in the roll and no more. Dùn thais, in the eastern hills, was walled in the same generation, and within its new wall the stag's people raised the first temple of their own ever built in stone rather than kept in a grove, {{date:II-0233}}, the elder school at Cnoc bhàn sending teachers down to see it properly served. Inis mhòr, on the northern coast, held by now a wool-fair at every shearing that drew buyers from the whole of that coast and some from Cathair dhearg itself, its silver weighed on the same weights Muileann chiar had sent to the northern road two generations before, {{date:II-0234}}; and further along that same coast, west of Seann Toll, the fishing town of Baile Mòr mhòr grew great enough to send its own member to the council at Dùn ìseal, a seat the Four Houses mislike to this day and gave, when they could no longer refuse it outright, at the foot of the hall rather than beside their own benches, {{date:II-0235}}. Seann Toll had by then become the home of the whole northern fishing fleet, its boatbuilders shaping the longest craft the island knew, built for a single day's run along the coast and never meant for anything further out, {{date:II-0236}}.

In the east a dry summer burned the pastures round Achadh shean and left the district's common granary empty by the following spring, {{date:II-0237}}; grain came up from Baile chaol and from Cuan ruadh under the shared-meal law that Lachlann Bàn and Raonaid Dhubh had made together three generations before, and the district repaid it, harvest by harvest, over the years after, the rolls of Baile chaol setting the repayment down measure by measure to the very last, with a clerk's own note added that no such debt in the whole of that roll had ever before been repaid in full, {{date:II-0238}}. The panthers crossed the southern road late that same autumn, and fewer of them than the carriers remembered crossing in any year before; the weather-watchers read it for a hard winter and were not wrong, {{date:II-0239}}, and the Keepers of that generation, whose own reckonings answered to the vein and not to the beasts of the south road, noted the crossing in their own roll without troubling to remark on it further. On the north-eastern coast a timber watch-tower went up at Baile ruadh against raiders out of the islands, manned by turns by the fisher-kin of Sliochd Raghnaill, the only tower built for war in the whole of this age, {{date:II-0240}}; and further south, at Cuan ruadh, the fishers gave boats and men to that same watch and were given, in return, a place on the council beside Sliochd Raghnaill's own seat, {{date:II-0241}}, a bargain both sides of it were still keeping at the close of the age.

At Inis chrom, on the western coast, the families of the drowned took up the custom of holding a feast on the shore each year with an empty place set for each of the lost, a custom that spread northward in these same years as far as Seann Warr, {{date:II-0242}}. Potters at Baile ghorm, on the south-western coast, learned to fire a hard red ware of their own clay in the same stretch, and their jars carried oil and grain the length of the western road, sherds of them lying, by a reeve's own count, on every waypoint-house site a rider had troubled to walk, {{date:II-0243}}; the same potters set up new kilns before long at Cnoc àrsaidh, where the clay ran better and the wood nearer, and the red ware of the western road was made there for the rest of the age, {{date:II-0244}}. On the southern coast, families crowded out of the harbours about Cathair dhearg settled at Cathair fhada and raised its long street along the ridge above the water, bringing with them a wood-watcher of their own rite and a copy of the laws, and the town grew, {{date:II-0245}}. At Cnoc naomh the people kept, in the old folk way, a shrine to the ancestors and to the sìth of their own hill, with no hawk in it, no wolf, no sting, no stag, {{date:II-0246}}; and the Hall's own list of rites, drawn up not long before in Seumas Bàn's day, named them among the keepers of the old reverence, a company by now as settled and as separate as any of the eight.

It is out of this same weave of small and settled things, and out of very little else, that the last generations of the Holy Age must be read, for the red hall in these years kept, for the most part, only the cup passing from hand to hand as cups on Dia-thìr had always passed, and troubled the roll with little more than that.

## II. Of Coinneach Bàn's Last Years

Coinneach Bàn, son of Anna Ruadh, who had copied the laws for every province in his own strong years and had them read at every waypoint house from the northern road to the southern cape, held the cup a long while after that work was done, growing old at Cathair dhearg the way a man grows old who has already done the one thing his own name will be kept for and finds himself, past it, with nothing further asked of him but to keep on living. He died in the ninety-first year of his own life, in his own bed at the red hall and not on any road, and the roll gives him no more than that, holding, as the roll had held of every rìgh since the long years went out of the line, that a death in a man's own bed at ninety-one years was a death like any other and owed no further telling than the fact of it.

His son, Coinneach Donn, took up the cup's keeping in the way sons of that house had always taken it up, without any meeting of the Four Houses troubling to be called, the cup by then passing so quietly from father to son at Cathair dhearg that a reeve of some outlying district might learn of one rìgh's death and the next rìgh's holding both from the same single line of a single roll, sent up months after the fact and answered with no more surprise than any other item of provincial business. Coinneach Donn held it thirty years and more, long enough that the carriers' road-guards of his own generation knew no other name to give the red hall, and long enough, too, that when he died in his turn, in the eighty-seventh year of his own life, it seemed to more than one reeve that the cup's passing that year ought to be as quiet as every passing before it had been.

It was not so quiet, this once, for Coinneach Donn left, besides his own daughter Mòr, a younger brother, Tormod Donn, who had held no cup of his own and wanted, now that his elder brother's line stood at a girl not yet twenty-three years old, to hold this one.

## III. Of Mòr's Claim Against Her Uncle

Mòr nic Coinnich had grown up, by her own later telling, expecting nothing of the red hall beyond the ordinary daughter's portion of a house that already held the cup, her father having never once, in the whole of her girlhood, spoken of the succession as a thing that might be contested rather than simply continued; and it was a hard schooling to her, in the first winter after his death, to learn from her own household's own steward that her father's brother had gone to the Four Houses at Dùn ìseal with a claim of his own, arguing before them that a girl of not yet twenty-three had no more right to a cup her own line had held three generations than any other daughter of any other house on the island, and that a man grown, of the same blood and twice her years, stood the plainer heir.

The Four Houses heard Tormod Donn's claim in the same hall where they had heard Gilleasbuig Mòr's claim and old Oighrig's line's claims before him, four centuries and more back, and found themselves, as their own predecessors had found themselves then, arguing a question the roll had never troubled to settle in words because no one had ever before troubled to ask it in words: whether the cup passed by blood alone, or by blood and by the house's own custom together, and whether a woman's blood ran thinner in the reckoning than a man's when both stood equally close to the line. Sliochd Mhuirich's own bench, silent as it had been silent at every such reckoning since the shut hall of Oighrig's own day, gave no voice either way; the bench that had once sat for the clerks' line spoke for Mòr, holding that a house's custom, once kept three generations running without challenge, was a kind of blood in its own right; and the two remaining benches split evenly between them, so that the matter stood, at the winter's end, exactly where it had stood at the winter's beginning, decided by no vote the Four Houses could agree to call a decision.

It was Catrìona nic Mhuirich, Keeper of the flame in those years and the last of her office the Holy Age was to know, who ended it, though the ending was none of the Hall's own business by the Small-Burning Law's own letter, and Catrìona said as much to the Four Houses before she said anything else. She had known Coinneach Donn her own self, she told them, in the years before either of them held the office each now held, and she had known his daughter as a child brought once a year to see the flame kept, wide-eyed at a burning no larger than a man's two hands cupped together; and she had watched Tormod Donn, in the same years, come to the Hall himself not once, holding, by his own account to anyone who asked him, that a younger son's business lay in his own house's lands and not in a flame he would never be called on to answer for. A cup, she told the Four Houses, was no business of the Hall's to give. She had set it, all the same, in enough hands over enough years to have formed her own opinion of which hands a house's custom had shaped for the holding and which had not; and she said, plainly, that she would set it in Mòr's hands should the Four Houses ask her to, and in no one else's.

Tormod Donn yielded within the season, quietly, in his own house at Cuan ruadh rather than before the Four Houses, sending word by his own steward that he withdrew a claim he had never, in his own telling afterward, much wanted to press so far as it had gone, and had pressed it chiefly, he said, because no one else of his own house had troubled to ask him whether a girl not yet twenty-three could hold a cup her own father had held without any man standing behind her to hold it for her. It was, by the roll's own later reckoning, the last such claim the house of Tormod was ever to see contested, and the reason given for that, by more than one clerk who wrote of it after, was Mòr nic Coinnich's own long and plain holding of the cup, which left no younger brother of her own generation any reason to think a girl's hand unfit for it.

Catrìona nic Mhuirich set the stone cup in Mòr's hands at Dùn ìseal in the spring of her own twenty-fourth year, as the Keepers had set it in Gilleasbuig Mòr's hands before her and in every rìgh's hands since, {{date:II-0247}}, and it is told that Mòr, taking the cup, asked Catrìona only one question of her own, low enough that the Four Houses standing by did not hear it: whether her uncle's claim had been, in Catrìona's own honest judgement, a wicked thing or only a foolish one. Catrìona is said to have answered that she had known few enough claims in her own long keeping of the flame that were purely one or the other, and that Mòr would do better, holding the cup thirty years or more as Catrìona judged she might, to spend her own reckoning on the cup's proper keeping than on her uncle's proper punishing; and Mòr took the answer, by every account that has come down of that day, for the whole of the counsel it plainly was, and never once, in the years after, brought any charge against her uncle beyond the one the Four Houses had already let lapse of their own accord.

## IV. Of Mòr's Reign

Mòr nic Coinnich wed, within two years of taking the cup, a man of Sliochd Raghnaill named Dòmhnall Dubh, whose own kin had kept the watch-tower at Baile ruadh and given boats to the coast's own defence in her father's time, and whose own reckoning of the sea, she is said to have told her household, sat easier beside her own reckoning of the cup than any lowland house's reckoning of grain and silver would have sat. Dòmhnall Dubh held no office at the red hall and asked none, keeping, through the whole of Mòr's reign, to the same trade his own house had always kept, boats and the watch of the north-eastern coast, and coming to Cathair dhearg only as a husband comes to his wife's own hall rather than as any rìgh's consort claiming a hand in her own business; and it is remembered of him, by the one or two tellers who troubled to remember him at all, chiefly that he never once, in twenty years at Cathair dhearg, learned to sit easy through a full council sitting of the eight companies by rite, finding the benches' own silence toward one another harder to bear, he is said to have said, than any storm his own boats had ever ridden out.

The reign these two years opened ran, for the better part of thirty years, very much as Coinneach Bàn's and Coinneach Donn's reigns before it had run: the cup at Cathair dhearg, the provinces sending their rolls up each year with little in them a teller would trouble to keep, the eight companies of the council sitting their own benches without disorder and without, so far as the rolls show, any of them troubling to speak across the gap between one bench and the next. Mòr herself is remembered as a rìgh who read every roll that came up to her rather than leaving the reading to a clerk, a habit her own steward is said to have found tiresome when she was young to the office and to have come, as his own service under her lengthened, to value above any other habit a rìgh had ever kept; and it was Mòr, reading the Hall's own roll one winter evening not many years before her death, who first had the count made that the roll's later keepers were afterward to set at its foot: one hundred and forty names of Keepers from Beathag Bhàn to Catrìona nic Mhuirich, and better than ninety names of rìghrean from Goraidh Mòr to her own, {{date:II-0248}}, a reckoning she is said to have sat with a long while by her own lamp before she troubled anyone else with it.

It was in that same reckoning, brought to her by a clerk who had made it chiefly to answer her own asking, that Mòr found the fact this book has made most of already, though she herself, by her steward's own account, made rather less of it at the time than they did: that no family on the whole of the island, her own included, now lived any longer than any other, the long years that had once carried Ìomhar Dearg to fourteen hundred summers and Eithne past three thousand having gone, generation by generation, clean out of every line that had ever carried them, so that a rìgh of Cathair dhearg in that year could expect, by the plain arithmetic the roll now showed, very much the same span of years as a potter of Baile ghorm or a weaver of Cnoc dhìreach. Mòr is said to have remarked to her steward only that it seemed to her a fitting enough thing, a cup meant from its first swearing for no one's blood having taken, in the end, the better part of three thousand years to make every blood on the island answer to the same measure; and the steward, setting the remark down afterward at Ruairidh Clèireach's own asking, added nothing of his own beside it, judging it needed none.

Dòmhnall Dubh died in the fiftieth year of his own life, at Baile ruadh among his own kin rather than at Cathair dhearg, and Mòr, who had kept the cup by then the better part of twenty years, is told to have gone north herself to see him buried beside the watch-tower his own house had kept three generations, a journey no rìgh of Cathair dhearg had troubled to make in living memory for a husband's burying rather than a province's own business. She held the cup three years more after that, alone in her own hall in a way she had not been alone in it since her uncle's claim was first brought against her, and it is of those three years that the least is told of the whole of her reign, the roll of that stretch holding nothing beyond the provinces' own small business and a single note, written in Ruairidh Clèireach's own hand, that the rìgh kept her own counsel more than she had kept it in any year before.

## V. Of Mòr's Death and Beathag's Crowning

Mòr nic Coinnich died on the one-and-twentieth day of an t-Iuchar, in the eight-and-fortieth year of her own life, at Cathair dhearg, in the same hall her father and her father's father had died in before her. She left two daughters, Beathag, not yet sixteen, and Mòrag, younger still, and no brother, no uncle, no cousin near enough in blood to raise the question her own reign had opened with; and it is told, by more than one hand that wrote of that year afterward, that the Four Houses, summoned in haste to Dùn ìseal within days of the rìgh's death, found themselves for the first time in living memory with a succession that raised no claim at all, Tormod Donn's own line having died with him some thirteen years before and left no son of an age or a temper to trouble a girl of sixteen for a cup her own mother and grandfather and great-grandfather had all three held before her.

Beathag was crowned at Cathair dhearg within the month, by her own house's word and the Four Houses' own assent, rather than by any journey to Dùn ìseal to have the stone cup set in her hands as her mother had had it set in hers; and it is this, of all the small departures the last years of the Holy Age made from the years before them, that the chroniclers who came after troubled most to remark on, some holding it a plain matter of a girl of sixteen being crowned in haste at her own hall rather than made to journey the fifty-five miles to Dùn ìseal in the depth of a northern winter, and others holding it the first sign of a distance opening between the red hall's own crowning and the Hall's own flame that no teller of that year, watching it happen, yet had words to name. Catrìona nic Mhuirich, by her own great age near seventy years, sent word to Cathair dhearg that she stood ready to travel to Dùn ìseal and receive the young rìgh there whenever the roads and the season allowed it, as the Hall's own custom asked; and it is recorded, without further comment from the roll, that the journey was never made in Mòr's own daughter's first year, nor troubled over again in the years the roll of this age had left to keep.

It fell to Ruairidh Clèireach, clerk of the red hall these twenty years and more, to find, in the same weeks, that the skin on which the king-list and the Keepers' roll together had been kept since Coinneach Bàn's own day was filled to its last line with Mòr's own name and the count her own reading had asked for, and no room left on it for any name after hers. He began a new skin that same winter, ruling its first lines himself by lamplight at his own table rather than waiting on any clerk under him, and set at its head, in the same plain hand he had kept the old roll in for twenty years, the name of Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill, crowned rìgh at Cathair dhearg in the year of her mother's death; so that the old roll, closed that winter and laid away in the same chest that held every roll of the Hall before it, carries to this hour no name later than Mòr's, and it is Mòr's name, not her daughter's, that stands last of all the names the Holy Age was to write down.

## VI. Of the Winter Morning

Five o'clock of the morning, by the almanac the red hall kept, on a day of Faoilleach in the year the roll had by then run to two thousand nine hundred and threescore, found the young rìgh Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill down at the harbour below her own hall at Cathair dhearg, her younger sister Mòrag beside her, both of them come down in the dark and the cold ahead of any steward's asking to see the last of the season's boats brought in before the weather the watchers had promised broke over the coast in earnest.

The boats came in by ones and twos through the last of the dark, the crews calling to each other across the water in the plain short words men keep for a cold morning's work, and were made fast along the same stone quay Seann Vell's own masons had raised the pattern for a hundred years before and every southern harbour since had copied; and Beathag stood at the harbour's edge in a cloak too thin for the hour, by her sister's own later telling, and counted the boats in against some tally of her own that she never troubled to share with anyone standing near her, whether it was the whole season's fleet she was counting or only the boats she had watched go out the evening before.

Mòrag, younger by four years and not yet crowned with any business of her own beyond a girl's ordinary place at her sister's side, asked her, standing there in the cold with the last boat still some way off the quay, whether she was afraid of the storm the watchers had promised; and Beathag answered, after a while long enough that Mòrag is said to have thought she would not answer at all, that she was not afraid of a storm that every fisherman on that coast had ridden out a hundred times before her, but that she found herself thinking, more mornings than not since her own crowning, of the stone cup sitting on its threshold at Dùn ìseal, waiting on a journey neither she nor the old Keeper who had asked for it had yet troubled to make, and wondering whether a rìgh who had never held that cup in her own two hands could rightly call herself the last name of an age that had begun with heirs quarrelling over who might hold a flame no hand was ever meant to keep.

The last boat came in as the light began, thin and grey, over the water east of the harbour, and the crews went up to their own hearths with the night's catch, and the two sisters stood a while longer at the quay's edge with nothing further asked of either of them that morning beyond the ordinary business of a cold season's turning. The sea beyond the harbour mouth lay open and empty to the edge of what the morning's grey light let them see, as it had lain empty of any wished-for shore through every one of the two thousand nine hundred and threescore years the roll behind them had troubled to keep; and it is on that sea, and on that morning, and on the stone cup standing yet on its own threshold a day's hard ride to the north, that the last roll of the Holy Age closes, with no name written after Mòr's, and no shore in sight from any coast the island's own boats had ever thought worth the sailing to.
