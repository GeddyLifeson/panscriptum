# Of the Eleven Names and Tormod the Writer

*FE 1,123 – 1,320*

> *Chan eil dà chuimhne aig dithis air aon oidhche.*
> No two people keep one night the same way in memory.
>
> — said of the reciters of Dùn dhearg, after Tormod Scrìobhaiche

## I. Of the Mill on the Northern Road

A mill was raised at Muileann shean in the year after Eithne died, on the northern road where the carriers' baskets came down off the pass heavy with grain and went back up light. It was built for no grander purpose than to save every household along that stretch of road the day's walk to the next stone, and it did that well enough that a town grew round it inside two generations, the way a town will grow round anything that saves a day's walk often enough. The miller paid the Hall in grain for his stone and his water-right, and paid the rìgh's toll in flour, and between the two payments he was left, most years, enough to feed his own house and complain that he was left nothing at all, which the carriers who stopped there took as a sign of a well-run mill rather than a poor one, since a miller who is not complaining is a miller counting a loss he has not yet found.

It was an unremarkable town in an unremarkable stretch of years, and the years themselves, for once, had little in them worth a teller's breath. Catrìona Ghlic, who had buried Iain Garbh in his own hall floor and mended what she could of the breach his war had opened, died at Dùn dhearg an old woman by the ordinary count and a young one by the count her blood had once kept, a hundred and twenty-two years old, which would have seemed to Ìomhar Dearg, had he lived to hear it, no age at all for a ruler of his line. Her son took the hall at ninety-two and held it eighteen years and died at a hundred and ten, and after him came another, and after that one another, and the king-list, when at last someone thought to make one, gave eleven names for the hall at Dùn dhearg between Catrìona Ghlic and the queen who came after them, and nothing more than the names and the order they sat in.

It is not that nothing happened in those years. Roads were kept, tolls were gathered, a harbour wall went up somewhere and a granary went up somewhere else, and every one of the eleven, no doubt, believed his own reign the equal in incident of any that the tellers still told whole. But the reciters who carried the hall's memory from one generation to the next carried it the way a river carries silt, taking up what a flood brings down and letting the rest settle unseen, and by the time anyone asked them for those years entire they had already settled into eleven names and a rough count of the winters each ruler held, and no reciter living could give back what had never been asked of them in time.

The Hall on the mountain kept its own roll for the same years, spoken, not written, and it gave five Keepers where the hall at Dùn dhearg gave eleven rulers, which any teller of the Fire will tell you is no more than the ordinary difference between a mountain's years, which are long, and a lowland's, which are not.

Somewhere in the middle of those eleven reigns, in a year no reciter troubled to fix more closely than "before Ealasaid Chiar and some while after Catrìona Ghlic," the last ruler of the house of Ìomhar and Goraidh who carried any of the long years in his blood died at a hundred years old, which had once, in his grandmother's grandmother's day, been a boy's age and was now, without anyone marking the day it happened, an old man's. The long years had been going out of that line since the ford at Àth na Fala, thinning a little in every child the way a dye thins with every washing of the cloth, and by the time the thinning reached its end there was no one left who remembered the blood as it had been, and so no one to mourn it going. The rulers after him lived and died at the pace of any household on the island, and the numbering of their years, wherever it is set down, reads no differently from the roll of any carrier's family kept beside it.

Of the eleven themselves, the roll, once it was made, gave little beyond their names and the years they sat, and this book, which owes the roll its facts, follows it in that restraint. No one, in their own day, thought a written hand would ever be asked to remember them, and by the time a written hand was, the deeds were gone and only the names were left standing, the way boundary stones stand long after the fence between them has rotted into the ground.

## II. Of Tormod mac Iain

Tormod was born at Dùn dhearg in the reign of the tenth of the eleven, a younger son of the house of Tormod, which had held no great office in the hall for three generations and was not expected, by anyone in it, to hold one again soon. His father Iain kept a modest stretch of the granaries on the crown's account, an honest post and a dull one, and Tormod grew up among grain-tallies and the smell of dust and mice, learning to count sacks by the age of six and to hate counting them by the age of eight, a hatred that would have surprised, later in his life, everyone who came to know him only as a man bent over a roll with a stylus in his hand.

He was fifteen, by his own later reckoning, when a stag-teacher passed through Dùn dhearg on his way east and stayed the night in his father's granary-loft, out of the rain, and told the household over supper of the letters kept at Muileann chiar, marks that could hold a teaching in a line cut on hide or bark without a teacher standing over it to keep the teaching straight. Tormod had never in his life heard of a thing that could remember without a person to do the remembering, and it struck him, he said afterward more than once, the way lightning is said to strike a man who has spent his whole life indoors and steps out at the wrong hour: a thing that happened to him and left him changed, whether he had chosen it or not.

His father thought little of it, a boy's fancy caught by a traveller's tale, and would likely have let it pass had the boy not gone on asking after it, at meals and in the fields and once, to his father's lasting embarrassment, in front of the granary-reeve who had come to check the tallies. In the end simple arithmetic sent Tormod east: a younger son of a house with no office to give him was a mouth the granary could feed more cheaply at Muileann chiar, learning a trade no one else in Dùn dhearg troubled to learn, than kept idle at home waiting for a post that would never come. His father called it practical sense and told the neighbours so. Tormod, walking the northern road with a carrier's basket-string for company and his own feet blistering inside a fortnight, called it, privately, the first stroke of luck his life had given him, and never after changed his mind on the matter.

## III. Of the Stag-Letters at Muileann chiar

The teachers at Muileann chiar took him in without much ceremony, as they took in most who came asking, on the reckoning that a mind willing to sit through the tedium of the marks was worth more to them than a mind merely quick, and tedium there was: weeks of nothing but the shapes themselves, the strokes for breath and the strokes for stop, drilled on a slate until the hand knew them without the eye's help, before any teacher would let a pupil near a real telling. Tormod was neither the quickest of that season's intake nor the slowest, but he was, by his teachers' own account, the one who complained least at the drilling, which they took for the better sign, and rightly.

He wrote his first whole leaf in the second summer, a tally of a household's grain-debt over three seasons, copied from a spoken account an old woman gave him twice over to be sure he had it right, and it survived, folded small and much handled, among the papers of his own house long after his death, the oldest thing in his own hand that the world still has. He kept it, his teachers said, tucked inside his shirt for a full year after the writing of it, the way a young cutter is said to keep the first coal his own hand brings up out of the vein, for the plain fact of having made a thing that had not existed the day before.

The better part of four years he stayed at Muileann chiar, longer than most who came only to learn the marks and go home to some clerk's post that wanted them and nothing more, and read what the teachers there kept, which was mostly grain-tallies and toll-counts and the odd letter between one waypoint house and another, and he found, reading it, that he wanted more than tallies: he wanted, though he could not have said so plainly even to himself at nineteen, to write down a thing that would still be readable when every mouth that had ever spoken it was dead, the way the mountain's own coal, once cut and burned, gives its light to hands that never touched the vein it came from. It was a want with no post attached to it and no household waiting to pay for its satisfying, and he carried it home to Dùn dhearg unspoken, because a young man who says aloud that he means to outlast every reciter in the hall is a young man inviting every reciter in the hall to make sure he does not.

## IV. Of Ealasaid Chiar

He served forty years at Dùn dhearg as a clerk of the granaries, his father's trade after all, before the chance came that his four years at Muileann chiar had been, without his knowing it, preparing him for the whole while. Ealasaid Chiar took the hall in the year Tormod turned fifty-nine, the granddaughter of the ninth of the eleven, a woman who had grown up hearing the reciters give the roll of her own house in a different order at every telling and had come, by the time she was crowned, thoroughly out of patience with it.

She had heard, from her own granary-reeve, that there was a clerk in her stores who could set a thing down in marks and have it read back the same way twice, which struck her, she said afterward, as either the most useful thing she had ever heard of a clerk or the strangest, and she sent for him to find out which. Tormod came before her in his working clothes with flour still in the seams of them, an old man by then in everything but the eye, and she asked him, without preamble, whether he could write down the names of every ruler who had held the hall before her, in the right order, so that her own grandchildren need not learn it eleven different ways from eleven different mouths.

He told her, honestly, that he did not know, since no one had ever asked such a thing of the marks before; the marks had been made, at Muileann chiar, for grain and debts and the odd letter, not for the memory of a house. But he said he would try, and Ealasaid Chiar, who had learned by then to value a clerk who said "I will try" over one who promised more than he could give, told him to begin that week and gave him a room off her own hall to work in, with a standing order that no reeve was to call him back to the granaries while the roll was unfinished, which his father, had he lived to see it, would have found the strangest turn of all: that his son's idleness, once the family's shame, should end as the crown's own business.

## V. Of the Reciters

Tormod began, sensibly enough, with the reciters, the old men and women whose whole office in the hall was to carry the ruler's line in their heads and give it out at feasts and namings, generation folded into generation the way a story-teller folds a long tale for a short evening. He sat with each of them in turn through the autumn and winter, asking the same questions of each and writing down what each one gave him, and it was in this work, more than in any drilling at Muileann chiar, that he learned the true use of the marks: to hold still, on a leaf, exactly what one mouth had said on one day, so that it could afterward be set beside what another mouth had said and the difference between them looked at plainly instead of argued over in the air.

The differences, when he set them beside one another, troubled him more than he let Ealasaid Chiar see at first. Two reciters gave the ninth ruler's mother a different name. One gave the seventh reign nine years and another gave it eleven, and neither would yield the point, each citing a teacher long dead who had told it so and could not now be asked. A reciter from the southern households, brought up before Ealasaid Chiar on some other matter and questioned by Tormod almost as an afterthought, gave an order for three of the eleven that put none of them where the hall's own reciters put them, and defended it with as much certainty as any of the others had defended theirs.

"They cannot all be remembering the same thing," Tormod said to Ealasaid Chiar, the first time he brought her his doubts rather than his progress, "and yet each of them is certain."

"Then which is right?" she asked him.

"I do not know," he said, "and I have come to think that not knowing may be the truest thing I can write down."

She was silent a moment at that, turning it over the way a woman turns over a coin she is not sure is good silver. "Write down the doubt, then," she said at last, "where you have it, and the sure thing where you have that, and let the two stand together on the leaf. A roll that hides its own uncertainty is a roll that will be believed too well by people who have no way of knowing better, and I would rather my grandchildren doubted rightly than believed wrongly."

It was not, in the end, an order many clerks after him chose to follow, since a roll with gaps in it looks, to most eyes, less finished than one without; but Tormod took her at her word, and in three places where his reciters' account disagreed with what he later learned the Hall's own roll gave, he set both readings down side by side and chose neither, a habit that earned him, among the few scholars who ever came to read his leaves entire, more honour than any of the names he did manage to fix beyond doubt.

## VI. Of the Roll's Making

The writing took the better part of six years, since Tormod would not set a name down until he had heard it from more than one mouth, or marked plainly that he had not; and because Ealasaid Chiar, once the habit of asking had been started in her hall, found she wanted more than the eleven names that had first sent her to him. She had him go back further, into the reigns the earlier reciters still just remembered whole, Goraidh Mòr and the war of the roads and the peace under Mòr nic Dhùghaill's roof, setting down in the same careful hand what until then had lived only in the telling.

He worked at a low table by the window of the room she had given him, a room that had been a store for undyed wool before it was a clerk's room and still smelled faintly of lanolin on a damp day, and he kept, through all six years, the two-summer boy's habit of the first leaf he had ever written: he read each finished page over to whoever had given it to him, reciter or steward or Ealasaid Chiar herself, before he counted it done, so that no name went into the roll that its own source had not heard back and owned. It was slow work, and it made him few friends among the reciters, who did not care to be read back to like children learning a lesson. It made the roll, when it was finished, a thing no one at Dùn dhearg could afterward call a single clerk's fancy, since every name in it had been sworn to twice, once in the giving and once in the hearing.

The finished roll ran from Goraidh Mòr to Ealasaid Chiar herself, the eleven names set at last in an order Tormod judged, after weighing every reciter's account, the least contradicted rather than the most certain, with the three doubtful places marked as doubtful and left so. He brought it to Ealasaid Chiar on a winter evening with the wool-smell of the room still on his sleeves, an old man of sixty-nine with a stylus-callus on his first finger that would not soften now for the rest of his life, and set it in her two hands the way a steward sets a harvest tally before a lord, though what he had brought her weighed nothing at all and had cost him six years.

She read it through by her own fire without speaking, and when she had finished she said only, "Now no one at Dùn dhearg need learn it eleven different ways again," which was, from a queen not given to open praise, as much praise as Tormod Scrìobhaiche ever had for his life's whole work, and by every account he asked for none.

He died the following year, at seventy, in the same room off the hall where he had written the roll, with the finished leaves stacked beside his bed and a half-begun copy of the disputed reign still under his hand. Ealasaid Chiar had the room's wool-smell scrubbed out of it afterward and gave it a proper clerk's floor, and named it, in the hall's own speech, no more than "Tormod's room."

## VII. Of the Shamed Hall

Word of the written roll reached the Hall on the mountain the following spring, carried up by a carrier who had heard it told at a waypoint house and thought it worth the telling twice, and it reached the Keeper of that year, Fionnlagh Dall's line by four generations though the years had by then taken his sight as they had taken his forebear's, in words that stung more than the carrier had meant them to: that the rìgh's clerk had set down in marks a memory the Hall had only ever kept in the mouth.

The Keeper heard it out and said nothing for three days, which those who knew him took for a bad sign, since a blind man's silence has nowhere to look and so settles, when it settles at all, on whatever is nearest. On the fourth day he called the reciters of the Hall before him, the old men and women who kept the Keepers' line the way Dùn dhearg's reciters had once kept the rulers', and told them, in words the reciters afterward repeated more often than any other he ever spoke, that a Hall shamed by a rìgh's clerk into writing down what it should have written down of its own accord a century before had no one to blame for the shame but itself, and that he would not sit blind at the head of a Hall that let a lowland granary-clerk out-remember it.

The writing of the Hall's own roll fell, in the end, less to any one hand than to a company of hands, since no clerk among the Hall's own people had Tormod's years of practice at the marks; a young cutter who had spent a season at Muileann chiar himself was set to the actual writing, while the reciters gave their account and the Keeper, sightless, listened to every leaf read back to him twice before he let it stand, a slower and stranger method than Tormod's but one that suited a blind man's hall as well as sight had suited Tormod's own working. It began, as the Hall's spoken roll had always begun, with Beathag Bhàn, and it ran longer than Tormod's roll by several centuries, since the Hall's own line reached back past the eleven rulers and past Goraidh Mòr both, into years the rìgh's own reciters had never troubled to keep at all.

## VIII. Of the Cord Untied

It was in the last summer of that writing, in the seven hundred and twelfth year, near enough, since the cord of the heirs had been cut at the Roinn, that Cairistìona nan Snaidhm came down from her own house to the Hall with the cord carried before her on a cushion of undyed wool, the last of Sìne nan Snaidhm's line still living who could read a knot the old way, without letters, by the shape and the tightness and the place of it along the length.

She was an old woman herself by then, the knot-keeping long since fallen from any real use in the Hall's business, since the Hall had kept its accounts by tally-stick and now by the marks for longer than she had been alive; but the old cord itself had never been untied, seven hundred and twelve knots still holding the shape they had been tied in on the night of the handful, and no one at the Hall could read what they held save Cairistìona, and after Cairistìona no one at all.

She sat with the young scribe two full days, the cord across both their laps, and untied it knot by knot from the first end, naming aloud, for each knot, the heir it had been tied for and the share that heir had taken, while the scribe set each name down in the new marks as she gave it. It was slow work, slower even than Tormod's had been, since a knot untied cannot be tied again to check the reading, and Cairistìona would not have a knot loosed until she had named it twice to her own satisfaction, turning it under her fingers the way her mother had taught her mother's mother to turn them, feeling for the small differences of tightness that seven hundred years of handling had not quite smoothed away.

"My mother could read this cord blind," she told the scribe, on the second day, when her own eyes had begun to tire of the light in the low room, "and her mother before her, and neither of them ever wrote a single name of it down, because the cord did the remembering and a Snaidhm's fingers did the reading, and between the two of them nothing was ever lost in six hundred years. I am the last who can do both. After me the cord will still exist, coiled in whatever chest you set it in, but it will not be able to speak, because there will be no hand left that knows its language, only these marks of yours, which speak a different way and cannot be unmade back into a knot if anyone should ever doubt them."

The scribe, a young man with no reply ready for so plain a grief, said only that the marks would keep what she gave them for longer than any hand could keep a cord, hers or her daughter's or any daughter after.

"That may be true," Cairistìona said, "and it is no comfort at all," and went back to the untying.

When the last knot came free, the seven hundred and twelfth, tied for a claimant so far down the great division that no reciter of either roll had carried the name whole into living memory, she held the loose cord a while in both hands before she gave it over, feeling its slackness the way a person feels the absence of a weight they have carried so long they no longer notice carrying it. Then she coiled it herself, in the old way, the way her mother had taught her to coil it for storage between one Roinn business and the next, and laid it in the chest the Hall had made for it, and did not ask to see it again.

In the three places where the rìgh's roll and the Hall's roll disagreed, as Tormod had marked they would, no one at either hall in that season thought it worth another six years of arguing to settle which was right, and both readings were let stand side by side, the one habit of Tormod's that the Hall's own scribes had, without quite meaning to, taken up as their own.

In the year the last knot came free it was only a cord, coiled small, going into a chest built for it by a hall that had learned, in the space of two clerks' careful lifetimes, to trust marks on a leaf over the memory of any single mouth or any single pair of hands, however faithful either had been. The chest was set in the Hall's own gallery, and the lid was closed on it, and no one that season thought to open it again.

## IX. Of the Names That Are Left

Eleven names stand now on Tormod's roll where before there had been eleven shapes in a reciter's memory, some clearer than others, none of them fixed. Five names stand on the Hall's roll for the same span of years, and neither roll, for all the care its makers gave it, can now be checked against the mouths that first carried the memory, since every one of those mouths is generations in its grave. What is left is marks on a leaf and a coiled cord that no living hand can read, and a room off a hall that still smells, faintly, on a damp day, of the wool it once stored, though no one now remembers why a clerk's room should smell of wool at all.

It is a small thing, set beside a war fought over fish-bones or a bier made of a host's own spears, eleven names written where eleven shapes had stood before; but it is the thing this book has to set down of those years, since the years themselves, for all the width of them, chiefly held themselves and did not choose, in their own day, to be remembered further than that. The next hand that comes to the roll, wherever it comes from, will read Tormod's doubt in the three disputed places exactly as he left it, neither resolved nor hidden, and will know, from that alone, more of how the roll was made than either roll's makers thought, at the time, they were troubling to tell it.
