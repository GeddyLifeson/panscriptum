# Of Mòrag of the Roads

*FE 754 – 879*

## I. Of the Hall Left Standing Open

Goraidh Mòr had ruled at Dùn dhearg two hundred and thirteen years, from the granary door where the lowland households had first called him rìgh to the timber hall he raised over it with a granary at each corner, and when he died in his sleep in the seven hundred and fifty-third year of the Fire the hall stood a night and a day with its doors open, as the custom of the household was, before anyone was let in to take his chair.

Mòrag nic Ghoraidh was a hundred and fourteen years old that night, and she had never once sat in it. She had stood beside it at feasts and at judgements, and had leaned on its arm once as a girl until her father lifted her off by the collar, laughing, and told her a rìgh's chair was not a thing to lean on; and now she stood in the doorway in the grey of the morning with the household behind her, and looked at the empty seat, and did not go in.

It was Tormod of the clerks' line, her father's steward, who broke the silence, because someone had to and no one else would. He said that the toll-sticks were a season behind in the counting, and that the eastern granary wanted its roof seen to before the rains, and that three waypoint-keepers had sent word asking whether the road-toll still stood now the old rìgh was gone, as if a toll were a thing that might have died with a man.

"Tell them it stands," said Mòrag, "and tell them I'll be asking after it myself before the year is out."

She went in and sat down. The chair was warm from nothing; it had stood empty a night and a day, as the custom asked, and the wood was cold under her, and she sat in it a while without speaking, getting the feel of a thing she had watched other people sit in her whole life. Then she stood up again and did not sit in it for the better part of a year, because she had said a thing in the doorway that she meant to keep, and there is no keeping a road-toll from a chair.

She sent for a staff first. Not a sceptre; her father's steward brought her one of those, cut and banded for the purpose, and she looked at it and sent it back to the woodworker with word that she wanted something a woman could lean her whole weight on across three hundred miles, and that if it looked well in a hall that was no fault of hers. What came back was a plain thing of black-thorn, taller than she was, worn smooth where a hand would fall on it before ever her own hand had touched it, because the woodworker had walked with it himself a week to be sure of the balance. She kept that staff the rest of her life, and it is the one thing of hers that is remembered by its own shape and not only by its name.

The roads her father had left her were his father's roads before that: cart-tracks widened where a toll-house wanted widening and left as they were everywhere else, fords that a wet spring made impassable for a week at a stretch, one stone bridge at the western crossing that had not been touched since the masons of an earlier age set the last course. The waypoint-keepers wrote to Dùn dhearg twice a year with their tallies and their complaints, and Goraidh Mòr had answered the tallies and let the complaints lie, being a builder of halls and not, in his heart, a mender of roads. His daughter read three years of those letters in a week, sitting up past the lamp's good hours with Tormod's ledgers stacked at her knee, and in the spring she called for her staff and a horse and went out to see the roads for herself, and she did not come back to sit in the chair again until the autumn.

## II. Of the Broken Stretch above the Ford

The first stretch she walked was the road up from the ford at Dùn dhearg, because it was the one nearest home and because a waypoint-keeper there had written three years running that the spring floods took the same twenty yards of it every year and that the carts went round it through a farmer's barley until the farmer complained, and then round it through his neighbour's until that one complained too.

She found it exactly as written. The road dropped to the ford between two low banks that the water undercut every spring, so that by autumn there was a raw scar of red earth on either side and a soft place in the middle that swallowed a cart-wheel to the hub. She stood on the near bank in her travelling boots with the staff planted beside her and watched a laden cart go through it while she was there, watched the carrier swear and the ox strain and the whole load lean sideways before it came free, and she said nothing until the cart was past and the carrier had seen who was watching him.

"How long has it done that?" she said.

"Since before my father's father, my lady," said the carrier, "and the toll-house has written every year, and every year they send word back that it'll be seen to."

"It'll be seen to," said Mòrag, "because I'm standing in it."

She had the waypoint-keeper of that stretch bring her every man and ox-team within a day's walk, and she paid them out of the toll itself, which no one had thought to do before, since the toll went to Dùn dhearg and the roads were left to whatever labour a household owed its lord besides. In eleven days they had cut back both banks, laid a bed of stone under the soft place, and raised the road a hand's breadth above the water on either side of the ford. She stood in the finished stretch herself when the first loaded cart went through it dry-wheeled, and said nothing at all that time, only watched it go, and then walked on to the next bad stretch, which a letter three years old had also told her about and which no one had touched.

Not every waypoint-keeper welcomed her. At a stretch two days east of the ford, where the toll-house had gone unanswered longest of all, the keeper met her at his own door with his arms folded and said that the road had been bad since his grandfather's time and would be bad after his own grandchildren, and that a rìgh who came once with a staff and a season's labourers would be forgotten before the next flood took the bank again, as every rìgh before her had been forgotten. Mòrag heard him out, and then had her road-guards break open his own toll-chest before him, in his own doorway, and counted the silver herself onto a cloth on the ground: enough, by her own reckoning, to have paid for the mending three times over in the years he had let it lie. She did not shame him further than the counting. She left half the silver where it lay, for the household's own use, and took the other half for the work, and told him that she would be back in four years to see whether the bank had held, and that if it had not, the next counting would be done at Dùn dhearg and not at his door. It held. He wrote to her twice a year after that, of his own will, whether the road wanted mending or not, and his letters are among the few from that stretch that the toll-rolls keep entire.

She walked the roads of the island that way for the better part of six years: every road a toll-house sat on, and every bad stretch the letters named, though there was no time in one life for every mile of every road besides. She had them mended in the order the letters had complained of them, oldest complaint first, a rule of her own devising that some of the older keepers called foolish, since a small hurt long borne got mended before a great one newly reported. It meant, all the same, that a waypoint-keeper who wrote and was ignored for ten years learned that writing to Dùn dhearg was no longer wasted breath. Some of the mending was small: a culvert cleared, a gate hung where a farmer's beasts had been getting onto the road and fouling it. Some of it was not small. She had a whole stretch of the northern road moved a quarter-mile east, off a slope that slid every winter, and the farmers whose barley the new stretch crossed were paid out of the toll for the ground, which had likewise not been done before, and which some of the older waypoint-keepers said was no way to run a road and would beggar the toll inside a generation. It did not.

## III. Of Niall Òg on the Cold Road

She met Niall Òg on the northern road in the seventh year of her reign, in a wet spring, on a stretch she had not yet reached and had no plan of reaching for another two years. He was sitting on a milestone with his cloak over his head against the rain, an old man by then past his sixteen hundredth year, small and bent and grey, with a stick of his own across his knees that he had never had cut for a purpose the way she had had hers cut, only picked up somewhere along some road forty years before and kept because it was a good length. She did not know him. He knew her at once, by the staff and by the four road-guards behind her, and by something in the way she stood looking at a rut in the road as if it had done her a personal injury.

"You'll be Goraidh's daughter," he said.

"I am," said Mòrag.

"You look at a bad road the way your grandfather's brother used to look at a bad debt," said Niall Òg, "and I knew him, so I say it kindly. Sit down out of the wet a while. There's room on the stone."

She sat, because a man of sixteen hundred years asking a rìgh of a hundred and twenty to sit is not easily refused, and because her feet were glad of it. He told her, without being asked, who he was, and she had heard the name from her father, who had heard it from his father, the way everyone on the island had heard the name Niall Òg by then, though fewer and fewer of them had ever sat beside him.

"I tied a knot once," he said, "at a door below Dùn ìseal, when the whole of what your grandfather's grandfather left was cut on a cord and laid on a floor for seven hundred and twelve of us to walk the length of and count. I found my own knot that night and sat by it a good while, the way a man does, looking for what he was owed. And do you know what I saw, sitting there?"

"No," said Mòrag.

"I saw that everyone else in the house was doing the same. Every one of the seven hundred and twelve of us walked that cord looking for his own knot, and having found it, stood up and went looking at everyone else's. I thought that a fine thing to have watched, at the time. I've had a long while since to think it a sadder one. We were all tied on the one cord and not one of us, that whole night, looked at the cord. Only at the knots."

Mòrag said nothing to that for a time, watching the rain come down the road ahead of them in grey sheets.

"And is that why you tell it to a woman mending a road?" she said at last.

"It is," said Niall Òg. "You're the first of the line I've met in a long life who looks at the cord and not the knot. A road doesn't ask whose share it crosses. It only asks to be walked on, by whoever's walking. Keep at that, and you'll have done something the seven hundred and twelve of us never managed among ourselves, and never will, being who we are."

He got up before she did, stiffly, with his stick, and went on down the road ahead of her household on foot, an old man alone in the wet, refusing the offer of a horse with a wave of his hand and a remark she did not catch over the rain. She never saw him again, though she heard of him twice more before her own death, both times at the funerals of people younger than himself, standing at the graveside with his stick, the last of the seven hundred and twelve who still came to such things.

## IV. Of the Golden House and the Sign Recut

She built the golden house in her ninth year, on the eastern road north of Seann Dunn, where the stage from the coast to the mountain had always been a hard day's carrying with no house to break it and a bad night's camping if the weather turned. It was not the first waypoint house on the island; there had been such houses since the carriers' roads themselves, built by whatever household held the ground and kept up out of what custom the ground's own people were owed. It was the first built at a rìgh's order and out of a rìgh's toll, and she meant it to be seen to be one, and had it built larger than the ground needed and roofed it with new-cut timber that stood pale and bright against the older grey houses about it, so that carriers coming up from the coast called it the golden house before the beams had so much as weathered, and the name held.

She opened it herself, on a night in the autumn, with every carrier who could be got word to in time asked to eat there free of the toll, and stood in the doorway of her own hall watching them come in wet and cold off the road and sit down to a fire and a full table that none of them had paid for, and she thought, watching it, that this was worth more of her father's granaries than anything he had built with them, and did not say so aloud, because he was three years dead and past hearing either the praise or the argument.

Five years after that she had the western crossing of the Abhainn dhomhain widened, where the old bridge below Cathair dhearg had stood narrow enough that two laden carts could not pass on it, and where every spring saw a shouting match on the span between a cart going up and a cart coming down, neither willing to back off first. The new stone went up wide enough for two, and when the masons came to the keystone she had them find, among the older stones taken down, the hearth-sign that Ailean Clachair had cut there in an age before her grandfather's grandfather was born, worn nearly smooth by the years and the weather, and she had it cut fresh into the new stone, in the same hand as near as a mason four generations removed from Ailean Clachair could match it. It is there yet. From that year the small fishing town at the bridge-foot, that had shrunk to almost nothing after the old wars and grown slowly back since, began to grow in earnest, for a wide bridge draws trade the way a narrow one turns it aside, and within Mòrag's own lifetime it had outgrown three of the towns around it, though no one troubled yet to give it any name grander than the ground it stood on had always borne.

## V. Of Dòmhnall, Who Could Not Walk

Her son had been born lame. He had not been born so; he came into the world with two sound legs and used them like any child until his sixteenth year, when a wheel went over him on the very road above the ford that his mother had mended in her first summer as rìgh, before he was old enough to remember it mended. He had been riding the back board of a loaded cart, as boys do against every warning, and the cart had struck the one stone in that whole stretch that Mòrag's masons had somehow left standing proud of the bed, and thrown him under the wheel that followed. The leg was set, and set again when it did not knit true, and in the end he walked with a stick for the rest of his life and could not do it far, and never rode a horse again without another man's hand at the bridle.

Mòrag never once, in the twenty years she outlived that day, ordered the stone taken up, though it was pointed out to her more than once that it still sat there. She had it left, and told no one why, though her own household guessed, and some of the older road-guards said among themselves that she went out of her way to walk past it whenever her road took her near, and stood by it a while before going on, as if it owed her an answer it never gave.

Dòmhnall could not do what his mother had done. He knew it before she did, and said so to her plainly, sitting in the golden house one evening in his thirtieth year with his bad leg stretched out before the fire, and told her that whatever she left him he would rule from a chair, and that she had better begin now teaching him how a chair could be made to reach as far as a staff had. She did not argue with him. That autumn he was carried the length of three road-stretches, in a covered chair on two men's shoulders, so that he should at least have seen with his own eyes the roads he could otherwise know only from a waypoint-keeper's letter, and before the year was out she named him at Dùn dhearg as her heir, an old woman by then and past her hundred and sixtieth, with the long years thinning fast in her line the way they had thinned in her father's and would thin further in her son's, so that where Ailean Mòr's own children had lived three thousand years and more, his great-great-granddaughter counted herself fortunate to see two hundred, and his great-great-grandson, sound of mind and short of leg, would not see even that.

## VI. Of the Last Circuit

She made her last circuit of the roads in her hundred and eighty-ninth year, against the advice of her household and her son both, who said a woman of that age had no business on a horse in the spring mud, and she told them that a road she had not walked in four years was a road she no longer knew, and that she would rather die on one of her own roads than in the chair she had spent a lifetime staying out of. She went the whole western circuit, from the wide crossing below Cathair dhearg round by the golden house and back, five weeks on the road with her staff worn now to a paler colour at the grip than anywhere else on its length, and she found, to her own satisfaction and to the surprise of the waypoint-keepers who had grown used to writing their complaints into a silence, that there was less to mend that year than in any year of her reign, and she said so at every waypoint house she stopped in, which the keepers took as high praise from a hard woman and repeated to their children after.

Four years later she died at Dùn dhearg, in the winter, of nothing anyone could name beyond her age, with her son beside her and the black-thorn staff standing in the corner of the room where she had had it set every night of her life since the woodworker gave it to her. She was a hundred and ninety-four years old. There were people living on the island by then, of her own kin among the waypoint-keepers' houses, who had never known a rìgh but her, and who found it hard to picture the roads with anyone else's name attached to their mending.

They laid her out in the hall her father had built, and the household came in as it had for him, and stood a night and a day before the doors were shut. Word went out on the roads themselves, carried by the same waypoint system she had built up out of a ten-year-old letter and a rutted ford, and it reached the far waypoint houses before the funeral could be held, so that carriers came in from three days' distance to stand outside the hall at Dùn dhearg, men and women who had eaten free at her opening feast or been paid out of her toll to cut back a riverbank, more of them than the household had thought to feed, and Dòmhnall, new in his chair and lame on his stick, had the golden house's own stores brought up to Dùn dhearg to feed them, which his mother would have counted, had she lived to see it, the rightest thing he ever did.

Niall Òg came, the last time anyone at Dùn dhearg saw him, older now than any living memory could measure him against, and stood at the grave a long while on his own stick, and when someone asked him, since he had known her, to say a word over her, he said only that he had told her once about a cord and seven hundred knots, and that she was the one heir of that whole gathering who had spent her life looking at the cord, and that the roads she had mended were the nearest thing the island had, after her, to that same cord laid honestly on the ground for anyone to walk. Then he went away down the road on foot, as he had come, and refused a horse again, and no one at Dùn dhearg saw him after that day, though word came, years later and from a great distance, that he was still living.

The black-thorn staff was laid across her feet on the bier, whole and unbroken after forty years of every road on the island, worn smooth from end to end by a single hand; and that is the last thing the tellers give of Mòrag nan Rathad, before they go on, as the roads themselves went on, into the reign of her son.

## VII. Of the Keepers of Ceann mhin and the Grain That Came First

Dòmhnall Crùbach ruled twenty years from the same hall, carried where his mother had walked, and where she had gone herself to every bad stretch of road, he sent the keepers of the waypoint houses in his stead, and trusted them to bring him an honest tally. Round the harbour town of Ceann mhin, where the road-toll came in heaviest off the eastern shipping, the keepers took to gathering it into their own hands within thirty years of his rule beginning and paying the hall what they judged a fair remainder, and their judgement, the rolls note without further comment, was generous to themselves in every year the rolls survive. Dòmhnall knew it, by report if not by his own eyes, and let it stand, for he could not walk to Ceann mhin to argue it and had no wish to send road-guards against men who kept the roads his mother had built running. It was a bargain no one wrote down as a bargain, and it held, more or less, for longer than his own reign.

In the west that same generation, settlers out from Cathair dhomhain took up the wet heath by the coast, where the ground was thin and the reed-beds thick with a small yellow scorpion the older shires thought a plague and the new settlers, within a few years of living among them, had come to think a kind of luck. A woman of that settlement, stung on the hand at a trade of cattle for salt fish, held her swelling fingers up before the other party rather than draw back from the bargain, and finished the trade with her hand still burning, saying that a hurt taken in the open was worth more than a promise given behind a closed door. Others took up the way of it after her, keeping a scorpion's sting, dried in a box, at every trading and swearing an oath on the pain of it rather than on a name or a god, and the settlement that grew up there took the name Baile àrsaidh, and its neighbours, who swore on hearth-fire or on the flame itself as everyone else on the island did, thought the whole custom a strange and a hard one, and said so, and traded there anyway, because a woman who would finish a bargain stung was a woman whose word could be trusted after.

Sìleas Dhonn, Dòmhnall's sister's daughter, was a girl of fourteen at Dùn dhearg when her uncle first began asking her to sit in on the reckoning of the waypoint-keepers' tallies, because her eye for a column of figures that did not add was sharper than any clerk's he kept, and she found, going back through ten years of the Ceann mhin rolls at his bidding, three separate years in which the keepers' own count of what they had gathered did not match the count of what had passed through their gates, though never by enough, in any single year, to be called outright theft before a council that had no proof to set against a keeper's word. She said nothing of it publicly while her uncle lived, being young and his niece and the ruler of nothing at all, and kept her own copy of the figures, written out fair in her own hand and locked in the same chest as her mother's marriage-cloth, and told no one why she kept it, only that a column of figures that did not add was a thing worth keeping until someone asked to see it.

In the last year of Dòmhnall's reign a storm out of the west drove the sea over the harbour at Seann Chwen and took the boats at their moorings and a third of the houses with them, and left the survivors on the high ground above the water with nothing but what they stood in. Word of it reached Dùn ìseal and Dùn dhearg within the same day, carried on the road Mòrag nan Rathad had raised above the flood twenty years before her grandson was born, the same road that had once swallowed a cart to the hub and now ran dry through worse rain than that storm brought; and it was the Keeper's grain-carts, sent down from the Hall's own stores, that reached the ruined harbour first, a full day ahead of Dòmhnall's, though he had sent his the same hour the word came to him and grudged no bushel of it. The people of Seann Chwen ate from both that winter and were glad of both, and never afterward forgot, in all the tellings that followed, which cart had come up the road first; and it is told that the road itself, when the tale is given whole, is remembered as being no less part of the saving than the grain that was carried on it, being the very stretch above the ford that a rìgh's daughter had raised out of the mud in the first summer of her reign, a hundred and twenty-five years before the storm that proved it.
