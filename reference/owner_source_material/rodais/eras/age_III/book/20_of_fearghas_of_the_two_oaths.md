# Of Fearghas of the Two Oaths

*FE 543 – 556*

*The reapers of Achadh mhòr tell this at the end of harvest, when the last sheaf is cut, and they cut it high.*

## I. Of the House Sworn Twice

Achadh mhòr lay where the Red Share's stones ran out and Eithne's plain began, and no one now living could say which of the two had held it first, only that both had held it long enough to be sure of it. The fields ran flat and wide between the two boundary lines, the best grain ground on the island, and the house that farmed them had been sworn to both lords since before Fearghas's grandmother was a girl. It was Ciorstaidh who had made the double oath, not by any wish of her own: she had stood up at the Roinn among the seven hundred and twelve, had her knot cut through with the rest, and married after into a family already sworn to Dùn dhearg, so that her children answered to two halls at once and her grandson after them. Fearghas had heard the story of the cutting so many times as a boy, at so many hearths, that he could have told it himself in his grandmother's own words, down to the catch in her voice at the place where the knife went through.

By the year the reapers now reckon as the true beginning of his trouble, Fearghas was a hundred and seventy-one years old, which on the island of that century was old but not yet remarkable, and had held Achadh mhòr's granary fields alone for longer than most men then living had been alive. He was called, and had been called since he was young enough to find it funny, Fearghas Dà-Mhionn, Fearghas of the Two Oaths, and for the better part of a hundred and fifty years the name had been a joke told at his own hearth as readily as at anyone else's. Twice a year, at the two greater cuttings, he sent a sheaf to Dùn dhearg and a sheaf to Cathair mhòr, each a little short of the full measure, measured on his own threshing-floor before both stewards' men so that neither steward could say he had been cheated more than the other. He used to say, when a stranger asked him how a man served two lords without being torn between them, that he had never yet met the lord who begrudged a short sheaf so long as his neighbour got no more, and that this was the whole of statecraft as he understood it. It got a laugh at Dùn dhearg and a laugh at Cathair mhòr both, which he counted, privately, as proof that he had struck the balance right.

He had a wife once, Beathag, who died young of a fever the healers at Muileann ruadh could not put a name to, and no children who lived past their first winter, so that by the year the reapers mean, the house at Achadh mhòr held only himself, his steward, and whatever hands the harvest needed hired in from the granaries round about. He kept a stone knife of his grandmother's on the lintel of the hall, the one she had carried after the Roinn and given him in his turn, since the knife that had cut her cord that day no one had kept, and he touched it going in and out of his own door the way another man might touch a charm, without troubling to say why.

The fields ran a good half-day's walk from stone to stone, east to west, longer than any granary holding on either side of the two shares, and a carrier coming up the east road with his basket could reckon he had reached Achadh mhòr a full morning before he reached the hall, so wide was the grain before any roof showed over it. Fearghas had walked every rod of it on foot at one time or another in a hundred and seventy years, first at his father's heel and then alone and then, in the last decades, with whichever steward the years had left him, and there was no boundary stone on either side, the Red Share's or Eithne's, that he could not have found blind by the shape of the ground underfoot. He knew, better than either hall that claimed him, exactly where his own oath ran out and the other's began, which was one reason, though not the only one, that neither Dùn dhearg nor Cathair mhòr had ever managed to catch him short on either sheaf.

## II. Of the War Fought in Fits

The joke held for most of a hundred and fifty years because the two houses it named had no quarrel that Fearghas's short sheaves could not paper over. It stopped holding the year a great-grandson of Eithne's, Torcall Donn, took to wife Muireall nic Ìomhair, of the line that held the Red Share, meaning the marriage to heal what the island still called, out of old habit, the strife of the heirs, though the Roinn that had begun it was by then five centuries gone and half forgotten outside the Hall's own rolls. Muireall died in her first winter at Cathair mhòr, of nothing anyone could point to, only the ordinary cruelty of a cold season on a woman not raised to it, and the lands she had brought to the marriage, three granges and a fishing-right on the Abhainn fhionn, were claimed at once by both houses: Cathair mhòr's, because the widower held them; the Red Share's, because Muireall had never sworn them away from her own line. Neither claim was new in its kind. What was new was that there were, by then, some hundreds of holdings across the island sworn to two lords the way Achadh mhòr was, households that had married and been given land and taken oaths without troubling, in quieter years, to notice that the oaths might one day pull in different directions, and every one of those hundreds of holdings now had to choose.

Fearghas watched it happen to his neighbours before it came near his own gate. The household two valleys east of Achadh mhòr, sworn the same double way, chose the Red Share within the first season, on no better reason than that the Red Share's steward asked first and asked more civilly, and Cathair mhòr's men burned it to the field-walls before the following harvest, taking the cattle and leaving the house standing empty and black-eaved as a warning to the next household asked to choose. A holding to the south, sworn the same way, tried the opposite course and chose neither, sending word to both halls that it would keep faith with whichever kept faith with it, and was burned within the year by both, first the Red Share's riders in a night raid that took the barn, and then, before the roof could be mended, a company out of Cathair mhòr that finished what the first had started, each host telling its own men it was only punishing what the other had already begun. Refugees from both kinds of burning came through Achadh mhòr on the road to wherever they still had kin, and Fearghas fed what he could of them from his own stores and asked no oath of any of them, since he judged, and said so once to his steward, that a man burned out for choosing badly had paid enough already without being asked to choose again at another man's gate.

One family from the southern burning, a widow and three grown sons with nothing left to their name but a hand-barrow and a milking cow that had somehow been walked clear of the fire, stopped three nights at Achadh mhòr on their way to a sister's holding beyond Ceann leathan, and the widow, warming herself at Fearghas's own hearth on the second of those nights, asked him outright, with the bluntness of a woman who had already lost everything a lord could threaten to take, why a man sworn twice himself had never once been asked to choose the way her own household had. Fearghas told her, and it was the only time his steward ever heard him say it in so many words, that he supposed neither hall had yet reckoned Achadh mhòr's grain worth the burning, and that he had made his peace, long before she ever came to his door, with the year they finally did. The widow said nothing to that, only looked at him a while over the fire, and went on to Ceann leathan the next morning without asking him anything further.

Fearghas rode out himself, once, in the third year of the burnings, to the black-eaved holding two valleys east, not to claim it, for it was Cathair mhòr's men who had burned it and no lord of the Red Share's would have thanked him for standing on ground his own house had never held a right to, but only, as he told his steward before he went, to see with his own eyes what a double-sworn holding looked like once the choosing was done and paid for, since he judged a man ought to know the shape of the thing he might himself be asked to choose. He found the field-walls standing and the house gone to its foundations, the byre likewise, and an old dog still living about the ruin that would not be coaxed away from it though no one remained there to feed it, and he stood a long while at what had been the threshold, saying nothing to the steward who had ridden with him, before turning his horse back toward Achadh mhòr without once setting foot inside the walls themselves. It was after that ride, the steward said, that Fearghas stopped speaking of the war as something happening to other men's holdings, though it was three years more before anyone at either hall heard him speak of it as something that might happen to his own.

It went on that way in fits rather than as one war fought straight through, a burning in one valley followed by two quiet years and then a burning in another, because neither the Red Share nor Cathair mhòr had the strength any longer, five centuries and more after the Roinn had first cut the island into shares, to keep a host in the field for a whole season. The lines that had once stood up seven hundred and twelve strong were down, by then, to a scattering of great-grandchildren holding what their forefathers had held whole and parting it further at every burial, and only the two strongest, the Red Share that Ìomhar's blood had never let be broken and Eithne's own plain, still had spears enough to call a muster worth the name. The island came to call it Cogadh an Dà Mhionn, the War of the Two Oaths, for Fearghas's own house before any other, since his was the oldest and best known of all the double-sworn houses, the one every teller reached for when the war wanted a name, though he himself had done nothing yet to earn it beyond going on sending his short sheaves and hoping, as he told no one but his steward, that the two hosts would exhaust each other on smaller holdings before either thought to reckon what a granary the size of Achadh mhòr's might be worth to whichever side finally took it whole.

## III. Of Torcall Donn

Torcall Donn did not live to see how long the war he had made, without meaning to, would run. He was a hundred and eighteen years old, and had never in his life led men further than the boundary of his own fields, when the Red Share's riders caught his company on the road below Dùn thais in the fifth year of the burnings, going, by his own account afterward given to no one, only to see the granges his wife had brought him and judge for himself whether they were worth what both houses were spending to hold them. He was killed there, on the road and not in any hall, and his own steward, who survived it, said after that no one on either side had struck the first blow with any particular hatred, only that a company of the Red Share's riders had met a company of Cathair mhòr's on a narrow stretch of road neither would give way on, and that Torcall Donn had been in front when the pushing turned to something worse. Word of it reached Achadh mhòr within three days, carried by a granary carrier who had seen the bodies still on the road and would not eat at Fearghas's table until he had told it all, start to end, twice over.

Fearghas heard it out standing in his own doorway with his hand on his grandmother's knife, and said only, when the driver had finished, that a man dead on a road solved nothing that a man alive with a claim on land had not already made insoluble, since the lands Muireall had brought were no less claimed by both houses for her husband's death, and might now be claimed with less patience on either side, there being no one left living to argue that the marriage had ever been meant to end this way. He was right in it, as the years after bore out: the Red Share pressed its claim on the three granges harder once Torcall Donn was gone rather than less hard, judging, not wrongly, that Cathair mhòr's grip on lands it held only through a dead man's marriage was weaker than it had been while he lived, and Cathair mhòr held to its claim all the harder for the same reason, so that the war, which might have guttered out on Torcall Donn's death the way a fire sometimes dies with the man who lit it, instead burned on past it into holdings that had nothing to do with Muireall's dowry at all, Achadh mhòr's own among the number that both houses now began, for the first time in Fearghas's long life, to count aloud.

Torcall Donn's body was carried back to Cathair mhòr and buried there rather than sent to whatever Red Share ground his wife's people might have claimed for it, and it was said afterward, though Fearghas never repeated it as more than a saying he had heard, that Eithne's own hall kept his grave apart from the family ground for a season, as though even the dead of the marriage that had begun the war could not be let lie too near the living who still argued over what it had bought them. Fearghas sent no man to the burial, judging that a lord sworn to both houses had no honest place at a grave either side might read as taking sides merely by his standing near it, but he sent, by the same granary carrier who had first brought the news, a measure of Achadh mhòr's own barley to be burned at the graveside in Torcall Donn's name, the old rite for a man who had died before his harvest was in, and asked that no word go with it saying who had sent it. The carrier kept that silence rather less carefully than the mason later would keep his, so that within the season it too was known on both sides of the war, and read, as everything Fearghas did in those years came to be read, two different ways by two different hearths.

## IV. Of Ciorstaidh's Stone

It was in the second year after Torcall Donn's death that Fearghas had his grandmother's stone cut and set into the wall of his own hall, a plain grey slab cut on its two faces with the signs of the two shares and, between them, the sign of the knot, the same sign the eleven had worn who had their cords cut through at the Roinn. He had put off doing it for a hundred years and more, telling his steward, when the man asked why, that a stone in a wall could not be unsaid the way a story at a hearth could, and that he had never been sure, until that year, what he wanted said permanently about a woman who had been sworn twice before he himself was ever sworn to anything. What decided him was neither grief nor age but the plain reckoning that the house he had inherited from her, along with her double oath, was now spoken of on both sides of a war as a prize rather than a farm, and that whichever host eventually took it, he wanted it known, cut in stone and not merely told, that the man who had held it last had held it from a woman who had cut her own cord at the Roinn and answered for it all her life without once choosing a side to betray the other.

The stone-cutter who came up from Muileann chiar to do the work said afterward, to whoever would listen, that he had cut a hundred memorial stones in his time and never one where the man who ordered it stood over his shoulder the whole day watching every stroke of the chisel, as though he feared the mason might carve some sign into the grain of it that was not true. Fearghas paid him double the going rate and asked him to say nothing of the day's work at Dùn dhearg or at Cathair mhòr, a request the mason kept for exactly as long as it took him to reach the next market, where the setting of Ciorstaidh's stone became, within a season, one more thing the tellers on both sides of the war repeated of Fearghas Dà-Mhionn, some saying it proved he still meant to hold for both houses to the end, and others saying, more shrewdly, that a man who set his grandmother's memory in stone before deciding anything else was a man already reckoning how he wished to be remembered, which was not the same thing as reckoning how he meant to act.

## V. Of the Hosts Called Out

The word that both hosts were finally on the road for Achadh mhòr came to Fearghas in the heat of Lùnastal, six years after the stone was set, carried by two riders arriving within an hour of each other, one from the east swearing that the Red Share's council had that week resolved to take the granary whole rather than go on arguing its worth in councils that settled nothing, one from the west swearing the same of Cathair mhòr, each rider plainly astonished to find, on arrival, that his own lord's host was not the first to move. Fearghas was a hundred and eighty-four years old that Lùnastal, and had outlived his wife, both his children, his grandmother, and every steward who had served him before the one now standing at his shoulder, and he heard the two riders out in the yard, one after the other, with no more visible feeling than he had shown at any of the burnings two valleys off, though his steward, who had served him eleven years by then and thought he knew the shape of the man's silences, said afterward that this one had a different weight to it.

The steward had asked, when the second rider's dust was still settling in the yard, whether word should go to the granaries round about so that the smaller households might carry off what stores they could before either host arrived, a question any steward of thirty years' less service might have asked out of plain decency, and Fearghas had answered only that word had best go instead to the reapers still working the western strips, to finish the cutting they were on before they thought about anything else, an answer the steward found so strange coming from a man who had fed every burned-out household on the road for six years that he did not know, telling it afterward, whether to set it down to age, to grief he had not shown, or to some reckoning the old man had already made and not yet spoken.

The barley that year stood ripe to the edge of the sky, the best crop Achadh mhòr had raised in a decade of uneven summers, heavy-headed and dry after a summer with almost no rain in it, the kind of harvest a granary saw perhaps once in a generation and long remembered for it, and Fearghas walked out into it that evening with his steward a pace behind him, the way he had walked his fields every harvest evening of his long life, and ran his hand along the heads of grain as he went the way another man might run a hand along a horse's neck, an old habit he had never broken himself of, though his eyes could see well enough on their own by then what his hand was telling him. He looked east first, to where a low haze of dust hung over the road that came down from the Red Share, too far off yet to show men or spears but unmistakable to anyone who had watched such roads for six years running, and then west, to where a second haze, thicker and nearer, showed that Cathair mhòr's host had the shorter way to come and meant to use it. Neither haze had yet crossed a boundary stone, and by the steward's own reckoning there was the best part of a day left before either host could reach the fields at all, time enough, he thought, for the two of them to talk over what might yet be done, and he was already turning over in his mind which of the two halls a rider might be sent to first when Fearghas spoke.

"Whichever I feed, the other burns me," he said, not loudly, and not as a question, so that his steward, who had half expected to be asked which host stood the better chance, understood only afterward that the old man had not been asking anything at all. "I've sworn to both of them." He was quiet a moment, still looking west, where the dust was nearer than the east's. "Let them both have it."

The steward said afterward that he took the words at first for a bitter kind of surrender, a lord too old and too tired to fight either claim any longer allowing that whichever host arrived first might simply take what it liked, and he answered Fearghas in that understanding, saying that a rider sent now to Dùn dhearg, or failing that to Cathair mhòr, might yet buy the granary a season's grace while the two halls argued the matter between themselves as they had argued the three granges for forty years without once coming to blows over Achadh mhòr. Fearghas heard him out and shook his head, once, and said that forty years of argument over three granges had already cost more burned households than either hall would trouble to count, and that he did not mean to add Achadh mhòr's own people to that number waiting on a season's grace that would only be spent arguing the next season's instead. He asked, then, whether the last strip in the western field had been cut, and being told it had not, said that it should be, that evening, before dark, the same as any ordinary harvest evening the two of them had ever kept together.

## VI. Of the Last Sheaf and the Smoke

The reapers had already begun the last cutting that week, working the western fields first as the custom of Achadh mhòr had always run, and it was among them, not in the hall, that Fearghas walked next, asking which sheaf stood ready to be cut last and highest, the honour every harvest gave to whichever binder's hands were steadiest at the end of a long day, and telling the reapers, when they showed him, to cut it as they always had and carry it in to the hall as they always had, war or no war on the road. Some tellings since have made more of that evening than the steward himself ever claimed for it, saying Fearghas told the reapers plainly what he meant to do with their harvest before he did it, but the steward's own account, given many times over at hearths from Achadh mhòr to Muileann chiar, says only that the old man watched the last sheaf cut and bound, said nothing of fire to any reaper living, and walked back to the hall alone once the dark had properly come down, leaving the reapers to carry the last sheaf in behind him not knowing it would be the last thing carried into that hall whole.

He took the torch himself from the hearth inside, not sending steward or reaper to do it, and walked the bounds of his own fields lighting the standing barley from the eastern boundary stones, the Red Share's own markers, clear across to the western ones that marked Cathair mhòr's edge, so that by the time either host came over the rises that bordered the valley there was nothing left standing for a rider to see but smoke going up in one long sheet from stone to stone. He walked slowly, the steward said, not as a man in any haste to be done, stopping twice to catch his breath the way a man of a hundred and eighty-four years must, and laying the torch to each new stretch of standing grain with the same unhurried care he had once given to the reaping, as though a field fired badly might somehow be fired again more honestly. The barley took at once, dry as it was after a rainless summer, and ran ahead of him faster than an old man could walk, so that by the time he reached the western stones the eastern ones were already lost behind a wall of smoke he did not look back to see.

The fire took the field-walls and the drying-barns and, before any hand thought to stop it or any hand any longer wished to, the steadings themselves, running up from the fields to the hall on a wind that had stood fair for burning all that dry summer, so that the hall Ciorstaidh's stone stood in was already alight by the time the first riders of either host crested their rise and understood, each separately and each too late to matter, what the dust of the other host had been racing toward all along. The reapers who had cut the last sheaf that same evening carried it clear of the burning before the wind turned, and it was that sheaf, and not the granary it came from, that the households round about kept afterward and still keep, cutting a new one high at the end of every harvest since in the field-strips that grew back green the following spring, on ground that had been ash the autumn before.

Fearghas went back into the hall while it burned and shut the door behind him, and did not come out again, and no one who was there that night, reaper or steward, ever gave an account of trying to stop him or of being asked to. His steward said only that he had followed the old man as far as the threshold and been told, in the same voice that had said *let them both have it*, to go and see the reapers safe on the road to Muileann chiar, and that he had gone, and that by the time he judged it safe to look back the hall's roof had already fallen in on itself, and no door remained for anyone to have opened even had they wished to.

The Red Share's host came over the eastern rise first that evening and found the barley already ash to the middle distance; Cathair mhòr's host, coming over the western rise not an hour behind them, found the same, and both hosts, meeting at last on ground that had cost neither of them a spear thrown, stood on the black stubble in the last of that evening's light with nothing between them any longer worth fighting for, looking down at Achadh mhòr's smoke going up straight in the windless dusk, and finding, when each looked for the granary they had marched to take, only more of it. Neither host stayed the night on that ground. The Red Share's riders turned east again before full dark, and Cathair mhòr's before the last light was gone from the sky, each company going home to a hall that had spent six years and more arguing over a prize that had put itself beyond either of their reaching in a single evening, and it was said after, on both sides, that no rider of either host ever cared to say aloud which of the two hosts had arrived the first.

The reapers came back the following spring to strips gone black and then, within two seasons, green again, since fire does a granary field less lasting harm than it does a hall or a house, and they cut their harvest there for generations on ground that had once been sworn twice and was sworn, after that Lùnastal, to no one living. Fearghas Dà-Mhionn had lived a hundred and eighty-four years, sworn for a hundred and fifty of them to two lords at once, and was remembered on both sides of the shares he had refused, in the end, to choose between, though never, on either side, with quite the same story of why. It is the reapers' own telling, not the Hall's roll nor either lord's tellers, that has lasted longest: that a man who could not feed two masters honestly chose to feed neither, and that the last sheaf of Achadh mhòr is still cut high at every harvest's end for the sake of the one that was carried clear of the fire the year the fields answered, at last, to no oath but the reaping.
