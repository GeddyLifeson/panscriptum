# Of the Wood-Watchers

*FE 1,321 – 1,595*

*Part Five: The Written Roll and An Talla Dearg*

> *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.*
> We did not come; we were here.
>
> — said by both companies of the dark wood, each of its own wood, and neither meaning the other

## I. Of the Years the List Does Not Count

Ealasaid Chiar died in the thirtieth year of her writing, having lived sixty years and reigned thirty-one of them, and was carried to the graves of Sliochd Ìomhair at Dùn dhearg. It is told that the clerk Tormod had trained stood at the grave with the roll his master had made her, Clàr nan Rìghrean on Dia-thìr, and read her own name out of it last, in its right place, because she had once asked Tormod whether a written name could feel it was written, and Tormod had not been able to answer her and had thought of the question every day he had left.

After Ealasaid Chiar the list falters. For a space the reciters cannot close, the roll Tormod began gives no ruler at all, only a silence where a name should stand, because in those years no household strong enough to hold the hall at Dùn dhearg troubled to have its claim written down, or because the writing lapsed with the clerk who alone knew how to keep it, or for some third reason no one living now can give. Clàr an Talla at Dùn ìseal keeps its own count through the same years. It was begun later than Tormod's and kept by a different hand, and where Clàr nan Rìghrean is silent Clàr an Talla goes on naming its own Keepers one after another, each dying and each replaced, so that a person wishing to know how many years passed in the gap must count them there instead, by the flame's own succession rather than by the hall's.

It was in this gap, some fifty years into it by Clàr an Talla, that Talla na Lasrach first set its own hand to teaching. Until then the stag-letters had been Scoiltean nan Draoidhean's alone, taught in their own schools to their own novices, and any at Dùn ìseal who wished to read sent a son or a daughter east to learn it as a guest. In the fiftieth year of the gap Coimheadaiche na Lasrach of that day judged that a Hall which could not read its own roll without borrowing another order's letters was a Hall too dependent on goodwill it had not earned, and set two of his own clerks to learn the stag-letters at Dùn ìseal, taught by a stag-teacher paid in coal for the winter of his teaching. It is remembered now as a small thing, though it did not feel small to the clerks who first sat down to a stranger's alphabet at their own Keeper's order. From that year Talla na Lasrach kept its roll in its own hand, and the roll it kept, once begun, did not stop, through every year of the silence that followed in Clàr nan Rìghrean, so that when at last a ruler's name returns to the record it is Clàr an Talla, not Clàr nan Rìghrean, that can say with any certainty how many years had passed.

Seventy years further into the gap a Keeper was named of whom the roll gives only the years of her reign, twenty-two, and the year of her death. It is the fate of a quiet age that most of what happens in it happens to no one the tellers thought worth naming, and a hundred small keepings, harvests neither good nor bad, coal cut and burned and cut again, children born and old people buried in the ordinary way, pass without a single teller troubling to set them down, because a teller tells what changes, and in those years, on the whole, nothing changed. The households burned their handful at dawn as their grandparents had burned it. The carriers walked the roads their grandparents had walked. Talla na Lasrach's clerks, in their new letters, wrote Coimheadaichean na Lasrach' names into the roll as each died and each was chosen, and nothing in the writing asked to be read twice.

It is not that the island slept through those years, only that it did what an island does when no one is fighting for it and no god has spoken and no famine has come: it went on, quietly, at the pace of a life rather than the pace of a telling, and the tellers, who love a fire or a war or a birth of note, left it mostly alone.

## II. Of the Black Horned Beast

Near the far end of that quiet a woman of the households of the north-west gave birth to a son she named Conall, and called him Dubh for the black hair he came into the world with and kept all his life, thick and unfading even in his last years, when every other hair he had gone grey turned white instead of thinning. He was the fourth of five children in a household that kept sheep on the high ground above Cnoc bheag, in sight of the wood the island had never cleared, a stand of old oak and thorn that ran back from the moor's edge further than any household's memory, and that no one, in all the generations since Aodh Crom had first divided the vein from the shares, had ever put an axe to.

Conall grew up half afraid of that wood and half in love with it, the way a child is of any dark place close enough to walk to and far enough that its parents forbid the walking. He heard from his grandmother, who had heard it from hers, that something walked in the uncleared wood that was not a stag and was not a wolf and was not any beast a hunter's dog would follow, a thing horned and black that let only those it chose come upon it, and that the wood was left uncleared on its account rather than on any rule of Talla na Lasrach's, because the first households to try clearing it had lost cattle in it and never gone back to try again. His grandmother told it as an old woman tells a thing she half believes and wholly enjoys telling, with a shiver in the voice at the right places, and Conall, who was a serious child even at six years old, asked her each time whether she herself had seen it, and each time she said no, but that her own grandmother had, once, at dusk, at the wood's edge, and had never afterward walked past it without making the old sign against ill luck, faith or no faith.

He was twenty years old, in the household's own reckoning, when he saw it himself.

He had gone up to the high pasture before first light to bring down two ewes that had strayed the night before, and came along the wood's edge in the grey hour when the birds have not yet begun and the light is more a lessening of dark than a beginning of day, and there, at the treeline, not thirty paces off and standing quite still, was a beast the size of a large hind but built heavier through the shoulder, black from nose to hoof but for two pale horns that caught what little light there was and held it, so that Conall, who told the tale only twice in his life and both times to people he trusted absolutely, said afterward that the horns seemed lit from within rather than by the sky. It looked at him. He did not move, and it did not move, for what he judged afterward to have been the space of ten or twelve breaths, though it might have been less, fear being a poor clock. Then it turned, without hurry, and walked back into the trees, and was gone before he had drawn a full breath to call out, though he had no one near enough to call to and would not, in any case, have known what to call.

He told his mother that night. She did not laugh at him, which surprised him more than the beast had. She said only that her own grandmother's grandmother, by the family's count, had seen the same thing in the same place, or something enough like it that the tellings could not be told apart, and that a household that farmed this close to the wood had best let it keep its own council and not go looking for it a second time.

Conall did not let it keep its own council. He went back.

## III. Of the Watch

He went back first alone, then, within a year, with his younger sister, who did not believe him until she saw the beast herself from the same spot at the same grey hour, and after her with two cousins, and within five years there were nine of them, none older than thirty, who kept a watch at the wood's edge by turns, not to hunt the beast and not, at first, for any reason any of them could have set down in words beyond a wish to be there when it came again, which it did, not often, perhaps once in a season, always at the grey hour, always at a little distance, always turning back into the trees before anyone had gathered courage or wit enough to follow.

The people of Cathair dhearg, hearing of it from carriers who stopped at the household on the northern road, called them the wood-watchers, a name none of the nine had chosen for themselves and all of them, in time, came to answer to. Conall, being the first and the steadiest, and having by then given up shepherding to his brothers so that he might keep the watch through every season rather than the seasons his flock could spare him, became without any choosing on anyone's part the one the others looked to. He did not call himself a teacher of anything. He said, when the carriers asked him what the watchers believed, that they believed a beast walked in the wood and that a wood a beast walks in ought to have someone by it who is paying attention, which was not, he knew, an answer that satisfied a person looking for a faith with rules and a name, but it was the only answer he had, and he gave the same one for the rest of his life whenever the question was put to him.

By his thirtieth year, when Conall was already greying a little at the temples though his hair kept its black, the watchers numbered near thirty and had built themselves a low turf hut at the wood's edge where two or three of them slept in every season, keeping the watch through the night as well as the grey hour, because the beast, once, had been seen at full dark by a watcher out later than she meant to be. It was in that year that Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, the uncleared wood, was first called sacred by anyone outside the watchers' own company, when a clerk of Talla na Lasrach travelling the northern road to count the season's coal wrote the word down in his own report, more from habit than from any judgement of his own, having heard the watchers speak of the wood in that way and having no better word to hand.

## IV. Of the Tree in the Stag's Grove

It was Conall's own doing, and he never afterward denied it or claimed it was other than his own doing, that brought the wood-watchers into their first quarrel with any other company on the island.

In the dark of a winter some seven years after the clerk's report, the beast came right up to the watch-fire, closer than it had ever come before, and stood at the very edge of the firelight while five watchers sat frozen and silent around the flame, and then walked a slow circle about the fire, and stopped, and looked long at one particular tree at the wood's edge, a black-barked oak no different to the eye from a hundred others near it, before it turned and was gone into the dark. Conall lay asleep in the turf hut that night, and heard it told before the fire had burned down, by watchers too shaken to sleep, and he went out himself before first light and stood a long while before the tree the beast had looked at, and decided, in the way a man decides a thing he has been circling toward for years without knowing it, that the beast had shown it to him.

He cut the limb himself, with his own axe, the only cutting the wood-watchers had made in all their years of keeping their watch without cutting anything, and he did not cut it from Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag at all. The beast, when it turned from the fire, had turned east, and gone east into the dark; and Conall, who had once as a young man driven sheep the length of the island and seen, at the western edge of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar at Muileann chiar, a stand of black-barked oaks like the one the beast had stood beside, took it into his head that the beast had been showing him the way. He walked east six days with the axe at his belt and one watcher for company, and at dawn on the seventh he cut a limb from the first black-barked oak at the edge of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, believing, once the thought had taken him, that a sign shown to the watchers belonged to the watchers wherever it stood. He carried the limb home to the turf hut and set it above the door, and the watchers kept a small fire under it that spring, calling it the beast's own wood, given by the beast's own choosing.

The teachers of the grove found the wound in the tree that same morning. The teachers at Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, who kept their own long watch over their own wood far to the east and had heard of the wood-watchers only as a strange household custom on the far side of the island, not as any rival order, sent two of their eldest west with the word that a tree had been cut from the stag's own ground, a theft of a teaching-place rather than merely of wood, for the black-barked oaks at the grove's western edge had for longer than any of the wood-watchers' memory been used by the stag's teachers for lessons, and the tree Conall had cut was one three generations of teachers had taught beneath.

Conall met the two elders at the wood's edge himself. He did not deny the cutting; he never in his life denied a thing he had done. He told them what the beast had done, circling the fire and looking long at the one tree, and said that he had read it as a sign meant for the watchers, and the elder who spoke for the pair, a woman old enough to have taught three generations of her own order's novices, heard him out in full and then said, not unkindly, that a sign was a hard thing to read from outside another order's own understanding, and that Scoiltean nan Draoidhean had their own long history of signs read wrongly by those who wanted them read a certain way, and that a man who read a sign as leave to walk six days into another order's wood with an axe had read it, at the least, very much to his own advantage.

## V. Of the Teachers in the Dark Wood

Scoiltean nan Draoidhean did not let the matter rest with words at the wood's edge. Within the season they sent teachers of their own west, straight into Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, the wood-watchers' own uncleared wood, and set up a teaching-circle at its western edge, saying that the first stag any teller could name had come from that wood before it came to any grove in the east, and that Scoiltean nan Draoidhean therefore held as good a claim to the uncleared wood as the wood-watchers held to any border tree.

Conall would not have it that the teachers had come to steal his own ground in answer, though several of the younger watchers said so plainly and said it to the teachers' faces. He thought, and said so to any watcher who would listen, that Scoiltean nan Draoidhean had done exactly what he himself had done at the border tree: taken a grievance and answered it the only way either order knew how, by claiming ground. He did not think it made either side wrong, only that it made both sides the same kind of wrong, which he found, when he had turned it over long enough in his own mind, oddly steadying, since a quarrel between two sides equally wrong is at least a quarrel that can be settled without either side needing to be told it alone was at fault.

For the better part of two years the two companies kept to their two ends of Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, each claiming both woods, and it is told, and Conall himself told it so in the one full account he ever gave of those years, that the watchers and the teachers met often enough in the wood between their two camps, gathering the same fallen wood for the same small fires, drawing water from the same spring, and that in all that time neither side spoke a word to the other beyond what courtesy required to pass on a narrow path, each nodding to the other and going on, as if an old agreement neither had ever made held them apart more surely than any boundary stone could have. It is the first quarrel remembered on Dia-thìr between two holy claims, and it was fought, if fought is the word for it, in silence, by two companies that shared a wood, a spring, and firewood, and would not share a sentence.

Word of it went to Dùn dhearg and to Dùn ìseal both, carried by the same carriers who had first named the wood-watchers, and both halls, hearing that neither company had raised a hand against the other and that the quarrel, such as it was, threatened no household's grain and no road's peace, judged it a matter for the two orders to settle between themselves, and sent no one to settle it for them. It was, in the end, a death that moved the matter, though not a death either side caused. In the second winter of the silence one of the teaching elders, the same woman who had spoken for the two who first came west, died of an ordinary winter cough in the teaching-circle's own camp, an old woman whose years had simply run their course, and Conall, hearing of it from a watcher who had heard it from a teacher too grieved to keep the old silence, walked alone to the teachers' camp and stood at its edge until he was noticed, and said, when he was asked what he wanted, only that he had come to say he was sorry for their loss, and that he thought, whatever else stood between the two companies, an old woman's death was not a thing either side should keep silent over.

## VI. Of Fearchar Òg, a Boy of Seven

The two companies kept the wood between them in their changed silence for fifteen years more, meeting at the spring, gathering the same fallen wood, nodding and passing on, and it is told that in all that span not one further word was cut in anger nor one further branch taken without leave, so that the quarrel, such as it remained, had by then worn itself down to a habit of politeness neither side troubled to explain to the other.

It was in the fifteenth of those years, though the wood-watchers did not hear of it for some months, that a boy of seven years old was brought to Coimheadaichean na Lasrach' hall at Dùn ìseal by his own mother's hand, a great-grandson, by a line the reciters had kept with more care than most, of the house that had once held Dùn dhearg before the gap swallowed its record. He was named Fearchar, and called Òg, the young, from the day he first stood before Coimheadaiche na Lasrach to be looked over, because there had been, within living memory of the hall, no ruler young enough to need the naming.

Coimheadaiche na Lasrach of that year judged the boy's claim as carefully as the gap-worn records allowed, weighing the line the reciters gave him against what little Coimheadaichean na Lasrach' own roll could confirm of it, and found nothing in it she could set aside. She did not crown him that day, a boy of seven being no ruler by any measure the island kept, but she had his name entered in the roll as heir acknowledged, and sent word to Dùn dhearg that a claim had at last been found worth the hall's confidence, the first such word in longer than Coimheadaichean na Lasrach' own count could say with certainty.

Fearchar Òg was raised after that within sight of the hall, in a household Coimheadaiche na Lasrach trusted, and grew, as boys of seven do, slowly and without any teller's notice, learning the roads and the halls and, in time, the names of the two companies quarrelling in silence in the wood his own great-grandmother's line had once held authority over, a quarrel that by the time he was old enough to be told of it at all had already run near twenty years without a blow struck on either side and showed no sign, to anyone watching from Dùn ìseal, of ever settling.

## VII. Of the Shared Keeping

It was Calum Liath, made Keeper in the boy's ninth year, a man of forty-five summers when he took the flame and known already for a patience the roll credits him with more than once, who judged at once that the wood-watchers and Scoiltean nan Draoidhean would go on sharing a wood and a spring and a silence forever unless someone with authority over neither order set a boundary between them that both could keep without either being told it had lost. He did not summon the two companies to Dùn ìseal, judging rightly that neither would come at the summons of a hall it did not answer to. He went to the wood himself, that same spring, and brought the boy-king with him, a boy still by any measure but for the office now laid on him, not yet crowned in any full sense the roll would call a reign.

They walked the wood's whole length together, Keeper and heir, from the western turf hut to the eastern teaching-circle, and spoke first with Conall, who was by then a man past fifty with more grey than black left in his hair though it had not, even then, whitened through, and then with the eldest of the teachers, a man who had come west in the first year of the quarrel and stayed every year since. Calum Liath asked each company the same question: what would you keep, if you kept only your own claim and gave up any claim to the other's ground. Conall answered that the watchers wanted only the western wood, where the beast had first shown itself and had gone on showing itself in all the years since, and would give up any claim to the eastern grove if the eastern grove gave up its claim to the western wood. The teacher answered much the same of the eastern grove, and it was Calum Liath, hearing both answers land so near the same ground, who proposed what neither company had thought to ask for on its own: that the black-barked stand be left to Scoiltean nan Draoidhean who had taught beneath it, with the watchers free to keep a watch there at their pleasure so long as they cut no branch of it again, and that each wood, western and eastern both, stay in the keeping of the company that had first claimed it, with each free to walk the other's wood unarmed and without cutting, as a guest walks a neighbour's ground.

Fearchar Òg, standing by, said little through the whole of it, being young and new to matters he had been raised to understand only in outline, but it is told that when Calum Liath had finished setting out the terms, the young king asked the two elders whether either side had, in near twenty years of silence, ever once wished the other gone from the wood entirely, and that both Conall and the teacher, after a long pause, said no, each in their own words, and that Calum Liath took the two no's as the surest sign yet that the terms would hold.

They held. Stones were cut that same season, at the boy-king's own order and Coimheadaiche na Lasrach's, and set at the edges of both woods, with the terms cut into them in stag-letters, so that any who could read might know, walking that ground, what was kept and by whom. No side was named the victor of the quarrel, because the roll that recorded it, kept in Talla na Lasrach's own hand as it had been since Coimheadaiche na Lasrach who first set his clerks to their letters two centuries before, judged there had been no victory to name, only a boundary two companies had at last agreed to keep.

## VIII. Of Conall in the Eastern Wood

Conall walked into the eastern grove for the first time in his life the day after the last stone was set, alone, without the axe he had carried at his belt for thirty years out of habit rather than any need of it, and without any of the younger watchers who would have gone with him gladly had he asked. He wanted, he told the one watcher he did tell, to walk it once as the stones now allowed, a guest in another order's wood, before he grew too old to walk it at all.

The teachers let him pass without a word raised against him, as the stones now bound them to, and he walked the length of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar in the slow hour before dusk, under oaks no different to look at from the oaks of his own wood, and came at last to the black-barked stand at its western edge, where the stump of the limb he had cut had long since healed over, and stood there a while in the failing light, an old man by then in all but the roll's reckoning, thinking, as he told it afterward, of nothing in particular, only that the wood was quiet, and that it had taken him thirty years and a stranger's death and a boy-king's question to learn that a company could keep a watch without needing the whole wood for its own.
