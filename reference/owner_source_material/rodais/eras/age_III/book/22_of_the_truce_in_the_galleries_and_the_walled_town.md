# Of the Truce in the Galleries and the Walled Town

*FE 557 – 634*

## I. Of the Rolls that Fill

In the years after Sìth an Stairsnich the rolls at Dùn ìseal began to fill the way a field fills with barley once the birds have had their share of it and stopped coming: slowly, unevenly, season on season, until a shelf that had held a handful of notched sticks and a coil or two of knotted cord held more than any one knot-keeper could carry in both arms. Luchd nan Snaidhm of Sìne's line were still the ones who kept it, cord and notch and nothing else, as their mothers and grandmothers had kept it since Roinn na Rìoghachd; and the cords themselves had begun to multiply past what any hand could hold at once, one for every share, one for every boundary stone Sìth an Stairsnich had fixed, one for every household that came up the mountain at the quarter days to swear an oath or settle a debt or ask Coimheadaiche na Lasrach's word on a matter no lord below would touch. The island was at peace, and a peace, it turned out, made more work for a clerk than a war ever had, for a war at least had the grace to burn its own tally along with everything else it touched.

Seonaid Chiar was born into this fatter, quieter island in the year the reapers of the low country still reckoned by the last burning, though by the time she was old enough to reckon anything herself the burned fields of Achadh mhòr had long since greened over and Clachan-crìche Sìth an Stairsnich had fixed stood as unremarkable as any others. She came of Sliochd Mhuirich, the one house among the four of custody that held no drop of the king's blood and had never claimed to, and she grew up on the mountain's own ground, among Na Naoi Teallaichean, with the smell of the galleries in her clothes before she had any clear notion of what lay under the mountain or why her grandmother's hands were always faintly grey at the knuckle no matter how often they were washed.

She was some six-and-twenty years old, by her own house's reckoning, when Na Naoi Teallaichean raised her to the flame; a young Keeper by any measure the office had known since Muireach Liath's own day, and younger than several of the cutters she would spend her first years learning to judge fairly. In those first years she held the cup the way a new-married woman holds a house not yet her own, carefully, and with an eye always on how the last Keeper before her would have done a thing, until enough seasons had passed under her own hand that she stopped asking herself the question and simply did what seemed right, which is, by most tellings, the mark of a Keeper who has finally grown into the office rather than merely into the name of it.

Her own grandmother, who had cut coal at Dùn ìseal all her working life and never once expected to see a grandchild raised above the galleries she had spent her back on, told her, the night Na Naoi Teallaichean' word came down, that a young Keeper's first year was worth two of any other, because a young Keeper is watched by every hearth in Talla na Lasrach to see whether she will bend to the first steward who flatters her or the first lord who frowns, and that a Keeper who bends once in her first year is asked to bend again for the rest of her keeping. Seonaid took the warning to heart, and it is said of her, by cutters old enough to have served under her from the first season, that she never once in six decades gave a different answer to the same question asked twice, however many years apart the asking fell, which her own knot-keepers came in time to reckon the surest mark of her rule.

## II. Of the Stag's People at Cnoc bhàn

It was in the third year of her keeping, some nine-and-twenty years old by the reckoning her own house kept for her, that the matter of Scoiltean nan Draoidhean came to the door of Talla na Lasrach and would not be turned away by silence, as it had been turned away by every Keeper before her since Gormshuil nic Mhuirich first shut the galleries against them.

Scoiltean nan Draoidhean, newly housed at Cnoc bhàn north of the mountain, had grown in the generations of the shutting-out rather than withered, as Talla na Lasrach had perhaps hoped it would. Its teachers still kept the question Sìleas nic Coinnich had left them at the far-eastern grove, generations before, unanswered and unforgotten: why the coal answered its colours to one hand and not to another, and whether the vein knew the difference between a cutter born to the galleries and one merely trained to them. They had not found an answer Talla na Lasrach would accept, nor, in truth, had they found one they were wholly satisfied with themselves; but they had gone on teaching all the same, and their own cutters, barred from Dùn ìseal, had found work at Cnoc bhàn's own small seams and at Muileann chiar's silver, and the school had not starved for it.

Seonaid received their asking through three old men who climbed the mountain road on foot, in autumn, with nothing in their hands but a single lamp, which they set down unlit on the stones before Talla na Lasrach's door as a sign that they had come to ask a flame and not to bring one of their own to compare against it.

"We do not ask to be believed," the eldest of the three said, when she had come out to them herself rather than send a steward, which Talla na Lasrach had not troubled to do for the school in living memory. "We ask only to be let back among the cutters who are already there. Let the question stay a question. We will not ask it of the vein aloud, if Talla na Lasrach will only stop asking us, aloud, whether we have any right to stand near it at all."

She thought on it through that winter, and took counsel of no one but her own galleries, walking them at odd hours with a lamp of her own until she had satisfied herself that the objection she had inherited from every Keeper before her — that a cutter trained outside Na Naoi Teallaichean might work some harm on the vein that a cutter born to it would not — was an objection she had never once seen answered with anything better than the fact that it had always been made. On {{date:II-0066}} she let the school's cutters back into the galleries, sworn, every one of them, to ask nothing of the vein aloud in Talla na Lasrach's own hearing, whatever they went on asking of it among themselves at Cnoc bhàn. Talla na Lasrach's own roll, in the dry words of whichever knot-keeper was set to keep it that season, calls the day a reconciling. The school, from that day to this, has never called it anything but a truce, and Seonaid, hearing both words used of the same morning in the same week, said only that she did not much mind which word a man chose, so long as the cutters kept the oath under either one.

The first of them to cross the gallery door again was an old man of the school whose teachers' teachers had been shut out by Gormshuil, and had spent the better part of his own long working life at Muileann chiar's silver seams believing he would die there without ever seeing the vein his own teachers had spent three generations arguing over. He stood a long while in the mouth of the gallery before he went in, by every account that survives of that morning, and said nothing to the cutters of Na Naoi Teallaichean who stood back to let him pass, and they said nothing to him, and it was, in its way, as full a truce as any words could have made it.

## III. Of the Island Filling Out

The rolls that came up to Dùn ìseal in Seonaid's years told of an island growing steadier than it had been in the five centuries since Roinn na Rìoghachd, though no single teller then living could have said, from any one report, quite how much the whole had changed.

Cathair mhòr, on the southern coast, raised a wall of fitted stone about its own headland in those years, the first wall of its kind the island had ever built, and would not say, then or afterward, what it had raised the wall against. The reapers of Achadh mhòr, when anyone asked them, said only that the town had not forgotten the smoke, and left it at that, for the burning of the great granary fields was still near enough in living memory that no one much doubted them, and far enough that no one troubled to ask Cathair mhòr's own people to confirm it. Ceann leathan, on the southern fishing grounds, grew fat on dried fish sent inland along the carriers' roads, and its harbour-master began keeping tallies on notched sticks, among the oldest counted things kept anywhere on the island; the man who cut the first of them, it is told, was no scholar and no clerk, only a fisherman's son who had grown tired of arguing with buyers over how many baskets a boat had brought in, and had found that a stick with a notch for every basket argued better than he could.

At Inis thais, on the western coast, a shrine was raised to the ancestors the sea had taken and never given back, and its keepers burned their season's handful on the open shore rather than under any roof, so that the drowned, gone west over the water, might see the flame from wherever the sea had carried them. Cuan shean, further up that same coast, dug salt-pans on the flats below the town and boiled the sea over peat fires that burned day and night through the drying months, and sent its salt inland on the western road until it kept the fish of half the island through the long storm season; and where the road from Cuan shean crossed the heath above Baile fhiadhaich, a scatter of salt-carriers settled by a stream that ran the summer through, on ground too poor for the plough, and kept goats there ever after, by every account that has come down of them.

On the eastern road, where the carriers out of Cnoc leathan met the southern coast path, a waypoint house was built on ground already known for its warmth, and took the name the ground had always had, An Taigh-òsta Grianach; its own builders, asked afterward why they had chosen that stretch of road over a dozen others no less convenient, said only that a house ought to be built where the sun already liked to stand, and that this was reason enough. Cnoc leathan itself, at the road's eastern end, grew into the market town of the whole south-eastern hill country, and its carriers bound themselves into a guild that held, by Talla na Lasrach's own grant, the sole right to carry for Talla na Lasrach on the stretch of road down to the coast, a right its members guarded, in the years that followed, with rather more jealousy than the road itself was worth to anyone who had not grown up walking it.

Seonaid heard all this, in her years at Dùn ìseal, from the tallies and the tellings the waypoint-keepers and the harbour-masters sent up the mountain each quarter day, more of them each year than the year before, until she found herself saying to her own knot-keepers, more than once, that she had come to know the shape of towns she had never set foot in better than she knew the shape of her own galleries' further reaches, which she had likewise never had cause to walk to their very end. It was, she said once, at her own hearth and not for any roll to record, a strange thing to rule an island chiefly through the reports of men who had never once seen her face; and stranger still, she added, that it seemed to trouble the island rather less than it troubled her.

The guild at Cnoc leathan brought its own quarrel to her door in the fourteenth year of her keeping, when two of its own carriers came up the mountain, each swearing the other had carried a load down to the coast road out of turn and taken the fee for it, and each demanding Talla na Lasrach strip the other of his place in the guild altogether. Seonaid heard them both out on the stones before Talla na Lasrach, in the plain grey light the office asked of every hearing, and asked at the end of it only one question of either man: which of them had first offered to give the disputed load back rather than argue over who had carried it. Neither had. She told them then that Talla na Lasrach's grant to the guild was a right to carry, not a right to quarrel, and that she would as soon strip the whole guild of its charter as decide between two men who had each thought a fee worth more than a friendship of twenty years' carrying together, and left them to settle it between themselves before she troubled to hear either man's case again. They came to an agreement on the road home, by their own later account, before they had gone a mile, which Talla na Lasrach's knot-keeper that season kept as proof, if proof were wanted, that most quarrels brought up a mountain could have been settled at the bottom of it, had either party troubled to try.

## IV. Of Ruairidh Glas and the Ford He Never Returned To

Of the old blood that had stood up at Roinn na Rìoghachd, few enough were left by the time Seonaid held the cup, and fewer still who had themselves shed blood in the long strife Sìth an Stairsnich had ended. Ruairidh Glas, holder of an Earrann Dhearg at Dùn dhearg, had come to the holding some hundred and eighty-five years before Seonaid's own birth, at an age, six hundred and fifty-eight years, that had seemed unremarkable enough among his own kin and would have seemed past belief to any household of the low country; and he had held it, by the time Sìth an Stairsnich was thirty years old, longer than any other lord then living had held anything.

He had sworn Sìth an Stairsnich himself, on the stone cup, with his own hand still upon it when he spoke the one thing beyond the agreed words that no one afterward thought to strike from the telling: that he had shed the first blood of the whole long strife at Dùn thais, over cattle on a wet slope, when he was young enough to think a stone worth a life, and that he would not buy such a peace again for twice the grazing on the island. He had never gone back to Dùn thais after that morning at Aonghas Bàn's body, not once in all the centuries between, though the road there from Dùn dhearg was no longer than a hard day's ride and he had ridden further, on lesser business, a hundred times over. His steward, in the last years, used to say that his lord knew every stone on every road on the island except the one road he could have walked blind, and had chosen, deliberately and for reasons he never once gave aloud, not to.

In his nine hundred and ninth year he sent to Dùn ìseal, unasked, for leave to come up and stand once more before the stone cup, which no lord of a share was required to do outside the quarter days and which none had troubled to ask for in Seonaid's own years of keeping. She granted it without asking his reason, and he came up the mountain road on foot rather than by any chair or litter, for his legs still carried him and he saw no cause to spare them for whatever years remained. He stood before the cup a long while, by the account of the knot-keeper who kept the door that day, and said nothing aloud that any living ear caught, and went back down the mountain the same evening to Dùn dhearg, and died there within the year, in his own bed, with his own household about him, having lived nine hundred and ten years and outlived every soul who had stood up in the gathering-house at Roinn na Rìoghachd, save only Eithne nic Ailein.

His son took up the holding without any summons to Talla na Lasrach, for Sìth an Stairsnich by then required none where an heir was named and undisputed, and the rolls of that season, which had grown careful about naming every lord however small, are strangely thin about naming this one; a fault, Seonaid's own knot-keeper said drily, that lay with the household's own silence about him rather than with any failure of her cords. He held an Earrann Dhearg after his father a very long time, by any ordinary reckoning, and a markedly shorter one than his father had, though neither he nor his own son after him would live to see the day when that shortening was spoken of, in Talla na Lasrach's own rolls, as anything but the ordinary way of things.

## V. Of the Fall in the High Gallery

It was in the three-and-fortieth year of Seonaid's keeping, when she herself was close on seventy years old and had begun, though she said so to no one but her own hands on a cold morning, to feel the mountain's damp in her joints the way the older cutters had always sworn a person eventually did, that the roof of the highest gallery at Dùn ìseal came down.

It fell in the dark hour before the second watch, when the gallery held fewer cutters than it would have by daylight but not so few that the fall went unwitnessed. The lamps went out with the dust before anyone standing further down the gallery understood what had happened, and it was the coughing, by every account that survives, that told the cutters below which way to run, since no one could see anything at all for the better part of a minute, and no one who was there afterward found much to say about that minute beyond the coughing and the dark. Four men died under it, cutters of Na Naoi Teallaichean every one; the eldest of them, a man named Aonghas Crom who had cut coal at Dùn ìseal since before Seonaid herself was born and had trained half the younger cutters now working the lower galleries, was found still gripping his pick, which the men who carried him up remarked on afterward more than they remarked on anything else about that morning. A fifth man was pulled out with a crushed hand that never again closed fully round a pick, and worked the lighter galleries for the rest of his years cutting one-handed, which the rolls note without further comment, as if a one-handed cutter working on regardless were no more remarkable, in that Hall, than a wall standing where a wall had always stood.

Seonaid went down into the gallery herself before the dust had finished settling, against every counsel her own stewards gave her, and stood in the broken rock with a lamp held high enough to see the shape of what the mountain had done, and said, to the men working to clear the fallen stone, only that she wished to see it with her own eyes before anyone told her what to think of it. What she saw, by every account, was rock enough to have killed twice the number it had, held back only by a seam of harder stone that had happened, by no planning of any cutter living, to catch the worst of the fall before it reached the floor where the other men stood.

The four dead were carried up and buried on Talla na Lasrach's own ground, the one ground on the island no feud had ever been let to follow a man, and Seonaid herself set the last stone over the last of them, which no Keeper before her, so far as the rolls remember, had troubled to do with her own hands rather than a steward's. She gave the season over to argument after that, not about whether the dead should be mourned, which no one disputed, but about what should be done so that no gallery should ever again be held up by nothing more than the luck of a harder seam catching a fall before it found a man. On {{date:II-0074}} she ordered timber props of oak set in every gallery Talla na Lasrach worked, the first timber Talla na Lasrach had ever spent on the mountain rather than on a road or a bridge or a waypoint house below it, and the first oak for the purpose was felled and cut within the season, so that by the following year's cutting no gallery at Dùn ìseal stood without a prop of its own between the roof and the men beneath it.

The walled-up mouth of the highest gallery, where the four had died and where the fall itself had been judged too dangerous to work again, stood sealed from that year on, and stands sealed still, by every telling that has come down since; and it is that walled door, more than any prop or any roll, that the cutters of Dùn ìseal point to, when a stranger asks them why every gallery below it now stands propped in oak that no earlier generation of cutters had thought to spend.

## VI. Of the Close of Seonaid's Keeping

Seonaid Chiar held the flame sixteen years more after the fall, and grew old in the office in a way none of Na Ceithir Taighean of custody had troubled to record of any Keeper before her, for the simple reason that few Keepers had held the cup long enough for anyone to notice the greying of a hand that had once been young. She died in her own quarters above the galleries, eighty-five years old, having kept the flame the better part of six decades, and Na Naoi Teallaichean that had raised her young chose another after her without any dispute that the rolls thought worth setting down, which is, of every Keeper's ending, the kindest sort a Hall can give.

Word of her death went out along the same roads that had carried her own rolls up to her for sixty years, and came, among other places, to the school at Cnoc bhàn, whose teachers sent up the mountain no message of grief and no delegation to mourn her, only the single unlit lamp their own three old men had once set down before her door, carried back up now by cutters young enough to have known no Keeper but her, and left again on the same stones, still unlit, as they had left it the first time: which Talla na Lasrach's knot-keeper that season understood well enough not to ask the school to explain, and did not.

Cathair dhomhain, on the western coast, was founded in the same years as her dying, by carriers who had settled at the western road's end above its harbour and raised a town on the rise there that they meant, from the first, to be known for keeping every measure strictly, in coal as in everything else a scale could weigh. Its own shrine, from {{date:II-0075}}, held Lagh an Loscaidh Bhig to the very grain from its founding day onward, and the town grew, in the years that followed, a name for a kind of pride that no other town on the island quite shared: pride in how exactly it kept to what it was owed, whatever measure of its own it had to show for it. It stood, that year, still a place carriers passed through on their way to somewhere else, its harbour no more than a fishing quay and its wall not yet raised; but its shrine's own strictness outlasted every one of the carriers who founded it, and was spoken of, by lowland households who had never once set foot on its own rise above the sea, as a strange, stiff-necked kind of pride to hold a whole town by.

At Dùn ìseal the walled mouth of the high gallery stood as it had stood since the year of the fall, sealed in dry stone, with the oak props running down every working gallery below it into the dark the way a spine runs down a back; and the cutters who passed the sealed door on their way to their own lower galleries had, within a generation, stopped remarking on it at all, the way the carriers on the roads below had long since stopped reading Clachan-crìche Sìth an Stairsnich had set. It had become, as Talla na Lasrach's own oldest things so often did, no longer a thing anyone spoke of, but a thing that was simply there, propping up, in its own silent way, everything built above and around it since.
