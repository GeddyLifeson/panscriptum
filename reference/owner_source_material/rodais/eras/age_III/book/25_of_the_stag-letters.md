# Of the Stag-Letters

*FE 880 – 914*

## I. Of the Boy Who Counted Wrong

Rònat was ten years old the summer he was sent up from his father's holding to the stag's school in the holy wood east of Muileann chiar, and he arrived with a bundle of clothes, a knife his mother had cut too sharp for a boy his age, and a talent, already noticed by every adult who had ever set him to watch a flock, for losing count of anything past thirty. The teachers of the stag kept their reckonings on notched sticks, one stroke to a beast, a bundle, a day, and it was the first task of every new pupil to cut such a stick clean and true and bring it to the old cutter for judgement, and Rònat's first stick, judged by Aonghas Cam, came back to him with a groove scored clean through the middle of it and the single word: again.

Aonghas Cam had taught at the wood longer than any pupil then living could remember him not teaching, and he had a leg that had set crooked after a fall from the oak he still climbed every autumn to mark the mast, and a patience with a wrong stick that did not extend to a boy who argued with him about it. He set Rònat cutting sticks for two years before he let him near a beast to be counted at all, and told him, more than once and always in the same words, that a stick that lied was worse than no stick, because a man believed a stick the way he did not always believe another man, and a wrong count believed was a debt paid twice or a debt never paid at all.

It was slow work, the counting, and Rònat was not, by his own later account, ever quick at it; what he was, once he had it, was exact, and Aonghas Cam came in time to trust his sticks over the sticks of pupils twice as fast with the knife, and to say so where the boy could hear it, which from Aonghas Cam was a kind of praise that other teachers spent whole speeches failing to match. By the time Rònat was one and twenty he cut the wood's own tally of the mast harvest and the new lambs and the toll owed the road-guards for the year, and it was put about, by pupils younger than himself, that Aonghas Cam trusted Rònat's stick before he trusted his own eyes, which Aonghas Cam neither confirmed nor troubled to deny.

The teaching itself, beyond the counting, was carried the old way, by a mouth and an ear and a memory trained to hold what it was given: the names of the beasts of the wood and which were the stag's and which were common; the turns of the year and which rite belonged to which turn; the descent of the teachers themselves, one from another, back further than any pupil could hold without losing the thread somewhere in the middle. Aonghas Cam held that whole thread, and said it over at every year's turning to whichever pupils could sit still long enough to hear the whole of it, and Rònat, sitting through it a dozen times by his twentieth year, found that he could hold perhaps two-thirds of it after, and lost the rest within a season, the same as every pupil before him had lost some part and no pupil after him would keep whole.

He said as much to Aonghas Cam once, in his twenty-fourth year, more out of a wish to be told he was wrong than any real complaint, and the old cutter looked at him a long while before he answered, in the flat way he had of answering a thing he had thought about longer than the boy asking it.

"You will lose more of it than I did," he said, "because I lost less of what was said to me than you will lose of what is said to you, there being more of it said now than there was said to me. And whoever comes after you will lose more again. It has always gone that way and there is no stick cut yet that holds a word instead of a count, so there is no help for it."

Rònat did not forget that answer, though he could not, at four and twenty, have said why it stayed with him longer than most of what Aonghas Cam said that same year.

The school itself was no more than three low houses and a byre set among the oaks of the holy wood, a morning's walk east of Muileann chiar along a path the mast-gatherers kept clear with their own feet more than any road-guard troubled to clear it, and it had stood in that clearing, teacher after teacher, longer than the wood's own oldest trees, or so the pupils were told and had no cause to doubt, there being no mark on any tree to say otherwise and no stick cut yet that held such a mark if there had been. The pupils slept two and three to a pallet in the low house nearest the byre, rose before the light to feed what beasts the school kept for its own table, and spent the day between the counting and the beast-lore and, on the turning of the year, the rites that belonged to the stag's people alone, which Aonghas Cam taught with more patience than he ever showed a wrong stick, and which Rònat, of all that he was made to hold in memory those years, found came back to him whole and unbidden even in his old age, long after the thread of names had thinned past keeping.

## II. Of the Sign Made for a Sound

Aonghas Cam's leg, crooked since his fall, took a fever in the wet autumn of Rònat's thirty-first year that no poultice the wood's healers laid on it would draw out, and he did not climb the oak that year to mark the mast, sending Rònat up in his place for the first time, and spent the winter in the low house by the fire, teaching what he could sitting down and losing his breath over what he could not. Rònat sat with him most evenings of that winter, less as a pupil now than as the man who fetched wood and water for an old teacher too proud to ask a younger one for either, and it was in those evenings, watching Aonghas Cam search for a name or a line of the old thread and not always find it before his breath gave out, that the shape of the thing came to Rònat, slowly and in pieces, the way a knot comes undone by first one strand and then another and not all at once.

He had been cutting a tally-stick that same week, one groove to a fish counted at the smoke-house, and it struck him, watching Aonghas Cam grope after a name he had said clean a hundred times before, that a groove held a count exactly and forgot nothing of it, and a mouth held a name or a line of teaching only as well as the mouth's own memory served it on the day, which was not always well. If a groove could be made to stand for a count, he thought, there was no reason in wood or knife that it could not be made to stand for a sound instead, and a row of sounds was a name, and a row of names in their right order was the very thread Aonghas Cam was losing by the fire, held now on something that did not have a bad night or a failing breath.

He did not say any of this to Aonghas Cam that winter, fearing to be told it was foolishness, and fearing worse, though he owned to this only later, to be told it had been tried before and failed for a reason he had not thought of. So he worked at it alone, in the hours before the school woke, cutting the same length of straight-grained ash over and over, one edge to lay the strokes against, as a stick already carried for a plain count. The trick of it, when it came, lay in giving each sound of speech its own small mark against that edge: some short and to one side, some short and to the other, some crossing the edge clean through, some slanting, so that a hand running down it in the dark could tell one sound from the next by touch alone before the eye had even looked. The vowels he set apart from the rest, five short cuts standing clear of the long consonant-marks, for a school-child learning them first would want the vowels easiest to the hand, and he had been such a child himself not so many years before, and remembered which sounds had come hardest.

By the spring he had cut his own name on a stick, and could read it back cold, without the stick in front of him, three days after cutting it; and then Aonghas Cam's name, and read that back too; and then, on an evening when the old man's breath was better than it had been in a month, he set the stick in Aonghas Cam's own hand and told him what the marks were, and asked him to run his thumb down the edge and say what he found.

Aonghas Cam was a long time silent over it, turning the stick, running his thumb back along the strokes a second time and a third. "This is my name," he said at last, "cut on a stick as if it were a count of fish."

"It is a count," said Rònat. "A count of sounds, in their order, same as a count of fish is a count of fish in their order taken."

The old man laughed at that, a short laugh that cost him more breath than he had to spend, and said that a boy who had once cut a stick so wrong it had to be scored through the middle had come, in the end, to cutting a stick no one on the island had thought to cut before him, and that he did not know whether that was a joke on him or a joke on the wood's whole way of teaching, and thought, on the whole, it was likely both.

## III. Of the Stone That Answered Nothing

Word of the sound-marks went round the school before the summer was out, and round the other schools of the stag within two years more, carried by pupils moving between them the way any new trick of the knife was carried, and by the time Rònat was five and thirty he had taught the whole scheme, strokes and vowels and the order of cutting them, to every pupil under him and to two teachers of the western school who came east on purpose to see it and stayed a season learning it from a man twenty years their junior without any evident shame in the asking. Aonghas Cam did not live to see the western teachers come; he died in Rònat's thirty-third year, in his sleep, in the same low house by the fire where he had first read his own name off a stick, and it was Rònat who cut, at the old man's own asking made some months before, the tally of his years on a length of the same ash, one stroke for each, rather than the sounds of his name, because Aonghas Cam had said, hearing what the new marks could do, that he wanted his years counted the old way at the last, having spent his whole life trusting a count over a name.

It was Sìleas nic Ruairidh, eldest of the western teachers who had come to learn the scheme, who first asked the question that sent Rònat and two others west to Cnoc bheag in Rònat's four-and-fortieth year, in the season the mast was gathered. She had been told, as every pupil of the stag was told somewhere in the long thread of teaching, that there stood at Cnoc bheag an old grey stone cut with marks no living teacher could read, set up by hands no living teacher could name, and it came to her, turning the new sound-marks over in her mind as she fell asleep one night, to wonder whether the old marks on that stone might be sounds too, cut by some maker whose speech the marks would give back if only someone knew now how to look.

They walked the road west together, the three of them, five days on foot with a tally-stick from the wood's own steward to see them fed at the waypoint houses along the way, and slept the fourth night at a turf longhouse with a hearth at either end, where the keeper, an old woman who had never heard of the sound-marks nor asked to, fed them barley and dried fish and told them, unprompted, that the road that far west ran along ground her own grandmother's grandmother had walked before there was any toll on it at all, which Rònat wrote down nowhere, having as yet nothing to write it in beyond his own memory, and thought afterward that this too was a kind of thread that would thin and be lost the way every unwritten thread was lost, whether or not anyone standing by the fire that night troubled to mourn it. They came to the stone on a grey morning, the rain still holding off, and stood before it a long while before any of the three so much as touched it. The marks on the old stone ran in lines and crossings not unlike Rònat's own scheme, cut deep enough that centuries of weather had not worn them smooth, and for a morning and half an afternoon the three of them set every stroke of the old marks beside every stroke of the new, turning the stone, comparing angle to angle and count to count, hoping, none of them said aloud what they hoped, that some line of it would answer to some line of theirs and give back, after all that silence, a single sound they could name.

Nothing answered. The strokes on the stone kept no order that matched the order Rònat had given his own, no vowel stood apart the way his stood apart, no five-and-five grouping of the marks gave up anything a mouth could shape. By evening Sìleas nic Ruairidh sat down on the wet grass at the stone's foot and said, plainly, that whoever had cut it had not taught them, and had not been taught by anyone who had taught them either, and that the two schemes, hers and the stone's, shared nothing but the accident of being marks cut in rows on a hard surface, which was no kinship at all, being the only way marks can be cut on a hard surface whether the makers ever knew of one another or not.

Rònat cut that finding onto a fresh stick before they took the road home, the first time, so far as any of the three knew, that a whole thought and not a single name or a single count had been set down in the new marks: that the makers of the stone at Cnoc bheag had not taught the makers of the stag's marks, and were not known to them, and left nothing behind that answered anything the three of them had brought to ask it. It was a small thing to have crossed five days of road to learn, and a smaller thing still to have carved, a handful of strokes on a length of ash beside a stone that had stood mute for longer than any of them could reckon; but it was the first thing the Dia-thìrich ever set down of the makers of that stone, and what it said was only what they were not.

## IV. Of the Ancient Man Who Laughed

Niall Òg came through the far east twice in those years, once before Aonghas Cam's death and once after, walking as he always walked, on his own two feet with a stick worn pale at the grip and no household about him beyond whatever family happened, that season, to be minding the wood he passed through. He was old past any counting the school itself could put a number to with confidence, well past his eighteen hundredth year by the reckoning the teachers kept among themselves from the tales told of Ailean's own fall, and he had outlived, by that reckoning, every one of the seven hundred and twelve heirs who had stood beside him at the cord's cutting save Eithne nic Ailein alone, and most of the children of those heirs besides, and he walked the roads still as a man walks a road he means to keep walking, without any particular hurry to be somewhere he had not yet been.

He came to the school in Rònat's forty-third year, the second of his two passings, an old man greeting an old man's school with the easy familiarity of someone who had watched it built in his own lifetime and expected, without saying so, to watch a good deal more of it before he was done, and he sat by the fire in the low house two evenings running, telling the pupils tales of the night of the handful that not one of them had heard told by a man who had stood there, and asking, when the tales ran out, after the trick he had been told of on the road, the marks that were said to hold a name the way a stick held a count.

Rònat cut his name for him that second evening, Niall's own name in the strokes, and set it in his hand, and told him to run his thumb along it the way Aonghas Cam once had, and said what each stroke gave. Niall Òg was quicker at it than Aonghas Cam had been, or perhaps only less proud to be seen slow, and read his own name back within the hour, haltingly at first and then clean, and sat with the stick in his two hands a while after, turning it, before he began to laugh.

It was not a short laugh. It took him by the chest and would not let go for the better part of a minute, an old man's laugh that ended, as old men's laughs will, in a fit of coughing that brought two of the younger pupils half out of their seats before he waved them back down with the hand that was not holding the stick, and when he had his breath again he said that he had been called by his name more times than there were fish in the western sea, by mothers and by kings and by every heir of the seven hundred and twelve who had ever wanted a thing off him, and that not one of those tellings had ever held still on a piece of wood after the telling was done, waiting for him to come back and hear it again whenever he chose.

"A name that stays," he said, "when the mouth that gave it is gone home to its supper. I have wanted that trick my whole long life and never once thought to ask for it, because I did not know there was a trick to ask for." He gave the stick back to Rònat, though Rònat pressed it on him to keep, and said that a man who had outlived every stick anyone had ever cut of him so far had no need to start keeping the ones that came after; better it stayed at the school, where a pupil could learn from it that even a name as old as his own could be held on so small a thing.

He went on east the next morning, refusing, as he always refused, the offer of a horse, and word of him came to the school twice more in the years after, from travellers who had passed him on some other road entirely, walking still.

## V. Of the Keepers Who Would Not Write

Dòmhnall Crùbach died at Dùn dhearg in his hundred and eightieth year, the same season Rònat turned four and forty, and the household stood the customary night and day with his chair empty before Sìleas Dhonn, his sister's daughter, went in and sat down in it and did not get up again for the better part of a year. She had kept, through all the years of her uncle's reign, her own fair copy of the waypoint-keepers' tallies locked in a chest with her mother's marriage-cloth, three years of Ceann mhin's figures that did not match the count of what had passed through Ceann mhin's own gates, and she had told no one why she kept them beyond that a column that did not add was worth keeping until someone asked to see it. In the first winter of her own reign she had that chest brought up and opened before the whole household, and sent word to every waypoint house on the island that the keeper of it should come to Dùn dhearg within the year and render account of the tolls taken in his own hand or another's, in whatever manner he judged fit to render it, so long as it could be checked against what the road-guards themselves had counted passing the same gate.

Word of the sound-marks had reached Dùn dhearg by then, carried the length of the island the way any new trick travelled, on the tongues of carriers and the word of stewards, though no keeper of a waypoint house had yet troubled to learn the scheme himself, it being, as far as most of them were concerned, a schoolboy's trick of the stag's people and no business of a toll-house. It was the keeper of Muileann shean, an old man named Beathan mac Dhonnchaidh who had held that house since before Dòmhnall Crùbach's own accession, who came to Dùn dhearg in the second year of Sìleas Dhonn's reign with his tally recited from memory in the old way, name for name and count for count, the way every keeper before him had ever rendered an account to any ruler; and Sìleas Dhonn heard him through to the end without once interrupting, and then asked him, plainly, how she was to know that what he had just recited was the same tally he had recited to the road-guard who rode through his gate that same season, there being no way to set one telling against the other once both were three seasons gone and held only in two separate memories that might, without either man meaning to lie, have drifted from each other in the telling.

Beathan mac Dhonnchaidh had no answer to that beyond his own long honesty, which Sìleas Dhonn did not doubt and said so, and let him go home without penalty; but she sent, within the month, to the far-eastern school, asking whether a teacher of the stag might be spared to come to Dùn dhearg and teach her own clerks the sound-marks, so that a keeper's tally, cut once at the gate in the road-guard's presence, might be carried whole to the hall instead of trusted a second time to a memory that had crossed the whole island since.

It was Rònat himself who went, in his forty-sixth year, the last long road he walked before age began to slow him, and he taught Sìleas Dhonn's clerks the strokes and the vowels over a winter at Dùn dhearg much as he had once taught them to pupils half his own age in the holy wood, finding the ruler's clerks quicker with the knife than any pupil he had taught before, being grown men and women already trained to a column of figures and wanting only the new marks to set beside the old ones they already knew. Ailig mac Ruairidh, keeper of the toll at Ceann mhin, whose house had been named in Sìleas Dhonn's own three years of mismatched figures, was the first keeper summoned to render account after the clerks were taught, and the first ordered to bring his tally cut in the new marks rather than carried in his head, checked against a second cutting made by the road-guard at his own gate the same season; and when the two tallies were set side by side at Dùn dhearg before the whole hall, in the second winter of Sìleas Dhonn's reign, they did not match, by a sum neither honest forgetting nor an honest drift of memory could account for, and Ailig mac Ruairidh, who had judged, like every keeper of Ceann mhin before him, that his own word carried further than any road-guard's, found for the first time that a stick cut at his own gate had carried further than his word.

He was not the last keeper found out that way, nor even, in that first year, the worst of them; but he was the first, and word of it ran the length of the carriers' road faster than any letter could have carried it, so that keepers who had never troubled to learn the new marks sent, within the season, to ask whether a teacher might be spared them too, having heard it said, by carriers who had stood in the hall at Dùn dhearg and seen the two sticks set side by side, that a keeper caught out by his own gate's stick had no more room left to argue than a man caught with the stolen thing still in his hand. The clerks who set Ailig mac Ruairidh's two tallies side by side and read out the difference between them, plainly, before the hall, did it in the new marks, off two sticks that neither man's memory could soften afterward however either of them told the story elsewhere. Sìleas Dhonn took no more from him than the sum itself, made whole, and let him keep his house, saying that a keeper caught once and shamed before the whole hall commonly kept an honester tally after than a keeper hanged for it would have taught any other keeper to be, there being no other keeper left alive to learn the lesson from a hanging.

It was in the same winter, cutting the record of Ailig mac Ruairidh's shortfall for the hall's own keeping, that Rònat set down, for the clerks to copy and learn by, the first whole sentence any hand at the hall had yet cut in the new marks: that the toll of Ceann mhin, counted at the gate and counted again at the hall, had come short by a sum the gate's own count made plain, and that the keeper had answered for it before Sìleas Dhonn and her household. It was a plain thing to say and a plainer thing to have needed saying at all, after so many years of tallies rendered on trust and kept only as long as one old man's breath held out to repeat them; but it stood after, cut whole on its length of ash in the hall's own keeping, saying the same thing every time any clerk's thumb ran down it, in a season when the man who had spoken it first was already three days gone home on the eastern road, and would not himself have known, walking it, that his own words had outlasted the telling.

He walked that road a slower man than he had walked the road to Cnoc bheag two years before with Sìleas nic Ruairidh beside him and a question about an old stone still open between them, and he thought, somewhere on the third of those days, of Aonghas Cam by the fire in the last winter of his life, groping after a name he had said clean a hundred times before and not always finding it, and of the answer the old man had given a boy of four and twenty who had complained, once, of losing the thread of a teaching within a season of hearing it. There was no help for it, Aonghas Cam had said, there being no stick cut yet that held a word instead of a count. Rònat came home to the holy wood in the last light of the fifth day, an old man himself now by the school's own reckoning, and found the pupils waiting on him for the evening's counting the way pupils had always waited on a teacher, and cut that evening's tally as he had cut ten thousand tallies before it, one stroke to a beast, before he sat down by his own fire and, for the first time in all the years since he had first set his own name on a stick, cut nothing further that night at all, being content, for once, to let the day go unrecorded and trust it, as every day before the sound-marks had ever been trusted, to memory alone.
