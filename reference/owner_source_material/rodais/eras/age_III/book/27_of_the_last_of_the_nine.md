# Of the Last of the Nine

*FE 1,010 – 1,122*

> *Dh'fhairtlich air an fhèath fhèin fuireach.*
> Even the calm could not stay calm.
>
> — said of Eithne nic Ailein by the reciters of Cathair mhòr, after her

## I. Of the Hall at Cathair mhòr

Eithne nic Ailein kept her hall at Cathair mhòr in the hundredth generation of her own house, though she called none of them her house, since a woman who has buried her own great-great-grandchildren counts her kin by some longer measure than generations. She was the youngest of the nine who had cut the cord at the Roinn, and by the tenth century of the Fire she was also the last of them living, though she felt it first in the way a hall feels emptier before anyone tells you who has died, and knew it for a fact only later.

She was old past two thousand nine hundred years and did not look it, or rather looked it in a way that had stopped changing: the long years did that, her father's blood held its shape once it had settled into age, the way a stone settles into the bank of a river and is not moved again by any flood after the first hundred. Her hands were thin and quick still. Her hair had been white so long that no one living remembered it any other colour, not even her own stewards, whose grandfathers had died in service to her. She sat in the lowland hall she had sat in since before the Peace of the Threshold, and she heard the island's news the way a person hears weather come in from a long way off: felt before it was named.

Her household kept her days simple, by her own long-standing order: bread and fish and the wool of her own sheds, a fire lit whatever the season, and no one troubled to bring her the disputes of Cathair mhòr's own share, which she had handed to a council of stewards a hundred years before and had not once taken back. She had, in her time, cut a knot at a gathering-house, sworn an oath on a threshold beside a carried chair, and buried more kin than most halls on the island had ever held living at one time; and by the tenth century of the Fire she had settled, without meaning to settle anywhere, into the office of an old woman who is asked nothing and told everything, which she had come to think, on her better days, the most restful office a person of her blood could hold.

In the tenth century of the Fire the news that came to her hall was mostly of the west, where a rìgh she had never met, a great-grandson of Sìleas Dhonn called Iain Garbh, was fighting a war over a road-toll with a household at Caol leathan that would not send its share to Dùn dhearg. Eithne heard the first of it from a wool-merchant come up from the coast with a load for her own weaving-sheds, a man who told the tale the way traders tell any tale, for what it was worth in the telling.

"They say the Keeper sent his messengers home with nothing," the merchant said, "and the rìgh burned a toll-house for it, and the whole west is up in arms over a basket of fish-bones."

"A basket of fish-bones," said Eithne, who had heard stranger reasons for a war and started one or two herself, long before this merchant's grandmother was born. "Tell it me again from the beginning."

She heard it three times more before the war ended, each telling a little different, as tellings are, and she did not go to it and did not send to it. Cathair mhòr's own share had kept its tolls and its peace both, and she had long since given up riding to wars that were another share's business to end. The last of the nine, whoever she was, stayed by her own fire.

## II. Of the Harbour Wall at Caol fhiadhaich

It was told to her afterward, by the same wool-merchant on his next journey, how the town of Caol fhiadhaich on the south-western shore had sent its own boats out in the worst of the siege to feed the besieged town of Caol leathan by night, running fish past the rìgh's watch-fires on the hill, because the two towns had markets in each other's children and would not see either starve for a quarrel neither had made.

"Iain Garbh gave them a share of the tolls for it, after," the merchant said, "the western tolls, a good share, and they've put up a wall round the harbour with the silver of it, the first stone wall that coast has had."

Eithne had never seen Caol fhiadhaich and never would; it lay three hundred miles from her own hall by the roads she no longer travelled, and she pictured it the way an old woman pictures a place from a stranger's words, as something both smaller and grander than it was, a wall of pale stone standing up out of grey water with boats drawn under it. She thought, hearing it, that it was a fair thing to do with a war's spoils, better than most she had heard of in her father's day, when spoils had meant the taking of a share and the burning of a hall. She said so to her steward, an old man himself by then, and the steward, who had heard her say such things before, only nodded and let her say it, the way a household lets an old woman have her thoughts undisturbed.

It was not her wall to build, and she sent nothing west. But she asked, in the years after, whenever a trader came up from that coast, whether the wall still stood, and was told each time that it did, a little higher each telling, as walls grow in the telling the way wars shrink.

## III. Of the Death of Iain Garbh

Iain Garbh lived long after the war he had made, and died at last an old man in his own hall at Dùn dhearg, some thirty-four years on, in the seven hundred and forty-fourth winter of Ailean's line, though by then no one troubled to count it that way any more. He had ruled forty years past the war's end, and had never once, in all that time, gone up the mountain road to Dùn ìseal to stand before the cup, though every rìgh before him and every rìgh after would go at least once in a reign.

Eithne had this from a Keeper's messenger, one of the young men the Hall sent out now with the season's news, a boy of perhaps sixty who had never known a world without the Small-Burning Law and thought it as old as the vein itself.

"He would not have the flame at his own burying," the messenger told her, standing in her hall with the cold of the road still on him. "His daughter had him laid in the floor of the hall at Dùn dhearg, under the hearthstone where his own father used to sit, and not on the slope at Dùn ìseal where the kings before him lie. The Hall did not ask for him, and I do not think it would have been given him if it had asked."

"He never forgave the flame for the war," Eithne said. It was not a question, though the messenger answered it as one.

"They say he never forgave it for saying no to him," the boy said. "Not for the war itself. He'd have forgiven a war. He didn't forgive being refused."

Eithne sat with that a while after the boy had gone on his way, because she had known men like Iain Garbh, more of them than this messenger could guess from a name in a chronicle: men who would take any hurt from a fight but not from a flame that would not fight beside them. She had watched Ìomhar Dearg wash his hands at a ford in the very first year of the Fire and known even then that a man who takes a thing by the sword remembers the taking longer than he remembers the thing. Iain Garbh had wanted the flame's blessing on a war the flame would not bless, and had gone without it, and had died still wanting it, buried under his own hearthstone rather than under the mountain, because a man's grave is the last word he gets to have.

"He had a daughter," Eithne said to her steward, when the household had gone in to their supper and she sat on alone by her own low fire, which she kept lit even in summer now, more for the company of it than the warmth. "Wise, they tell me. Let us hear of her instead."

## IV. Of Catrìona Ghlic and the Breach Mended

Catrìona Ghlic ruled at Dùn dhearg from her father's death, and the name the island gave her within ten years of her crowning was Catrìona the Wise, which is not a name given lightly to a ruler by the people who pay her tolls, and Eithne, hearing it, thought it the kind of name a daughter earns by undoing what her father did rather than by doing anything grand of her own.

What Catrìona undid, first of all, was the coldness between the hall and the Hall. She sent her own son, a boy not yet come to his full growth, to sit a season with the flame at Dùn ìseal, to carry the baskets and learn the galleries the way any hearth's child might, and this had not been done by the royal house since the division of keeping in Fionnlagh Dall's day, when the rìgh had kept the spears and the Keeper had kept the flame and the two had gone their separate ways for the better part of nine centuries.

Eithne heard of it from a knot-keeper's granddaughter, one of the last of that dwindling trade, who still came to Cathair mhòr twice a year with the tallies the old way, on a cord, though the letters were beginning by then to be spoken of in the east, a strange new thing out of the stag's schools that Eithne had heard of but not yet seen.

"They say the Keeper wept to see him come up the path," the woman said, "a boy of the royal blood carrying a basket like any hearth's son, and asking no more of the Hall than any other boy would ask. And when his season was out he did not want to go home."

"Did they let him stay?"

"They sent him home. A rìgh's son belongs at the rìgh's hall, whatever he wants for himself. But he goes back every year since, at the turn of the season, and they say the Keeper keeps a place for him at the long table without being asked."

Eithne thought of the flame kept once in a keepers' hut of turf and stone, before the Hall was built at all, and of the long quarrel of spears and flame that had run under every reign since Ìomhar Dearg first won a share with the sword before any was given him. It seemed to her, sitting in her own hall so long a time on, that the quarrel had never once been about the flame itself, which asked nothing of anyone, but about which of the island's two powers would be seen to bow first, and that a boy carrying a basket without being asked to had done what nine hundred years of rulers and Keepers, careful of each other's pride, had not managed between them.

"Tell them at Cathair mhòr," she said to her own steward, "that when the boy is a man and rules at Dùn dhearg, this hall will send its own tithe to the Hall's handful without waiting to be asked, the way it has not done since my father's father's day."

It was done, and it held, a small thing set beside a war and a wall and a king's grave, but Eithne, who had seen a great many large things come to nothing, had learned by then to trust the small ones that held. She thought, sending the order, of Aodh Crom standing on the hill in the first year of the Fire and asking that the vein be in no share. He had meant the flame for every hand equally, and it had taken the island's two powers nine hundred years to learn the meaning of it, a rìgh's pride and a Keeper's pride each finding it harder to swallow than any war.

## V. Of the Two Houses and the Wool of Inis mhòr

Catrìona built two houses on the northern road in the years after, and Eithne heard of both from carriers before she heard of them from anyone at court, which was the way of it now: the carriers' roads carried news faster than any messenger the halls could send, because a carrier told his tale at every waypoint house between one end of the island and the other, and a messenger told his only at the end of his riding.

The first house stood above the river port in the north where the wool trade gathered, on ground that had held nothing before but a burned foundation and a bad name; Catrìona had it built fresh and gave it to the carriers of the northern baskets, who took the Hall's grain up to the wool country and brought wool back for it. The second stood further on, out on the open moor between the port and the northern coast, a plain stone house built for no other purpose than to be there when the snow came down early and caught a string of carts between one hearth and the next with nowhere to shelter. The Hall paid its keepers in grain and the crown paid them in cloth, and the keepers, being carriers' people themselves and used to complaint as a form of thanks, grumbled at both payments and stayed at their post through every winter all the same.

"The wool town has grown past every other town in the north," a carrier told Eithne, an old man himself, near the end of his last run before his knees gave out the trade for good. "They've asked the crown for a road-guard of their own, the way the wolf-sworn keep the west, and the crown has given them one. There's more silver moves through that port in a season now than moves through Dùn dhearg in a year, they say, though I'd not swear to the saying, only the telling of it."

Eithne had seen the wool herself once, in bales brought south along the very road the carrier spoke of, soft grey fleece from the northern flocks that her own weavers turned into cloth finer than anything the lowland sheep gave. She held a handful of it in her hand, that last winter she was strong enough to walk her own sheds, and turned it over between her fingers the way her own weavers did when they were judging a fleece's worth, though she had no craft in the judging and only liked the feel of it, soft and slightly greasy with the lanolin still in it, smelling of a country she would never see. She thought that she had outlived the four rules and the nine shares and the double oaths and a war fought over fish-bones, and here at the end of it all was a town she had never seen growing rich on wool, asking the crown for a guard the way a child asks a parent for a coat, and being given it without argument, as if the asking and the giving were the most ordinary thing in the world.

It seemed to her a good sign of the age, though she said so to no one, having learned that an old woman's good signs are mostly taken for an old woman's fancies. She thought, too, though she kept this thought closer still, that a town could grow that rich and that fast only in an age when no lord's household needed to fear another's spears across its own boundary stone, which had not always been true of the island and had been true for less than half the years of her own life; and she set the fleece down and went back into her hall a little comforted by a thing she could not have explained to her own steward without sounding, to her own ear, like an old woman talking to herself.

## VI. Of Niall Òg on the Last Road

Niall Òg came to Cathair mhòr in the hundred and ninth year of that century of the Fire, unannounced, on foot, with a single household man behind him carrying nothing but a blanket and a skin of water, and Eithne's steward came to her chamber before the sun was well up to say that an old man was asking at the gate who gave his name as Niall, grandson of Ailean, and would not be turned away though he looked in no state to be let stand in the yard.

She had not seen him in over four hundred years. She went down to the gate herself rather than have him brought up, because some meetings are owed the courtesy of going to them, and found him leaning on a stick worn smooth by a use far longer than the making of it, a small bent man with a face gone the colour and texture of old bark, and eyes that had not dimmed at all, which was, she thought, the cruellest part of what the long years did to a person: the eyes went on seeing everything clearly long after the rest had begun to fail.

"You've taken your time," she said, "coming to see me."

"I've been coming for four hundred years," said Niall Òg. "I kept getting distracted by roads. There was always one more stretch of it I hadn't walked, and a man doesn't like to die owing the island a road he never saw."

"And have you seen them all now?"

"Most," he said. "Enough. I met a rìgh's daughter on one of them once, mending a broken stretch with her own staff, and told her a thing about a knotted cord that I don't think she's forgotten, and I've been glad of that meeting ever since, more than of most things I did with a spear in my hand in the early days, which were more than I'd care to count now."

She had him brought in and fed and given the best chair by her own low fire, and he sat in it the whole of that day and would not be moved to a bed until the light was long gone, because, he said, he had slept in enough beds in four centuries and would rather sit up a night talking to the only other person alive who remembered the smoke in the gathering-house on the night of the handful.

They talked of it. Eithne had not spoken of that night to anyone in a hundred years or more, because everyone she might have spoken to about it was long dead, and a memory kept alone in one head begins to feel less like a memory and more like a thing one dreamed; and it was, she found, a relief beyond anything she had felt in a long while to say to another living person, "I knelt by Ciorstaidh's knot a long while before I cut it," and have that other person say, "I remember. You were the only one who knelt. The rest of us just cut and walked on."

"I've thought since," Eithne said, "that kneeling changed nothing. She still lost her share to mine that night."

"It changed you," said Niall Òg. "I watched it. You were never quite so quick to cut anything again, all the years after, not a knot and not much else. I don't think anyone else in that house noticed. I noticed, because I was watching you instead of my own knot, which is a poor way to conduct yourself at a gathering of seven hundred and twelve heirs, but I was young and you were the one worth watching."

He told her the night of the handful once more before he slept, the whole of it, every hearth burning its measure at once and the smoke lying in the rafters and the nine knives going round the cord, and she saw that he told it now the way a teller tells a tale rather than the way a man remembers a thing that happened to him, smoothed by four hundred years of telling into its final shape, every pause in the right place; and she understood, hearing it told that way for what she guessed rightly would be the last time, that this had become his life's whole use in his last years, the carrying of that one night down through every year since, so that when the last of the nine who had lived it was gone, someone would still be able to say how it had truly been, in the smoke, with the lamps.

He died in her own hall three days later, in the chair by her fire, in his sleep, without pain that anyone could see on his face after. The evening before, he had told her that he had walked the road to her gate on purpose, the last road he meant to walk, because he did not want to die on some empty stretch between waypoint houses with no one by to hear the story out, and would rather it were told once more, to her, than carried into the ground with him half-spoken.

She sat with him the night after he died, alone, because she found she did not want her household in the room, and thought that of the seven hundred and twelve who had walked that cord with their lamps, she was now the only one who could say she had been there, in body and in memory both, and that a night no longer walked by a living foot begins, from the moment of the last such walker's death, to belong entirely to the tellers, who had not been there, and would tell it their own way from then on, well or badly, as tellers do.

She had him carried up the mountain road herself, at her own cost, though he was no kin of her own share and she owed his line no such honour by any rule the Roinn had made; she had him laid near the old graves at the slope below Dùn ìseal, among the first heirs, because she judged, and no one troubled to argue her judgement, that a man who had spent four hundred years walking every road on the island back to that one house had earned the right to lie near it at the last.

## VII. Of the Last of the Nine

Eithne outlived him thirteen years, and in those years she felt, for the first time since her father's death at Àth na Fala, entirely alone in a way that was not loneliness so much as a kind of clear sight, the sight of a woman standing at the top of a stair and looking down and finding no one else on any step of it. She had outlived Murchadh, who had cut first at the Roinn; she had outlived Ìomhar, whose blood had thinned in his own line faster than any of the others'; she had outlived Fionnghal and Artair and Cormac and the four whose shares the tellers named in different orders; and now, with Niall Òg gone, she had outlived every one of the seven hundred and twelve who had walked that cord with a lamp on the night of the handful, and every child of Ailean Mòr who had stood weeping at the ford on the third night of his falling.

Loud grief, she had learned long before, was mostly for the living to hear, and there was no one left of her own generation to hear hers, so she grieved the way she had grieved for the others: quietly, at her own low fire, in the evenings, when the household had gone to its supper and left her the room. Her hall went on as it always had. She heard the island's news as it came to her, of a market that had begun at the fords of Baile Mòr dhomhain, where the lords of the deep river gathered cattle and sheep and horses and hides and the white stone of the hills for sale on the day after each new moon, and took one fleece in forty from every seller for the privilege of the ground; a small thing, a market like a hundred others the island had grown since the Peace, but it pleased her to hear of it, a new thing built on old ground by people who had never heard her name and would not have known it if they had.

Her steward, in those last months, thought her mind had begun to wander, because she asked him more than once what the day was, and made him say the year over twice to be sure he had it right. It had not wandered at all. She wanted, at the end, to know exactly how long the count had run: three thousand years and then some, a number that had stopped meaning anything to her decades before and had become, in her last year, a thing she wanted fixed precisely, the way a weaver wants to know the exact length of a cloth before she cuts it. When he told her the tale of it, one last winter evening, he found himself weeping before he had finished the sum, though she herself only nodded, as if he had confirmed a measurement she had already taken by her own hand and only wanted checked against another's.

She died in her hall at Cathair mhòr on a quiet morning with no illness anyone could name, simply not waking when her women came to her at the hour she always rose, and the reciters who kept the rolls of that hall wrote of her afterward in the old formula they gave to the children of the first king, so that the tale should be told the same way it had always been told of Ailean's line: and all the days of Eithne were three thousand and twenty-one years, and she died.

After her, none of the nine who had cut the cord at the Roinn was living, and the great shares that had once been carried in seven hundred and twelve living memories became names cut into boundary stones, read by carriers who did not know whose hand had first claimed the ground they walked, or why. The long years went on thinning in the king's kin after her, generation by generation, the way a river thins as it runs from the mountain to the sea, until there would come a day, though no one then could have named it, when no one on the island could be told from anyone else by the length of the life allotted him.

At the fords of Baile Mòr dhomhain that same season, on the day after the new moon, the market ran as it always ran: cattle lowing, a fleece in forty going into the lords' baskets, a boy haggling over a length of the white hill-stone with a trader who would not come down on his price, none of them sparing a thought for an old woman who had died three hundred miles off in a hall most of them would never see, whose knife, seven hundred years and more before any of their grandparents were born, had cut the last knot on a cord that no longer existed, in a house that no longer stood, for a kingdom that had long since forgotten it had ever been undivided.
