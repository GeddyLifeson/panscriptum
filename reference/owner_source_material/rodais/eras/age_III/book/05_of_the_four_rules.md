# Of the Four Rules

*FE 1 – 2*

## I. Of the Grass That Did Not Come Back

Seven hundred and twelve knots had been counted, and not one thing settled by the counting. That was what Niall Òg came to understand in the days after the cord was carried out of the gathering-house and put by, and he was slower to understand it than most, because he had gone about the valley all that autumn in the good humour of a man who has just watched something enormous be done, and it took the first frost to teach him that the doing was only the naming of the trouble, not the end of it.

The camps did not break up. He had thought they would: that the heirs, having stood up and given their lines and had them tied, would scatter back to whatever hall or headland or hill-farm they called their own and wait to be sent for. A handful did. Most did not. Word went about the fires that the shares could not be cut until it was agreed how they were to be cut, and that this could not be agreed in a day, and the heirs, who had waited weeks for the tying of a cord, found that they could wait a good deal longer for the cutting of it, so long as they were near enough to have a voice in how it went. By the turn of the month there were more tents in the valley than there had been at the king's death, because every heir who could afford to had sent home for more household, more baggage, more argument.

The valley could not carry it. It had fed the king's own following for a season and the host of the ford for three days and the whole gathering for a fortnight of knotting, and each of those had left a mark on it that the next one found still raw. The grass along the river, which had been grazed to the earth by the second week of the gathering, did not come up green again that spring; it came up thin and grey and was grazed to the earth again before midsummer, and it did not come up at all the spring after that, or the spring after that. Sìne's daughters said afterward that it was three years before a beast could get a full belly on that ground, and that carriers coming down from the north for the first time after Roinn na Rìoghachd asked what blight had struck the valley, not knowing that the blight had been seven hundred and twelve households and their horses camped on it through two winters running.

Niall walked the camps most days, because he had nothing else to do that anyone would let him do, and because he found that people would say things to him on a walk that they would not say sitting down. He was not one of the great lines. His mother had been a daughter of the king who died two thousand years before the ford, and his father, a grandson of Fionnghal's line, had died a hundred years back of a fever that a longer-lived man might have thrown off; so Niall stood in the cord as a small knot in a small line, owed twice over as he liked to say, and owed little enough by either half. It left him free to walk where the greater heirs could not, because nobody thought his good opinion worth buying and nobody feared his bad one. He carried word between fires that would not otherwise have spoken, and he came, before that year was out, to know the shape of every quarrel in the valley better than any of the men who were having it.

There were four shapes to it, when he had walked enough camps to see them clear. Four rules, and each one had a hundred heirs standing behind it, and every heir standing behind one thought the other three so plainly wrong that he could not understand how a grown man came to hold them.

## II. Of the Eldest and the Living

The first rule was Murchadh Mòr's, though he did not call it a rule; he called it the way it had always been done, in the households he knew, when a man died and left more than one child. The eldest took the whole and owed the others a living out of it. Murchadh was himself the eldest of all the king's living children, near three thousand years old and built like a doorway, and he put the case the way a man puts a case he has never had to argue before because everyone around him has always agreed with it: the eldest holds, because the eldest is closest to the blood and was longest at the king's side, and the rest are fed from the eldest's hand as they always were. He did not trouble to say what this would mean for himself. Every heir in the valley could count that it would mean Murchadh Mòr holding the whole of Ailean Mòr's kingdom and everyone else in it standing at his table for their supper, and this was, as Niall said to Eithne one evening, a reason Murchadh liked the rule a good deal better than most people did.

The second rule was harder to fix on one mouth, because half the lowland heirs held it and none of them had thought it out past the shape of their own complaint. It said that the living should hold, in the order of their nearness and no further back than that: sons and daughters first, if any lived, and their children after, but no weight given to a man's place in the birth order, only to whether he still drew breath. Eithne, who was herself the youngest living child of the king and stood to gain a good deal by it, argued it hardest and argued it well, because she had the wit to see that a rule of pure age would leave her with nothing at all, being the youngest, while a rule of nearness to the living blood put her on a level with Murchadh whatever the years between them. Niall did not think the less of her for arguing her own good; he had never met an heir in that valley who was arguing anything else, under whatever coat of principle he wore over it, and he thought Eithne wore hers more honestly than most, because she never once pretended the rule was fair to the dead.

It was over the dead that the third rule stood or fell, and it was the hardest fought of the four, because it was fought less between the living heirs than between the living and a silence. Ailean Mòr had been having children for four thousand years, and a great many of them had died before him, in wars and by accident and by the slow thinning that came even to the long-lived at the last, and their children and grandchildren stood in the valley in their hundreds, wearing black wool at the throat as Sìne had knotted it, and they said, through whichever of their number could speak loudest, that a line did not end because the man at its head had died before his father; that the dead's children stood in his place exactly as they would have stood in it had he lived one day longer than the king; and that any rule which forgot the dead was a rule made by people who found it convenient to forget them, which was true, and said so plainly that even the heirs who found it convenient did not much enjoy hearing it.

The fourth rule Niall heard first from a boy of perhaps four hundred years, a great-great-grandson of the king who could not have told you the name of his own great-grandfather, who said to him at a fire one night that the whole of the argument was foolish, because the fairest thing, the only thing that could not be quarrelled with afterward, was to throw lots for the shares and let the ancestors decide it as they decided everything else worth deciding. Niall asked him whose idea this was, and the boy said it was his own, and Niall believed him, because he heard the same idea independently from a dozen more of the youngest heirs before the winter was out, always from someone with the least claim and the least to lose by chance, and never once from anyone with an old man's memory of exactly how many grandchildren stood ahead of him.

"You want the lot," Niall said to one of them, "because you're young enough that anything is better than what the other three rules would give you."

The boy said that was so, and did not seem ashamed of it, and Niall found he liked him rather better for saying so than he liked most of the older heirs, who wanted exactly the same thing and dressed it up as justice.

## III. Of the Words Niall Carried

He did not mean to become a carrier of words between the two loudest voices in the valley, Eithne and Murchadh, and he told it afterward as though it had happened to him rather than by his own doing; but the truth, which he told less often, was that he had gone to Murchadh's fire of his own will in the second week of the sitting, to ask a question about grazing on his own account, and had found himself asked in turn what Eithne had said of the eldest's rule that morning, because word had come to Murchadh's fire garbled, and Murchadh wanted it straight. Niall told him straight. He had heard Eithne say it herself, at her own fire, not an hour before: that a man's eldest right to his father's cattle did not make him fit to hold nine hundred grandchildren's worth of a kingdom, and that the day the eldest rule was sworn, half the lines in the valley would begin quietly arming, because no one under the age of a thousand years would sit still under it. Murchadh heard this without moving his face, which Niall came to know was how the old man showed that a thing had gone home.

After that it became a habit. He went from Eithne's fire to Murchadh's and back several times a week, carrying nothing that either of them could not have sent by a steward, except that a steward would have softened it, and Niall did not. He found, to his own surprise, that both of them preferred it that way. Murchadh told him once, gruffly, over the head of the fire, that Niall was the only man in the valley he could be sure was repeating the words and not the meaning some go-between had decided to give them; and Eithne told him much the same thing in her own way, which was to say nothing and simply begin, when he arrived, "Tell him this," without wasting breath on why she trusted him to carry it straight.

He did not always enjoy it. There were nights he came away from Murchadh's fire with words in his mouth that he knew would put Eithne into a rage before he had finished the sentence, and he carried them anyway, because a message half-told was worse than one told whole, whatever it cost the teller. He watched the two of them, over that winter, come to understand each other's positions better than either understood any of the smaller lines, and hate each other's positions no less for the understanding; and he thought, though he did not say it to either of them, that this was very likely the nearest thing to peace that the eldest rule and the rule of the living were ever going to manage, two people who despised each other's case and had stopped mistaking the other for a fool.

The lines of the dead he did not carry for, not at first, because no single mouth spoke for them the way Murchadh spoke for the eldest or Eithne for the living; they spoke through whichever grandson had the readiest tongue that week, and the readiest tongue changed. But by midwinter one voice had risen above the rest among them, an old man with a stick who had felt the hollow knot at the door and said, "That's my mother," and who now stood at the gathering-house steps most mornings and told anyone who would listen that the dead's children were being asked to disappear twice, once by their fathers' early deaths and once by a rule that would not count them; and Niall began carrying his words too, to Murchadh and to Eithne both, because he saw that neither of the two great voices had troubled to hear the old man out in full, each being too busy answering the other.

## IV. Of Mairsile in the Cold

The first winter of the sitting was hard on the valley's ground, and harder on some of the people in it than on the ground.

Mairsile nic Ailein, the king's white-haired daughter who had begun the keening in the valley on the night the herd-boy's news came, was old even among the king's children, older than Murchadh, and na Bliadhnachan Fada in her were nearly spent; and she had outlived a husband and two of her own children already before that autumn. She had sat through the knotting with her women about her, near the upper fires, and had said little in the councils of the four rules, being past the age of the kingdom mattering to her the way it mattered to the younger heirs. What she wanted, she told her women, was to go home before the snow; and she did not go home, because there was always one more day's argument that her line's steward thought she ought to hear, one more vote of the assembled dead that could not be taken without an elder of her age to lend it weight.

She died in the cold, in the second month of the new year, in a tent on the upper slope with her women about her and a brazier that had run low in the night because the wood had run short in the whole valley that winter, every hearth burning the same short ration as every other. Niall was not there. He heard of it in the morning from a woman of her household who came down to the lower fires white in the face, and he went up with her, because there seemed nothing else to do, and stood at the tent door while Mairsile's women wound her in her own cloak, and thought, not for the first time that winter, that the argument over who should hold the kingdom had already begun to cost the kingdom people who had never asked to be part of it.

She was carried down to the river two days after and laid in the ground there, near enough the ford that the carriers passing with their baskets could see the mound from the road; and for once in that whole long sitting there was no quarrel over where she should lie, because she had asked for the place herself before she died, plainly, to her women, and nobody thought it worth their trouble to dispute a dying woman's one wish when there were nine hundred living wishes still to be fought over. Niall stood at the graveside with Eithne on one side of him and, a little apart, Murchadh on the other, and neither of them spoke to the other across it, but neither of them left before the mound was closed, and Niall thought that this too, in its way, was a kind of agreement, the only one the valley had managed since the cord went on the floor.

He carried the word of her death to every fire in the valley himself, that day and the next, because he found he could not sit still with it; and at more than one fire he heard the same thing said, in different words, by people who had never met her: that the sitting had already killed one of the king's own children, and that whatever else the shares came to, they had better come to it before the next winter found another.

## V. Of the Share That Could Not Be Taken

Through all of it there was one line in the valley that argued for none of the four rules, because none of the four rules touched what it already held.

Ìomhar Dearg sat that winter much as he had sat through the knotting, apart from the loudest fires, with his own household about him and little enough said. He had held Dùn dhearg since before the cord was ever tied, taken it by the sword in the days after the ford, and he had made no claim on the four rules at all, because he did not need one; what he had, he had, and the argument of the valley was over what the rest of them were to have. This did not sit well with everyone. Four lines in particular, none of them close enough kin to Ìomhar to have any hope of the fort themselves, took to saying at their own fires, and then at other people's, that a kingdom parted by rule ought to be parted by rule entire, and that Dùn dhearg with its double bank and its burned halls and its living taken under one man's hand was as much a part of Ailean Mòr's kingdom as any acre still standing green, and ought to come into whatever settlement the rest came into rather than sitting outside it as a thing already decided.

Niall carried this to Murchadh, who said only that he did not see how it was to be done; and he carried it to Eithne, who said the same in fewer words; and when he pressed her on why, since she plainly agreed the complaint was just, she told him something he thought about for a long while after. "Ask any of the four how they mean to take it from him," she said. "Ask them who is to stand in that gap in the bank and tell Ìomhar's household that they answer to somebody else now. He didn't win it by a rule. He won it because he was there and the rest of us weren't, and a rule made after the fact can't put us there instead of him. You can make a hundred rules for dividing what nobody has taken yet. You can't make one that takes back what's already held."

He put this to the four lines himself, more than once, in something like her own words, and none of them had an answer for it beyond repeating the complaint louder, which told him, as much as anything did that winter, that the complaint and the remedy were two different things, and that a valley could go on being angry about a wrong for a very long time without ever finding a hand strong enough to put it right.

## VI. Of the Word Nobody Would Wear

There was one more thing that came up at every fire that winter, sooner or later, and it came up so often, and was refused so often, that by spring it had stopped being argued at all and had simply become understood: that whatever the shares came to, and whoever held the largest of them, no one of the nine was to be called what their father had been called. Niall heard it first from an old steward of Murchadh's line, half as a joke, that Earrann Mhurchaidh would want a rìgh over it the way the whole island had once had one, and heard Murchadh himself answer, not laughing, that he would sooner burn the share to the ground than sit in his father's seat and be called by his father's name for it, because he had seen what the name had cost the man who bore it and had no wish to invite his own kin to come and try to take it from him the same way.

It was Eithne who put the clause first in words that could be sworn to, at a fire late in the summer when the heirs of the nine strongest lines had begun, almost without anyone declaring it so, to meet apart from the rest: that none of the nine, whatever they held and however they came to hold it, should ever be called rìgh, or take the word for themselves, or suffer it to be given them by their own households; that the kingdom Ailean Mòr had made was ended with him, and what came after it was shares, held by heirs, and not a kingdom held by a king. Murchadh swore to it first, and swore it hardest, because he above all of them had spent a winter watching what the shape of a single crown could still do to men three thousand years old who ought to have known better. Ìomhar Dearg swore to it from his own fire, sending word rather than coming himself, which some of the others remarked on and none of them pressed. Every one of the nine swore it in the end, and Niall, standing near enough at more than one of the swearings to see the faces as well as hear the words, thought that a good half of them swore it the way a man swears off a thing he still wants, with the private thought that the word might yet come to one of their own children or grandchildren, once enough years had passed that nobody living could object; and he judged, rightly as it turned out, that this reservation would trouble nobody at all so long as the nine kept it out of their own mouths in their own lifetimes.

## VII. Of the Settlement

It was Aodh Crom, in the end, who put the four rules together, though it was not his place to put anything together, being no heir and holding no share; but he had sat through enough of the councils that summer, at the edge of the gathering-house with the flame's business finished for the day, that the heirs had come to ask his opinion the way men ask the opinion of someone who has nothing to gain by giving it.

He did not choose between the four rules. He told the assembled heads of the strongest lines, one evening in the last days of summer, that he did not think any one of the four could be sworn alone without half the valley taking up arms against it before the winter turned again, because each rule left out a claim that a great many people in that valley were not going to consent to seeing left out; and that the only settlement he could see holding was one that gave each rule enough to keep its people quiet, though none of them all it asked. Let the eldest lines hold na h-Earrannan Mòra, since the living heirs of most substance were, in the main, the eldest surviving of their branches in any case; let the shares run to nine, since nine lines could be found among the living with claim enough and following enough to hold a share and defend it; let a portion be set aside out of all nine together for the lines of the dead, so that no line vanished from the reckoning because its head had died a day too soon; and let the youngest, who wanted the lot, take some comfort in this much of it, that within each of the nine shares the parting among the living children and grandchildren should still be settled by drawing, where an equal claim could not otherwise be told apart.

It satisfied nobody entirely, and Niall came to think that this was very likely why it held. Murchadh had Earrann Mhurchaidh, which was the largest thing on the table and more than answered the eldest's rule so far as he was concerned; Eithne and her allies among the living heirs had shares of their own, proof that nearness of blood counted for something whatever a man's place in the birth order; the lines of the dead had their portion, grudging and smaller than they had asked for, but a portion, and a name for it, *Cuid nam Marbh*, so that it could never afterward be said the dead had been forgotten; and the youngest, muttering, had their throw of the lot within whatever share they fell under, which was not the throw across the whole kingdom that the boy at the fire had wanted, but was a throw, and kept a few hundred of the youngest and least-landed heirs from making trouble that autumn over having nothing at all.

Ìomhar Dearg's share was let stand where it stood, Dùn dhearg and the ground he had already taken, let stand beside the nine as a tenth that no rule had made, won by the sword and not by any of the four rules; the four lines who had argued against it argued once more, at the council itself, and were heard, and were not answered beyond Eithne's old words given back to them in Aodh Crom's mouth, that no rule made that day could put them where Ìomhar already stood; and they gave way in the end: nine of the strongest lines in the valley had already agreed among themselves, and there was no tenth strong enough to stand against the nine.

## VIII. Of the Agreement to Cut the Cord

They settled it, in the shape it would keep, in the last days before the turn of the year, at a council that ran two days and a night without the heads of the nine lines leaving the gathering-house floor; and Niall, who had carried words between them all that year and stood at more firesides than any other man in the valley, was let in to hear the end of it because Eithne asked for him by name, saying she wanted one honest ear in the room besides her own.

It was agreed, when it was agreed, without much ceremony. There was no swearing that night; the clause against the crown had already been sworn separately, at a dozen fires, over the summer, and needed no repeating. What was agreed was simpler and, to Niall's mind, colder than anything that had gone before it: that the cord Sìne had tied, seven hundred and twelve knots long, should be carried back into the gathering-house and laid on the floor again as it had been laid before, from the hearth to the door and back; and that on Oidhche an Dòrlaich, when every hearth in the hills burned its measure together, as it always had, the heads of the nine lines should each take a knife to it in turn, and cut it into nine lengths where the shares fell, each length carrying the knots of the heirs who stood under that share; and that whatever oaths needed swearing over the cutting, the clause against the crown and the peace of Talla na Lasrach's own ground among them, should be sworn that same night, once, in front of the whole valley, so that no line could say afterward it had not heard.

Murchadh Mòr agreed to it first, with the same still face he had shown all that winter. Eithne agreed after him, and looked once at Niall across the fire as she did, which he took, and told her afterward he had taken, for something between thanks and warning: thanks for a year's carrying, warning that the carrying was not finished, only changed. Word went out that night from line to line, fire to fire, down through the valley and up into the hills where the smaller households were camped, that the cord would be cut at the handful, and that every heir who meant to hold anything under na h-Earrannan Mòra had better be at Dùn ìseal to see it done.

Niall walked the camps a last time before the year turned, as he had walked them at the start, and found the valley much changed from the one he had walked with a light heart the autumn before: the grass gone from the riverbank, three winters before it would come green again though none of them yet knew that; the mound by the ford with Mairsile under it; Ìomhar's fires apart from the rest as they had always been; and at every hearth, instead of the seven hundred and twelve separate quarrels he had carried between all that year, one single date now, Oidhche an Dòrlaich, that every line in the valley was waiting on together, for the first time since the cord went first upon the floor.

He came back down to the lower fires as the light went, and found Eithne's steward waiting for him with a message, which was, he said afterward, the last one he ever carried that winter, and the shortest: that she wanted him at her own fire that night, to hear how the cutting was to be done, so that when it came he would know, better than most of the valley, exactly what he was watching.
