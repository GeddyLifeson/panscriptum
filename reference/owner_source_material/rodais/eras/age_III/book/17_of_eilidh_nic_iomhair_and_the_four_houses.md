# Of Eilidh nic Ìomhair and the Four Houses

*FE 381 – 397*

*Part Three: The Long Strife and the Threshold*

## I. Of the Empty Threshold

The Keeper who had held the flame after Eimhir nic Mhuirich was an old woman of the hearth-line by the time Eilidh nic Ìomhair first came to Dùn ìseal to ask a season's grace on a debt of coal her household owed the Hall, and she remembered the meeting afterward only for the Keeper's hands, which shook badly by then pouring the measure, and for the patience with which the woman waited for them to steady before she would let anyone else pour it for her. That Keeper died in the winter of the three hundred and eighty-first year of the age, at an age nobody troubled to reckon exactly, having kept the flame quietly and without incident for longer than most people at Dùn ìseal could remember any other way of things being kept. She died on the low stool beside the cup, having sat down there after the evening measure was poured to rest her legs before the walk to her own bed, and the boy sent in at first light to relight her lamp from Brìde's own flame found her still sitting upright with her hands folded in her lap, so that he stood a long moment in the doorway unsure whether he had come upon a woman asleep or a woman dead, and only knew the second when he had gone close enough to see that the coal in the small lamp beside her had burned out in the night for want of any hand to feed it, the first time in living memory that any lamp in Talla na Lasrach had been let go dark. The nine hearths gathered at the Hall in the spring to choose whoever would keep it after her, as they had gathered eight times before since Beathag Bhàn first took the coal from the slab.

Eilidh was two-and-forty that spring, a granddaughter's granddaughter of Ìomhar Dearg through a line that had long since stopped naming itself anything grander than a household of Dùn dhearg, and she had spent the better part of her life doing exactly what any woman of that household did: keeping accounts for the Red Share's steward, walking the granary rolls at threshing, and raising three children on ground her own grandmother had walked before her. She had never asked to be considered for the flame, and said so plainly to the two hearth-elders who came to Dùn dhearg to sound her out, sitting her down at her own table and asking her, in the roundabout way such questions are always first asked, whether she thought a woman of her particular blood might be persuaded to sit a season with the coal.

"You mean the king's blood," Eilidh said, because she had never in her life found much use in letting another person finish a sentence she could see coming from three words off, and the elders admitted that yes, that was rather the difficulty they had come to discuss.

It was late in the evening when they came, the two of them having walked from Dùn ìseal since morning and eaten nothing since, and Eilidh's own household gave them bread and a bowl of the same broth her children were fed on before either elder was let say a further word about hearths or flames or blood. One of the two, a thin man with a cutter's scarred hands who had spent forty years in the galleries before the nine hearths thought his knees too poor for the climb and set him instead to walking their errands, watched Eilidh feed her youngest from her own bowl while the older two argued over whose turn it was to fetch water, and said afterward, to the other elder on the road home, that he had gone to Dùn dhearg half expecting a woman puffed up with her own blood and had found instead a woman who could not get a full sentence out of her own table without being asked twice for the salt.

The difficulty was old and plain enough that nobody at the table needed it explained twice. The Binding had sworn the flame to no one of the king's blood, and had been kept to that oath, without exception, since Beathag Bhàn herself had been chosen for no reason grander than that she was of nobody's blood at all. Muireach Liath, Gormshuil, Fionnlagh Dall, Eimhir, the woman just carried up to the slope: none of them had been able to claim so much as a great-grandmother among Ailean's own children, and the nine hearths had never, in all that time, needed to ask themselves whether the rule still held, because no candidate worth considering had ever forced the question. Eilidh forced it. She was, by every reckoning the hearth-elders could make sitting at her own table that afternoon, plainly the ablest of the three names put forward that spring, and just as plainly a great-great-granddaughter of a man who had once burned a hall full of the living and called it a bier.

"Then choose one of the other two," Eilidh said, not unkindly, and meant it; she had said as much to her own husband the night before, and he had told her that a woman who did not want a thing badly enough to argue for it seldom got it, and seldom deserved to.

## II. Of the Season of Refusal

The nine hearths chose her anyway, in the spring of the three hundred and eighty-second year, by a count neither Sliochd Mhuirich nor anyone else who kept the tally troubled to say aloud, and word of the choosing reached Sliochd Mhuirich at Dùn ìseal before Eilidh herself had finished packing a single basket to travel there. The house that had held the flame since Muireach Liath's daughter first took the coal from her father's dying hand did not send word back that it welcomed the choice. It sent word that it did not accept it, and for the better part of a year afterward the cup at Talla na Lasrach sat under the care of the hearth-line's own eldest, unfilled by any new hand, while every household with an opinion on the matter found occasion to air it at every market and every threshing floor on the mountain.

The argument made loudest that year, and the one Eilidh herself was made to answer more times than she could count, was put to her first by a cousin of her own who meant it as comfort: that there was, by then, hardly a household left in the hills without some thread of the king's own blood run through it somewhere, a great-great-grandmother here, a disowned younger son there, so that the Binding's old rule against the blood had become, in practice, a rule against nearly everybody or nobody at all, and Eilidh's own blood was no different in kind from half the blood in the valley, only better remembered. Eilidh heard the argument out from more mouths than her cousin's, and found it no comfort whatever the number of times it was offered her, because it answered a question nobody at Dùn ìseal had actually asked. Nobody doubted that half the mountain carried a drop of Ailean's line by now, diluted past any household's power to name it. What Sliochd Mhuirich doubted, and said so without troubling to soften it, was whether a woman who could stand up at any gathering of the Red Share and be named, plainly and without dilution, as a granddaughter's granddaughter of Ìomhar Dearg, could be trusted to keep a flame the Binding had sworn against exactly that kind of naming.

"They are not wrong to ask it," Eilidh told her cousin, the third or fourth time he brought the argument to her door meaning to cheer her with it, and the flatness with which she said it left him with nothing further to offer. She did not, in that whole season of waiting, once send word to Dùn ìseal pressing her own claim, nor did she withdraw it; she went on keeping the Red Share's granary rolls as she had always kept them, and let the argument run its course among people who had more patience for arguing it than she did.

The waiting wore harder on her household than it did on her. Her husband, a cutter's son with no blood worth naming on either side, took to answering for her at the granary door whenever a steward's runner arrived with some fresh word from Dùn ìseal, saying only that his wife was at the rolls and had nothing to add to what she had said the last time, until the runners stopped troubling to knock and simply left whatever they carried with him instead. Her eldest daughter, a girl of nineteen who had grown up hearing her own great-great-grandfather's name spoken at markets the way other households spoke of weather, asked her once, straight out at the supper table, whether she wanted the flame at all or only refused to be told she could not have it, and Eilidh considered the question longer than she considered most, turning her spoon in the broth a while before she answered that she had stopped, some years back, being able to tell the two apart in her own mind, and did not think the difference mattered as much as her daughter seemed to.

## III. Of the Condition

What finally settled it was a condition offered rather than any argument won, and it came, as such things at Dùn ìseal generally did, from the hearth-line's own eldest rather than from any voice on Eilidh's side of the question. She was a woman named for no one now living to remember, old enough to have helped carry Fionnlagh Dall's own body south along the eleven days' road, and she came to the Hall's threshold on the morning appointed for the nine hearths to meet again and said, before any other business could be raised, that Sliochd Mhuirich would yield the flame to Eilidh nic Ìomhair on one condition and no other: that the stone cup Fionnlagh Dall had cut to the measure of two cupped hands should never, for any cause, for any lord's asking, for any war or famine or royal wedding the island might yet see, be carried out through the door of Talla na Lasrach.

It was not, on its face, much of a condition to ask, since no Keeper before Eilidh had ever once thought to carry the cup anywhere, and several of the hearth-elders who heard it said as much, wondering aloud what the house of Muireach thought it was guarding against that had not already been guarded a hundred times over by the Binding. But the eldest of Sliochd Mhuirich, when pressed to say plainly what she feared, answered only that a cup that never left a house could never be carried off by whoever next held the strongest claim to whatever else that house kept, and that she would rather ask for a small thing plainly than a large thing she could not name, and left it at that. Eilidh, told of the condition before she had even finished the walk up from Dùn dhearg to swear to it, said she would have sworn to it unasked, and did so before the nine hearths that same afternoon with her hand flat on the cup's rim, the coal poured level to her palm exactly as it had been poured for every Keeper before her, and the flame carried to her own lamp before nightfall.

It never did leave the Hall, not once in the whole of Eilidh's keeping nor in the reigns of any Keeper who came after her, and the tellers of a later age, looking back on that single afternoon's bargain, would say that Sliochd Mhuirich had asked for the one thing it could be certain of getting and got, by the asking, a good deal more besides.

## IV. Of the Four Houses Sworn

Eilidh had been three years at the flame, and had by then heard the argument about her blood raised so many times by so many mouths that she had stopped troubling to answer it at all, when the shape of a settlement came to her less from any single voice than from the plain fact of who kept coming to Dùn ìseal to argue it. Four houses, and no more than four, had by then taken to sending their own elders to speak at every gathering of the nine hearths as though they alone had a right to be heard on the matter of the Keeper's office: Sliochd Mhuirich, holding the office by more than two hundred years of custom and now by Eilidh's own oath besides; Sliochd Ìomhair, claiming a voice through Eilidh's own blood and unwilling, now that the thing was done, to let anyone forget the connection; Sliochd Thormoid, whose eldest stood up at the third such gathering and named, for the first time in anyone's hearing, an ancestor called Tormod Liath who was said to have held the hill under the old king before ever a flame was bound to it; and Sliochd Raghnaill, whose claim ran through a man named Raghnall Ceannfhionn and rested less on any hill than on the plain fact that Sliochd Raghnaill's boats had carried more coal down the coast in a bad season than any other house's carts had carried inland.

Eilidh, who had grown tired of hearing four houses argue a question the other five hearths had long since stopped troubling to weigh in on, put the settlement to the nine in the spring of the three hundred and eighty-fifth year in words plainer than any house had used to argue its own case: that the office of Keeper would be bound, from that season on, to these four houses of custody and no others, that the other hearths would keep their seats on the council as they always had but surrender any further claim on the flame, and that the four houses might settle among themselves, by whatever quarrel or marriage or patience the years gave them, which of their own would sit the flame when her own turn at it ended. Three of the four, she noted aloud before the nine hearths that same afternoon, claimed some thread of the king's blood or said they did, and only the house that had first raised the flame claimed none at all, which she thought, she said, a tidy enough shape for an office the Binding had meant to keep clear of blood and had somehow, across three centuries, filled with almost nothing else.

It was sworn that same spring, on the same threshold where the condition about the cup had been sworn three years before, four elders standing where one had stood alone, each with a hand laid flat in turn on the same stone rim Eilidh's own hand had rested on at her own swearing, and a fifth hand, the hearth-line eldest's, laid last of all and longest, as if to remind the other three whose custom it was they had come late to share in. Most of the great families known on the island now trace themselves, rightly or by their own telling, to one of the four, and it had already become, within two or three generations, a common enough boast at any market to claim descent from one house or another of the swearing, though Eilidh herself, asked once by a grandchild years later which of the four her own blood belonged to, said only that she supposed it belonged to whichever house wanted the claiming least, since that was the one nobody troubled to dispute with her.

## V. Of Seann Dunn and the Fullest Lamp

Sliochd Thormoid did not wait long to make its claim a visible one. Within the year its eldest had raised, at Seann Dunn on the river's northern bank, a hall and a shrine of squared timber facing the mountain across the water, with a coal-lamp set to burn at the Law's own measure day and night in a niche cut for it beside the door, so that any traveller on the road below could look up from the ford and see the house's light burning steadily whatever the season. The other three houses grumbled about it from the first, not because a lamp at the Law's measure was against any rule Eilidh had made, but because Seann Dunn's lamp, by every account that reached Dùn ìseal, always seemed to burn a shade fuller than anyone else's handful managed to, and no cutter sent to check the measure ever found so much as a grain over what the Law allowed.

"It is the same coal as everyone's," Eilidh told a steward of Sliochd Ìomhair who came to complain of it on his own house's behalf, turning the man's own grumble back on him with more patience than the complaint deserved, "and if Seann Dunn's hearth burns brighter on it than yours, I expect that says more for their draught than for their honesty." She did not trouble to have the lamp measured a second time, judging, as she told the hearth-elders privately, that a grievance a house could not prove was a grievance best left to wear out on its own, and it did, more or less, though Seann Dunn's neighbours went on saying it of the lamp for as long as the shrine stood.

Sliochd Raghnaill, meanwhile, made no shrine at all, having chosen for its own seat Seann Tarr on the north-eastern coast, the same town whose household had been the very first sentenced under Fionnlagh Dall's Small-Burning Law two centuries before, its name still carried on the Hall's own roll with the old offence beside it. The house that took its seat there kept no galleries and cut no coal of its own, choosing boats instead, and grew rich enough on the fishing within two generations that the other three houses stopped mentioning the old offence at all, which Eilidh privately thought a stranger silence than any grumble about a lamp.

## VI. Of Tormod Ruadh and the Coast That Was Only Looked At

It was a household of Sliochd Raghnaill's own boats, in the twelfth year of Eilidh's keeping, that did the one thing none of the other three houses had thought to try, and did it, by every later account, less from ambition than from an argument over a wager that had gone on rather longer at Seann Tarr's own quay than the ale served there could reasonably sustain. A steersman named Tormod Ruadh, no kin to Sliochd Thormoid whatever the shared name might suggest to a stranger, had spent the better part of a winter's evenings insisting to anyone who would listen that a boat built to weather the open water past the sea-monster's rocks could as easily be sailed clean round the black island the fishers called Eilean dhubh as fished within sight of it, as every boat out of Seann Tarr had always done before him, and by spring enough coin had changed hands on the wager that Tormod found himself with a crew of four and no honest way left to back out of the claim.

They put out at the first fair tide of an Cèitean in the three hundred and ninety-seventh year, provisioned for eight days though the crossing and back, by Tormod's own reckoning, ought to want no more than five, and the quay that saw them off did so with more laughter than blessing, a dozen wagers still being settled on the strand as the boat cleared the harbour mouth. The weather held fair the first two days, wind enough to spare the oars, and Tormod's own account, told afterward more times than any man should have patience to repeat a single tale, made much of the third night, when the boat lay becalmed under a headland none of the four crewmen could put a name to and a light rain came on that none of them troubled to shelter from, having by then long since given up any dryness worth keeping. The fourth day brought the cliffs of the island's far side into view, taller by half, Tormod said, than any cliff on the coast he had grown up fishing under, white with birds from the waterline to a height no stone could be thrown to, and the noise of them, he told the quay more than once, was a thing no teller before him had ever troubled to mention, a great ceaseless clamour that did not stop for the whole day they coasted under it, so that the crew found themselves shouting to be heard over birds rather than over any sea.

Eilidh heard nothing further of the voyage until word reached Dùn ìseal that the boat had been sighted rounding the island's far side by fishers of a strand that had never before had reason to look for any boat of Seann Tarr's coming from that particular quarter of the sea, and the word that reached her carried, along with the fact of the sighting, a good deal of alarm from a strand that had taken the strange boat, at first glimpse, for something worth lighting a warning fire over. They were nine days out before the boat came home, a day longer than provisioned and no worse for it, and the whole of Seann Tarr's quay turned out to meet them, less, by most accounts, out of any fear for their safety than out of an eagerness to settle the wager one way or the other.

What Tormod brought back, when pressed by half the quay at once to say what lay round the far side of Eilean dhubh, was rock, and birds enough to darken the sky over certain stretches of the northern cliff, and the plain fact of having gone there and come back, and nothing else that any teller since has been able to add to it however many times the tale has been told at Seann Tarr's own hearths. He had looked for a landing and found none worth the name; he had looked for any sign that another boat, of any coast, had gone that way before him, and found none of that either; and when a woman of the quay asked him outright whether the far side of the island was worth the wager he had won by seeing it, he is said to have answered only that a man does not sail nine days to bring home an answer to a question nobody but a drunk crowd at a quay had thought worth asking, and that he would do it again all the same, given the ale and the wager to make him.

Word of the voyage reached Eilidh at Dùn ìseal within the month, carried up by a carrier who had heard it fresh at Seann Tarr's own quay and told it, by his own account, rather better than Tormod himself ever troubled to tell it again. She asked the carrier only one question in return, whether any of the four houses had claimed the voyage as their own doing, and was told that Sliochd Raghnaill had certainly not discouraged the telling, whatever truth there was in a wager settled over too much ale being any house's doing at all.

## VII. Of the Watching from the Threshold

Eilidh kept the flame a further three-and-thirty years after that spring, long enough to see the argument about her own blood fade from something argued at every gathering into something an older generation remembered arguing and a younger one had simply always known to be settled, and long enough, too, to see boats out of all four coasts sail a little further past their own headlands than their grandfathers had thought worth the risk, though none again quite so far, nor quite so needlessly, as Tormod Ruadh's had gone.

She was standing on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach on an evening in that same summer of the three hundred and ninety-seventh year, the cup behind her exactly where it had stood the whole of her keeping and the whole of Sliochd Mhuirich's before her, when the last of Seann Tarr's own boats came up the coast road as a rumour before it came as a fact, carried by traders faster than any boat could travel, and she watched, from that same threshold, the small dark shapes of the fishing fleet come in past the headland below the mountain in the last of the light, each boat finding its own strand as it always had, none of them, that evening or any evening after, troubling to sail any further than the fishing required. It was, she thought, watching them come in, a smaller thing than the argument over her own blood had ever made it sound, and a larger one than any wager settled at a quay had any right to be, and she found, standing there with the flame at her back and the cup that would never leave the house behind her, that she could not have said with any certainty which of the two she believed the truer measure of what her own long season at the flame had actually kept.
