# Of Ìomhar Dearg and the Bier of Spears

*FE 1*

*Part One: The Cord*

> *Cha do leag aon duine e; leag an sluagh uile e.*
> No one man brought him down; the whole host brought him down.
>
> — said on the east bank at Àth na Fala, the night the king fell

## I. Of the Fires on the East Bank

*The people of Dùn dhearg tell this, and they tell it low, for it is their own shame and their own lord's pride together.*

On the night the king fell the host of the east did not sleep. It stood a long while in the water where he had gone down, and then it came out of the river by twos and threes, as men come away from a barrow of their own dead, and lit its fires again along the east bank. Nobody gave the order. The fires were lit because it was dark and the men were wet to the breast and the river had turned cold all at once.

There was a lad at one of those fires, a lad of the eastern hills who had been in the river on the second day and had come out alive. He sat with his knees drawn up and his spear across them, and the older men about him kept asking what the king had been like, close to. He told them the king had been warm. After that they let him alone.

Nobody went back across the ford that night to take the body up out of the river. They had drawn it onto the west bank, where the stone was, and there they had left it; and nobody on the east bank would say why he did not go and stand over it, or strip it, or cut the grey head off to carry home as the old wars were said to have done. The lad thought afterward that it was fear. He thought also, when he was old, that it was something else that has no plain name, the thing a man feels in a hall when the fire has gone out and the house is still warm from it.

The greybeard who had given the counsel for the third day walked up and down behind the fires until near dawn. At every fire he stopped and said, "It is done. We have done it." At the lad's fire a man threw a stone at him, and he went on.

Before first light the saying had gone the length of the bank: *no one man brought him down; the whole host brought him down.* The households of Dùn dhearg meant it as a boast when they first said it, and by morning it had become an excuse, and before a year was out it was the only thing any of them would say at all.

## II. Of the Son Who Came Late

Ìomhar Dearg left the valley under Dùn ìseal at noon on the third day of the fighting, which was the day his father fell, and he left it in a temper.

He had been three days in the gathering-house arguing for a stretch of river-grazing below the Red Hill that a nephew of his claimed by his mother. He was fourteen hundred years old, near enough; he had been a grown man when the stones were first raised at Tobar dhìreach round the slab, and there was not a cattle-path in the east he had not ridden. On the first day word had come that the spears of Dùn dhearg were out and that the king had gone down to the ford. He had heard it as they all heard it, with half his mind, as one hears that a neighbour's roof is being mended. The king had gone down to fords before. He had come back from all of them.

On the second day a man of his household came in from the eastern road and said that the host was greater than anyone had thought, and that the king had sent the handful of his own people back up the road and held the ford alone. Ìomhar said that he would go when the grazing was settled. He remembered afterward that he had said it standing up, with his hand on the post of the gathering-house door, and that his nephew had laughed.

On the third morning the grazing was still not settled, and he found that he could not sit still. He went out into the valley and looked east. From the lip of the valley on a clear day a man can see the smoke of Dùn dhearg over the shoulder of the hill, and that morning there was smoke all along the low ground where the river runs, the smoke of many fires, and none of it moving. He stood there a long time. Then he went down to his own camp and told his steward to call up the household, every man who could carry a spear, and the steward asked him whether the grazing was settled.

"It will keep," said Ìomhar Dearg.

"Your nephew will have it by tonight."

"Then he will have it," said Ìomhar, and the steward looked at him, and went.

He rode east with a little over two hundred at his back, and he did not hurry them, which he never forgave himself. The road from the valley to the ford is a few miles, no more than a morning's walk for a carrier with a basket. He could have been at the water by the middle of the afternoon. But the host held the road beyond the second rise, and his scouts came back and told him so; and he turned his people north over the hill-grazing to come down on the ford from above, out of the heather, where the host would not look for them; and the hill was wet and steep and the light went. They lay that night in a fold of the hill a mile above the river. From there they could hear the fighting, and then, toward midnight, they could not.

Ìomhar did not sleep. He sat against a stone with his cloak over his knees and listened to the silence come up out of the valley, and he knew what it was. His steward came and sat by him in the dark and said nothing for a while.

"Maybe he has driven them off," the steward said at last.

"Then we would hear them running," said Ìomhar Dearg.

## III. Of the Ford in the Morning

They came down out of the heather in the grey light.

What he saw first was the water. The river below the ford had spread out over the meadow in a sheet, brown and still, with the grass standing up through it, and it did not look like a river at all. It looked like a field in a wet spring. Then he saw why. At the shallows below the ford the dead lay across the whole width of the stream, piled on one another, thrown up against the gravel as the current had rolled them, so thick that the water came over them in a low fall and made a sound like a mill-lade. The ford itself lay above the dam, wide and slow and dark. Crows were already walking on it.

On the west bank, by a grey stone at the head of the ford, there was one body lying by itself on its back, with nothing near it.

He knew it from the hillside. He knew it by the length of it and by the grey hair, which was the only grey on that bank. Ìomhar did not go to it. He said afterward that he could not have gone to it then and done what came next, and that he chose what came next.

On the east bank the host of Dùn dhearg lay in the heather where it had dropped. It had come out of the river in the night and lit its fires, and the fires had burned down, and the men had gone to sleep where they sat, three thousand of them or what was left of three thousand, wet and bloodied and spent, some with their spears still in their hands and many without. A few were awake. They saw the spears come down out of the hill on the far side of the ford, and they stood up and shouted, and the shout went along the bank; and the men who heard it got up slowly, like men in a dream, and looked for their spears and did not find them.

"They are finished," said the steward, at Ìomhar's elbow. "Look at them. They can't lift their arms."

"Neither could he," said Ìomhar Dearg. "At the last."

He took his household across the ford at a run, over the stones his father had held for three days, through water that was still red at the edges and stank, and up the east bank into the host. It was a slaughter, and the tellers of Dùn dhearg, who have never had much to say about that morning, say at least that much. The men of the east had spent everything they had on the king and had nothing left for his son. Some stood and fought and died. More ran, up the valley toward Dùn dhearg, and Ìomhar's people ran after them and killed them in the heather. Some sat down where they were and put their hands over their heads.

Ìomhar himself fought at the head of it until noon, and then he stopped. He found that he had come to the end of wanting to. He stood on the east bank above the dam of the dead and looked across at the grey stone and the body beside it, and his steward, coming up, found him weeping, and waited a while before he spoke.

By evening he held the ford. It was the thing his father had held, and he had it now; and none of the host who were left alive could have taken it from a child.

## IV. Of the Double Bank of Dùn dhearg

Dùn dhearg stands on its hill above the river a few miles from the ford, and in those days it was a fort of the old kind, with two banks of earth about its crown and a ditch between them, and timber halls within where the households of the east met to swear that they owed nothing to Dùn ìseal or its king. It had never been taken. It had not often needed to be.

Ìomhar Dearg came up to it on the second morning after the ford with his household and some hundreds of the host who had surrendered to him and had nowhere else to go. The gate in the outer bank stood open. There was nobody on the bank at all. The women and the old men of the households had heard what had happened at the ford, and many of them had gone up into the eastern hills with the cattle, and those who had not gone stood in the doors of the halls within the inner bank and watched him come.

He walked in through both gates. He did not draw. In the middle of the inner ground, where the households had sworn their oath against his father, he stood and asked who among them had given the counsel of the third day, that every spear should go into the river together and keep going in.

Nobody answered. Nobody had to; the greybeard was not among them. He had been found at dawn of that same morning lying face down in the shallows at the head of the ford, below the grey stone, and whether he had fallen or gone in of his own will, nobody on the east bank would say.

Then Ìomhar asked who had struck the blow that brought the king down in the dark. Nobody answered that either, and this time it was not from fear. He went from face to face, and every face he looked into told him the truth, which was that no one knew, and that no household there would own it if it could. He understood then what the saying on the east bank meant. The whole host had brought him down. It was the only boast they had, and it was also the only thing they could bear to have done.

He burned the halls. He had the people carry out what they wanted first: cloaks, pots, the old men's stools, a loom with the web still on it. Then his household set fire to the thatch, and the halls within the double bank burned through the afternoon, and the smoke went up over the hill and was seen in the valley under Dùn ìseal, where some of the heirs took it to be the host of the east coming on, and armed themselves at last.

When it was done Ìomhar Dearg stood on the inner bank with the hot ash blowing past him and said, loudly enough for the whole ground to hear, that the households of Dùn dhearg had sworn they owed nothing to Dùn ìseal, and he would hold them to it. They owed nothing to Dùn ìseal. They owed everything to him.

Among the people who came in from the hills in the following days there was a lad who had been in the river on the second day and had lived. Ìomhar's steward brought him forward, because the lad had said to someone that he had been close to the king, and the steward thought his lord would want to hear it. Ìomhar asked him what his father had been like at the end.

"He was warm," the lad said.

Ìomhar Dearg looked at him a long time, and then sent him back to his mother, and gave orders that nobody was to touch him. The lad lived to be old, and he never went back to the ford, and it is his telling of the ford that the east keeps.

## V. Of the Bier of Spears

On the fifth day after the ford Ìomhar had the body brought up from the west bank.

He went down to it himself at last, alone, with the rest held back on the east side. He had expected to find the face changed, as the faces of the dead are changed. What he found was a young man's face, with no line on it, and the grey hair lying wet across the brow as if he had only that moment come up out of the river. The eyes were open. The wounds were all in front, many of them, and none of them seemed to be enough. He knelt by the stone, where his father had sat between the rushes of the host and drunk from the river and washed his hands, and he closed the eyes. Then he did what his father had done on those evenings. He went down to the edge of the ford and washed his hands in the water, which was clean now above the dam, and cold.

He sent for spears. There were more than enough; they lay in the ford and along both banks and in the heather where the host had dropped them, ash shafts and hazel shafts and a few of old dark oak, and his people gathered them by the armful. The wrights of his household lashed them into a bier with thongs cut from the host's own belts, two long spears for the poles and a lattice of shorter ones across them, and over the lattice they laid the cloaks of the east. Then they set the king on it.

It took twelve men to carry him. The bier was heavy with the spears, and he had been a big man, bigger than anyone living. Ìomhar chose the twelve from among the host of Dùn dhearg: twelve who had been in the river. He told them that they had brought the king down between them, and between them they would bring him home.

Some of his own household thought that a hard thing to ask. Some thought it a mercy. The twelve went without a word.

Ìomhar Dearg did not go with the bier. He said that he had the east to settle, which was true. He said also, to his steward only, that he would not walk up the valley road behind his father's body among the fires of his brothers and sisters and nephews and cousins who had not come, and be looked at by them as one who had come and been too late; and that when he went up to Dùn ìseal he would go as what he was, and not as a mourner who had been at the wrong gathering.

So the bier went up the valley road without him. It went slowly, with the twelve changing their grip at every stream, and a few of Ìomhar's household walking before it with green branches, as the old custom was for a death on the road. At the lip of the valley two of the king's sons came down to meet it. They had seen the smoke from Dùn dhearg and heard the herd-boy's news, and had ridden out at last, with the spears of their households behind them, and met the bier coming up instead. They got down and walked behind it. Both of them were grey.

## VI. Of the Twelve Who Came Back

The twelve came back to Dùn dhearg on the evening of the eighth day, walking, with the two long poles of the bier over their shoulders and nothing on them. The heirs had kept the lattice of short spears and the cloaks, and sent the poles home. Nobody in the valley had wanted to burn them, and nobody had known what else to do with them.

Ìomhar had them brought to him where he sat by the new posts on the inner bank. They stood in a row in the dusk and would not sit, though he told them to. The eldest of them was a man of the barley-fields named in no telling, grey-bearded, with a cut across one hand from the second day of the ford that had gone bad and smelled. He did the talking.

He said that the bier had gone up the valley road slow, as it had been ordered, and that the two grey sons of the king had walked behind it all the way. He said that the heirs had come out of their tents on both sides of the road to see it go by, more of them than he could have counted, the old and the young together, in good cloaks, and that none of them had said a word to the bearers, good or bad, but only looked at the king.

"And they looked at you," said Ìomhar Dearg.

"Some did," said the man. "Most did not. We were only the bier."

He said that a woman of the valley had come down from the spring with a pail and given each of the twelve water as the bier passed, and had not asked who they were, though she must have known. He said that the keening had started up again as the king went by, at the upper camps first, and run down the valley from fire to fire until it reached the bottom of the road, and that he had heard nothing like it in his life and did not want to again. He said that they had carried the king as far as the long house below the hill, and that there the old men of the slab had taken him from them, and the twelve had been given bread and sent away.

"Was anything said of me?" said Ìomhar.

The man thought about it. "One of them asked where you were," he said. "A young one, laughing. He asked whether you had come up with the bier, and when he heard you had not, he said, 'He has found something to hold, then,' and some of them laughed with him and some did not."

Ìomhar Dearg was silent a long while. Then he asked the man's name, and the man gave it, and the telling has let it go. Ìomhar gave each of the twelve a cow from the herds of the east that had come down out of the hills, and a place on the new ground within the bank, and told them that they were his people now, first of all his people, and that if any man of Dùn dhearg ever threw the ford at them he was to answer for it to him.

The poles of the bier he had set up on either side of the inner gate, where they stood for many years, bleaching, until the weather had them.

## VII. Of What Ìomhar Kept

For nine days Ìomhar Dearg stayed at Dùn dhearg.

In those nine days he had the dead of the ford drawn out of the river and buried on the meadow in long trenches, the host of the east all together, because there was no telling them apart. He had the bank of the ford cleared and the grey stone left standing, and he forbade anybody to move it. He sent riders round the eastern hills to the households who had gone up with the cattle and told them to come down, and that they would keep their cattle and their lives if they came down to him and to nobody else. Most of them came. Those who did not were hunted, and the tellers of Dùn dhearg do not say how those hunts ended.

He counted what he had. It was a hill with a double bank and a burned crown, and the valley under it, and the lowland fields toward the south where the eastern households had their barley, and some thousands of people who had come against his father and now had nobody to answer to but him. It was more than any share he had been arguing for in the gathering-house. He had not been given it. He had taken it with a sword, on the morning after the king fell, from men who could not lift their arms, and he knew very well what his kin would say of that, and he did not greatly care.

On the ninth day word came from the valley that the king was laid in the earth, and that the heirs were sending to every coast of the island for the kin who had not come in, so that the whole of the king's blood might sit down together at Dùn ìseal to part what he had left.

"Part it," said Ìomhar Dearg. He was standing on the inner bank where the halls had been. The ash had been cleared and the first new posts were in the ground. "Let them part it. They will not part this."

He went up the valley road the next morning, riding, with his household behind him in the cloaks of the east. The heirs came out of their tents to watch him pass as they had come out to watch the bier. He did not look at any of them. He went up past the fires of the valley to the long gathering-house below the hill, and got down, and stood in the door with the light behind him, and the people inside stopped talking.

His holding was called afterward *an Earrann Dhearg*, the Red Share, for the hill and for the blood. It was the one share on the island that was won with a sword before the parting and never given in it; and the heirs who sat down to part the rest had to part it around him, as a river parts round a stone it cannot move.
