# Of the Carriers and the First Bridges

*FE 149 – 163*

> *Fìrinn nach do bhris i a rinn an drochaid làidir.*
> The truth it never broke made the bridge strong.

> — the masons' saying at the crossing of Ros fhionn

## I. Of the Carrier's Trade

Fionnlagh Dall was nine and fifty in the year the Small-Burning Law first bore hard on the households it bound, and Calum Beag, the boy who had led him about the Hall since his own choosing, was grown to a man who no longer needed telling which gallery he stood before, though he still told him anyway, out of a habit neither of them had ever troubled to break. The law that had hardened at Samhain three years before had made the Hall, for the first time in the mountain's memory, a giver-out rather than merely a keeper: a household came to the threshold of Talla na Lasrach with its claim, and the stone cup filled and emptied, and what the cup gave out had then to travel, on somebody's back, to every hearth of every share that had sworn to take no more than its measure and not a handful over it.

It had travelled, before that year, along whatever track a foot had worn first and every foot after had worn a little deeper, up over the mountain's shoulder and down through country that had never once been asked to bear a laden basket every week of every year, in whatever weather a year chose to send. A carrier's trade had grown up around the coal the way a trade grows up around any need that will not go away of its own accord, households of no land and little standing who carried for hire or for their own dues both, their baskets marked by hearth so that a household's own measure could be told from its neighbour's on the road, and it was a hard trade and a thankless one, praised by no one when the basket arrived whole and cursed roundly when it did not, and it was the carriers, more than any lord or Keeper, who came first to know exactly which miles of the island's paths a winter's rain could not be trusted to leave standing.

The southern households, whose dried fish came up the same tracks the coal went down by, felt the want of a proper road before any other share did, since the last mile above the harbour of Ceann chaol dropped so steep between two shoulders of bare rock that a carrier with a full basket went down it sideways, one shoulder to the stone, or did not go down it at all in wet weather, and more than one had gone down it faster than his own feet meant to and come up afterward with an ankle that never again carried a load the same way.

## II. Of Ailean Clachair and the Steps to Ceann chaol

Ailean Clachair was four and twenty that year, a cutter of the fourth hearth who had spent the better part of his growing years with a chisel in his hand rather than a basket on his back, the same boy, grown now to a man, who had gone up into the hills above Dùn ìseal eleven years before to help a child named Calum Beag carry down the grey stone the blind Keeper had wanted for his cup. He had cut stair-steps into a hillside before, for a byre that needed them and a well that needed them worse, and when the council at Dùn ìseal asked, in the hundred and forty-ninth year, whether anyone among the nine hearths' cutters would take on the steep mile above Ceann chaol, it was Ailean who stood forward first, more from a young man's wanting of a task worth his hands than from any thought of what the task would grow into.

He worked at it through the better part of two years with six others of the fourth hearth, cutting the steps where the rock allowed a step to be cut and setting fitted stone where it did not, a foot's width at a time down a fall that had killed no one outright but had turned more ankles than the Hall's own roll troubled to count. That work taught him what stone would hold a man's step wet and what stone would not, a different knowledge from the knowledge of a well-shaft or a byre wall, and a knowledge, though he did not know it yet, that the whole of the rest of his life would be built upon. The path was finished in the hundred and fifty-first year, steps enough that a carrier with a full basket could go down to Ceann chaol on a wet morning without once setting a hand to the rock, and the Hall's baskets went down it after that, and the dried fish of the south came up it, faster and drier than either had ever gone before.

## III. Of the Rising of the Abhainn dhomhain

It was the Abhainn dhomhain that took what the steps to Ceann chaol had spared, in the hundred and fifty-fourth year, on a night in the last days of an t-Samhain when a rain that had fallen steadily for three days turned, sometime past midnight, into a rain that fell as though the sky itself had broken open. The river ran deep in ordinary weather, deep enough that its name had never asked for any better reason, but it ran fordable at the one crossing the carriers' road used, a wide gravel shallow where a man could cross with a basket on his back and the water no higher than his knees on any day the Hall's own memory reached back to.

Eleven carriers crossed it that night, a party bound south with the Hall's baskets for the households beyond the ford, walking by the last light because the rain had already cost them half the day and a household waiting on its handful in a bad season was not a household a carrier liked to keep waiting past its need. They had crossed that ford a hundred times between them, in every season the mountain sent, and it is told that the leader of the party, a woman of many years on the road named Beathag nan Cliabh for the baskets she had carried since she was a girl, said only that the water looked no worse than a spring flood she remembered from her own youth, and led them into it on that word.

The river that had stood knee-deep at dusk stood chest-deep and rising by the time the last of them was halfway across, the current carrying down branches and, before long, whole trees torn out by their roots somewhere upstream in the dark, striking a man's legs from under him before he ever felt the water grow too strong to stand in. It stood over a tall man's head, by the account of the one carrier who turned back at the water's edge rather than follow the rest in, before the party on the far bank could so much as call a warning across it, the sound of the river by then too loud for any voice to carry over. Eleven were lost that night, baskets and all, and the one who had turned back, a young man new enough to the trade that his own fear had not yet learned shame, walked the two hours to the nearest hearth in the dark and the rain to say so, arriving with nothing in his own hands but the knowledge of what he had watched happen and had not been able to stop, and it is told that he did not carry the Hall's coal again for the better part of a year afterward, though no one at the Hall ever once asked it of him.

## IV. Of the Names of the Drowned

The bodies came down with the flood over the days that followed, most of them within a few miles of the ford, two of them not at all, taken out to the sea by a river running harder than any living carrier had seen it run before or would see it run again for the better part of a century. The Hall kept a roll of names it had never before had cause to keep in quite that shape, eleven names set down together under one date, and it is told that Fionnlagh Dall, blind as he was, asked to have each name read to him twice before he would let the roll be closed, so that he might be certain he had heard each one and not merely the sound of a list.

Beathag nan Cliabh, who had led the party in on her own word that the water looked no worse than a flood she remembered from her girlhood, went into it near the last, having stood in the current a long while calling the others on before her own footing gave way beneath her; the households of the road kept her name afterward the way a trade keeps the name of one who is blamed by no one and forgiven by everyone, since a leader's word, honestly given from a life's worth of crossing, could fail a party as surely as a coward's could, and had failed no one before that night. Griogair mac Ailig went into the water early, by the turned-back carrier's account, having gone ahead to test the ford's depth as the party's habit was on a rising night, and Sìneag nighean Dhonnchaidh went in trying to reach a hand to him rather than let go of her own basket's strap, which the households afterward held against her not at all, though some said privately that a strap was a poor thing to die holding. A boy not yet come to his full growth, whose name the Hall's roll gives only as young Tormod of the salt shore, had asked to go on the crossing that night in place of an uncle whose knee had gone bad in the wet, and it was that uncle, whole and dry on the near bank, who lived after to tell what his own knee had cost.

It is told, and told still by the households of that stretch of road, that the carriers who came after made a joke of their own grief within the season, saying that the Hall mourned its coal along with its dead, since eleven baskets of the mountain's own coal had gone into the sea with the eleven who carried them, and that a joke told against oneself in that manner was the only kind of grief a carrier's trade had ever found room for. Fionnlagh, hearing the joke repeated to him by Calum Beag, did not rebuke it. He said only that a mountain that measured its coal so carefully against a household's need might, in fairness, be allowed to grieve a basketful lost the same way its people grieved a life, and that he saw no unkindness in a carrier who let both griefs sit in the one sentence, since both, that night, had gone into the water together.

## V. Of Fionnlagh's Asking

Fionnlagh brought the matter to the council himself within the fortnight, led to the Hall's own ground by Calum Beag as he was led everywhere, and asked the assembled lords and hearth-elders a question none of them, by the plain evidence of the silence that met it, had thought to ask in all the years the ford had served them. He asked for a crossing the river could not take, whatever weather might come against it, so that no household waiting on its handful should ever again cost a carrier's life for the waiting.

The lords who spoke first spoke of cost and of years, a ford having served well enough for longer than any of them had lived, and one of the elders of the southern hearths, a stout old man named Neacal who had buried two carriers of his own household to lesser fords in his own lifetime, said plainly that a river which rose once in a generation to kill eleven people was, taken across the whole of that generation, no worse a risk than a cart-track in winter or a boat on the western sea, and that the Hall could not build itself proof against every misfortune the mountain's own weather might send it. "You would have us throw stone at every ford on the island," he said, "against a flood that comes once in a man's life, and leave the coal the poorer for the labour spent on it." Fionnlagh heard him out, as he heard out every argument brought against him, sitting with his own hands folded in his lap the whole of the old man's speaking, and answered only that his question was whether the risk could be made smaller. "A cup that measures fair by the fill of two hands," he said, "asks no household to trust its fortune to luck. I see little reason a carrier's life should trust to luck what stone and labour might answer for instead." It is told that he asked, last of all, whether any man in the hall could name him a mason who had already proved his hand against water and weight, and that more than one voice answered him with the same name, which was Ailean Clachair's, the cutter of the fourth hearth whose steps above Ceann chaol a wet winter had not yet found a way to break.

## VI. Of the Forty of the Nine Hearths

Ailean was sent for the same week, and stood before the council a young man of five and twenty with stone-dust still under his nails from a well he had left half finished at another household's asking, and heard the Keeper's charge laid on him in fewer words than the council's own arguing about it had taken: a single crossing above Ros fhionn, where the Abhainn dhomhain ran narrower and its banks stood higher and firmer than anywhere else along its whole reach, built to stand against any flood the river might ever again choose to send it. He answered, by his own later telling of it, that he did not yet know whether such a thing could be built at all, a span of stone thrown clear across water that wide having been done nowhere on the island that any living mason could name him, but that he would rather try it and fail than tell a Keeper who had just buried eleven carriers that it could not be tried.

He went to Fionnlagh once more before the calling began, alone, without Calum Beag to hear it, and asked the Keeper plainly whether he understood that the thing might fail, that a mason four and twenty years old with nothing behind him but a stair of steps and a boyhood spent carrying a blind man's cup-stone might throw a season's labour and the Hall's own coin into a river and have nothing to show for it but a heap of cut stone on either bank. Fionnlagh's answer, which Calum Beag had from Ailean's own mouth many years after and kept it word for word the rest of his life, was that he had asked no mason living for a certainty, having buried eleven carriers already on the strength of a ford everyone had called certain, and that he would rather trust a young man's honest doubt than an old man's easy promise, since a promise too easily given was, in his experience, the surest sign of a thing not properly thought through. Ailean carried that answer with him through the whole of the building that followed, and told the forty, more than once when the work went hard, that a Keeper who could not see the bridge at all had trusted it further than any of them yet trusted their own hands.

He drew from the nine hearths, over the winter that followed, every cutter and mason he judged worth the calling, forty in the end, men and women who had cut wells and byres and boundary-stones and the steps above Ceann chaol and had never, any of them, cut anything that had to hold a whole river rather than a roof. They quarried through the worst of the cold at a face of grey stone not far from Ros fhionn, close enough grained, Ailean judged, to bear the load without splitting, and spent the better part of a season only in learning how a stone cut to a wedge's shape would lean upon its neighbours and hold up its own weight by leaning, a thing none of the forty had understood in their bones before that winter taught it to them by the plain evidence of stones that fell when cut wrong and stones that stood when cut right.

## VII. Of the Single Span above Ros fhionn

The span went up through the spring and summer of the hundred and sixtieth year, one course of fitted stone above another, each stone cut and set and tested by hand before the next was laid, Ailean himself walking the scaffold at every hour of daylight and, by Calum Beag's telling of what he heard from the masons afterward, at a good many hours of dark besides, unwilling to trust any course to the morning that he had not first trusted to his own eye by lamplight the night before. The forty worked from both banks toward the river's middle, on wooden centring that carried the weight of each course until the arch could carry it itself, and it was on that centring, in the sixth week of the building, that a mason of the ninth hearth named Aonghas Beag put a foot wrong on a wet timber and went down toward the water with a half-cut stone still in his arms, caught at the very edge of the fall by two others who had his belt in their hands before his own mind had understood what his feet had already done. Ailean would not let the man back onto the scaffold that day nor the next. He told the forty plainly that he trusted Aonghas Beag as much as any of them; a mason frightened in his own body simply cut stone worse than a mason who had learned to steady his fright first, and a bridge meant to answer for other men's lives asked steadied hands before it asked anything else.

The arch closed at last on a morning in the height of summer when the keystone, the single wedge of stone that would hold every other stone in its place by the same leaning weight that held them all, was raised on ropes and levers by every hand the forty could spare and set into the gap the rest of the arch had left for it. Ailean himself guided it the last hand's-width into its place, kneeling astride the gap with the river running loud beneath him, and it is told that the whole forty stood silent on both banks while the ropes came free, since a keystone wrongly seated could bring the two half-arches down together before anyone standing near enough to help could do more than watch it happen.

It held. The scaffold came down within the week, board by board, until the arch stood alone over the water on nothing but its own weight and its own shape, a single span of fitted stone thrown clean across the Abhainn dhomhain where eleven carriers had gone into it four years before, and it is told that Ailean, standing beneath it on the gravel bank where the ford had been, wept without shame in front of the whole forty, having not, by his own account, believed with his whole heart until that morning that the thing would truly stand.

## VIII. Of the Hearth-Sign in the Keystone

Before the scaffold came down Ailean climbed back up to the keystone and cut into its face, with his own hand and no other's, a small mark that the fourth hearth had used among its own cutters for as long as any of them could remember, a sign no household outside the trade would have known to read but every mason of the nine hearths knew at a glance, meant to say, to any eye that troubled to look for it, that the stone above it had been raised by hands that answered for their own work. He had not been asked to cut it there, and told the forty afterward, when some asked why, that a bridge a man's own name might be forgotten from within a generation ought at least to carry some mark that said a living hand, and not chance, had set it standing.

The mark caught on faster than the bridge did, in the way of things a trade takes up before the wider Hall has quite noticed it happening. Every span the forty raised after Ros fhionn's carried the same sign cut somewhere into its keystone or its central course, and within a handful of years it was said, first among carriers and then more widely, that a crossing without the mark was a crossing built by hands that had not troubled to answer for their own work. That was an unfair thing to say of many an honest builder who had simply never learned the fourth hearth's sign, and it was said all the same, often enough that it became, before Ailean himself was an old man, very nearly as good as a law.

## IX. Of the Oath Sworn on the Stone Cup

It was in the winter following the bridge's raising that the forty, gathered at Dùn ìseal to be paid what the council had promised them for the season's labour, asked Fionnlagh whether they might swear something of their own before the Hall, a fellowship bound together the way the nine hearths were bound, so that a mason called to one crossing might trust a mason called to another to hold the same measure. Fionnlagh heard the asking with what Calum Beag remembered as a plain pleasure, and set the matter before them at the threshold of Talla na Lasrach, on the same worn stone where the cup that measured the coal sat open to any eye that cared to look.

They swore two things that day, each of the forty laying a hand on the stone cup in turn as the lords of the shares had laid their hands on it three years before, Aonghas Beag among them, his own hand steady on the stone though Calum Beag said afterward it had shaken through the whole of the walk to the threshold: that no span they raised would ever be closed to a traveller in the dark, a bridge shut against the night being, in a mason's own reckoning, no better than a bridge that had never been built at all; and that no span would ever be paid for in coal, since a crossing built to spare carriers' lives ought not be bought with the very coal those carriers' lives were spared to carry. Fionnlagh, who could not see a single hand laid on the stone that day any more than he had seen the lords' hands laid on it before, said to the forty what he had said to the council then, that he trusted the cup to hold them to an oath better than any spoken word would, and it is told that Ailean answered him, half in earnest and half in the plain wit of a young man still giddy with his own bridge standing, that a mason trusted stone before he trusted anything else living or dead, his own self not excepted, and saw no reason a Keeper should not do the same.

## X. Of the Lodge at the Western Ford

The forty did not scatter, once Ros fhionn's crossing was finished and paid for, the way a gathering of cutters called for one task commonly did, but kept themselves together under Ailean's leading and took on, over the two years that followed, three crossings more where the carriers' roads asked for them, the last and widest of the three above a ford in the country of Ros dhomhain that the western share's own herds had used, badly, for longer than any living herder's memory reached. They raised there, once the western span was finished, a plain stone lodge of their own beside the crossing, a hall no grander than any nine-hearth's own gathering-house, where a mason called from any share could find a roof and a fire and a fellow of the trade to hear what stone he had cut that season and how it had answered him.

It was at the western crossing, in the hundred and sixty-third year, not at Ros fhionn's own span, that the fourth hearth's mark was tested for the first time against a bridge that did not carry it. A cart-builder of Ros dhomhain, impatient of the mason's slow season and the mason's higher wage, had thrown a lighter crossing of his own a half day's walk upriver, unmarked, over a narrower reach that his own eye judged safe enough for a laden cart, and a carrier of the Hall's baskets, coming to it after dark on a night the western span lay a weary two hours further on, stood at its near end a long while by his fellow's account and would not cross it, saying only that he had sworn no oath himself, that he knew well enough whose oath a bridge without the sign had not been built under, and that he would rather sleep cold on the near bank than trust his basket, and his own neck besides, to a crossing no mason living would answer for. He slept cold that night, and carried word of it to Dùn ìseal within the week, and it is told that Fionnlagh, hearing it, asked Calum Beag to set the carrier's name on the same roll that had once held the names of the eleven drowned, this time among the living the fourth hearth's mark and the oath sworn upon the stone cup had kept that way.

The Hall had been, in the years before the Small-Burning Law, a keeper only, a thing a household went up to and came down from, its business finished at the threshold where the cup filled and emptied. It was a giver-out now, and a giver-out, Fionnlagh had come to understand before that ninth year was out, owed its people more than a fair measure at the door; it owed them a road the measure could travel by without asking a life for every basket lost to bad weather or bad stone. The forty of the nine hearths went on cutting spans across the shares for a long while after Ros fhionn's, under Ailean's leading and, when he was old, under the leading of masons he himself had taught the trade to, and it was said of every one of their crossings, long after the flood on the Abhainn dhomhain had passed out of any living carrier's own memory into the Hall's roll alone, that a bridge with the fourth hearth's mark in its keystone was a bridge a man might cross in the dark without once wondering whether the stone beneath his feet had been asked to answer for its own standing.
