# Of the Silver of Muileann chiar

*FE 69 – 96*

> *Chan eil fuil san airgead.*
> There is no blood in the silver.
>
> — Muireach Liath, of the seam apart from the vein

## I. Of the Furnace that Answered to Nobody

The smiths of Muileann chiar say still, when they tell the making of their own trade, that it began on a morning when three men stood about a wet fire and waited for a thing they had never made before to decide, on its own account, whether the fire held metal or only ash, and that no one among them could have told you, had you asked, what they meant to do if it chose ash. Fearchar Airgeadach was the youngest of the three, and it is his name the households kept, though he himself said, in old age, that the finding was none of his and the waiting that morning belonged as much to old Tormod who had fed the peat as to the seam, which had lain under the hill a great while before anyone troubled to ask what it was.

The seam was found in the sixty-ninth year of Clàr an Talla, a few miles north of Dùn ìseal along a spur the mountain's own vein had never reached, where a shepherd looking for a ewe caught in bracken saw a grey stone with a bright wound in it where her stick had scraped the moss away, and carried the stone down to Talla na Lasrach more out of curiosity than any thought that it might matter. It was the first thing dug from the ground of the island that was surely not coal: no household could name a use for it, no keeper could say whose it was, and it sat on a sill at Dùn ìseal for the better part of a season, turned over now and again by whoever passed, bright where the shepherd's stick had scraped it and dull everywhere else, until Fearchar, who carried baskets for the ninth hearth and had spent more hours than most men watching the fires that fed the vein, asked to take it away and see what a fire hot enough would do to it.

He was not a smith. There were no smiths on the island in those years in the sense the word later came to carry, only households that shaped bone and horn and wood, and heated stone for building, and Fearchar's whole claim to the work was that he had watched the vein's fires longer and more closely than most, banking them and starving them and coaxing them by turns through three winters of carrying baskets for keepers who trusted him with the watching precisely because he asked so few questions of it. He built his own fire apart from Talla na Lasrach's, on the spur where the grey stone had been found, of wood first and then, when the wood alone would not answer, of peat cut and dried the way the vein's own peat was cut, and he fed it through most of a day and half a night before old Tormod, dozing against a rock with the second basket of peat still unopened beside him, woke to find Fearchar standing over the ashes with something in his hand that had not been there when the fire was lit: a bead of metal no larger than a thumbnail, soft enough to press a mark into with a fingernail, and brighter, where it caught the last of the firelight, than anything either man had ever pulled whole out of the ground.

It is told that Fearchar said nothing for a long while, turning the bead in his fingers the way Talla na Lasrach's own keepers turned a coal to judge its burning, and that when he did speak it was only to ask Tormod whether the fire had answered to Talla na Lasrach in any way that either of them could account for, since he himself had lit it on the spur and fed it with his own hands and asked leave of no one to do either. Tormod, who had spent thirty years of his life keeping fires that answered to nobody but Talla na Lasrach, said that this one, so far as he could tell, answered to nobody at all, and the two men sat a while longer by the dying peat without either of them saying what they both plainly thought, which was that a fire owing nothing to the vein and nothing to the flame was a new thing on the island, whatever else it turned out to be.

Word of the bead reached Dùn ìseal within the week, carried by Tormod himself, who could not keep from telling it, and within the month Fearchar had built a proper furnace of clay and dry stone on the same spur, banked into the hillside the way the vein's own galleries were banked, and was smelting the grey stone by the basketful into beads and then into small ingots and then, once he had learned to hammer the soft metal without cracking it, into rings. The households of the ninth hearth called the place Muileann chiar, the dark mill, after the smoke that hung over it on a still morning, and the name held, though there was already another place of that name far to the east, near a holy wood, and the two were never once mistaken for each other by anyone who had walked to both.

## II. Of the Ring that Showed No Colour

The seam lay apart from the mountain's own vein by a distance no gallery had ever crossed, and it was not long before someone asked, as someone always asked of anything new on the island in those years, whether the new metal fell under the flame the way the coal did, and whether Fearchar's furnace, answering to nobody, ought in fact to be made to answer to Talla na Lasrach the same as every other fire that burned near Dùn ìseal.

Muireach Liath heard the question at Talla na Lasrach's own hearth, from two of the ninth household who put it to him plainly enough, and it is said that he asked first to see the metal before he answered anything, and sat a long while with one of Fearchar's rings turned in his fingers by the light of the flame he himself kept, much as Fearchar had turned the first bead by the light of his own fire on the spur. Whatever he was looking for in the ring, Talla na Lasrach's own coal never showed it to anyone: a coal, cracked open at the vein's own edge, showed its colours in the break, now one of the seven and now another, to any eye of the island that looked, and it was that colour and nothing else that the flame answered to and the households paid dues on. The ring showed nothing of the kind. It broke, when Muireach pressed a thumbnail hard enough into the softest part of it, with no colour at all beneath the break, only the same pale sheen the whole ring already wore, and Muireach set it down on the hearth-stone between himself and the two who had asked and said the thing that every teller of Talla na Lasrach's history repeats afterward in nearly the same words.

"Chan eil fuil san airgead," he said. *There is no blood in the silver.* He meant, and said so plainly when one of the two asked him to say more, that the coal of the vein bled a colour when it broke that every keeper since the first had sworn to answer for, and that this new metal showed nothing of the kind under any breaking he could give it, and so belonged, so far as the flame's own oath reached, to nobody at all. "Fearchar dug it, and Fearchar may dig on," he told them, "and whatever comes of the digging is his to keep or give as he sees fit, since Talla na Lasrach has no claim on a thing that shows no colour when it breaks." He gave the ruling once, at Talla na Lasrach's own hearth, and never troubled to give it again, though more than one household, watching the digging swell, muttered afterward that they wished the flame had claimed a share of it.

Muileann chiar dug on. Within two years the spur held four furnaces where Fearchar's one had stood, worked by his own kin and by a handful of households of the ninth hearth who had grown tired of carrying baskets for a vein that gave them coal and asked for their whole lives in return, and the place that had been a shepherd's path a few years before had a name of its own on every carrier's tongue between Dùn ìseal and the coast. Fearchar himself kept the first furnace and taught the others what he had learned of the fire's temper by trial and by the burns on his own hands, and it is remembered of him, by those who worked the spur in his own lifetime, that the first ring off every new furnace he raised was never sold nor worn nor traded but set instead, whole, on a flat stone at the mouth of the workings, an offering to Goibhniu, the smith beyond the smiths, before the second ring was so much as begun; and that no furnace of his ever failed in its first season, which the smiths of Muileann chiar took, for as long as the custom held, as proof enough that the god had taken the offering kindly.

## III. Of the Two Cousins and the Ground Between

The seam's freedom from the flame did not make it free of every other claim a household on the island knew how to press, and it was not two years before the ground the seam ran under was claimed, in the same season and with equal certainty, by two cousins of Earrann Caol shean who had never before had cause to trouble themselves over a stretch of hillside neither of them had thought worth a boundary stone.

The two were grandsons of one grandfather through different daughters, and the boundary between their households had run, since their grandfather's own day, along a burn that dried in most summers and was never once in living memory worth arguing over, since the ground either side of it was thin pasture that fed a scattering of goats and little else. The seam changed that reckoning inside a single season. Both cousins sent word to Dùn ìseal within days of each other, in the seventy-fifth year, each swearing that the spur where Fearchar's furnaces now stood lay on his own side of the dried burn and not his kinsman's, and each bringing, when the keepers of the slab asked for it, an account of the boundary that agreed with his own claim to the seam and disagreed with his cousin's in every particular that mattered.

It was not a matter the flame could rule on, the seam having already been ruled outside its reach, and the keepers of the slab, who had no more standing over a boundary between two heirs' households than the flame did, sent the two cousins back to settle it as households of their kin had always settled such things, by the word of elders both sides trusted and, failing that, by whatever each side could hold and defend. Neither cousin would yield the claim, and for the better part of a year the smiths of Muileann chiar worked their furnaces under the eyes of two households who watched each other across the dry burn more closely than either watched the fires, until a solution presented itself that satisfied neither man's pride and both men's purses at once: Fearchar himself, weary of digging under a dispute he had not started and could not settle, offered a ring to each cousin from the season's working, in the seventy-sixth year, on the plain condition that both let the digging go on undisturbed while their elders argued the boundary at their leisure.

Both cousins took the ring, neither gladly, since taking it looked, to anyone watching from the valley, a good deal like admitting that the ground in dispute was worth less to argue over than a season's silver was worth to hold; but neither man found, once the ring was in his hand, that he could think of a dignified way to give it back, and the digging went on. The burn's true course was settled at last by a boundary stone the two households raised together at the driest point of the burn's bed, cut with a mark on either face and set so that each cousin's flock could be seen from his own side of it, and the stone came to be known, to every household of Earrann Caol shean, simply as the silver stone, though not every household that used the name could have said why.

## IV. Of the Rings for Salt and Sealskin

It was carriers, not smiths, who first thought to take the new rings anywhere beyond the spur that made them, and it was to the river quays at Ros fhionn, a day and a half's walk north along the old path the pilgrims of Allt an Àigh had first worn, that the earliest of them were carried, in the same season that Fearchar's second furnace first began to answer as steadily as his own.

Ros fhionn stood where the river widened enough for boats out of the northern coast to come up on a rising tide and go down again on the next, and its households had long since learned to want more of everything than the land immediately about them could give: salt, which no household inland could make for itself in any quantity the winter's fish-curing wanted, and sealskin, which the boats brought down from the colder waters north of the cape and which no inland household had any way to come by at all except through a coast-dweller willing to trade it. What Muileann chiar's smiths had that Ros fhionn's boatmen wanted was less obvious to either side at first than it seems from this distance, since a ring of soft grey metal fed nobody and mended no net and kept out no weather, but a boatman who had carried salt fish up every river landing on that coast for twenty years put the two things together within a single evening's talk at the quay, reasoning that any household inland that wanted salt badly enough would find some way to want the rings too, once it was known that the coast would take them.

He was proved right within the season. The first exchange the quays remember plainly was a single ring for a full boatload of salted fish and sealskin together, struck between a carrier of the ninth hearth and a boatman whose name the tellers no longer agree on, and it stood, once it was known, as the measure every later trade was weighed against, so that for a long while afterward a boatman naming his price for a load of any size would say simply "a ring's worth" or "half a ring" and be understood by anyone on the quay without further reckoning. It was the first trade the island had ever struck in anything but food and shelter and labour owed, and the first whose price outlived the season it was struck in, carried in memory from one bargaining to the next the way a coal's colour was carried from one keeper to the next, and carriers who had grown old walking the path between Muileann chiar and Ros fhionn said, when they were old themselves, that they had watched a whole new kind of reckoning grow up on that path within their own lifetimes, though none of them, pressed to explain what the reckoning was for, could give an answer much better than that a ring bought what a ring bought, and everyone on both ends of the path had come, somehow, to agree on it.

The salt came back up the path in barrels lashed to poles carried two to a load, and the sealskin in bundles that smelled, more than one household said, of the whole northern sea at once, and Muileann chiar, which had been four furnaces and a scattering of turf huts a year before, grew a smokehouse and a salting shed within two more, until a traveller coming up from the coast could smell the place before the smoke of its furnaces ever came in sight of him. Fearchar himself went down to Ros fhionn only once, in the last years before his hands grew too stiff for the hammer, to see with his own eyes the place his rings had been going all that time, and is said to have stood a long while at the quay watching a boat unload salt he had never once thought, when he stood over his first bead of metal by a dying fire on the spur, to have any hand in bringing ashore.

## V. Of Ceallach's Long Years Ended

Not every household of that generation measured its years the way the smiths of Muileann chiar measured theirs, in furnaces raised and rings hammered, and it was in the seventy-ninth year of Clàr an Talla, while the boundary stone between the two cousins was still new-set and the salt road to Ros fhionn still young enough that old men remembered walking it for the first time, that Ceallach mac Ghille-Bhrìde died in the high valleys at the age of a hundred and forty years, the last of Somhairle's line in whom na Bliadhnachan Fada still showed.

He was Gille-Brìde's son, and had kept, since his father's death twenty-six years before, the coal of A' Chiad Lasair on his own hearth in the high valleys where the air ran thin and cold and few households troubled to build above the tree line at all, tending it the way his father had tended it since An Ceangal, while his own line reached back through years that the valley itself could no longer count with any certainty, since every teller who tried gave a different tally. His own years had begun to fail him some seasons before his death, the way a slope fails under snow that has been building a good while before anyone marks the first crack in it, and by his last winter he no longer walked the high paths himself but sat by his own hearth and let his grandchildren carry word up and down to him, watching the flame he kept grow, in his own words to more than one of them, "no dimmer, though I am."

He died in his sleep on a night no household afterward could fix with certainty, since the valley learned of it only when a grandson came up at dawn with bread and found the old man cold and the hearth-coal, by some account or other, already grown ash, though other tellers of the same death swear the coal still glowed when the household gathered and was carried down, in a covered dish, to be given at last to Talla na Lasrach's own flame, the line of Somhairle's long keeping ending where the line of Beathag Bhàn's shorter keeping had begun. His children, none of whom had inherited anything of his years beyond the ordinary span any household on the island by then expected of itself, did not stay in the high valleys once he was gone. They went down into the lower ground within the year, scattering among households of the ninth and the fourth hearths, and though the name of Somhairle's line was remembered a good while longer in the telling of Ceallach's own death, no household after him in that line was ever again spoken of as one that a stranger might mistake, meeting an old man on a slope, for someone who had watched the mountain since before Roinn na Rìoghachd.

It is told, and no teller of Talla na Lasrach's history has ever thought to doubt it, that word of Ceallach's death reached Fearchar at his furnace on the same day the last ring of that season was set out on the flat stone for Goibhniu, and that Fearchar, hearing it, set the tools down for the rest of the day, though the two men had never once met in life, on the plain reasoning, as he gave it to Tormod that evening, that the island had just grown a great deal younger all at once, and a man ought to mark that the same as he would mark anything else worth marking.

## VI. Of the Flame Passed to Gormshuil

Muireach Liath kept the flame ten years more after Ceallach's death, years in which Talla na Lasrach's roof held and Na Naoi Teallaichean came and went in their turns much as they had under Beathag Bhàn before him, and if he thought often, in those years, of the ring he had once turned in his fingers and found to show no colour when it broke, he gave Talla na Lasrach no further ruling on it, the first one having settled, so far as anyone could tell, every question the households afterward thought to ask him.

He died at Dùn ìseal in the eighty-ninth year, at eighty-one years of age, in much the same manner Beathag Bhàn had died before him: no illness the households could put a name to, only a slow failing of strength over one winter, until a morning came on which he did not rise, and Na Naoi Teallaichean were called together within the week to choose who should carry the flame after him, as they had been called to choose him after Beathag. Three of the households put forward candidates of their own that year, each with a claim the keepers of the slab judged sound enough to weigh seriously: a grandson of the sixth hearth who had already sat two seasons with the flame and shown, by every account, a steadiness the keepers valued; a daughter of the third hearth whose grandmother had been one of the nine who chose Beathag Bhàn herself, and who argued, through her own household, that the choosing owed something to that memory; and a nephew of Muireach's own, of the ninth hearth, whose chief claim, as more than one keeper noted privately, was blood and little more. Na Naoi Teallaichean heard all three argued for at length, over the better part of two days, and passed over all three in the end, choosing instead Muireach's own daughter Gormshuil, then forty-nine years of age, who had sat her own seasons with the flame without either household or grandmother's memory to speak for her beyond the work itself.

It is told that the three passed-over candidates took the choosing, each in his or her own fashion, better than the households behind them had expected: the grandson of the sixth hearth is remembered to have said only that the flame had chosen rightly before and would likely go on doing so without his help; the daughter of the third hearth kept her own grandmother's memory alive in another fashion, knotting at last, on a cord of her own, the names of every household that had sat a season with the flame since Beathag Bhàn, a cord Talla na Lasrach kept and went on adding to; and Muireach's own nephew, who alone among the three might have pressed his claim further on the strength of blood, chose instead to go north to Muileann chiar within the year and take up work at one of Fearchar's furnaces, where it is said he prospered a good deal more than he might have as a Keeper who had come to the flame by kinship rather than by the households' own choosing.

Gormshuil nic Mhuirich took the flame from her father's own hands before he died, in the last days when he could still hold it steady enough to pass it on, and carried it into Talla na Lasrach the evening Na Naoi Teallaichean' choosing was made known, past the same door that faced the vein and away from the sea that every Keeper before her had passed through. Seven years into her keeping, in the ninety-sixth year, when the smiths of Muileann chiar had grown from Fearchar's single furnace to a settlement the carriers named without needing to explain which Muileann chiar they meant, the kin of Gormshuil herself at Dùn ìseal began to be spoken of, by households up and down the valley, by a name of their own, and no longer by their hearth alone: Sliochd Mhuirich, the first house of the flame's keeping that the island had ever troubled to call by a name rather than a number. No account that has come down of those years tells of the other eight hearths raising any objection to it, whether because none was raised or because none thought worth remembering, and the name held, as the ring's silver had held against every argument brought to keep it from the smiths who first hammered it, and no household of the ninth hearth troubled any further to ask why a house needed a name at all when a hearth had always done well enough.
