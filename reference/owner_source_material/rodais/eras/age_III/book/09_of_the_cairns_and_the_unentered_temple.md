# Of the Cairns and the Unentered Temple

*FE 16 – 44*

> *Chan eil dia air a' bheinn; tha cuimhne.*
> There is no god on the mountain; there is memory.
>
> — said still among the nine hearths, of the reverence that took no priests

## I. Of the Name the Hearths Gave It

It is told at Dùn ìseal, by those who keep the memory of the nine hearths the way the knot-keepers keep the memory of the cord, that the reverence they practised about the mountain had gone a long while without any name at all, because a thing done by everyone from birth has no more need of a name than breathing does, until a stranger asks what it is called and there is, for the first time, nobody ready with an answer.

The stranger, when he came, was no stranger to the valley: a walker of the eastern share who had married into the ninth hearth and came to sit his season with the flame in the eighteenth year after the choosing of Beathag Bhàn, and who asked, on his first evening in the Hall, what the households called the thing they did at every dawn and every dusk and every turning of the moon, the small bowl of milk left on the sill for the dead, the coal-dust rubbed into a threshold before a birth, the silence kept on the ridge above Cnoc ghlas when the cloud came wrong. Beathag herself did not know the answer. She had grown up inside the doing of it, as everyone about the mountain had, and had never once needed a word for a thing that was simply what a morning was for.

It was Beitris, old by then and past climbing to the cairns herself, who gave it its name that winter, sitting by the Hall's own hearth with the flame between her and the stranger's question: *Seann Spioradan nan Dia-thìreach*, the Old Spirits of the Dia-thìrich, and when the stranger asked what that meant, exactly, whether it was one spirit or many, whether it was the ancestors or the hills or the vein itself that a person honoured with the milk and the coal-dust, Beitris told him it was all of these and none of them separately, the way a man does not honour his own left hand more than his right, and that the reverence kept no priests because it needed none, having nothing to teach that every child did not already learn at its mother's knee: that the dead were owed remembering, that the sìth of the hollow hills were owed courtesy, that the vein was owed its season and its silence, and that the weather ruled all three and was owed the most careful watching of anybody's.

The name went down the valley faster than most things went, carried by carriers who had no carrying yet to speak of beyond their own two legs and the goods they could sling across them, and within a few years it was the name every household used for what it had always done without a name, so that a thing that had been nameless for the whole of living memory came, in the end, to seem as though it had always been called that, and the households who used the name forgot, within a generation, that anyone had ever needed to ask.

Beathag Bhàn heard the name from Beitris's own mouth, and turned it over for a long moment before she answered, the way she turned over most things that came to her fast. "It will do," she said at last. "It says what it needs to and claims nothing it shouldn't." She meant, though she did not say so plainly even to Beitris, that a reverence that kept no priests and claimed no single mouth to speak for it was of a piece with the flame she herself kept: a thing held by everyone's hands together and by no one's alone, which was, as near as she could tell, the whole of what the choosing on the slope had been for.

## II. Of Mòrag nan Càrn and the Watch on the Ridge

Above Cnoc ghlas the ground climbed in long grey shoulders to a ridge that caught every weather coming off the high passes before the valley below had so much as felt the first of the wind, and it was there, in the years after the Hall was raised, that the watching of the cloud was given its proper cairns, three of them at first and more after, built by hands that knew already, from a hundred years of losing coal to a bad reading, that a pile of stones on a height could save a season if a person only troubled to read it right.

Mòrag was of the fifth hearth, a quiet child who had shown, from the time she could walk the slope without being carried, an unaccountable gift for telling one grey sky from another that no one in her own household could match and no one much troubled to explain. She could stand at the door of her mother's house on a morning that looked, to every other eye in the yard, exactly like the morning before it, and say whether the day would hold or break by noon, and be right often enough that by the time she was grown the old watchers of the ridge had stopped asking her how she knew and started simply asking her.

She went up to the cairns for the first time in the tenth year, not yet twenty and sent, at first, only to carry water and turf for the watchers' fire, and stayed, by the end of that first season, because Aonghas Ruadh, the eldest watcher then living, told her plainly that he had watched that ridge for forty years and had never once seen a child read the western cloud as fast as she did, and that a man who did not use a gift like that when it stood in front of him was a fool twice over, once for having it wasted and once for knowing it was wasted and doing nothing. Within three years the elder watchers named her to the cairns in her own right, the youngest ever given them to keep, and she went on keeping them, by the end, longer than anyone before her.

The three cairns had not always stood where they stood. The first watchers, in the years before Mòrag's own grandmother was born, had kept no fixed mark at all, only a habit of climbing whatever height seemed nearest and trusting their own eye to the cloud, and it was a watcher of that older kind, an eastern woman named Sorcha whose grave no one now could point to, who first began piling stones at the three places on the ridge where she found she read the sky truest, so that a watcher coming after her would have the same three points to stand on rather than a fresh guess every morning. The households never troubled to hallow the cairns with any rite, and Mòrag herself thought that fitting, since a cairn's whole worth lay in standing exactly where the last watcher's eye had proved it useful, and a stone moved for the sake of ceremony was a stone moved for the worse.

The watch was simple to describe and hard to do well. Three cairns stood along the ridge, each built to a height a watcher's own eye could use as a mark against the sky, and from each of them, on any morning the galleries below were meant to open for the season's cutting, a watcher climbed before first light and read the cloud coming over the high passes: whether it carried only wind, which the coal-cutters could work under well enough with their cloaks drawn tight, or whether it carried the wet, low weight of rain that would find its way down through every crack the mountain had and turn a gallery's footing to mud before the cutters below had cut a single basket. A wrong reading either way cost something. A watcher too cautious kept the galleries shut on a day that would have given good coal, and the households went short for want of coal that was there for the taking; a watcher too bold sent the cutters down under a sky that broke by mid-morning, and more than once, in the years before Mòrag climbed the ridge, a gallery had come down on men working under rain they had been told would hold off.

"The sky doesn't lie," Mòrag told the two youths sent up to learn the watch from her in the twentieth year, standing with them at the first cairn in a wind that had already found every gap in their cloaks. "It only speaks a tongue most people never learn to hear right, and it doesn't repeat itself for the slow." She showed them the shape a cloud took when it meant only wind, high and torn and moving fast, and the shape it took when it meant the wet, lower and thicker and slower, though the difference, to the boy from the second hearth who had come up mostly because his household had nowhere else to send him that season, looked for the first two months like no difference at all, and he said so plainly, and she did not scold him for saying it, only told him to come back up the next morning and look again, and the one after that, until the difference stopped needing to be pointed out to him.

## III. Of the Season the Mountain Lost

It was in the twenty-ninth year, on a morning that Mòrag herself was not on the ridge to read, that the wrong reading came that the tellers still speak of, and it came, as the worst of such things generally do, from a watcher who had done the work long enough to trust himself past the point his own eyes deserved.

Fearchar Glas had watched the second cairn for six years by then and never once been badly wrong, and on the morning in question he read the cloud coming low and grey off the passes and judged it wind only, the kind a cutter could work under with a cloak drawn tight, and sent the word down to the Hall that the galleries might open. The cutters went down in good spirits, since the season had been slow to start and every household wanted its measure filled before the shorter days came, and they had not been at the coal three hours before the sky that Fearchar had judged wind-only opened into the heaviest rain the mountain had seen that year, water finding every seam and crack above the low gallery and running down into it faster than any hand could bail, so that the gallery had to be given up entirely, its footing gone soft and unsafe for the rest of the season, and with it the whole autumn's cutting that the households had been counting on.

Fearchar did not try to excuse it. He came down off the ridge himself that evening, before anyone had sent for him, and stood before the keepers of the slab and told them plainly that he had read the cloud wrong and knew it the moment the rain began, and that he would not watch the cairns again unless they told him to. They did not tell him to. The tellers say it was never spoken as punishment, and Fearchar himself never called it that; the keepers judged, plainly, after that season, that a watch on which a whole mountain's coal for a whole season could turn deserved a choosing at least as careful as the choosing that had once put a flame in a girl's hands on the slope below the slab, and from that year the cairns were given to no one who had not first stood a full season under an elder watcher's eye and been judged, by more than one pair of eyes, fit to read a sky alone.

Mòrag herself sat on the small council that judged the new watchers after that, and she was harder on the youths who came to her than Aonghas Ruadh had ever been on her, because she had watched a season lost from a mistake she herself might have made on a lazier morning, and she told anyone who asked why she pressed the young ones so hard that a watcher who had never once doubted himself had simply not yet met the morning that would teach him to. "Fearchar wasn't a fool," she said, more than once, in the years after. "He was only sure. Sureness is the one thing this work can't afford, and it's the one thing that comes free to anyone who does it long enough not to be careful."

## IV. Of the Cold on the Doire uaine Slopes

The Doire uaine hills lay north-west of the mountain, a low green country of birch and rowan where the old inscriptions on the standing stones, cut by hands nobody living could name, forbade any fire, of wood or of coal, to be lit within sight of them, and the keepers of the slab held to that prohibition as strictly as they held to anything the Binding itself demanded, though none of them could say, when asked, what the inscriptions meant by it or whose the hand had been that first cut the warning into the stone.

It fell hardest on the herders who wintered their flocks on those slopes, since a hillside with grazing enough for sheep through the cold months is not a hillside a household gives up lightly for want of a fire, and the households that held grazing rights on the Doire uaine slopes had held them since before the fall of the old king and were not about to move their flocks elsewhere on the strength of an inscription none of them could read. They wintered there instead as they always had, and ate cold, as they always had, boiling nothing, warming nothing, wrapping themselves in as much wool as a body could carry and moving enough, through the short grey days, to keep the blood working, and told their children, as their own parents had told them, that a fire lit within sight of those hills was a debt taken out against something none of them wanted to meet the collector of.

The thirty-third year was harder than most, a winter that came early and stayed late, with a wind off the western sea that found its way through wool the way water finds its way through unfired clay, and it was that winter the tellers remember best, because it was the winter a boy of nine, sent out from Doire uaine's northern steading to bring in a ewe caught in a drift, was himself caught by the same wind and found two days later, alive but barely, huddled in the lee of a boundary stone with no fire he had dared light to warm him, though he had carried flint enough to strike one and had chosen, even half out of his mind with cold, not to strike it within sight of those hills.

His mother carried him down to the Hall herself rather than risk the shorter road past the standing stones, and Beathag Bhàn sat with the boy through the night he was brought in, since a Keeper's hearth was the one fire on the mountain that had never asked anyone's leave to burn, and she asked him, once he was warm enough to speak, why he had not struck the flint, cold as he was and alone as he was and with no one there to see whether he broke the old rule or kept it. "Because it isn't only about being seen," the boy told her, through teeth that had not yet stopped chattering. "My grandmother says the hills know a fire whether anyone's watching for it or not." Beathag did not answer that at once. She had, by then, kept a flame for near thirty years that the whole valley had agreed to trust without watching over her shoulder every hour of it, and she found, turning the boy's words over, that she had no better account of her own reverence than his grandmother's, and did not try to find one.

The ban was never lifted in all the years after, and no herder of those slopes, so far as the tellers know, was ever proven to have broken it, though more than one household lost a beast, and once very nearly a child, to a winter they might have made easier with a fire they would not strike.

Beathag sent word to the northern steading that winter that any household of the Doire uaine slopes was welcome, whatever the hour, to bring a sick child or a frost-bitten hand up to the Hall's own hearth, since the ban spoke of fires lit in sight of those hills and said nothing at all of a fire kept a good many miles off at Dùn ìseal. Few households made the journey, the distance being what it was and a sick child a hard thing to carry so far in the cold, but the offer was remembered longer than it was used, and the boy who had chosen the drift over the flint told his own children, in his turn, that the Keeper's hearth had once been held open for his sake, and that he had never once needed to walk to it to know the offer had been meant.

## V. Of Beathag's Walk to the Ruin

There was a place, some way east of the mountain along a spur no gallery had ever been cut into, that the valley called Làrach an Teampaill, the temple ruin, and had called it that for longer than any household could account for, a low broken wall of dressed stone standing in bracken with an altar-slab at its centre that had clearly been shaped by hands and clearly, from the wear on it, had not been shaped recently.

It belonged, by every account the households gave, to nobody: not to the keepers of the slab, whose reverence had its slab at Dùn ìseal and no other; not to the Hall, whose flame was sworn to touch nothing outside its own hearth; not to any heir's line, since no share's boundary had ever been drawn to take it in, whether by accident or by the deliberate choice of stewards who wanted no part of a ruin nobody could explain. It sat in the bracken, uncounted and unclaimed, and every teller who spoke of it in those years said the same thing in more or less the same words: that it had stood before the Roinn, before the Binding, before, by the look of the moss on its oldest stones, the fall of the old king himself, and that nobody now living had ever heard a true account of who had raised it or for what.

Beathag walked out to it alone in the thirty-fourth year, on a morning she told no one she meant to keep, not even Beitris, though Beitris guessed something of it afterward from the mud on her shoes and said nothing until Beathag was ready to speak of it herself. She had heard, by then, every account the valley gave of the ruin, each one different from the last, and had come to feel, tending a flame she herself had been given by no explanation better than "hold it for Brìde, and answer to Crom", that a place so near her own mountain and so wholly unaccounted for was a thing she owed it to her own office to see with her own eyes rather than take entirely on other people's word.

The walk took the better part of a morning, over ground broken enough that she lost the path twice and found it again by the shape of the spur against the sky rather than by any mark on the ground itself, and she came on the ruin from the north, through bracken taller than her waist, before she quite expected to, so that the first stone of the old wall was almost under her hand before she saw it for what it was.

What she found inside the broken walls was less than she had braced herself for and stranger for being less. No image, no carved face, no sign she could read as belonging to any god or spirit the valley named by any name she knew: only the altar-slab, waist-high and long enough for a person to lie on, standing at the ruin's centre with its face turned, unmistakably and without any accident of the ground to explain it, toward the mountain, toward the very shoulder of rock under which she herself kept the flame. She stood before it a long while, in a stillness broken only by wind moving through bracken that had grown up through gaps in the old paving, and found, the longer she stood there, that she could not decide whether the turning troubled her or steadied her, since a thing that faced her own mountain might have been raised in reverence of it, or might, for all she could tell standing there with the wind pulling at her cloak, have been raised to keep some older watch on it that had nothing to do with reverence at all.

She came back down off the spur before the light went, and told the keepers of the slab what she had found, plainly and without adding to it what she could not swear to. "It is not Brìde's," she told them, "and it is not Crom's. Whatever hands turned that stone toward the mountain, they were not ours, and I would not have us claim a place we cannot account for, the same as I would not have any of the heirs claim the flame." Some of the keepers wanted to send masons out to clear the bracken and set a watch on the place, the way a watch was kept on the cairns; others wanted it left exactly as it stood, on the plain reasoning that a place three ages might have left alone was not a place four ages ought to disturb on a guess. Beathag herself, when the argument had gone on longer than she cared for, put an end to it the way she had learned to put an end to most arguments that could not be settled by evidence: she said that no keeper of the slab, and no Keeper of the flame after her, would set foot inside that ruin again, since a reverence built on remembering the dead and courtesy to the sìth was no reverence at all if it went stamping over ground it could not read.

The vow held. Whose place it truly was, the tellers say, the keepers never afterward claimed to know, and it seems, from everything that has come down of those years, that they did not know, and were, in the end, content not to.

## VI. Of the Flame Passed On

Beathag Bhàn kept the flame ten years more after her walk to the ruin, quieter years than the ones before them, in which the nine hearths came and went in their turns and the Hall's roof held against every winter it was given, and if she thought often of the altar turned toward her own mountain, she said little of it to anyone but Beitris, and Beitris, by the time the two of them were old women together, had learned not to press her on it.

She died in the forty-fourth year, at Dùn ìseal, within sight of the mountain she had answered to for forty years, and the manner of her dying was as plain as the manner of her choosing had been: no illness anyone could name, only a slowing, over one winter, of a body that had carried water up a slope for two summers and shielded a flame against every wind the high passes could send down, until one grey morning near the year's turn she did not rise from the bed Cailean's own children had built her, and Beitris, sitting with her as she had sat with so many others by then, understood before anyone told her that the watch had changed hands.

They laid her on the slope below the Hall, facing the vein under the mountain, with her back to the sea, though every teller in the valley knew, and said so plainly at the burial, that the dead of the Dia-thìrich went west over that same sea to whatever waited for them there, and more than one of the nine households wondered aloud, in the days after, whether a Keeper ought not to be laid the same way as everyone else who had ever died on the island. Beitris answered that question before it could grow into a quarrel, standing at the graveside with a certainty she had not always owned in her younger years: "She never once, in forty years, turned her back on the vein while she lived. I'll not have her do it the one day she can't choose for herself." No one argued the point further, and every Keeper after Beathag Bhàn was laid the same way, facing the mountain, though the reason given for it, in the generations that followed, slowly stopped being Beitris's grief and became simply the custom, the way the reverence itself had gone, in Beathag's own lifetime, from an unnamed thing everyone did to a name everyone used without asking why.

The nine hearths gathered within the week to choose who should hold the flame after her, as they had gathered once before to choose Beathag herself, though this time there was no slope crowded with heirs looking on, only the households and the keepers of the slab and Sìne nan Snaidhm's daughter come up with a fresh length of cord in case a knot should be wanted, which in the end it was not, since the choosing this time needed no long argument to settle it.

Beathag had a sister, long since married into a household of the fourth hearth, and that sister had a son named Muireach, quiet and grey-eyed even as a boy, who had come up to sit his own season with the flame twice already and had shown, both times, a stillness about the work that more than one of the keepers had already marked without saying so aloud. He was named that week, the nine hearths agreeing on him faster than any choosing the mountain had yet seen, and Beitris herself, old now and near the end of her own years, put the rush into his hands with words close enough to the ones Aodh Crom had once used that more than one person standing near enough to hear thought, for a moment, that the old keeper had somehow come back to say them himself.

"Hold it for Brìde," she told him, "and answer to Crom for what is taken from the vein." Muireach Liath took the flame the way his aunt had taken it before him, without much to say for himself, and carried it into the Hall that same evening, past the door that faced the vein and away from the sea, to keep it there through years the mountain had not yet begun to ask of him.
