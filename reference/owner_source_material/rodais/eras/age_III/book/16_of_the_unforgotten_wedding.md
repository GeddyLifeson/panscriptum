# Of the Unforgotten Wedding

*FE 190 – 380*

> *Cha do scuir an fhuil ged a scuir an sabaid.*
> The blood did not stop when the fighting stopped.
>
> — a saying of the road, of the long strife between the king's blood

## I. Of an Old Man's Telling

Niall Òg told this tale more often than any other he kept, and told it, by every account the carriers give of him, the same way each time, sitting at whatever hearth would have him with a bowl going cold in his hands and his eyes on the fire rather than on whoever sat across from him, so that a listener new to the man might have thought he spoke to the flame and not to them at all. He was well past a thousand years old before the tale's own ending caught up with him, and older still before he stopped telling it, and those who heard it from him more than once said he never shaved a detail from it nor added one, though the households he told it to grew, across those same centuries, from grandchildren to great-grandchildren who had not known the people in it and would not live to see how it came out.

It was not a tale of a battle, he would say first, by way of warning a listener not to expect one, and it was not much of a tale of a wedding either, whatever its name implied, but a tale of how little a household needs to begin a grudge that will outlive every soul who remembers what it was for. He had watched the whole of it from somewhere near enough to see faces, being kin himself to both sides of the quarrel and welcome, or near enough welcome, at either table, and it was this, he said, more than his years, that let him carry the story whole from one end to the other where a teller born to only one side of it would have carried only half.

The strife of the heirs was old by the year he set his telling in, older by then than the mountain's own carriers' road, and it had long since stopped being, in any single man's memory, a quarrel about anything a living man could name. It ran instead the way a river runs, from a source no one now living had seen, through banks it had cut for itself over generations, carrying forward whatever fell into it without much caring what that thing had originally been. Men who had begun feuds in their own youth lived, by the strange mercy or curse of the king's blood, to grow old inside them, to see their own grandchildren born into a hatred they themselves had once started for a reason that no longer signified to anyone but them, and some, Niall said, forgot the reason before they forgot the hatred, which he judged the saddest thing a long life could do to a man.

He would say, to any listener young enough to ask why he troubled to tell so long and so joyless a tale at all, that he told it because it was the truest thing he knew of his own people, truer than any account of a war won or a Hall built, and that a man who wished to understand why the island's governors spent so many centuries governing so little beyond their own grudges had first to understand how small a thing a grudge could be at its beginning, and how patiently, given long enough years to work in, it could grow into something neither side any longer knew how to set down. He had buried enough of his own kin by the year he first told it this way to know the shape of the thing from the inside, having lost cousins and great-nephews to quarrels he himself had never once raised a hand in, and he counted himself, more than once in the telling, luckier than most of his blood only in having been born to no share worth fighting over, a grandson twice removed and content to stay so.

Those who carried his sayings on after him, hearth to hearth, kept this tale nearer the front of their own tellings than its length alone would have earned it, on the plain reckoning that a people who forgot how their own quarrels began were in danger of repeating them exactly, and that Niall's own telling, however sad its shape, was meant less as an entertainment than as a warning kept warm at the hearth for whoever next felt a boast rising in him at a wedding table.

## II. Of the Wedding at Ros dhomhain

It began, so far as any beginning could be put to a thing so old, at a wedding held in the two hundred and fourteenth year of the age, at Ros dhomhain, in the western share that had come down by then, three generations removed, from Fionnghal nic Ailein, the king's own daughter, who had held it quietly from her own hall until that very year. The couple being married that day were of no great account in the quarrel to come, a daughter of one of Fionnghal's household stewards and a young mason of the fourth hearth, and it is one of the tale's own small cruelties, Niall would say, that neither of their names is now much remembered, though the day itself is remembered by everyone.

Among the guests, as was the custom at any wedding held on a share's own ground, sat a scattering of the wider kin: cousins of the bride's mistress, cousins of cousins, and among them a great-grandson of Fionnghal's own line named Griogair mac Lachlainn, a man of some five-and-forty years, quick-tempered by every account and quicker still with drink in him, who had come from the western share's own hall to stand for his kinswoman at her wedding. Across the same table, sent as guest by a marriage two generations back that had tied a thread of Baile ghorm's salt-shore blood into the mason's own family, sat Tormod mac Ruairidh, a great-grandson of Artair mac Ailein and near the same age as Griogair, a man no less proud of his own descent and no more patient with drink than his counterpart.

The two had never met before that day, and might, by every reasonable turn of chance, never have troubled to meet again, had the ale not run generous and the talk not turned, as talk at such tables will, to whose blood carried further in the island's reckoning. The hall at Ros dhomhain was hung that evening with rowan and late heather, the mason's own kin having brought down whatever the hillside above the town still offered so far into the season, and the long table had been set with three separate jugs of the western share's own brew, strong enough that a guest who drank steadily through the toasts might find, by the fourth or fifth, that his tongue moved somewhat ahead of his judgement. Niall himself sat near the door with a cousin of the bride's, an old habit of his at any gathering he had not been asked to lead, and watched, without much alarm at first, the talk at the middle of the table climb from the mason's own trade to the standing of the two families who had lent the wedding its wider guests.

Griogair said something of the western share's age and standing that Niall, questioned closely on it across the whole rest of his own life, would only repeat as "a boast no worse than any man makes drunk at a wedding": that Ros dhomhain had held its ground unbroken since the king's own daughter first claimed it, while other shares, he said, had passed through more hands than a common toll-stick. Tormod answered it with something sharper, a word about the salt shore's own dignity set against the west's, that carried, by the account of three separate guests who told it afterward and agreed on this much if nothing else, a plain slight against Fionnghal's own memory, dead scarcely a season by then, and the manner of her death, which he named, in front of her own great-grandson and half her own household, an old woman's slow going rather than the peaceful end the family itself preferred to speak of. Griogair struck him across the table with the flat of his own drinking-horn before a third word could be spoken, the horn's rim catching Tormod high on the cheekbone and drawing a line of blood that both families would remember, in later tellings, at very different lengths, and the wedding that had begun in a house garlanded for a mason's daughter ended with two great-grandsons of the king's own children dragged apart by six men apiece and sent home to their separate shares nursing separate grievances that neither, in the whole of his own life afterward, would consent to set down. The mason and his bride, by every account Niall gathered afterward, danced out the rest of their own wedding with as much cheer as they could summon, and are remembered, where they are remembered at all, only as the two people whose feast it had briefly, uselessly, been.

## III. Of How a Small Thing Grows

It would have ended there, Niall always insisted, any ordinary quarrel between two ordinary men ending, as such things do, in a season's coldness and a slow forgetting, had the two men in question been of no particular blood. But Griogair and Tormod were each, in his own share, a man whose word carried weight beyond his own person, and each carried the insult home as an insult offered to the whole of his line through him, and never once, in all the years after, as a private hurt he alone might have chosen to forgive, so that within the year the western share and the salt shore, which had traded fish for grain across that same stretch of coast for three generations without a cross word between them, found their carriers refused lodging at each other's waypoint houses and their tolls counted twice as carefully at each other's borders.

A raid followed within two years, a small thing as such things are first counted, a dozen head of cattle lifted from a salt-shore pasture by men who called it no more than payment owed for an insulted household, and answered within the season by a barn burned on the western share's own ground, and by the fifth year of the quarrel neither side could any longer say with certainty who had struck first, only that the other side had certainly struck since, and struck badly, and owed for it. Niall Òg, who had watched the wedding itself from a bench near the door and thought little of it at the time beyond the ordinary regret any man feels at a spoiled feast, told listeners across the centuries that he marked the moment the quarrel changed from a grudge between two drunk cousins into a strife between two households as the exact moment he understood, better than any lesson the Hall's own reciters had ever given him, how the whole vast tangle of the king's blood had come to hate its own kin so thoroughly for so long. A hundred small wrongs, each answered a little harder than it was given, had done the work no single great wrong could have managed alone, until the answering became the whole of what either side remembered wanting, and the wedding itself, the mason and his bride and the ale and the garlands, was forgotten by everyone but the two men it had made enemies.

## IV. Of Eimhir nic Mhuirich and the Flame

The Hall at Dùn ìseal kept its own business through these same years largely apart from the quarrels of the king's blood beyond it, as the Binding had always meant it to, though the Keeper who held the flame through the middle of this strife, a woman named Eimhir nic Mhuirich, of the hearth-line rather than the king's own, watched the widening feuds with a plainer eye than any heir could manage for want of a stake in either side of them. She was chosen by the nine hearths in the two hundred and thirty-sixth year, a woman then of six-and-twenty and thought young for the choosing, and she kept the flame four-and-fifty years after, longer than any Keeper since Fionnlagh Dall and calmer, the tellings say, than most before him.

Eimhir's own rule of the Hall, which she was heard to repeat to every heir who came to the threshold to plead one side of the quarrel or the other, was that the stone cup filled and emptied by measure and by nothing else, and that a household's dues were owed to whichever lord's stewards came first to the door in the season they fell due, whatever quarrel that lord's blood carried against his neighbour's. She would not judge between Sliochd Fhionnghail and Sliochd Artair, as the two lines had come by then to call themselves, nor take either's account of who had wronged whom first, and it is told that when Tormod mac Ruairidh himself came to Dùn ìseal some years into her keeping of the flame to ask the Hall's blessing on a raid he meant to call justice rather than vengeance, she heard him out in full, sitting the whole while on the low stool the Keepers kept beside the stone cup, and asked him, when he had finished, whether the salt shore's own measure had gone short that season. He said it had not. She told him then only that the Hall blessed no man's raids, being in the business of coal and not of grudges, and that he was welcome to as much of the one as his household's measure allowed and none of the other whatever his measure, and when he pressed her a second time, naming his great-grandfather's blood as a claim on her sympathy if not her blessing, she answered that the flame she kept had been lit before Artair mac Ailein was born and would, with any luck the Hall was owed, still be burning after every quarrel his blood had raised was as forgotten as the ash of it, which she judged a truer measure of what mattered than any grudge he cared to bring her.

He left without the blessing he had come for, and it is told, though not by Tormod's own household, that he raided anyway within the season and lost two men doing it, and never again troubled the Hall to bless what he had already decided to do without it. Eimhir died in the two hundred and ninetieth year of the age, some eighty years old, having kept the flame steadier through the widening quarrel than the quarrel's own principals kept their tempers, and the nine hearths chose after her a younger Keeper who would hold much the same course, the Hall's own indifference to the king's blood's private wars having become, by then, as settled a custom as the measure itself.

## V. Of the Deaths of the King's Children

The old children of Ailean Mòr, who had held their shares since before the strife had any name at all, went one after another into the ground during these same decades, each death loosed a little more of the discipline their long presence had kept over their own descendants, whatever quarrels those descendants ran beneath them. Fionnghal nic Ailein, who had held the western share since the earliest days of the age and had watched the wedding quarrel begin from her own hall without living to see where it led, died in the two hundred and thirteenth year at some two thousand eight hundred years of age, the mountain's own reckoning agreeing that no soul born of the grove before or since had held so western and quiet a share so long or so lightly. Her own funeral, held at Ros dhomhain within the same season as the wedding, was the last occasion on which Sliochd Fhionnghail and Sliochd Artair stood under one roof without a blow struck between them, and even that peace, Niall said, was kept only because Griogair mac Lachlainn had the sense, sober at his own great-grandmother's grave, to send word to Tormod's household that no man of the salt shore would be turned from the door that day whatever lay between them, a courtesy neither line troubled to repeat afterward.

Artair mac Ailein, whose salt-shore blood ran now through Tormod's own veins, died in the two hundred and ninety-eighth year at two thousand six hundred years old, at his own hall above Baile ghorm, the tellings agreeing only that his last years were harder than his first, the widening feud a weight he had not asked to carry into his own old age and could not, by then, set down. Cormac mac Ailein, whose eastern share at Caol shean had taken no direct part in the quarrel between west and salt shore but felt its cold in every market that dealt with both, died in the three hundred and thirty-first year at some two thousand four hundred and fifty years, the last of the king's sons to hold a share, and it is told that his final word to his own household was a plain wish that they keep clear of a quarrel that was, by his own long reckoning, no business of theirs, a wish his descendants honoured for rather less than a generation.

"A death among the king's own blood," Niall would say at this point in the telling, looking up from the fire the one time in the whole tale that he looked up at all, "is a thousand years of patience lost all at once, and a household that has just buried two thousand years of it does not, in my long experience, grow more patient for the burying." He had watched three such funerals within his own span by the time he told the tale this way, and would watch a good many more before his own turn came, and he counted each one, he said, a small defeat for whatever peace the island might otherwise have kept.

## VI. Of Niall's Peace at the Long Table

In the three hundred and fifty-second year, wearied at last of watching a quarrel he had first seen begin over spilled ale at a stranger's wedding grow, across well over a century, into raids that now cost real lives on both sides, Niall Òg did what he had done once before at Mòr nic Dhùghaill's own table and tried, on a larger and more deliberate stage, to bring the two lines to a peace. He sent word to the heads of both Sliochd Fhionnghail and Sliochd Artair, by then men two generations removed from Griogair and Tormod themselves, that he would host a meeting on ground that belonged to neither, a long table set up in the open beside the carriers' road at the crossing near Ros fhionn, and that he asked only that both sides come unarmed and hear each other out before the day was through.

Both sides came, which Niall counted afterward the meeting's one true success, though it proved the only one. He had chosen the crossing near Ros fhionn deliberately, remembering the bridge Ailean Clachair's masons had thrown there near two centuries before with a hearth-sign cut into the keystone for luck, and thought a place built to be crossed by everyone might carry something of its own purpose into the men who sat beside it. He set two benches facing each other across a single board laid on trestles, no lord's own hall to lend one side or the other the comfort of its own hearth, and had a single pot of plain stew set between them, enough for both tables and belonging, by his own design, to neither.

The head of the western line, a great-great-grandson of Griogair's named for a grandfather Niall had known and buried, spoke first and at length of every wrong the salt shore had done his household across fourteen decades: cattle lifted, a barn burned, a cousin's arm broken at a market and never answered for. "You count your dead," he said, when he had finished, looking across the board rather than at Niall, "and I will count mine, and we will see whose column runs the longer, and that will settle it as well as anything else has." The head of the salt-shore line answered with an accounting no shorter and no less bitter, matching burning for burning and broken arm for broken arm down a list he seemed, from the ease of his own telling, to have carried ready in his head for years against exactly this occasion, and finished it by saying that he had not come to apologise for what his own blood had done in answer to what the west had done first, and would not, whatever an old man's long memory of a widow's table led him to hope.

By the time both accountings were finished the sun had gone down and the pot of stew sat cold and largely untouched between them, neither man having troubled to eat from it, and neither had once, in all that speaking, offered the other anything that could fairly be called an apology, only a longer and more particular list of grievances than either had troubled to draw up before. Niall, who had hoped the mere act of speaking plainly to one another might loosen something the years had knotted, told the two men before they parted that he had sat at this same trick of a long table once already in his own life and watched it work between a widow's guests who owed each other nothing, and that he did not understand, and did not expect ever to understand, why it failed so completely between men who shared the very blood he shared himself. "Because," the salt-shore man told him, rising, "your widow's guests had a debt between them that ended at her door. Ours has no door it ends at." Neither man answered Niall again after that. They rose from the table at the same moment, an agreement neither side would ever plainly own to, and walked back to their own shares by the same road they had come by, leaving the cold stew for whoever wanted it, and the raids resumed within the season, if indeed they had ever truly stopped for it.

## VII. Of Eachann Ruadh's Bed

Not every death of the king's blood in these years came of the wedding quarrel or its like. Eachann Ruadh mac Mhurchaidh, who had killed Ìomhar Dearg at the ford in vengeance for his own brother a full four generations before and had lived the whole of the strife's long middle as an old man at the edge of the Hall's own ground, neither forgiven nor pursued, died in his bed in the three hundred and seventy-seventh year of the age, at seven hundred and twenty-nine years old, the last of his own generation of the king's kin and by some distance the quietest death among them. He had outlived the killing that made his name by longer than most men's whole lives run, and it is told that in his final seasons he spoke of it seldom and without pride, saying once to a grandchild who asked him of the ford that a man's youngest deed followed him further than any of his later ones, however small it had seemed at the time he did it, a lesson he offered, Niall said, with more weariness than wisdom, though the two are not always easily told apart in an old man's mouth.

His own line, unlike Fionnghal's and Artair's, took no fresh quarrel from his death, having settled, a generation before, into a peace with Dùghall Dearg's Red Share that neither side troubled to disturb, and it was remarked at the time, by those who kept account of such things, that the one death among the king's old blood in these years to pass without raising a single spear was the death of a man who had himself once raised one in earnest. Niall, telling this part of the tale, would let it sit a moment without comment, as though the fact spoke for itself better than any moral he could set beside it.

## VIII. Of the Households Going On

Through all of it, the wedding's small insult widening across nearly two centuries into a habit of raids the households themselves had long since stopped troubling to explain, the ordinary business of the island went on largely undisturbed beneath the quarrelling. The handful was measured out at the stone cup as it had been measured since Fionnlagh Dall's own day, the tallies kept, the carriers walked their roads with their baskets, and a household sworn to whichever lord's stewards reached its door first in a given season paid its dues to that lord and troubled itself no further with which side of which grudge he stood on, so long as the measure he asked for stayed within the Law.

A household of the fourth hearth at Cnoc leathan, midway between the two quarrelling shares and sworn to neither, kept through these same generations a single tally-stick hung above its own door, notched once for every season the western share's steward called first and once, on the stick's other face, for every season the salt shore's steward came first instead, a plain record kept for no reason grander than settling, at the year's turn, whose grain-store the household's own custom bound it to feed first at threshing. The stick, by the time of Niall's own telling, carried better than a hundred and fifty notches on each face, more marks by far than either quarrelling line's own reciters kept of the raids that had made the two households enemies, and it is told that the family who kept it could say, without hesitation, exactly which steward had called first in any given year of their own grandfathers' lives, while neither Sliochd Fhionnghail nor Sliochd Artair could any longer say with any certainty who had struck the first blow of the whole quarrel that had cost them both so dear.

Marriages continued to be made across the very lines that quarrelled, as marriages will, sons and daughters of Sliochd Fhionnghail wedding into households that owed nothing to either feud, the strife itself running always a little apart from the great slow ordinary current of ploughing and lambing and the mending of roofs that made up the greater part of any year for any household on the mountain, whatever its blood.

Niall Òg, closing the tale as he always closed it, would set down his bowl at this point, cold by then whatever hearth he told it beside, and say the one line that every listener across those centuries carried away with them whole, word for word, more surely than they carried any other part of the telling: that the island, in all his own long years of watching it, was never so well governed as when all its governors were too busy quarrelling with each other to trouble the rest of it. He meant it, those who knew him best agreed, only half in jest, and the other half of it, he would add if pressed, was the saddest thing he knew how to say about his own blood, three centuries deep into a grudge that had begun over nothing more than a boast and a drinking-horn at a stranger's wedding, and showed, in his own lifetime at least, no sign at all of ending.
