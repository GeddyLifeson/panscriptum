# Of Sìleas and the Stag

*FE 97 – 125*

> *Carson a tha an fhèith a' freagairt aon làmh, is a' diùltadh làmh eile?*
> Why does the vein answer one hand, and refuse another?
>
> — asked first by Sìleas nic Coinnich in the grove at Muileann chiar

## I. Of the Coal that Would Not Answer Evenly

At the far-eastern Muileann chiar, where the seam ran shallower than under the mountain and a cutter could work standing instead of on her knees, Sìleas nic Coinnich had cut coal since she was twelve years old, and she had never once, in all that cutting, seen the thing she began to see in her twelfth year at the face.

It was a small thing to begin with, the kind of thing a tired woman notices and puts away with the rest of the day's noticing, meaning to look at it again when there is less to do. Two cutters worked the same wall that season, she and a broad, slow, good-natured man named Dòmhnall Beag, and where his pick broke the coal it came away dull, dark, honest coal that burned steady and gave no more light than coal ought to give; where hers broke it, now and again, some strokes and never quite all of them, often enough that she began to count, it came away threaded through with colour, a green and a blue and a deep red running in it like oil on water, and that coal, burned in the evening at the hearth, gave a light that made the women mending by it complain they could not tell their thread.

She said nothing of it for a year. The vein was the vein; it gave what it gave, and a cutter who asked too many questions of it, the older women said, was a cutter who would end by asking it for more than her handful, and no good had ever come of that. But she went on counting, in her head, at the face, which strokes broke bright and which broke dull, and she began to notice that it was not always her stroke and never Dòmhnall's; sometimes his pick broke bright too, on a day when hers broke dull the whole shift, and once, memorably, a boy new to the galleries who had never in his life given the coal a moment's reverent thought broke three bright pieces running before the overseer took the pick from him for wasting his strokes on show.

"It isn't the hand," she said to Dòmhnall one evening, over the ale that ended every shift at Muileann chiar. "I thought it was the hand. It isn't."

"What is it, then?"

"I don't know. That's the trouble of it." She turned her own hands over, looking at them as though the answer might be written on the palms and she had simply not held them to the light the right way. "Carson a tha an fhèith a' freagairt aon làmh, is a' diùltadh làmh eile? Why does the vein answer one hand and refuse another, if it isn't the hand at all?"

Dòmhnall, who was not a man for questions that could not be answered by supper, said only that she would wear herself thin asking a wall of rock why it did what it did, and went to his bed, and thought no more of it. Sìleas did not go to hers for a long while after. She sat by the last of the fire with a lump of the bright-veined coal in her lap, turning it in the light, and it was that night, though she did not know it yet, that the question took hold of her in a way it would not let go of again for the rest of her life.

She was twenty-six years old, cutting coal in the galleries of a mountain her whole family had cut coal in since before her grandmother's grandmother, and she had a question nobody at Muileann chiar could answer and, so far as she then knew, nobody anywhere on the island had ever thought to ask.

Her mother, who had cut the same wall before her and had never in her own long life seen anything in the coal but coal, told her plainly that a question like that one was better left where she had found it. "The vein gives what it gives," she said, "and it has given this house bread for four generations without being asked why. Ask it why, and the next thing it gives you may be nothing at all." Sìleas did not argue with her, having been raised to a proper respect for the vein and for her mother both, but she noticed that her mother, saying this, would not meet her eye, and that the coal her mother's own pick broke that season ran bright as often as dull, though her mother never once remarked on it herself. It was the first time Sìleas understood that the not-asking was a kind of answer in its own right, chosen by people who had looked at the same thing she looked at and decided, long before she was born, that looking was as far as a body ought to go.

## II. Of the Wood and the First Lesson

She stopped cutting, in the end, when she had run out of people at the face willing to be asked, and the galleries, whatever else they might still have taught her, had nothing left to say to a woman the overseer had learned to watch. A cutter who wanted to know whether the coal answered her better on a fast day or a slow one, on an empty stomach or a full one, standing or kneeling, singing or silent, was a cutter the overseer began to find reasons to move to another wall, and after the third such moving Sìleas understood that Muileann chiar's galleries would give her no more than they had already given, and that whatever came next would have to come from somewhere else.

There was a wood above the town that the town called holy without being able to say, if pressed, precisely why: Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, oak and rowan on the shoulder of the hill behind the workings, older than the cutting by anyone's reckoning, where a woman who had lost a child or a man whose luck had turned against him went sometimes to sit alone among the trees, and came down again saying only that it had done him good, without ever saying how. Sìleas went up into it one evening in the autumn of her thirty-third year with no more plan than to sit where it was quiet and think, and she found, when she got there, that three others from the galleries had followed her without being asked, having heard she meant to go and having questions of their own that the face had never answered either.

She did not set out to teach. She set out only to say aloud, among people who would not move her to another wall for the saying of it, the question that had kept her from her bed a year before, and to hear whether anyone else in the wood that evening had noticed the same thing at their own face and never spoken of it, for fear of being thought a woman who asked more of the coal than the coal owed her. Three had. By the second evening there were nine. By the spring there were near forty, cutters and basket-carriers and two of the overseer's own children, sitting on roots and fallen trunks in a clearing where the light came down thin and green through the oaks, and Sìleas, who had never meant to be anyone's teacher, found that she had become one without noticing the day it happened, in the same way she had noticed her own question without meaning to.

She taught them nothing she did not herself believe, which was very little, set against how much she did not know. To count as she counted, first: which strokes broke bright, which broke dull, on what day, by what hand, after what meal, in what temper. That the counting was worth doing even when it answered nothing, next, because a question said aloud and returned to was a question that might one day be answered, where a question only felt and never spoken sank back into the dark of the gallery and was lost. And, most of all, that revering the vein and asking a question of it were not the same act, whatever the old women at the hearth believed, so that a hand might do both in the same day without offence to either.

The clearing had grown too full for the old fallen trunk she liked to sit on by the eighth spring of the teaching, and it was that spring the stag came.

An ordinary stag, or so the tellers who came after always insisted on saying, a red stag such as any hunter on the eastern slopes might raise in a season and take without ceremony, came out of the trees at the clearing's edge on an evening in early summer while Sìleas was in the middle of setting out, for the fortieth time that spring, the question of the bright coal and the dull, and did not run. Its head up and its ears turned toward the clearing, it stood at the tree-line the whole length of the lesson, through the counting and the arguing that followed the counting and the long quiet in the middle where nobody spoke and Sìleas herself said afterwards she had not known what to say next; when the lesson broke up at dusk the beast turned and went back into the wood as though it had somewhere else to be, and was not seen again that season.

Nobody in the clearing that evening claimed it meant anything. Sìleas herself, asked about it many times over the years that followed, would say only that a stag had stood at the tree-line through a lesson once, and that she did not know why, and that she had never claimed to know why, whatever was made of it afterwards by people who had not been there. But the forty who had been there told it to others who had not, as tellers do, and by the autumn the school in the wood above Muileann chiar had given itself a name, claiming nothing about the stag's meaning, wanting only a name and taking the thing that had happened to them that nobody else's teaching could boast of: they called themselves the stag's people, and the wood's own name, Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, began to be spoken alongside the older reverences as somewhere a different kind of asking was done.

Sìleas let the name stand. She had never had much patience for naming things, holding that a name settled a question before the question had earned its settling, but she saw well enough that forty people needed something to call themselves besides Sìleas's people, which she liked less than any name they might have chosen instead, and the stag's people cost her nothing to allow.

What the name cost the school was harder to weigh, and came only later. A stag that stands still through one lesson and is never seen again is, to the forty who saw it, a fact and nothing more; told a second time, by someone who was not there, it becomes a wonder; told a third time, it wants a meaning, and Sìleas spent more evenings than she cared to count turning aside hearers new to the clearing who arrived already certain that the stag had been sent, and by whom, and for what purpose, none of which she had ever claimed and none of which she was willing to teach as though she had. "I saw a stag stand at the trees while I talked too long about coal," she told one such hearer, a girl down from the northern shore who had walked a fortnight to sit in the clearing on the strength of a story grown taller with every telling between her home and the wood. "That is the whole of what I know of it. If you have come for more than that, the wood will not give it to you, and neither will I."

The girl stayed anyway, and cut coal beside Dòmhnall within the year, and learned, as most of the forty learned in time, that the counting was the true teaching and the stag only the door that had brought her through it.

## III. Of the Ford

Word of the school in the wood travelled slowly east to west, the way most word travelled that was not carried by a runner with a reason, and it was two years and more before anyone at Dùn ìseal troubled to ask what the far-eastern Muileann chiar was teaching under its trees. What travelled faster, that same year, went the other way, west to east, carried by carriers who had it from other carriers at the waypoint houses and told it at every fire they stopped at from the Red Share to the last inn before the eastern wood, and it reached the clearing above Muileann chiar on an ordinary morning in the autumn of Sìleas's forty-fourth year, brought by a basket-carrier who had walked three days out of his way to be the one who told it.

Ìomhar Dearg was dead.

He had ridden out from Dùn dhearg that spring, old even by the reckoning of his own blood, fifteen hundred years and more behind him and not a grey hair to show for it, to see for himself a boundary stone on the Red Share's northern march that his stewards swore had been moved in the night, a small thing, the kind of thing that on any other share would have been settled by two men and a rope before noon. At a ford on the Red Share's northern march, which he had crossed with four riders a hundred times before, Eachann Ruadh mac Mhurchaidh, who had sworn over his brother's body six score years before that the blood spilt at Dùn thais would be answered by his own hand or his sons' after him, without naming the man he meant, had been waiting in the alders on the far bank with a spear he had carried, by his own account afterwards, every day of those six score years, though no one had ever seen him carry it before that morning.

He did not throw it from cover. That much was granted him, by friend and enemy both, in every telling that came after: Eachann Ruadh stepped out onto the ford in plain sight, called Ìomhar Dearg by name across the water, and said only that Aonghas Bàn had waited longer than a man ought to wait, and threw the spear when Ìomhar's own guard was already turning their horses toward him. It took Ìomhar Dearg in the throat. He was off his horse and into the water before his riders understood what they had seen, and Eachann Ruadh was back into the alders and gone north before they had him out of the current, and the fords of the east, which had taken the first king a hundred and twenty years before, took now a son of his, one more child of the king to die at another's hand as Iain Dubh had died, in a quarrel begun before either of the men who fought it that morning was born.

Sìleas heard it standing at the edge of the clearing with the basket-carrier still catching his breath from the last of his three days' walking, and she found, turning the news over as she had once turned the bright coal over in her lap, that she felt less for the dead man, whom she had never seen and whose quarrels were no quarrels of hers, than she did for the plain unfairness of a thing that had waited six score years to be paid, on a morning that began, for Ìomhar Dearg, with nothing more than a moved stone.

"They'll cut the Red Share now," Dòmhnall said, at her side. "Four lines have been waiting since the Roinn for an excuse to claim a piece of it."

The Red Share held whole. That was the second part of the news, arriving a season later and travelling more slowly, since it interested the carriers less than a king's son dead at a ford: Dùghall Dearg, Ìomhar's eldest son, eight hundred and twenty-three years old and already grey at the temple in the way his father had never been, took up the Red Share whole the week his father was buried, and told the four lines who came to Dùn dhearg with their old claims in hand that the share had been won with a sword once and would be kept with one again if it came to that, and none of the four, weighing what it had cost the last man who tried Ìomhar Dearg's household, thought the claim worth pressing. It was, the tellers said afterwards, the one wise thing the line of Ìomhar ever did in all its long history of feud and grazing-right and boundary stone, and it did it, as the same tellers were careful to add, for no better reason than that nobody wanted to fight for a piece of what a dead man's son had made plain he would defend to the last of his household.

Eachann Ruadh did not go home. He rode west through the night of the killing and the day after, past his own hall and past his own household, who would have hidden him gladly and been hunted for it, straight to the ground before the Hall of the Flame at Dùn ìseal, where he threw down the second spear he carried, the one he had not thrown, and sat on the bare earth within sight of Brìde's flame until the Keeper's cutters came out to him. The Hall's ground had been declared, in the first years after the Roinn, the one place on the island a feud might not follow a man, and it had never yet been tested by a killer walking onto it with the blood still on his hands. Gormshuil nic Mhuirich, who had held the flame thirty years by then and buried more of her own certainty than she cared to count in that time, came out herself to look at him.

"You have killed the king's son," she said.

"I have. Six score years late, and he never threw the first spear at Dùn thais. I have killed him all the same, and I'll not pretend to you or to Crom that I haven't." He did not raise his eyes to her. "I ask nothing of the flame. I ask only the ground."

"The ground was never yours to ask. It was sworn to every hand on the island, killer's and king's alike, or it was sworn to nothing." She looked at him a long while, an old woman by then, though not so old as the man she looked down at, and said at last, "No spear follows you past that boundary stone. What follows you past it, into whatever household still owns you, is not mine to answer for."

He lived out the rest of his life on the Hall's ground, in a hut the Keepers let him raise at its edge, cutting turf for the Hall's own fires and speaking to almost no one who came to the mountain, and when Sìleas heard the whole of it, months later and third-hand as she heard everything that happened west of the eastern wood, she thought less about the killing, which was an old story dressed in a new man's blood, than about the ground itself, sworn to hold a killer as safely as it held anyone else who stood on it, and what it meant that a place could be asked to be fair to a man in a way that no household, weighing six score years of a debt unpaid, had ever been asked to be.

## IV. Of the Shut Galleries

Gormshuil nic Mhuirich heard of the stag's people in the same season she heard of Eachann Ruadh's flight to the ground, though it came to her by a slower and quieter road: a cutter of her own galleries, home from a season working the far-eastern seam on loan to Muileann chiar's overseer, who mentioned at the Hall, without thinking much of it, that the eastern cutters had taken to counting their strokes and asking why the coal answered one hand and not another, as though the coal were a thing that owed an answer to anybody.

She did not act on it at once. Gormshuil had held the flame long enough to know that most things said of a season's loaned cutter came to nothing by the next, and she let two more years pass, hearing the same report grow a little each time it reached her, before she sent a cutter of her own east to see the wood above Muileann chiar with her own eyes and bring back a plain account of what was taught there.

The account she got back was plain enough to trouble her. The stag's people did not deny the flame, did not refuse the handful, did not set themselves against Brìde or Crom in word or in oath; what troubled Gormshuil, hearing the cutter's account by the light of the flame she alone was sworn to carry, was something harder to answer than a rival reverence would have been, because a rival reverence could be met with the flame's own claim and weighed against it before the council. The stag's people asked, beyond any question of whether the vein should be revered, why it behaved as it behaved, and a Keeper who had spent thirty years learning that the vein's behaviour was not hers to question, only to serve, found she had no ready word for a school that treated the asking as a kind of reverence, and a better one, some of its own hearers were beginning to say, than kneeling and asking nothing.

She summoned Sìleas to Dùn ìseal in the spring of Sìleas's forty-eighth year, and Sìleas came, because the mountain under the Hall was where the vein itself was thought to run truest, and she had never in her life had the chance to put her question to it directly.

They spoke in the keepers' hut, the two of them and no one else, on either side of the same fire Beathag Bhàn had once been given to hold, and neither afterwards ever gave a full account of what passed between them, holding, both of them, that some things said in that hut were said to each other and not to be carried further. What is known is only what followed. Gormshuil forbade the hearers of Sìleas nic Coinnich to cut in the galleries of Dùn ìseal, holding, when the ruling was given out at the Hall, that a question asked of the vein was a demand made of it, and that a Keeper who let a demand stand unanswered in her own galleries answered for it to every hand that trusted the flame after her. She did not forbid the school itself, having, whatever passed between them in the hut, no ground and no wish to forbid a woman from asking why a stone burned bright under her own hand and dull under her neighbour's; she forbade only the mountain to the asking, and left the wood above Muileann chiar to it entire.

Sìleas walked out of Dùn ìseal that same week and did not come back to it in the years that were left to her. She said little of the ruling to the forty and more who waited for her at the wood, beyond the plain fact of it, that the galleries under the mountain were shut to any hand that had sat in her clearing, and that the school would go on where it had always gone on, under its own trees, asking its own question of its own seam, and troubling the Hall's mountain no further.

"She is not wrong to be afraid of it," Sìleas said once, to Dòmhnall, who had never stopped asking her, all those years, what the coal would answer next. "A question, once it is let loose in a gallery, doesn't stop where the asker meant it to stop. I have never once pretended otherwise to her or to anyone."

"Are you afraid of it, then?"

"No," she said. "I only think it fair to be afraid of the thing you have started, if you mean to go on starting it."

The stag's people went on where they had always gone on, cutting their own seam in the far-eastern hills, counting their strokes, teaching a lesson that had once been interrupted by a stag and had, by then, grown too large for any single clearing to hold in comfort. In the last years of Sìleas's teaching a dozen of her oldest hearers walked east out of Muileann chiar altogether, following the wood where it thickened and the seam where it ran deeper, to raise a house of their own further from the mountain's shadow than the first clearing had ever stood, and it is that walking, the last of the mountain's cutters going east under their own trees with their own question still unanswered, that the tellers of the school still keep, in the years after Sìleas nic Coinnich, as the true beginning of all that came from her question: not the day the stag stood at the tree-line, which anyone might have seen and made nothing of, but the day forty of the eastern cutters chose to walk further from the flame than any reverence on the island had walked before, asking nothing of the mountain any longer but to be let alone with their own coal, and their own counting, and the question that had kept one woman from her bed on an ordinary evening a lifetime before.
