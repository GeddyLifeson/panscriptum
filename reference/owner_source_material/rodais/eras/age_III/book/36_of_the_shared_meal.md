# Of the Shared Meal

*FE 2,392 – 2,474*

> *Chan eil aran aig aon taigh a dh'easbhaidh aig taigh eile.*
> No house lacks bread while another has it.
>
> — still said at Àth dhìreach, of the extra bowl set out at the turn of the year

## I. Of the Carriers' Road

The coal road out of Muileann chiar ran high before it ran anywhere else, up out of the galleries by a track no cart could keep to, so that everything the mountain sent down to the lowlands and everything the lowlands sent up in trade went on a back or a pony's load, carried by families who had carried for their fathers and would carry for their sons, and who owned, most of them, nothing else worth the naming. Calum Ruadh of Àth dhìreach was such a man, broad through the shoulder from thirty years of it, and in a hard year in the middle of Lachlann Bàn's reign, before any granary law was thought of, he carried down more coal than his own household ate grain in trade for, a plain enough arithmetic that needed no clerk to read it. He was not the only one. Àth dhìreach that year, and Muileann gheal beside it, and the smaller houses of Doire uaine strung along the same track, all carried more than they were given back, because the lowland households they traded with had, in a poor season of their own, less to spare than the mountain had coal to sell, and no custom on the island yet bound one district to feed another when the trade between them ran short.

The small halting-house below the pass, where every carrier of that road broke his journey whether the day was fair or foul, kept in those years a rough tally of loads carried and grain owed, scratched on a board by the fire, and it was there, as Calum Ruadh was fond of saying, that a man first saw the whole shape of the trouble rather than only his own household's corner of it: mark after mark for coal going down and next to nothing coming up, in every hand that had cut a line on it that winter. The keeper of the house, an old woman named Mòr who asked no man his business before she fed him, told the carriers who stopped there that she had seen three such winters in her own life and buried a husband in the second of them, of a chest that a thinly fed winter had never let mend; she said it plainly, over the fire, to men too tired to answer her, and let the silence after do the rest of the telling.

Nothing came of that year worse than short commons and a hard winter got through on less than anyone would have chosen, and no teller troubled to set it down at the time, there being nothing in an ordinary hungry season that the island's rolls thought worth a line. But Calum Ruadh remembered it all his life, and told it after to his son, when the boy was old enough to understand why his father counted a full granary the way another man might count silver: that a mountain which lived by carrying could carry its own households into want as easily as any other house, and that no strength of back had ever yet fed a family through a winter when there was nothing at the bottom of the road to carry home.

It was in the years before, at Cathair dhearg on the plain below, that An Talla Dearg had passed by a road only Clàr nan Rìghrean itself troubles to keep whole, from Seonag Chrom's line to a house that traced its own line back to Goraidh Mòr, the rìgh who had first climbed to Dùn ìseal on foot with his spear left at the last house. Of the years between, the island's own rolls say little, and this telling has no better to add to them; but the line held, as lines on Dia-thìr mostly did, quietly, one reeve's book handed to the next, until a young man of six-and-twenty took the crown in his own right and gave his name, before his reign was very old, to a law that outlasted every other thing he did.

## II. Of Lachlann Bàn and the Granaries

Lachlann Bàn came to An Talla Dearg in his twenty-sixth year, and the household that raised him said of him afterward, when he was long past caring what was said, that he had been from a boy the kind who counted a room before he sat down in it: how many benches, how many at each bench, how much bread would need to be cut before the meal was called. It was not a gift that made him quick company, and it made him none at all with the reeves who had grown used to a looser hand at the granary door, but it served him, once he held the hall himself, in the one matter he is chiefly remembered for.

He found, in his first years, a hall whose granaries filled and emptied by no rule but the harvest and the whim of whichever steward kept the key that season, so that one province seat might sit on grain enough for three winters while another, a hard week's carry away, went short in the same year for no reason but that its own harvest had failed and nobody's business but its own to mend it. Lachlann Bàn thought this a poor way to keep a household, whether the household was his own kitchen or the whole island, and he set about building a granary in every province seat that would agree to keep one, of stone where stone could be had and of timber banked with turf where it could not, each one built to the same plan so that a steward moved from one to another would know, on the first morning, where the grain lay and how it was kept.

Cnoc mhòr, close under Cathair mhòr on the plain, built the first of them before any other seat on the island, and sent word of it to Cathair dhearg within the same season the roof went on, its reeve plainly hoping to be first noticed as well as first built. Lachlann Bàn sent back a silver ring for the reeve's own hand, a small enough thing beside the cost of the granary, but it did what he meant it to do, for within two years every province seat on the plain had a granary of its own or was building one, none of them wishing to be named last where Cnoc mhòr had been named first.

## III. Of the Law of the Common Granary

A granary built and never filled is a shed, and Lachlann Bàn knew it, so that the building was only the half of what he meant to do. He went himself to Dùn ìseal, in the fourth year of his reign, to put the other half to Coimheadaiche na Lasrach who then held the cup, Raonaid Dhubh, a woman of the north country who had come to the flame at six-and-twenty herself and held it, by the time Lachlann Bàn climbed the last path to her door, for the better part of forty years.

What he asked her for was not new. Every household on the island already kept, as an unwritten custom older than any king-list, the habit of giving a neighbour grain in a hard year on the understanding that the neighbour would do the same when the turn came round; it was a kindness owed and repaid the way a fire was banked and relit, without anyone troubling to write down the terms of it. Lachlann Bàn wanted only to set that same custom under the granaries he had built, so that it ran district to district, at the pace of a full granary and an empty one, rather than house to house at the slower pace of one neighbour's memory: a household with grain above its winter's need would give the surplus to its district's common granary before the storm season closed the roads, and the granary would feed, in turn, any household of the district that ran short, without asking first whether the shortfall was the household's own fault or the year's.

Raonaid Dhubh heard him out at her own hearth, and it is told that she asked him only one question in return, whether he meant the law for his own reign or for every reign after his: and when he answered that a law worth the writing was worth writing for longer than one life, she set the cup down between them and said the custom was old enough already, and needed only a king willing to be bound by it as much as any carrier, before she would put her own hand to it. She asked him further, being a woman who had heard kings promise things before, whether he meant his own hall's own granary bound by the same terms as any household's, or held apart as a king's granary is so often held apart; and when he answered that An Talla Dearg's surplus would go in on the same terms and be drawn on the same terms, no household's need weighed heavier for being poorer than the crown's, she told him that was the answer she had been waiting to hear rather than the one she had asked for, and put her hand to the writing.

Lachlann Bàn swore, before her and before the flame, that his own hall's surplus would go into the granary at Cathair dhearg on the same terms as any household's, and the successors of Peigi nic Tormoid wrote the law into the rolls in full that same year, the first ever made by rìgh and Keeper together rather than by the hall alone.

Under the new law the granaries went up faster than before, and none faster than in the mountain districts round Muileann chiar, where the households lived wholly by cutting and carrying and grew almost nothing of their own, so that a common granary there was less a convenience than the difference, in a bad year, between a hard winter and a starved one. The lowland districts that were asked to fill those granaries grumbled at it more than once, being asked, as they saw it, to feed households that grew no grain of their own to put in return, and more than one reeve of the plain wrote to Cathair dhearg to say so plainly. Lachlann Bàn's own answer to one such letter is kept in the rolls yet: that a household which carried the plain's coal down every winter of its life had already paid, in the coin of its own back, for whatever grain the plain sent up in return, and he would hear no more of the matter unless the letter's writer wished to carry a season's coal himself before he judged what it was worth.

At Cathair mhòr, whose fishing towns grew no grain either, a granary was raised within the old town wall, the first anywhere to stand behind one, and the southern strands that fed it sent dried fish rather than barley for their own share, packed in baskets the same way the toll fish had once been packed for Talla na Lasrach, so that in a hard winter a mountain household might find itself, without knowing it, eating the same salt herring a fisher's wife had gutted a season before on a strand two days' walk from the nearest coal seam.

## IV. Of the Eastern Raiders and the Childless Queen

Not every trouble of those years bent to a granary's cure. Out beyond the eastern strands, where the fishing boats of Seann Tarr worked the shallows, outlaws in low black boats had begun raiding from the offshore islets, taking nets and dried fish from any strand too small to keep a watch, and running before any boat large enough to give chase could be launched after them. Sliochd Raghnaill, whose own house had held the boats of that coast since the reign of Eilidh nic Ìomhair, armed three of its own craft at Seann Tarr in answer, and caught the raiders at their own anchorage one grey morning with the tide against them, burning two of the low boats where they lay and losing, in the burning, more of the raiders' plunder than they recovered whole. The rest of the raiders' boats slipped the harbour mouth before the fire had well caught, and were not seen again on that stretch of coast for the length of the telling, though the strands kept a closer watch after, and Seann Tarr's own boys grew up knowing the shapes of the low black hulls the way other boys knew the shapes of gulls. The captain who led that morning's chase, a grandson of the house's own line named Ruairidh mac Nèill, was asked afterward by a trader why he had not pressed on and taken the rest of the raiders while the tide still ran against them; he answered only that a boat burned at its own anchorage was a lesson the strand's own watch would remember longer than a raider hanged at Seann Tarr, and that he had come out that morning to end a season's raiding, not to fill a gallows he had no order to fill.

Lachlann Bàn's own household, meanwhile, grew by one child only, a daughter, Oighrig nic Lachlainn, born in the four-and-thirtieth year of his reign and raised, from what the rolls of her own household say of her upbringing, with more of her father's habit of counting a room than any softness to balance it. She married, in her turn, a lord of no great house who died young of a fever some years before her father, and she bore him no children before his death nor after it, a plain fact that the hall's own clerks noted without comment at the time and that the island's greater houses, who noted everything touching a childless heir, did not let pass so quietly.

In the fourteenth year of Lachlann Bàn's reign, Raonaid Dhubh, who had kept the flame at Dùn ìseal since before he was crowned, died at last in her own eightieth year, having seen the law she had sworn to stand unbroken through its first years. Coimheadaiche na Lasrach who followed her is named in Talla na Lasrach's own rolls, but nowhere in the mountain's telling of what came after, the mountain households having troubled themselves, in the years that mattered most to them, far less with who kept the flame at Dùn ìseal than with who kept the granary key at Muileann gheal.

Lachlann Bàn held An Talla Dearg five-and-fifty years from his crowning, longer than most of the reigns before or after his, and died in his own bed at one-and-eighty, mourned, the rolls say, chiefly by the reeves whose granaries he had built and by the carriers of the mountain roads, who had never once, in all his reign, gone the whole distance of a winter without knowing where the next grain was coming from. Oighrig nic Lachlainn took the hall in her own one-and-twentieth year, the same year her father was laid among Sliochd Ìomhair at Dùn dhearg, and it was remarked at the time, by more than one house with an eye already on the succession after hers, that a queen with no child of her own body was a queen whose reign would be watched more closely at its end than at its beginning. The houses did not press the matter while she was young and the hall still full of her father's own men, but they began, before her reign was very old, the slow business of counting what each of them might claim should she die without an heir, a counting that went on quietly under the ordinary business of the hall for the whole of her reign and longer. Sliochd Ìomhair counted its claim by blood, being of the same house that had held Dùn dhearg since Ìomhar Dearg's own day; Sliochd Thormoid counted its claim by the clerks who had kept the hall's own rolls since Tormod Scrìobhaiche first set a ruler's name on oiled hide; Sliochd Raghnaill counted its claim by the boats it had armed at Seann Tarr and every strand its own captains had kept safe since; and Sliochd Mhuirich, which held Coimheadaiche na Lasrach's own office and had held it since An Ceangal, counted nothing at all, being of the one house on the island that had never once, in all the age, put its hand to the cup for itself.

None of Na Ceithir Taighean spoke its counting aloud in Oighrig's own hearing, and she gave no sign, in all the years of her reign, that she had heard of it; but a clerk of that room, copying a routine grant of land some years into her reign, noted in the margin of his own copy, in a hand meant for no eye but his own successor's, that the hall's business went on as it always had, and that a hall which goes on as it always has is a hall whose ruler has begun to be watched for the day it will not.

## V. Of the Late Snow

The trouble that came was weather, and it came the way weather on Dia-thìr had always come to the mountain, without asking leave of any reign. The watchers who kept the cairns above Cnoc ghlas, whose whole office was watching the high passes for the sign that told Talla na Lasrach when the galleries might safely open, looked up one spring in Oighrig's eleventh year and saw snow lying white on the passes long past the day it should have gone, and looked again a month later, and saw it lying still. Talla na Lasrach, on their word, kept the galleries shut a full season longer than the custom of the mountain expected, for a cutter caught by a late fall on those slopes was a cutter not coming home, and no reeve at Dùn ìseal was minded to weigh a season's coal against a company of cutters buried under it.

When the galleries did at last open, they opened late and closed early at the first sign of the passes closing again, and the coal that came down that whole season was not a third of what a common year gave. For the mountain households, who had nothing of their own to fall back on but the coal they carried and the grain they carried it for, the shortfall was not a hardship to be borne so much as a debt with no way to pay it: Muileann gheal, Doire uaine and Àth dhìreach alike found themselves, by the season's end, with nothing left to trade for grain at all, their own granaries already thin from the two poor years before, and by the following autumn the households of all three were eating, one careful handful at a time, the very seed they would need in spring if the next planting was to be anything at all.

Calum Ruadh, old now and past the heaviest carrying himself, sat that autumn at his own hearth with his son Iain Caol, a boy of some fifteen years grown already near his father's own height, and told him plainly, the way he had never troubled to soften a hard fact for the boy before, that this was the very want he had carried through as a young man, come round again in his own old age, and that neither of them would live to see it not come round a third time unless something below the mountain had changed since the last. Iain Caol asked him, being at the age when a boy first begins to ask his father questions the father cannot easily answer, whether the new law the old king had made would hold any better than the old custom had. Calum Ruadh told him he did not know, never having seen a law tried by a true want before, only by grumbling reeves with grain enough to spare in the end whatever they wrote to Cathair dhearg about it.

## VI. Of the Granaries Opened

The law was tried, and it held. Word went down from Cnoc ghlas to Cathair dhearg of the mountain's want before the worst of the winter, and Oighrig nic Lachlainn, whose own household had never once gone hungry in her life, gave the order the same week the word reached her: the common granaries of the mountain were to be opened at Muileann gheal, the largest of the three seats, and every household of the three settlements was to draw on it as its own need asked, without a steward standing over the door to weigh whether the need was proved.

The granary at Muileann gheal ran low again within the month, the three settlements together holding more mouths than one granary had ever been built to feed through a whole winter, and it was here that the law was tried a second time, in a matter the writing had never troubled to say in so many words. The reeves of Dùn dhearg, who had for generations kept their own granaries close and their own accounts closer, apart even from the rest of the plain, opened the old stores of Sliochd Ìomhair to the mountain the same week word of the shortfall reached them, and sent the grain up the carriers' roads before any order came down from Cathair dhearg telling them to. Oighrig nic Lachlainn wrote afterward, in a letter Talla na Lasrach's own clerks kept, that she would have given the order herself had Dùn dhearg only waited for it; the clerk who copied her words into the roll added beneath them, in the same dry hand every clerk of that room seemed to inherit from the one before, that Dùn dhearg had not waited.

Calum Ruadh was among the carriers who went down for that grain, too old by his own reckoning for the full weight of a load but unwilling, he told his son, to sit at his own hearth while younger men carried what his own life's work had once carried in a leaner year with no granary at the bottom of the road to catch it. Iain Caol went with him, not yet old enough by the town's own reckoning to carry a man's load but old enough to carry half of one, and it was the boy, walking the last mile into Àth dhìreach behind his father with the light going and the first sacks of Dùn dhearg's grain on both their backs, who first heard what the town meant to do for the men who had brought it.

The carriers took no fee for that winter's carrying, no household on the mountain having anything to pay them with, and in the town's own account of what came after, it was this refusal, more than the grain, that Àth dhìreach chose to answer. The reeve of the settlement called the whole town together before the spring thaw, in the same hall the households used for weighing tolls and settling boundary disputes, and asked the carriers who had walked the road that winter to stand forward where all could see them; Calum Ruadh, being the eldest of them and the one whose own hunger the town remembered longest, was asked to stand first, and stood with his son beside him though the boy had carried, by his own honest word to the reeve, no more than half a man's share of it.

The reeve granted, before the whole gathering, that every carrier who had stood forward that day should have the right to rest, for the whole of his life after, in any house of Àth dhìreach without payment asked or expected, and asked the town's own households, one by one round the hall, whether each would keep the grant as its own word rather than as any order out of Cathair dhearg. Every household answered that it would, Mòr of the halting-house loudest of any of them, being the one among the town's own who had fed the coldest of that winter's carriers longest before the grain ever came. It was kept, from that day, as a right the town's own households owed rather than any Talla na Lasrach recorded, on the reasoning, as one of the elders present put it, that a kindness owed to a neighbour was the neighbour's business to keep, not Talla na Lasrach's to write down. Not one soul of the three settlements is known to have died of hunger that whole winter, a fact the mountain's own tellers set beside the granting of the right whenever the tale is told, as though the one could not be properly understood without the other.

## VII. Of the First Load and the Bowl Still Set

Iain Caol grew to a man's full height and strength in the years after, and it was known of him, by those who remembered his father's own carrying, that he walked the coal road with much of Calum Ruadh's own steadiness, though the two men rarely put their loads down at the same waypoint house any longer, Calum Ruadh's back having given out at last, past sixty, to more years of the road than most carriers' backs were asked to bear. Iain Caol took up his own first load as a carrier proper in his three-and-twentieth year, in the autumn of Oighrig nic Lachlainn's nineteenth year in the hall, walking the same track his father had walked before him with a company of four other young men of Àth dhìreach, none of whom had carried a working load before that season.

He was not one of the carriers the town had named in the granting after the famine winter, being too young then by the settlement's own reckoning to have carried a full share, and it was a plain fact he never once, in all his life, tried to claim otherwise. But the men the town had named grew old through the years of his own carrying, one by one, and as each of them died the town found itself, without ever quite deciding to, extending the same rest to whichever carrier of that road was oldest living at the time, on the reasoning that a custom kept for the men who had walked the famine's road was a custom that belonged, in the end, to the road and not only to the particular feet that had first walked it hungry. Iain Caol, by the time he was old himself, had carried that road the better part of fifty years, through easy seasons and two lean ones after, and had buried, along the way, every man who had carried grain up to Muileann gheal in the year he was a boy of fifteen at his father's hearth.

In his own last years, when his legs would carry him no further than the halting-house and back, Iain Caol took to telling the whole of it himself, at the turn of every year, to whatever children of the town would sit still long enough to hear an old man out: his father's own hungry youth, the late snow above Cnoc ghlas, the granaries opening at Muileann gheal, and the night the reeve had called the whole town together and asked every household, one by one, whether it would keep the grant as its own word. He never once, in the telling, put himself among the carriers the town had named that day, being as careful an old man about the plain truth of it as he had been a young one; but he set out, every year he told it, a bowl at his own hearth all the same, for whichever carrier might be on the road that night, and let the children carry that habit home with them the way he had once carried grain, without troubling to explain to them why a boy too young to have earned a right should be the one keeping it.

He died at Àth dhìreach an old man, in the ninetieth year of his own life, the last then living of any who could say he had carried on that road in the years the famine and the right to rest were both still new, and the right the town had kept for its carriers died with him, there being none left of that particular reckoning to extend it to. It is told, and the town does not trouble to soften the telling, that no carrier's back has since been asked to bear a winter's want the way that mountain once bore it, the coal roads having grown wider and the granaries fuller with every reign since Lachlann Bàn's; and so the right was never renewed for want of the hunger that had first earned it, though what remains of it is kept all the same, at the turn of every year, in the one bowl still set out at Àth dhìreach's own hearths for whatever carrier is on the road that night, whether he has any claim to it by the old reckoning or none at all.
