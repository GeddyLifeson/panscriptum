# Of Anna Ruadh's Two Circuits and the Parting of the Rites

*FE 2,792 – 2,863*

> *Aon lasair, iomadh ainm.*
> One flame, many names.
>
> — said of the companies at Dùn ìseal, after Seumas Bàn had done his counting

## I. Of Anna Ruadh's First Circuit

Between Tormod Òg and Anna Ruadh's own father the house of Tormod had sat the red hall nine reigns running, and the rolls of those years hold little beyond harvests, tolls and the mending of bridges, which the tellers of Cathair dhearg took, not unreasonably, for a sign that the island had been doing well. Anna Ruadh's father was the ninth and quietest of them, a man remembered for having asked, in thirty years, no question of the provinces that a reeve could not answer sitting down.

Anna Ruadh took the red hall at Cathair dhearg in her six-and-twentieth year, {{date:II-0205}}, and the clerks who had known her father's quiet reign found her a restless kind of ruler from the first council she sat. She asked the reeves who came up from the provinces how the roads stood between one seat and the next, and when none of them could tell her past the borders of his own district, she said she would go and see for herself, as Mòrag nan Rathad had gone before any road-guard rode it and before any bridge stood at Cathair dhearg to be widened. No ruler had walked the whole circuit since. Cathair dhomhain's own reeve, who kept the Law by the letter and had little patience for a queen's whims, told her plainly that a hall was better kept from a hall. Anna Ruadh answered that a hall built by a woman who had walked every mile of it might be worth the keeping, and left the following spring with a dozen of her household and no more baggage than a carrier takes. She took no litter and no guard beyond what any well-off traveller kept on the road, and told the household she left behind that a queen who could not be spared from her own hall for a season was no queen worth the keeping either, a saying more than one later ruler of her house repeated without ever once acting on it.

She came, on the mountain road, {{date:II-0206}}, to the house that carriers still called Am Buabhall Fortanach, the Auspicious Buffalo, where the War of the Roads had been ended so long before that its own keepers no longer troubled to say in which reign. The house had passed by then through five generations of a line that still called itself Mòr nic Dhùghaill's own, though none living had known her, and its hearth had not gone cold since. The keeper of the house, a broad, unhurried woman who had heard a queen was coming and set nothing extra on the table for it, gave Anna Ruadh the same jugged buffalo she gave any carrier who stopped there, in the same wooden bowl, with the same crust of bread beside it. Anna Ruadh ate it at the long table among the household's own carriers, asked for a second helping, and got it without ceremony. It is the keepers of that house who tell the tale still, not the rolls, and they tell it exactly so: that the queen ate what was put in front of her and did not ask for better.

Further east, on the road that climbed toward Cnoc leathan, she came to the turf longhouse the carriers called the sunny house for the way its south wall caught the morning, {{date:II-0207}}. Its keepers had mended the worst of that road themselves for three generations, out of their own stores and their own backs, because no province seat claimed the stretch as its own to mend. Anna Ruadh heard the whole of it from the youngest of them, a woman missing two fingers from a rockfall on that same road, and before she left she granted the house a toll of its own for the road's keeping, Slighe Cnoc leathan, the first and only charter any waypoint house on the island holds. She is said to have told the woman, when she thanked her, that a road mended by a stranger's charity for three generations had earned the right to be mended by its own money instead, and that this was no great gift but only a debt the hall had been slow to notice. The woman, whose name the rolls never troubled to keep though the toll they keep to this day, asked only that the charter be cut in stone rather than skin, since skin could be lost in a house fire and a road could not; Anna Ruadh had it cut in stone before she left the district.

At Ceann mhin on the south-eastern coast, {{date:II-0208}}, she found a quarrel old enough that neither side troubled any longer to be civil about it: the fishers of the strand and the carriers of the mountain road, both claiming the right to carry the dried fish up-country, each accusing the other of spoiling a trade that had fed them both for generations. Anna Ruadh heard the fishers first, in the low stone shed where they dried their catch, and then the carriers, at the halting-house above the strand, and gave her ruling under the open sky between the two, where both households could hear it together: that either might carry, and the trade would go to whichever kept the fish drier and the road shorter, and no household of either kind was to be turned back at the other's door. The fish going inland doubled within two years, and its price at Dùn ìseal fell by half, and both households, it is told, claimed the doubling as proof they had been in the right all along. An old fisher of the strand, asked years after whether the queen had favoured his own side in the end, is remembered to have said only that she had favoured the fish, which was more than either side had thought to do.

## II. Of the Contest Field and the Wood-Watcher

At Ceann leathan the road-guards had kept a staves-contest on an open green since before living memory, the kind of match where a young man made his name and an old one kept it a season longer, and Mòrag Dhubh of Cnoc mhòr was still spoken of there for a win two centuries gone; her ring, or one so old it was taken for hers, still hung by the green's own well, and every fighter who came to try the green touched it before he took up a staff, whether he had heard her name told rightly or garbled past all knowing. Anna Ruadh watched a match fought in the mud of a wet spring and thought it a poor return for the skill on show. She gave the green a wall of dressed stone and a drained floor, {{date:II-0209}}, and set a silver ring from the red hall's own store as the prize, and ruled besides that whoever lost a match at her contest should eat at the crown's cost before going home, win or lose being no reason, she said, for a man to go home hungry. Fighters came after that from every province of the south to try the stone field, and the year Fearchar Ruadh of Doire mhin took the ring, he was carried on the shoulders of men who had lost to him not an hour before. He had come to the green as a mason's apprentice with nothing to his name but a borrowed staff, and he ate, by Anna Ruadh's own new law, the same crown's meal as every man he had beaten, at the same board, before the ring was ever put in his hand; it is remembered of him that he never afterward sat down to eat without asking who else was at the table.

It was on the same circuit that Anna Ruadh took into her household a wood-watcher out of Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, a spare, sun-browned man who read the fall of a dropped stick or the turn of a bird's flight the way a clerk read a slate, and asked him to read the omens of the hall at Cathair dhearg as he had read them under the trees. It was the first time the rite of the wood-watchers, which held Macha for its goddess, sovereignty and horses both, had come so far east, {{date:II-0210}}. Anna Ruadh's own household saw him first read the hall on a morning when three geese crossed the yard from the north before the queen had broken her fast, and the wood-watcher told her, without being asked, that a message she had given up waiting for would come before the week was out; a rider from the southern coast rode in on the sixth day with an answer to a letter she had sent a full year before and thought lost on the road, and after that no one in the household troubled him with questions he had not been asked first. The Keeper at Dùn ìseal sent a protest up by the same road Anna Ruadh had walked, asking by what right a hall built on the Binding kept an omen-reader who answered to no measure of the vein at all. The rolls give no answer to it, and none is remembered as having been sent.

Cathair dhomhain took the matter harder than Dùn ìseal had. Its own shrine had kept the Law strictly since the town's founding, its Keeper's cutter tried every season without complaint, and its people said openly, where Anna Ruadh's messengers could hear it, that the capital had handed its omens to folk who sat in trees, {{date:II-0211}}. Cathair dhomhain and the capital had quarrelled before over tolls and over precedence at the council bench; from that year the quarrel carried a weight of faith as well; and it never again lost it. A generation later a Cathair dhomhain reeve, asked why his town still would take no wood-watcher of its own, is said to have answered only that a bird did not judge a king, and left it at that. Cathair dhomhain's own shrine, in the same season, doubled its cutter's visits to the Keeper's measure without being asked, as though the strictness alone might answer an insult that no letter to Cathair dhearg had ever troubled to make plain, and for a hundred years after, a Cathair dhomhain man swearing an oath was known on the roads by how exactly he kept to the letter of it.

Anna Ruadh walked the circuit a second time some years on, in her middle years, slower than the first, stopping longer at each house and asking after names she remembered from the first walking; more than one keeper who had fed her as a young queen fed her again white-haired, and told their own children afterward which meal it had been. At Am Buabhall Fortanach the same broad-handed keeper who had served her the jugged buffalo unceremoniously the first time set the bowl down before her a second, with grey in her own hair by then, and this time asked, since the queen had come so far twice over, whether she would not sit a while longer than the meal took. Anna Ruadh stayed the night, the only night of either circuit she is known to have slept two together under one roof that was not her own.

She came home from that second circuit already tiring, though she said nothing of it to her household, and she held the red hall four years more before she died of no sickness anyone could name, in the way that a candle goes out at the end of its wick rather than by any wind, {{date:II-0212}}, in her nine-and-fortieth year. In her last winter she had the household read her the toll rolls of the sunny house and of Slighe Cnoc leathan aloud at her own bedside, twice, as though to be sure the charter still stood as she had cut it, and asked after no other matter of state before she died. She was carried to Dùn dhearg and laid among the house of Ìomhar and the kings of her own house together, as her line had been promised at its choosing.

Her son Coinneach Bàn took the hall the same year, four-and-twenty years old and already known to the reeves for asking the same close questions of a slate that his mother had asked of a road. He did not walk the circuit as she had. He said, when a councillor asked him whether he meant to, that his mother had already walked it twice and left him little to see that she had not seen first, and that he would rather have every province seat send him a true copy of its own rolls than walk out to read them himself. It was the beginning of the work that filled the better part of his reign, begun in earnest in his fourth year, {{date:II-0213}}: seat after seat sent its clerk down to Cathair dhearg over the following years with the district's laws copied fresh, and Coinneach Bàn read every copy through himself before it was shelved, so that within a decade a province seat's own clerks, wanting to settle a point of law, more often sent to the capital for a copy of their own rolls than trusted the skins in their own keeping, which the damp of a mountain winter had usually got to first.

## III. Of Seumas Bàn and the Roll

Sliochd Mhuirich had held the Keeper's office, without ever once claiming the red hall in its own name, since long before Oighrig nic Lachlainn's death, and through the shut hall that followed it, when the house had stood back from the choosing at Dùn ìseal while three other houses quarrelled over the red hall; and it was said of Sliochd Mhuirich, half in respect and half in the way a rival house says such things, that it had learned early to want the keeping of a thing rather than the owning of it. Seumas Bàn was the youngest son of that house's youngest branch, raised at Dùn ìseal within sound of the flame, and no one who had known him as a boy running the Hall's own passages was greatly surprised when the flame came to his keeping in his turn.

Seumas Bàn came to the flame at five-and-thirty, {{date:II-0214}}, and did what every new Keeper before him had done, and sat down with the Keepers' roll to learn the names of those who had kept the flame before him. He read it by the light of Brìde's own flame, in the stone room below Talla na Lasrach where the roll was kept, with a jug of small beer going flat at his elbow and no company but the clerk who had shown him where the oldest skins were stored. The names ran down the page in the old stag-letters, a hundred years and more to a skin, some hands cramped and some sprawling, a few so faded with age that he had to tilt the whole page to the firelight to read them at all. He had meant, when he sat down, only an evening's reading, the way a man learns the names of his own grandfather's brothers before a feast; the roll ran to nine skins by the time it reached him, and he was still at the fourth when the clerk came in to tell him the fire in the outer room had gone out and been relit twice without his noticing either time.

He read for the better part of two nights before he noticed the pattern, and a third before he stopped reading and began instead to count, going back to the beginning and setting a mark on a scrap of hide for every name of his own house, and counting the whole roll through twice to be sure of himself. When he had finished he sat a long while looking at the two rows of marks, which agreed with each other exactly. More than half the Keepers of the age, from Beathag Bhàn down to himself, had been of Sliochd Mhuirich. He went further, though the roll gave him no cause to and no one afterward thanked him for it, and reckoned how many of the rest had married into the house or fostered a child of it, and found the true number nearer to two in every three.

He told no one at once, sending the clerk to his bed and sitting alone with the roll until the fire burned low, and it is the clerk's own account, given in his old age and kept unstruck in a hand not his master's, that Seumas Bàn said only one thing aloud that night, to no one: that a Hall built so that no heir might hold the flame had, without anybody meaning it so, let one house hold the watching of it instead. In the morning he sent for the same clerk and a knife, and had the count struck from the roll's preface where he had written it the night before in a heat that had not survived till daylight, {{date:II-0214}}. The clerk did as he was bid and said nothing of the copy he had already made for his own hand before the knife came near the page, and it is that copy, not the scraped one, that answers for the fact today.

That winter, done with counting his own house, Seumas Bàn turned to counting everyone else's. He had the clerks of the Hall set down every rite on the island that burned a handful at dawn or laid any claim at all to the vein, so that the council should know, he said, what it was that it kept, {{date:II-0215}}. The list, when it was finished, ran longer than he had expected: the Hall's own rite of the flame and the stone; the old keeping of the ancestors, which asked nothing of the vein and troubled no one; the dawn-hawk of the northern coast; the wood-watchers of the western wood; the wolf-sworn of the road-guards; the sting-oath of the harbour courts; and both schools of the stag, the elders' and Donnchadh mac Iain's, still trading their letters after three generations of disagreement. The clerk who wrote the list added, at its foot, that a thousand years before no such list would have been needed, since there had been, in the memory the roll kept, only the one flame and the one measure. Seumas Bàn read the note and let it stand.

## IV. Of the Rites Counted

In the spring he sent a cutter named Ruairidh Òg north to Seann Skell, to try the hawk-keepers' dawn-burning against the Hall's own measure, as every rite on the list was tried in its turn. He came back within the month, {{date:II-0216}}, wind-burned about the face and short in his answers, with a strip of tanned hide folded inside his coat. He had put the Hall's question to the household of Ealasaid Bhàn, eldest of the fowlers, who fed her hawk before her own table was laid, as the rite required, and who heard him out at her own door without asking him in. She had told him, he said, standing in her own doorway with the hawk hooded on her wrist and the dawn fire already lit behind her, that the measure he had come to try was the hawk's own, not the Keeper's, that her people burned exactly what the Law required and had done so every dawn of her life, and that they had put it in writing so that no cutter after him could mistake the matter again. When Ruairidh Òg asked whether he might at least see the handful weighed, she answered that he might watch from the yard as any neighbour was welcome to, but that watching was not trying, and the two were not the same thing however he chose to name them to the Keeper. The strip of hide he carried was that writing. It was the first time any rite on the list had turned the Hall's own trial away outright, and Seumas Bàn read it twice by the fire before he put it in the chest with the roll rather than answering it.

From Caol leathan came word, a few years on, that the wolf-sworn had raised a hall of their own about the white stone at the town gate, the second such stone the order had ever set, and that the oaths sworn there now were no longer the plain guards' oaths of the road but something graver, {{date:II-0217}}. The stone, its keepers said, watched the whole island as any guard watched his own stretch of road, and would cry out under a rightful king as under no other; men had begun to call it Lia Fàil, the Stone, and the wolf-sworn who kept it An Fhianna, though the road-guards among them still rode the same roads they had always ridden. A young guard swore the new oath that same season with his hand flat on the stone in a hall still smelling of its own fresh mortar, and told his own household after that the stone had felt warm under his palm on a cold morning, though the mason who had set it swore he had cut and laid nothing but plain grey rock out of the same quarry that built half the town's walls. Seumas Bàn had known most of those men by their given names before they were anything of the kind, and it troubled him more, he told the clerk, that a name could change a man's oath than that the oath itself had changed.

At the western Seann Skell the harbour court's old sting-oath, sworn on a scorpion carved for the purpose since before anyone living could say why, had by then become a settled creed, {{date:II-0218}}. Its keepers held that one power alone stung the liar wherever he stood, naming him Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea, and that the scorpion was no more than his sign; and it was the first teaching on the island to claim, in so many words, that it alone was true, where every other rite on the list had been content to be its own. From that court's founding the oath was sworn thereafter over a cup marked with the scorpion, called Manannan's Cup of Truth, which was said to break in three at three lies spoken over it and to be made whole again by three truths, though no clerk of the Hall ever saw it broken, and a merchant of the harbour town who was once heard to wonder aloud what would happen if it were tested found no one willing to lend him a boat that season. Seann Vell, below Cuan shean on the same coast, took up the oath from its neighbour within the year and carved its own scorpion in yellow stone, {{date:II-0219}}, the first image of the creature cut for worship rather than for swearing on, so that a child of that town grew up knowing the scorpion's shape before it ever heard the oath explained. The stonecutter who carved it, a woman better known for gravestones than for shrines, is said to have complained that no one had ever shown her a living scorpion to work from, and to have carved it in the end from a fisherman's telling of one washed up dead on the strand, so that the image every child of Seann Vell learned to fear owed at least as much to a fisherman's memory as to the god it was meant to serve.

The wood-watchers went furthest of all. They stopped sending their quarrels to the Hall for judgement and began to settle them instead under the trees of Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, where no clerk of Dùn ìseal had ever sat in council, {{date:II-0220}}. They held that the black beast of the wood had two natures, one that kept and one that devoured, and that the vein under the mountain had the very same two, though it showed only one face to any cutter who had never gone hungry a winter for want of it. Some among them told it further, in a telling the order took up afterward as its own, of two horses harnessed to a single chariot, the black Dubh Sainglenn and the grey Liath Macha, pulling one load between them and neither able to run without the other. A cutter of Cnoc bhàn and a fowler of the Abhainn ìseal brought a boundary quarrel to the wood-watchers that same year rather than to Dùn ìseal, over a stretch of scrub neither the Hall nor any province seat had ever troubled to survey, and came away with a judgement neither had asked for and both, it is told, accepted: that the ground belonged to whoever kept it fed through a hard winter, and to no one in a good one. Seumas Bàn heard the tale from the same wood-watcher who still read the omens of Anna Ruadh's old household, now grown old himself in Coinneach Bàn's service, and said only that he had heard stranger doctrines argue less sense.

## V. Of the Parting

By the time the last of these reports had reached Dùn ìseal, the one practice the island had kept since the Binding had plainly come apart into as many pieces as it had reasons to, {{date:II-0221}}. Men had begun to call the whole scattering of rites An Creideamh Sean, the Old Faith, and set beside it, standing a little apart as it always had, the older reverence of the hearths, which asked nothing of the vein and was known by its own name, Na Seann Spioradan, the Old Spirits; and it was no longer possible, Seumas Bàn told the council plainly, to speak of the belief of the Dia-thìrich as though it were one thing, whatever the roll of the Hall had once assumed.

The council gave up the pretence a few years on, in its own turn. It had sat, since the days of the Roinn, by hearth and by house, a man beside whoever his neighbour happened to be; that year it began instead to sit in companies by rite, each on benches of its own, {{date:II-0222}}, and the seating held for the rest of the age without anyone troubling to reverse it. The carpenters who built the new benches were given no instruction beyond a count of heads for each company, and it is remembered that they set the hawk-keepers' bench nearest the door, for no reason any clerk ever wrote down, so that latecomers to every council after sat longest among strangers before they reached their own.

Seumas Bàn stood at the door on the first morning they sat so and watched the benches fill, knot by knot, none of them so much as glancing at the bench beside it, and said to the clerk at his shoulder, loud enough, by his own wish, to be set down: that he kept the one flame now for eight peoples, and each of the eight was certain it was the only one that kept it truly.

Two things alone stayed outside the companies, and Seumas Bàn is said to have taken more comfort from them than from anything else that year. Pilgrims of every rite still walked to Allt an Àigh at the proper turn of the moon, as they always had, and asked no one's company or leave to do it; the Hall's own list, drawn up to set every rite in its place, had found no place to set that one and left it out of the reckoning entirely, {{date:II-0223}}. That same season pilgrims of four rites, meeting on the path from Caol shean by no arrangement anyone could afterward account for, fell to mending its worn stones together, hawk-keeper beside wolf-sworn beside a stag scholar of the elders' school, and finished the work before any of them thought to ask whether the others belonged there.

And at Muileann ruadh, where the warm springs still ran from under the mountain as they had before the Binding was ever sworn, the keeper of the bath-house, a broad, plain-spoken woman named Beathag Fhionn, ruled that year that the sick of every rite should be carried up and bathed at dawn without being asked first which company they kept, {{date:II-0224}}. Her ruling was copied that same season into the rolls of every province seat that had a copy of anything at all, and it is remembered as the last ruling of the whole age that every company of the council accepted without a single voice raised against it, coming as it did from the one office in the Hall's whole keeping that had never had anything to do with the coal.

Seumas Bàn kept the flame five-and-forty years in all after that winter of the roll and the roll's scraping. What is chiefly told of him is one morning, standing again at the door of the council as he had the first day the benches were parted, and looking down the length of the Hall at knot after knot of his own eight peoples, none looking at any other, when he said nothing further to the clerk beside him. He had come to the flame meaning only to learn the names of the dead who had held it before him, and had found instead, by the same reading, the shape of the thing he now kept: no longer the one measure the Binding had set over every hearth alike, but eight measures each certain it alone answered to the vein, sitting as far from one another as the carpenters' benches would allow and closer, in the end, than either the hawk-keepers or the wolf-sworn much cared to be told. None of the eight companies, from that day to the last day the rolls of the age were kept, ever sat together again by hearth and house as one; and the flame on the threshold at Dùn ìseal burned on regardless, keeping, as it had always kept, the one measure no company of the eight had yet found cause to dispute.
