# Of Mòr nic Dhùghaill's House and the Blind Keeper's Last Road

*FE 164 – 189*

> *Cha tarraing lann fo mo mhullach.*
> No blade is drawn under my roof.
>
> — the carriers' word for the road-peace, kept first at the house of Mòr nic Dhùghaill of Doire shean

## I. Of the House at Doire shean

It is told at the long table still, by carriers who never knew her and never will, that Mòr nic Dhùghaill kept the first house on the mountain road for no better reason than that her husband had died leaving her a widow's third of a poor herd of buffalo and a worse third of a stony croft above Doire shean, and that a woman with more beasts than grass to feed them on had to find some use for the meat before the winter found it for her. The carriers' road had not long been cut then, the stepped path down to Ceann chaol still raw where the masons' picks had bitten it, and the baskets going down to the southern households and the dried fish coming up passed within a stone's throw of her own door twice in every week the weather allowed it, which in the mountain's early seasons was not every week.

She began, the telling goes, by boiling a haunch for her own table on a night three carriers stopped to ask shelter from a rain that had turned the stepped path to a chute of mud, and by finding, the next morning, that word of a widow who fed a wet man hot meat and asked no coin for it travelled the road faster than any basket on it. Within the year she had built a second room onto the croft with her own hands and a neighbour's, and within two she had given up the herd's wool trade entirely and kept buffalo for the pot alone, jugged slow over a low fire the whole night through the way her mother had taught her against a lean season, until the smell of it, carriers said afterward, could be caught a quarter-mile off on a still evening and would turn a tired man's feet toward her door before his own head had properly decided to. The Hall at Dùn ìseal, which had built the road for its baskets and thought little else about it, gained thereby a waypoint it had not asked for and would not, in all the years after, have known how to do without; and it is because of that house, raised for no purpose grander than a widow's grass and a widow's grief, that every circuit-house on the island since has kept a name rather than a number, the way the Hall's own galleries never did.

Mòr herself, by the account carriers older than these ones gave of her, was a broad, plain-spoken woman of thirty-four when the first three men came in out of the rain, grey-eyed like half the households of that mountainside, with a laugh that came easily and a temper that came more easily still to any man who thought a widow's table was owed him for nothing. She fed every traveller the road sent her the same portion in the same pot regardless of what share he carried a token from, a lord's man and a smith's boy and a beggar off the coast road eating from the one ladle in the order they arrived rather than the order their blood might have claimed, and when a steward of the Red Share once objected, early on, to eating below a fisherman who had come to the door first, she told him, in words the carriers still repeat nearly word for word, that the pot did not know one man's blood from another's and neither, under her roof, would she trouble herself to. He ate below the fisherman, and came back the following season, and ate below whoever the road had sent ahead of him that time too.

## II. Of the Second House at the Meeting of the Roads

The road grew faster than any one widow's pot could feed it, and within a handful of years a second house stood where the carriers' way down from the mountain met the road running west to the harbours, raised by three families of the fourth hearth together, who had watched what a fed and rested carrier was worth to a basket's safe delivery and judged, rightly enough, that a second such house a day's walk from the first would lose them nothing and gain the road a great deal. They built it long and low of turf and timber both, a hearth burning at either end of the one room rather than the single hearth Mòr kept, so that carriers coming down off the mountain with the day's cold still in their bones might sit at one fire while carriers going up, dry and rested and dreading the climb ahead of them, sat at the other, the two fires never quite meeting in the middle of the room though the same roof covered both and the same door let a man choose which end he wanted before he had so much as shrugged off his pack.

It became the custom of that second house, within its own first year, that a man might cross from the down-fire to the up-fire and back as many times as he liked in an evening, trading news of the road behind him for news of the road ahead, and carriers who had never once spoken a word to each other on the mountain path, being strangers of different hearths with baskets to mind and no leisure for talk, found themselves, over a bowl at that long central table, telling each other things they had not troubled to tell their own households: where a ford had shifted since the spring flood, which of the lodgings kept a dog that bit and which kept one that only barked, whose basket had been light at the last weighing and whose scale a wise man watched. It was at that table, more than at any council held under the Hall's own roof, that the road first began to feel like one thing rather than a scatter of separate paths each household walked alone, and it is at that same table, the carriers say, laying a hand flat on the wood as they say it, that the custom which came to be sworn a law was first kept without anyone troubling to call it one.

## III. Of the Chase Stopped at the Door

The custom found its first true test in the hundred and seventy-fifth year, when a man of the eastern shares came pounding down the mountain road at a run in the last light of an autumn evening with three of his cousin's household not far behind him and a grievance between the two houses old enough that neither side troubled any longer to say aloud what had started it, only that a knife had been drawn somewhere upriver that morning and a man's arm opened to the bone, and that the arm's owner's kin meant to have the opening answered before the sun rose on a second day.

He came in at Mòr's own door, the telling has it, white to the lips and past speech for the first several breaths, and threw himself down on the bench nearest the fire with no more ceremony than a stranger begging shelter from rain, and Mòr, who by then had fed carriers and lords' men and beggars alike under that roof for near thirty years and had learned to read a hunted man's face the way another woman read weather, did not ask him his business before she set a bowl in front of him, only told him, once the bowl was down, to eat it while it was hot, since a man eats slower with food in his mouth than with none, and a slower man was easier to talk sense to. His cousin's three came in not a quarter-hour behind him, knives still at their belts and their own faces set for whatever the door might hold, and found the man they had chased sitting at Mòr's own table with a bowl of her jugged buffalo half gone in front of him and Mòr herself standing between the table and the door with her arms folded and her feet planted as if she had grown there.

What passed between them the carriers tell several ways, and none of the ways quite agree on her exact words, but every telling keeps the shape of it the same: that she told the three men plainly there would be no blade drawn under her roof for any grievance whatever, old or new, hers or another's, because a house that fed every traveller from the one pot regardless of his blood could not, in the same breath, let one traveller's blood be spilled across the same floor, and that whatever the two houses owed each other could wait until both sides of it had walked back out through her door and off her own ground, at which point it was no longer any affair of hers what they did to each other on the open road. The three stood a long moment weighing a widow's word against a debt of honour older than most of them could properly account for, and then, the telling says, the eldest of the three sat down at the table's far end, a careful distance from the man he had chased, close enough to watch him and far enough to say nothing by it, and asked Mòr, in a voice gone suddenly and plainly tired, whether there was more of the pot for three hungry men who had run the best part of five miles for nothing they could now collect on. She served them, and the four of them ate in a silence that held the whole length of the meal, and when it was done the hunted man rose first and walked out into the dark without a word to any of them, and the three cousins gave him a full quarter-hour's start, by Mòr's own later telling of it, before they rose themselves and went out after him into the same dark, and what happened on the road that night beyond her door the house never learned, and did not, the tellers say, greatly wish to.

## IV. Of Niall Òg at the Long Table

Word of the chase stopped at Mòr's door ran the road faster than any basket that season, carried hearth to hearth and house to house the way the smell of her pot had once travelled it, and it reached, before the year was out, the ears of Niall Òg, who by then had outlived so many quarrels of blood that a new one seldom troubled him to cross a valley for, but who came anyway, on foot as he came everywhere, to see for himself the house where a widow's word had held two feuding houses to the one table.

He arrived unannounced on an evening much like the one that had made the house's name, and sat himself at the long table's middle without asking leave of anyone, an old man by the mountain's own reckoning though not so very old by the reckoning of his own kin, who ate what was set before him and said little for the better part of an hour, watching the room fill and empty around him the way a man watches a river he has crossed many times before. When Mòr herself came at last to see who the quiet stranger at her table was, he told her his name plainly, as he told it to whoever asked, and watched her face for the flicker of recognition that came to most faces at the hearing of it, the name of a grandson of Ailean Mòr being one that carried its own weight on any share of the island by then, whatever the man himself had done or not done to earn it.

She gave him no more and no less than she gave any other traveller, the same bowl and the same place at the same table, and it is told that he thanked her for it with more warmth than he had thanked a host in a long while, saying that he had sat at more tables in his life than he cared to number, heirs' tables and lords' tables and the Hall's own table more than once, and that most of them had given him a better seat than his neighbour's for no reason but his blood, which he had come, over the centuries, to find a wearying kind of courtesy rather than an honour. He asked her, before he left the next morning, whether she had ever turned a man from her door for the house he carried a token from rather than for anything he had done himself, and when she answered that she had not and could not see the sense in starting, he laughed, the carriers say, longer and more freely than the household had ever heard a stranger laugh at that table, and told her that she had managed, without ever once sitting at the gathering-house floor or hearing the cord counted, a trick the whole of Ailean's scattered kin had spent a hundred and seventy years failing at among themselves. He left a ring behind him on the table when he went, more coin than the meal was worth by any measure, and when Mòr sent a boy running after him to give it back, as she gave back every payment offered under that roof, he would not take it, saying only that a man who had outlived every one of his own quarrels could afford, once in a great while, to pay for a peace he had not made himself.

## V. Of the Names Kept at the Harbours

In those same years the road's reach carried households far past the mountain's own shoulder, down to two harbours on the western shore of Loch chrom that no one of the nine hearths had settled before, though both had stood, empty and roofless, since some earlier people's time the oldest tellers could not put a name to. Fishers of the nine hearths came first to the nearer of the two, finding a scatter of old house-footings above a good landing and a name for the place already fixed in the mouths of the few herders who grazed the ground nearby, a name not of their own tongue and not, so far as any of them could tell, of any tongue spoken on the island in their fathers' time either. They kept the name rather than giving it one of their own making, on the plain reasoning, passed down since in the carriers' own song of it, that a name found already standing on a place, like a house-footing found already dug, had more right to stay than any new one a stranger could invent to replace it; and Seann Mhorn it remained, the carriers still singing, on the road between the mountain and the sea, that the stones came with the name and the name came with the stones, and no one who settled there afterward had ever troubled to ask whose.

Families walking the same road a season or two later found the second harbour a short way along the same shore, house-footings there as well and a name of its own already fixed to it the same way, and settled it in the same spirit, building on the old footings without asking, the song has it, whose hands had first laid them. Seann Warr they kept it, as their neighbours up the shore had kept Seann Mhorn, and the two harbours grew together in the years after as two ends of one growing town might grow, sharing a landing and a market day and, before long, a single waypoint house of their own where the road from the mountain came down at last to meet the sea, so that a carrier walking the whole circuit from Dùn ìseal to the western shore and back again might sleep, by the century's end, under a roof at every day's remove of the journey and never once lie out in the open unless the weather denied him a door.

## VI. Of the Keeper's Last Asking

Fionnlagh Dall kept the flame at Dùn ìseal a full fifty-six years from the day the nine hearths first chose him, longer by a good margin than any Keeper before him, and it was in the hundred and eighty-fourth year, an old man of four-and-ninety with a body that had begun, in its last seasons, to fail him in ways his blindness never had, that he called Calum Beag to his own bedside in the small room off the Hall and told him, in a voice gone thin but not yet unsteady, what he wished done with him when the failing was finished.

He did not ask, he said, to be laid on the slope above Dùn ìseal with the Keepers who had gone before him, Beathag Bhàn and Muireach Liath and Gormshuil nic Mhuirich each in their own turn, though no one in the Hall would have thought it strange had he asked for exactly that. He asked instead to be carried south, to the old graves near Cnoc dhìreach that no household or share on the island had ever claimed for its own, a burying-ground older, by every account the mountain's oldest tellers could give, than any name the island now used for anything upon it. Calum Beag, who had led him by the elbow through the Hall's own galleries for the better part of forty years and had grown, in that time, from a boy of ten who followed him out of plain curiosity into a man grey at the temples himself, asked him why, since a Keeper's own ground lay ready and waiting not a stone's throw from the Hall he had served his whole life. Fionnlagh's answer, which Calum carried afterward the whole rest of his own life and repeated to whoever asked though he never claimed to understand it fully himself, was that a man who had spent fifty-six years judging what belonged to whom, coal to household and household to share and share to the whole island besides, wished at the last to lie somewhere that belonged to no one, in ground that had never once been divided by any cord or counted by any hearth, and that if the Hall's own roll could not say who the old graves at Cnoc dhìreach belonged to, that was, to his own mind, the surest recommendation the ground could carry.

He died within the month, in the small room off the Hall with Calum Beag's hand in his own, and it is told that his last words were not a ruling of any kind, as some at Dùn ìseal had half expected of a man who had spent his whole life giving them, but a plain question to Calum about whether the boy — he called him that still, at the last, though the boy by then had grey in his own beard — had remembered to see the flame fed before he came to sit, since a Keeper's dying, Fionnlagh said, was no excuse for a household to go cold. Calum told him it had been fed, which was true, and Fionnlagh said only that he was glad of it, and closed his eyes, and did not open them again.

## VII. Of the Eleven Days' Road

Word of the asking went out from Dùn ìseal before the body was a day cold, and by the time the bearers set out southward with Fionnlagh Dall carried between them on a litter of plain wood, every hearth along the twenty miles between the Hall and Cnoc dhìreach had already heard, and every hearth along that road asked, when the bearers passed, to be allowed to carry him some part of the way themselves, so that what should by any ordinary reckoning have been a journey of three days at most, twenty miles being no great distance for men walking a road they knew, stretched instead to eleven, the litter passing from one household's shoulders to the next at every waypoint house and every crossroads shrine the road offered, each hearth keeping him a night and sending him on in the morning with fresh bearers and, more than once, with the whole household walking some distance alongside out of a wish to have been some small part of the carrying.

Calum Beag walked the whole eleven days at the litter's head, by every account the only man on the journey who never once handed his own share of the bearing to another pair of shoulders, though he was by then a man of six-and-sixty and the road no easier for him than for anyone half his years. He led the way, as he had led Fionnlagh through the Hall's own galleries for forty years before, though there was nothing now for him to lead a blind man's feet around, only the road ahead and the growing weight, the carriers who told it afterward all agreed, of a grief he did not trouble to hide from anyone who cared to see it plainly on his face. At Am Buabhall Fortanach the whole household turned out at the door as the litter passed, Mòr nic Dhùghaill herself standing at the threshold with a bowl of the jugged buffalo she pressed on every bearer in turn whether he had asked for it or not, saying, when Calum tried to refuse his own portion out of some sense that grief should go hungry, that a man could not carry a Keeper's weight on an empty stomach any better than he could carry a basket of coal on one, and that Fionnlagh himself, blind as he was, had never once in her hearing turned down a bowl set honestly in front of him.

## VIII. Of the Grave at Cnoc dhìreach

They came to the old ground on the eleventh day, a scatter of grave-mounds and standing stones so ancient that no household living could say with any certainty which of them marked what or belonged to whom, the whole of it grown over with the same rough grass that covered the hillside around it, undisturbed, the tellers say, since some people's time so far back that even the mountain's own memory did not reach it plainly. Calum Beag chose the spot himself, a low rise near the ground's centre from which, on a clear day, a man could see the smoke of three separate hearths without turning his head, and the bearers dug there, and laid Fionnlagh Dall in ground that had never before, so far as any record the Hall kept could say, held a burial anyone had troubled to mark by name.

The Keepers who came after him, when the question was put to them why he had asked for that ground rather than his own, gave two different answers between them and would not choose which was truer: some said he had wished, at the last, to belong to something older and larger than any measure he himself had spent his life keeping, and others said he had simply wished, being a man who had judged every household's claim to every handful for fifty-six years, to lie at last somewhere no one could claim at all. Calum Beag, asked the same question more than once in the years that followed, would say only that he had asked Fionnlagh himself and been given an answer that satisfied him, though he never repeated it whole to anyone, holding, he said, to the same silence a Keeper's own eyes owed the galleries, borrowed eyes included.

The ground was hallowed that same season by the Keepers who travelled down from Dùn ìseal to do it, the first burial the place had ever received that any living hand had set down by name, and from that hallowing the burying-ground that had belonged to no one at all became, within the year, a ground that belonged, by the Hall's own reckoning, to everyone. A handful of families from the households nearest Cnoc dhìreach settled by it after, to tend the grave and keep the ground from growing wild over it again, and the Hall paid them for the work one measured handful of coal each a season, the only payment the Small-Burning Law was ever known to allow in coal rather than in coin or kind, on the ruling that a service kept for a dead Keeper's own sake was owed the one payment a Keeper himself would have judged fitting.

## IX. Of the Stone Calum Beag Laid

Mòr nic Dhùghaill did not live to hear the whole of how the burying finished. She died at her own house above Doire shean in the hundred and eighty-sixth year, two years past sixty and grey as the buffalo she had jugged for near her whole adult life, found by her own daughter one morning sitting upright in the chair by the fire where she had sat every evening of the household's memory, gone as quietly, the telling has it, as she had lived loudly, and the road kept her house running without a single night's fire missed, the daughter and a niece taking up the pot the same week their mother was laid in the ground behind the croft, because a house that had never once in thirty years failed to feed a traveller was not, the household judged, going to begin failing on the strength of one woman's death, however dearly the road had loved her.

Calum Beag came to the funeral himself, walking the road he had walked with Fionnlagh's litter two years before, and it is told that he stood longest of anyone at the grave's edge afterward, an old man himself by then, though he outlived her by a good many years still, and said to no one in particular that the island had lost, within two seasons of each other, the two people he had spent the better part of his own life walking beside, one who had taught him to see with another man's hands and one who had taught him, without ever once meaning to teach it, that a table with no blade drawn under it was as fine a law as any the Hall had ever sworn.

It was in the hundred and eighty-ninth year, some years past the burying, that Calum Beag went back to Cnoc dhìreach a last time, an old man now in truth, to finish a thing he had put off since the day of the burying, uncertain, he told the grave-keepers who met him at the ground, whether his own hand was the right one to do it. He had brought with him, carried the whole way on his own back rather than trusted to any cart, a plain stone the size of two cupped hands, cut by a mason he had paid for out of his own purse and asked to carve nothing on its face but the shape of the stone cup Fionnlagh had set on the threshold at Dùn ìseal, since no name, Calum judged, could say what that shape already said of the man well enough to be worth the cutting. He knelt at the grave's head a long while, the grave-keepers standing back to give him the ground, and set the stone down with his own two hands, pressing it into the turf until it sat level and would not rock, and then rose, an old man's knees complaining of the kneeling, and stood looking at it a moment before he turned and walked back down the road toward the mountain, leaving the stone to mark, without a single word carved anywhere upon it, what the hands that had once filled a cup by touch alone had judged, by the last measure any of them ever needed, enough.
