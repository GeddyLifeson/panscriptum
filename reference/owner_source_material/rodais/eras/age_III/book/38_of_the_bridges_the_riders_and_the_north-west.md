# Of the Bridges, the Riders and the North-West

*FE 2,559 – 2,791*

> *Chan eil clach gun neach a chuir ann i.*
> No stone stands without a hand that set it there.
>
> — the fourth hearth's own saying, said over every keystone since Ros fhionn

## I. Of the Carts on the Lowland Roads

The list at Dùn ìseal keeps no ruler's name between Tormod Òg, who died in the seventieth year of his own life without ever walking the whole circuit his father had been chosen to walk, and Anna Ruadh, who came to the red hall two hundred and thirty-six years after him and walked it twice. Of the reigns that filled those years the rolls say only that the red hall passed from hand to hand at Cathair dhearg as it had passed since Gilleasbuig, without war and without a name any teller troubled to keep past its own generation; and where the red hall has so little to say, this telling turns, as it has turned before, to the roads, the bridges and the shore, whose keeping fell to no single house and so was never once let lapse for the want of one.

The lowland roads in those years carried more than they had ever carried before, for the cart had come onto them at last in numbers, where for the whole of the age before it a back or a pony's load had done most of a household's carrying between one waypoint house and the next. A cart asked more of a road than a back ever had: it asked a level bed where a foot could climb a rut without complaint, and it asked, above all, a bridge wide enough to take it, where the narrow footbridges of the earlier reigns had answered a walking man and nothing wider. The bridge at Cathair dhearg, first thrown by Ailean Clachair's own fellowship in the reign before Mòrag nan Rathad and widened once already in her own time, was widened a third time in these years, its old piers kept and a new span laid across them broad enough for two carts to pass abreast without either driver giving way to the other. The hearth-sign was cut again into the new keystone, the same small mark the fourth hearth had cut into every crossing since Ros fhionn, and the masons who cut it left, beside it, the old stone that had carried the sign before, set into the parapet where any hand might touch it, rather than break it for rubble as an easier fellowship might have done.

It was in this same stretch of years that the road-guards of Dùn dhearg first took to riding the lowland roads rather than walking them, mounted on the small tough horses of the southern grass country, bred for a light rider and a long day rather than for weight or war. A guard on foot had answered a quarrel at a waypoint house only after it had cooled into a grievance nursed over a night's telling and retelling; a guard on a horse could be at the same house before the first blow had properly landed, and it changed, within a few years, the whole temper of the road-guard's own trade, from a company that judged what had happened to one that could, on a fast enough horse, sometimes stop it happening at all. The riders of that first company are the earliest whose names the road-guard's own roll troubles to keep, and for a long while after they were the only ones so kept, every rider before them having ridden nothing faster than his own two feet and so left no mark on a roll built to record a particular kind of speed.

## II. Of the Mending at Ros fhionn

The flood that took the eastern approach of Ailean Clachair's own span above Ros fhionn came in a wet spring some way into this stretch of years, a season of rain on rain that swelled every river of the western shares past any bank built to hold it, and it carried off, along with a good stretch of the road on the eastern bank, three households built too near the water on the strength of a hundred quiet years. The masons of the fellowship, called down from Cathair dhearg within the same season, found the span standing where Ailean had left it, its piers untouched by a flood that had taken lesser work on every side of it, and set about mending only the washed-out approach, laying a new causeway on stone footings deep enough, this time, that no flood within living memory of the mending was ever expected to trouble it again.

It was in clearing the old approach for the new footings that the masons came, at last, to the keystone Ailean had cut with his own hand in the hundred and sixtieth year of the age, the small mark of the fourth hearth that said, to any mason's eye that knew to look for it, that living hands and not chance had raised the stone above it. The sign was as sharp as the day it was cut, the grey stone of that quarry having proved, as Ailean himself had judged it would when he chose the face near Ros fhionn over an easier one closer to hand, harder wearing than most that went into a bridge on Dia-thìr; and the masons who found it, none of them born when Ailean cut it, left it exactly as they found it, cutting their own new work a full course below rather than disturb a sign that had already outlasted the man who set it and looked fit to outlast a good many masons after. It became, from that mending onward, a small custom among the fellowship, never written into any rule of theirs but kept all the same, that a mason sent to mend an older crossing left the old signs standing wherever the stone beneath them still stood, and cut his own work apart from them rather than over them, so that a bridge mended twice might carry, in its own stones, the mark of every hand that had answered for it.

## III. Of Dùn chrom and Ceann àrsaidh

Two crossings went up in those same years that the fellowship built new rather than mended, and neither answered quite the same want as Ros fhionn's had.

The first spanned the river below Dùn chrom, joining the southern road to the western by a route that saved a full day's cart-journey over the long way round through Cathair dhearg, and the fellowship set its toll, by a custom of its own that no rule of the red hall had ever told it to keep, at half of what the road-toll asked at the capital's own crossing, the mason who led that building holding it no fair thing to charge a full toll for half the road. Cathair dhearg's own reeves complained of it loudly and at length, in letters the rolls still keep, arguing that a toll set anywhere on the island by anyone but the crown's own reckoning was a toll that would, in time, be set anywhere by anyone, and calling the fellowship's half-measure at Dùn chrom the year the red hall's own word on tolls first went unanswered. The fellowship answered, through the mason who had set the toll, that a bridge built by its own hands on its own stone might be priced by its own reckoning until the red hall chose to build one of its own beside it, and offered, should Cathair dhearg wish it otherwise, to hand the whole crossing over to the crown's own keeping, tolls and upkeep together. No reeve of Cathair dhearg ever took up that offer, the upkeep of a bridge proving, as it generally does, a less welcome inheritance than the toll alone, and the half-toll at Dùn chrom stood unaltered for as long as the fellowship stood.

The second crossing, at the river town of Ceann àrsaidh, was the fourth stone bridge raised across the lowlands, and it was built, from the first stone laid to the last, by forty of the fisher-kin of Sliochd Raghnaill rather than by cutters bred to the trade, men and women who had learned the mason's craft in a season when their own boats lay idle after the burning at Seann Tarr had thinned the raiders from that coast and left, for a while, more hands on the strand than there was fish to occupy them. The fellowship's own roll at Cathair dhearg names all forty, a courtesy it did not always trouble to extend to a crew of borrowed hands, and the reason given in the same roll is plain enough in its own way: that forty people who had learned a new trade rather than sit idle through a season deserved, whatever their birth, to be named among the masons who had spent a life at it. The bridge at Ceann àrsaidh stood a little rougher in its coursing than Ros fhionn or Dùn chrom, the fisher-kin's hands being surer, that first winter, with a net than with a chisel, but it stood, and it carried, within the year, more cart-traffic between the river towns of the eastern lowlands than any crossing before it had carried in three.

## IV. Of Beathag Clachair

Among the households that settled, in these same generations, along the fellowship's own roads rather than at any single hearth, was a family of Doire mhin on the southern road, cutters of ordinary boundary-stones and byre-lintels for three generations before a daughter of that house was born, one whose fellowship remembers her still for a single small mark on a bridge no grander than a dozen others of its own age, though every boundary-stone her grandfather ever cut has weathered past reading.

Beathag Clachair came to her family's own trade the way most of that trade's daughters and sons came to it, at an age too young to remember choosing it, her first task no harder than sweeping chips from beneath her father's chisel and her first lesson no deeper than which face of a stone would split true and which would shatter if a hand did not first read the grain of it. She was quicker at the reading than most of her father's own apprentices, a fact her father noticed early and said aloud often, in the plain way of that house; and by the time she had seen twenty summers she was cutting boundary-stones of her own, sound work that stood where it was set and troubled no household with a crooked line, though nothing in it yet asked more of her than the trade had asked of her grandfather.

The bridge that made her name was a small one, built to carry the southern road over a stream at Doire mhin that flooded each spring past any ford a cart could safely trust, no grander in its span than the footbridges of an earlier age and no harder, by the fellowship's own reckoning, than a dozen others its masons had thrown in those same years without any one of them being remembered for it after. Beathag was three-and-thirty when the fellowship set her to lead the cutting, the first time a woman of that house had been given a crossing of her own to answer for rather than a stretch of some other mason's work to finish, and she led it, by every account the fellowship kept, exactly as her father and his father before him would have led it: a sound bed, a true arch, the fourth hearth's own sign cut into the keystone where every crossing since Ros fhionn had carried it.

What she added, beside the hearth's own sign and beneath it, was her own name, cut in her own hand in letters no larger than the sign: *Beathag Clachair a chuir seo*, Beathag the mason set this. No mason of the fellowship, in all the generations since Ailean Clachair first cut the fourth hearth's mark at Ros fhionn, had ever cut a name of his own beside it, the hearth-sign having been understood, from the very first crossing, to speak for every hand that had answered to it rather than for any one hand alone; and when the fellowship's elder masons came down from Cathair dhearg, as they did each season, to look over the year's work, more than one of them stood a long while at the small bridge at Doire mhin before they said anything of it at all.

It is told that the eldest among them, a woman near seventy who had cut stone since before Beathag's own father was born, asked her plainly why she had done it, whether she held her own hand a better one than the hearth's, or thought her own work owed more remembering than the forty who had died, over the generations, cutting crossings no less sound than hers and left no name behind them. Beathag answered, by the fellowship's own telling of it, that she held her hand no better than any hearth-mark ever cut, and had cut her own name to stand beside the hearth's sign rather than above it, so that whoever stood at that stream a hundred years on and asked who had set the stone might, for once, be given an answer rather than only a sign that said hands had done it, without saying whose. The eldest mason gave no answer of her own that day; within two seasons masons of every hearth on the island had begun cutting their own names beside the fourth hearth's sign wherever a fellowship crew finished a crossing, a custom that held for as long as the fellowship held, and the island's own masons still call it, to this day, by no other name than Beathag's mark.

Beathag Clachair lived to see her own mark cut on four further crossings before her hands grew too stiff for the finer work of a keystone, and died at Doire mhin in the seventieth year of her own life, an ordinary age for a mason's death by then, no age on Dia-thìr since Fearchar Òg's own generation having asked a life to run much longer; she was buried, at her own asking, at the foot of the bridge she had cut her name into rather than among the household's own dead, and the fellowship's own masons passing that road are told to have kept, for a long while after, the habit of laying a chip of good grey stone on the grave whenever their own work carried them by it.

## V. Of the Far-Off House and the Fire-Water

The waypoint house on the site the carriers called, half in earnest and half in the plain joke of the road, the far-off house, having stood so long from any town that a traveller was never nearer to reaching it than halfway, was rebuilt in stone in these same years, the old timber and turf of its earlier building having at last given out under one too many storms off the western sea. It kept, in its rebuilding, the same name the carriers had always known it by, the fellowship's own masons holding that a name earned by a hundred years of true telling was not a thing to improve upon, whatever a stone building might otherwise have suggested to a prouder hand.

Further north, at a waypoint house on the northern road, a keeper new to that house began, in the same generation, to serve a spirit distilled from barley rather than the small beer every waypoint house on the northern road had always kept, a stronger drink than any the carriers had trade with before, that warmed a man through on a cold night in a way small beer never troubled to and left him, more than once, warmer still the next morning than the night's own cold could account for. The carriers took to calling it fire-water, for the way of it going down and the way of it sitting after, and the name held faster than any other the drink was ever given. Word of it reached Dùn ìseal before two winters were out, more than one household of that road having found its own hearth's peace disturbed by a carrier who had taken more fire-water than sense the night before, and the Keeper of that generation was asked, by more than one reeve troubled at the change in her own district's roads, to rule whether the drink fell under the Small-Burning Law that governed every other burning on the island.

The ruling, when it came, was as plain as most of that Hall's rulings had always been: that a fire kept in a man's own belly, however hot it burned there, was no burning the Law had ever been written to answer for, the Small-Burning Law having concerned itself, since Fionnlagh's own day, with what a hearth or a forge sent up into the air rather than with what any household chose to swallow of its own free will, and that the Hall would rule on drink the day it began ruling on food, and not a day before. The keeper of that house took the ruling for the leave it plainly was, and the house's trade doubled within the season, carriers of the whole northern road coming to hear of a house where a cold night's journey might be answered with more than small beer and a hard bench. What quarrels the fire-water caused after, the rolls of that road are content to let the waypoint houses settle, as they had always settled a carrier's own excesses without troubling Dùn ìseal for a ruling on every one of them.

## VI. Of Caol mhòr, Ceann mhòr and the Edge of Things

Of the north-west in these years the island's own rolls have little to say, and what little they keep is mostly complaint, the north-west having always sat at the far edge of whatever reach the red hall's own writ carried, and further still from whatever reach its own attention troubled to follow. The fisher-families of Caol mhòr, on that coast, had by these generations gathered themselves under a headman of their own choosing rather than any reeve sent out from Cathair dhearg, and sent their tolls to the red hall only in the years a road-guard actually came so far to collect them, which by the fisher-families' own telling was not every year, nor even most of them. A reeve of Ros dhomhain, whose own district lay nearest the north-west of any that troubled to keep a written roll, wrote to the red hall more than once in these years to say that Caol mhòr paid what it pleased and answered to whom it pleased, and that a coast so far from the capital's own eye might as well keep its own law for all the crown's writ reached it; the red hall's own answer, where the rolls keep one at all, is generally no answer, the north-west's tolls having never yet amounted to a sum the red hall thought worth a road-guard's whole season to collect.

Ceann mhòr, further up that same coast, found in these years a trade that the rest of the island came, in time, to depend on more than it ever troubled to thank Ceann mhòr for: seal-oil, rendered from the seals of the northern rocks and carried down the western coast to Ros bheag in exchange for silver rings, burned in the beacons of every western harbour from Ros bheag to Cuan shean. The seal-oil lamps of the western harbours have burned, since, the brightest light on the whole of Dia-thìr, a fact every sailor of that coast came to know before he knew much else of the trade that fed him, and Ceann mhòr grew, on the strength of a trade in nothing grander than rendered fat, into a town of some standing on a coast where standing of any kind was otherwise hard come by.

In the high country above Muileann leathan, further north again, herding families took to the summer pastures for the wool of the mountain sheep that grazed there, sheep whose fleece the weavers of Inis mhòr had long prized above any other on the island; the herders held no right to the handful that the census of hands had reckoned two generations before, having never held one, their pastures lying too high and too far from any waypoint house for a road-guard to trouble counting them, and they were not written into that census nor into any that followed it, a people the island's own rolls knew existed without ever quite troubling to know how many of them there were.

Inis mhin, a small fishing harbour on the northern shore further east again, came into the rolls for the first and, in this stretch of years, the only time, when its own headman walked the whole distance to the wool-fair at Inis mhòr to lay a complaint against the road-guard's toll before whichever reeve would hear it. The complaint is kept in full in the rolls of that fair, a longer telling by far than the harbour otherwise earned in the whole of this age, and it is the plain truth of that coast, as one clerk of Inis mhòr noted in the margin of his own copy without troubling to soften it, that a small harbour's whole history on the written page might amount to nothing more than the one grievance it once walked far enough to voice.

## VII. Of the Raid, the Second Sighting and the Round of the Islands

The western strands, in these same generations, suffered a season of raiding out of the north-western waters, low boats coming in at first light to take a night's catch of dried fish from any strand too small to keep its own watch and running before any boat large enough to give chase could be launched after them, much as an earlier generation of raiders had once troubled the eastern islets in Lachlann Bàn's own day. The sting-sworn merchants of Ros bheag, whose own trade in seal-oil and silver rings ran along that whole coast, paid from their own purses for two boats armed and crewed to watch the strands through the worst of that season, a cost none of them welcomed but every one of them judged cheaper, in the end, than a season's fish lost to raiders with no watch to answer them. The raiders moved north before the two boats ever caught one at open sea, the western strands having grown, within a season, too well watched to be worth the risk, and the coast north of Ros bheag kept, for a good while after, a wariness it had not troubled to keep before.

It was off that same coast, in the north-western water, that two boats of Baile chrom told of something vast moving beneath them one grey morning, neither crew able to say more of it than that the sea had seemed, for the length of a held breath, to breathe under their own keels, and that whatever it was had passed beneath them without touching either hull and was gone before either crew could properly say they had seen anything at all. It was the second such telling the coast remembered, an earlier crew of the same harbour having told a like tale some generations before in Ruairidh Liath's own reign, and the second telling was believed more readily for the first having gone before it, the way one strange thing believed makes the next easier to credit. One of the two boats that morning did not come home, its own crew never found nor any wreck of it either, and Baile chrom, which had kept a wake for the earlier loss out of the first telling, kept the same wake after for both losses together, the harbour holding, as its own elders put it, that a sea capable of taking two boats a generation apart owed no household an explanation before it was owed a proper mourning.

Boats of Sliochd Raghnaill, in a calmer season of these same years, sailed out from Seann Tarr round the whole ring of the offshore islands, Eilean ruadh, Eilean dhearg and Eilean fhada among them, and came home again within the one season, the first crew of the age known to have made the whole round rather than only visiting one island at a time as the fishing or the grazing asked. No crew of that whole stretch of years is known to have gone further from the coast than those islands, nor is any told to have wished to, the open water beyond them holding, for every sailor of that generation, the same plain want of anything worth the sailing to that it had held for every generation before. Families of that same coast took, in these years, to carrying sheep over to Eilean ruadh each summer for its grazing, building turf shelters there against the worst of the weather and bringing the flocks home again before the autumn storms closed the crossing, a custom the coast's herders keep up still, using the same shelters the first families built, patched season by season rather than rebuilt, so that a herder of any later summer shelters, without knowing it, under turf his own great-grandmother first cut and laid.

## VIII. Of the Mark That Held

Of the two hundred and thirty-six years between Tormod Òg and Anna Ruadh, this telling has told what the roads and the shore troubled to keep, and left, as the red hall's own rolls left, whatever passed at Cathair dhearg in those same years to a silence no teller has since thought worth disturbing. It is not a silence this telling judges a loss. A road bridged, a coast watched, a mark cut once and copied after by every hearth on the island: these are not lesser things for having no king's name fixed beside them, and the fellowship of masons that carried the fourth hearth's sign from Ros fhionn to Doire mhin and on to every crossing after asked no ruler's leave to keep its own trade, nor any ruler's remembering to make the keeping worth the labour.

The bridge at Doire mhin stands yet, by every account kept of it, its stream no less given to spring flooding than it ever was and its span no grander than the day Beathag Clachair set the last stone of it; and it is told that a mason passing that way, whatever hearth he answers to and whatever crossing he is bound for next, still stops, before he passes on, to read the small cut letters beneath the fourth hearth's own sign, and to set, if his own errand allows him the moment, a fresh chip of grey stone at the bridge's foot before he takes the road again.
