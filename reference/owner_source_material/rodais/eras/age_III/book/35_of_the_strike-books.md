# Of the Strike-Books

*FE 2,218 – 2,391*

> *Cha do dh'iarr an fhèith oirnn breith a thoirt.*
> The vein never asked us to give judgement.
>
> — the Keeper Raonaid Dhubh, at Dùn ìseal

## I. Of the Strike-Books Compared

At Cnoc bhàn, in the stag's house among the pines the wood-watchers had once kept and the teachers now kept in their turn, the strike-books that a novice called Rìoghnach had begun with three words and a day, a hundred and thirty years before, had grown by the year FE 2,218 into a store no single shelf could hold: chests of oiled hide leaves in the long room behind the grove, each leaf a season's account of one apprentice's hand, kept by masters who had themselves once been the apprentices of masters long dead, so that the room smelled, winter and summer both, of oil and old smoke and the particular dryness of hide that has been read too many times by too many lamps. No one at Cnoc bhàn any longer thought the keeping strange. It had become, in that century and a half, simply what the school did, the way a smith banks a fire or a fisher mends a net, a habit so worn into the place that a child born there took the sight of the chests for granted before it could read what was written in them.

Donnchadh mac Iain was born into that house in the year FE 2,260, the second son of a cutter's widow who had brought him to the teachers herself rather than send him down to the vein at ten years old as his elder brother had gone, on the reasoning, given plainly enough to the master who took him in, that one son owed to the coal was as much as her house could spare and the other might as well learn what use could be made of the marks. He grew up among the chests before he grew up among the seams, and by his twelfth year could find, faster than any master then living, the leaf of any apprentice whose name he was given, a skill that earned him no particular honour at Cnoc bhàn, where finding a leaf was reckoned a small thing beside cutting a fair day's coal, but that stayed with him after the honour of cutting had long ceased to interest him.

He came to his own reading of the chests in his thirtieth year, in the dead of a winter when the vein ran thin and the school's own cutting fell behind its measure, so that the masters set him, for want of better work to give an idle hand, to going through the older leaves and striking out any apprentice long since dead whose account no longer served any purpose but to take up room. It was a task given him as a punishment more than a trust, Donnchadh said himself in later years to more than one of his own pupils, and he did it at first the way a man does a punishment, without much thought beyond finishing it; but somewhere in the second month of that winter, with the strike-books stacked higher than his own head on the table before him and the lamp burned low at his elbow more nights than he afterward cared to count, he began setting a private tally of his own beside the school's: a count, leaf by leaf, of when in a cutter's years the coal had come brightest from the vein.

The answer, when he had it, was plainer than he had looked for. It made no difference whose house a cutter had been born to, the son of a master's line or a cutter with no line worth naming; every hand, without exception among the hundred and more he had gone through, cut its brightest and truest coal in its later years at the seam, seldom or never in its first, the vein answering, leaf after leaf, to years spent at it however little blood had been carried to it. Some nights the tally would not come out even, a leaf or two running against the grain of all the rest, and he set those aside in their own small stack rather than force them, on the reasoning, which he kept to all his life afterward, that a count a man has to bend to make it agree with him is no count at all. He brought the whole of it before the elders of the house on the night nearest midwinter, the whole school gathered as it gathered every year for the turning, and read it out plainly, leaf and year and hand, until the room had gone quiet enough that the lamp's own guttering was the loudest thing in it, and a cutter near the door, a young woman with no schooling in the chests at all, said afterward that she had understood, for the first time in her life, why the teachers made so much of a thing so small as a mark on hide.

The elders did not deny his count. They answered him instead, through the eldest of them, a teacher named Aonghas Cam who had held the chests longer than Donnchadh had been alive, that the vein answered what a cutter was born with, and that the years only showed what had been there from the first, the way a seed shows, in its ripening, no more than what it was planted as. "A seed planted poor does not ripen rich," Aonghas Cam said, "whatever the season does for it." It was an old answer, older than Cnoc bhàn's own strike-books, and Donnchadh had heard it given to lesser questions than his own more times than he could count; but he asked, that night, the one thing no apprentice before him had thought, or dared, to ask back: if the coal answered only to blood and the years showed nothing new, why did every hand in the chests, without one single exception among a hundred and more, show more with every year that passed than it had shown the year before. Aonghas Cam was quiet a while at that, turning it over, and said at last that a seed might ripen slowly and still be no richer a seed for the slowness, which struck more than one in the room as an answer to a question Donnchadh had not asked; and the school let the matter close there for that winter, as the school had let harder matters close before it on nights when no one present had the heart to press further.

## II. Of the Twenty-Two Hearers

Donnchadh did not let it close in his own mind, and for ten years he kept his count, adding to it every leaf that came into the chests in that decade and reading his tally aloud at every midwinter gathering, until the masters of Cnoc bhàn came to expect it of him the way they expected the turning of the year, some listening each time with more patience than the last and some with less. He gathered, over those years, a following among the younger teachers and the older apprentices that the house never troubled to give a name to until the followers had a name of their own choosing: those who heard Donnchadh out to the end of his tally before they answered him, as against those who answered before he had finished, and by the eighth year of it the two groups sat, at the midwinter gathering, on opposite sides of the same long room without any master having told them to.

What broke the house at last was smaller than the tally. In the winter of FE 2,290, on the eight-and-twentieth day of an Gearran, Donnchadh brought before the whole school not his count alone, which by then most of the house had heard often enough to recite along with him, but a plain question set beside it: if the vein answered practice and not blood, as his own hundred leaves and more showed it did, why did the house still write a cutter's parentage at the head of every leaf, before a single stroke of the cutter's own hand was set down. Aonghas Cam, older now and greyer, gave the same answer he had given ten years before, that the vein answered what a cutter was born with and the years only showed it; and Donnchadh, who had by then heard the answer so many times he could have spoken it himself in the old teacher's own voice, said only that a school which wrote a cutter's blood before it wrote a single stroke of the cutter's work had already answered its own question before the question was asked, and that he, for his own part, would keep no more books that way.

He did not leave that night, nor that winter. It took him the better part of a year to gather what he meant to take, twenty-two of the house's own hearers, apprentices and younger teachers both, who had sat on his side of the long room long enough that the choice, when it came, cost most of them less than it might have cost a stranger to the quarrel. Not all who had once sat on his side came with him in the end; three stayed at Cnoc bhàn on the plain grounds, given to Donnchadh himself without much softening, that a house was still a house whatever it wrote at the head of a leaf, and that they had no wish to spend the rest of their own years proving a point at the cost of the roof they had grown up under. Donnchadh took the refusal, by his own later word, harder than he let any of the twenty-two see at the time. They left Cnoc bhàn on the thirtieth day of am Faoilleach, FE 2,300, ten years to the season after Donnchadh's own question had first split the room, carrying between them copies of the strike-books they judged their own, made in their own hands over that last year from the chests that still stood, as they always had, in the long room behind the grove. The elders who watched them go called it theft, and said so plainly to the twenty-two as they loaded what they carried onto two carts borrowed from a carrier of the Abhainn ìseal for the day; Donnchadh, standing at the second cart with the last of the leaves in his own arms, answered only that a copy taken from a book left whole behind it was no theft that any court on the island would know how to try, whatever a grieved house chose to call it, and that the leaves themselves, every one, still stood where they had always stood, for any hand at Cnoc bhàn that cared to read them.

They walked the two days south and east to Muileann chaol, a mill-town on the same river the school at Cnoc bhàn stood beside, further down where the water ran slower and the banks stood wide enough for a settlement to spread without crowding the mill it was named for. Donnchadh had chosen the place himself, in the year of the gathering, for no reason he ever gave the twenty-two beyond that it lay far enough from Cnoc bhàn that the two houses would not trip over each other's grounds within a single season, and near enough that a letter could still cross between them inside a week. He was forty years old the day the carts came into Muileann chaol's own square, grey already at the temple though no greyer than his father had been at the same age, and it was there, in a millwright's storehouse the town's own reeve let them at a rent none of the twenty-two could then have paid in coin and did pay, that first winter, in mended roofs and re-hung doors for half the town, that the second school of the stag began.

## III. Of the School Without Parentage

The rule Donnchadh set for the new school's own strike-books, once the storehouse had a proper chest to hold them and a proper roof to keep the damp from the leaves, was the one his ten years of asking had led him to and Cnoc bhàn had never once been willing to grant him: that a cutter's parentage should not be written in the book at all, not the father's name nor the mother's, not the house nor the line, only the cutter's own name, the day, and the stroke. An apprentice's leaf at Muileann chaol carried, from its very first line, nothing that a stranger reading it could use to say whose child had cut it, and more than one of the twenty-two, in those first years, found the omission harder to keep to than they had expected, a hand's habit of thirty years being slower to unlearn than a rule was to set down. One of the elder teachers among them, who had kept the Cnoc bhàn chests longer than any of the other twenty-two, was caught out at it three winters running, writing a father's name from long habit and scraping it out again before the ink had dried; she said of herself, to any apprentice who asked, that she had unlearned worse habits in her time and expected the fourth winter to serve where the first three had not, which it did. Donnchadh himself kept to the rule without exception, in his own leaves and in every leaf he checked over another's shoulder, and by the fourth or fifth year of the school's own keeping the omission had stopped feeling, even to the oldest of the twenty-two, like an omission at all.

Word of the new house reached Cnoc bhàn within the season, as word of anything done on that river reached the other bank soon enough, and for some years the two schools kept apart entirely, each teaching its own apprentices from its own chests and answering questions about the other, when a trader or a carrier asked, with no more than a dry word and a shrug. It was the school at Muileann chaol that broke the silence first, in the year FE 2,330, when Donnchadh, seventy years old by then and less able to travel the two days himself than he once had been, sent a letter north by a carrier bound that way regardless, addressed to no one master of Cnoc bhàn by name but to the house at large, asking only whether the elder school's own count, kept now for near a century and a half, showed the same rise in a cutter's years that his own hundred leaves had shown thirty years before, or whether the years at Cnoc bhàn told some other tale entirely.

The answer came back within the month, in the hand of a teacher none at Muileann chaol had heard of before that winter, and said, in fewer words than Donnchadh's own letter had asked its question in, that the count was the same at Cnoc bhàn as he said it was at Muileann chaol, and had been the same for as long as any leaf in either house's chests could show it, and that this changed nothing whatever in what the elder school believed the coal answered to. Donnchadh read the letter twice at his own table, by his own account given to a pupil who asked him of it years later, and laughed once, without much humour in it, at a house that could grant him every fact he had spent forty years gathering and refuse him, in the same breath, the single conclusion he had built the facts to carry. He wrote back once more, a shorter letter than his first, saying only that the two houses, having agreed at last on what the vein showed, need not agree on what it meant to go on writing to each other about it; and it was that second letter, the reeve of Muileann chaol's own clerk noted years afterward in a private hand at the foot of a copy he kept for himself, that began the correspondence the two schools would keep for the rest of the age, neither house ever again letting a full season pass without some word crossing the river between them, whether the word carried agreement or its plain refusal.

Donnchadh mac Iain did not live to see the correspondence outlast its own quarrel. He died at Muileann chaol in his eightieth year, FE 2,340, in the same storehouse-turned-schoolhouse the twenty-two had first taken shelter in forty years before, having taught, by the school's own later reckoning, some six generations of apprentices whose leaves, true to his own rule to the last, carried no name of father or mother anywhere upon them. He was buried, at his own asking given some years before his death to the pupils who would carry it out, not under any stone cut with his own house's mark, which by then none at Muileann chaol troubled to keep any longer, but under a plain leaf of oiled hide set in the ground above him and left to weather away in its own time, a grave-marker that told, within a season of rain, nothing of who lay beneath it but the one word he had asked cut upon it before it went into the ground: *sgrìobhaiche*, one who writes.

## IV. Of the Vein That Asked No Judgement

In the same year that Donnchadh mac Iain died at Muileann chaol, a girl was born at Dùn ìseal, in the household of a clerk of the Hall's own roll, and given the name Raonaid Dhubh for the black hair she kept, unusually, from her first day, when most children of that household had been born fair. She grew up nearer the mountain's own vein than either school on the river had ever stood, in the long galleries the strike-books of the elder generation had first been born to keep account of, and came to the Hall's own service in her twentieth year as a clerk of the Keeper's own chest, the roll that had once been Peigi nic Tormoid's and had passed, in the generations since, through more hands than any single leaf of it now bore a name for.

Her rise in that service came on a plainer reason than her own house cared to admit, an elder cousin's death leaving the Keeper's own stool empty at a season when no one else in the room wanted the sitting of it, much as the same stool had once been given to Peigi nic Tormoid herself for the same reason two hundred years before her; and the cup was set at last in Raonaid Dhubh's own hands on the fourth day of an t-Samhain, FE 2,366, at six-and-twenty years old, the youngest to hold it in three generations. Word of the giving reached the granary households before the ink of the roll's own entry had dried, one reeve remarking to another, as the story was told afterward, that a Keeper young enough to be his own daughter would want watching the first year or two, a remark the reeve himself was said to have taken back within the season.

Raonaid Dhubh had known of the two schools' quarrel long before she came to the cup, as any clerk at Dùn ìseal knew of it, the letters between Cnoc bhàn and Muileann chaol having by then grown into a small archive of their own that more than one idle clerk had read through for want of livelier business on a slow afternoon. It came, by her own later account, as no great surprise to her when both houses, within her first year in office, sent word asking the same thing of the new Keeper that they had once asked, without much hope of an answer, of every Keeper before her: that the Hall say, once and plainly, which school's reading of the vein was the truer one, so that a matter forty years unsettled might at last be settled by a voice neither house could answer back to.

She heard them both at Dùn ìseal, in the same hall her own predecessors had heard lesser disputes in for longer than the mountain's own chests could number, a delegation from each school standing on either side of the same floor as the two halves of Cnoc bhàn's old long room had once sat on either side of a single table. The elder school's own delegation spoke first, an old teacher naming leaf after leaf of a hundred and fifty years to show how long the house's own reading had stood unshaken; the younger school answered with Donnchadh's own tally, carried north in a hand not his own, since none of the twenty-two who had first walked it south still lived to carry it themselves. Raonaid Dhubh let each speak its full case before she answered either, asking no question of her own beyond a plain point of date now and again, which one of the clerks with her noted afterward she had asked chiefly, he thought, to be certain both delegations knew she had listened to every word.

What she said, when both had finished, was fewer words than either delegation had spent making its case, and the Hall's own roll set it down that same evening in the plain hand of the clerk who had once been, before her own rise, no more than one voice among many reading old letters for want of livelier business: that the vein had never asked the Hall to give judgement on what a cutter was born with, and she saw no cause to give one now that it had not asked for, either school being free to keep its books as it judged best so long as neither book claimed for itself an authority the mountain had never granted to any book at all.

It was, the roll's own later summary noted, the plainest refusal any Keeper of that century had given, and for that reason, more than any ruling could have been, the one most often cited afterward by clerks weary of a dispute they had no wish to settle themselves: a Keeper's own words, once given, standing thereafter as a kind of ruling in everything but the name, though Raonaid Dhubh, asked years later by a younger clerk whether she had meant her answer to stand as one, said only that she had meant it as what it said and nothing further, and that a clerk who read more into four sentences than the four sentences carried was doing the roll no favour by it.

## V. Of Cnoc ghorm's Stone and the Diggers' Road

The two schools kept their letters after that hearing as they had kept them before it, neither claiming the Keeper's refusal for its own side though each, in its own private correspondence with its own house, read the silence somewhat to its own advantage; and it was scholars of the stag from Muileann chaol, ten years on from the hearing, who made the journey east to Cnoc ghorm in the autumn of FE 2,376 to look at a standing stone the town had kept at its own centre longer than any teller there could say, cut in a hand no school on the river, elder or younger, could make out a single letter of. The scholars stood before it the better part of a day, turning its worn face to the low autumn light from every angle a careful reading asks, tracing a finger along grooves that might have been letters once and might, as one of the younger scholars said, be nothing more than what any stone standing that long in that weather comes to wear on its own account. They left it, by evening, no nearer an answer than they had come with.

It was the people of Cnoc ghorm themselves, who read no more of the stone's own cutting than the scholars had, who gave the visitors what the stone would not give up on its own: that the stone said, as it had always been told in that town from mother to child longer than any single house there could trace its own line back, *cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann*, we did not come; we were here. The scholars wrote the saying down exactly as the town gave it to them, together with the plain fact that neither they nor any hand before them had read the stone itself to confirm it, and left the matter there, in a hand as careful of what it did not know as of what it did; and no scholar of either school, nor any teller after them, ever explained the stone or the saying further, the one being, as the elder among the visiting party put it to the younger on the road home, no poorer a thing for having no explanation than a great many older things on the island that had gone unexplained a good deal longer.

Ten years after that again, in the summer of FE 2,386, a company of men from Baile ghorm to the south, hearing a tale grown old in their own district that the fallen walls of Làrach an Dùin held riches enough to buy a poor man's house twice over, went up under cover of a single night and broke into the ruin's lower ground with picks and torches. The oldest of them, a mason's son named for no history now troubles to keep, had heard the tale from his own grandfather, who had heard it in turn from a grandfather of his own, so that none of the company digging that night could have said with any certainty how old the promise of riches was, only that it had never once, in all the tellings, been said to be false. They found, when the dawn came up on their digging, nothing beneath the fallen stone but ash, cold and undisturbed, that no fire any of them could name had left there.

The Keeper, word of the digging having reached Dùn ìseal within the fortnight, had the diggers brought before her and sentenced them as though they had broken the Law, though no law then written covered a ruin no living hand had built or owned; and it was that sentence, given for a wrong the roll admitted no statute named, that stood from that summer on as the rule for every old place the island held, that a thing standing from before any house now living could claim it was a thing no living hand had leave to break into, riches or no riches beneath it.

The families of the diggers did their penance, as the Keeper set it, by keeping in repair, at their own house's cost and their own house's labour, the road that ran from Baile ghorm south to the tomb-ground at Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, a stretch of track that had gone to mud and broken stone in more winters than any reeve had troubled to count before that summer; and they kept it, the families and their children after them, long past the years any sentence could properly have bound them to, until the road, by the time the age drew toward its own close on the three-and-twentieth day of an t-Iuchar, FE 2,391, had gone by no other name in all the southern hills but the one the penance had given it, the diggers' road, a name that told, to anyone who asked why an ordinary stretch of track between an ordinary town and an ordinary tomb-ground should carry so plain a title, the whole of the story in the two words alone.

By that same year the letters between Cnoc bhàn and Muileann chaol had crossed the river between them for near sixty years without a season's gap, two chests of correspondence standing now in each house beside the chests of strike-books that had begun the whole quarrel, neither school any nearer agreeing with the other on what the vein truly answered to than Donnchadh mac Iain and Aonghas Cam had been on the night the question was first asked aloud, and neither house, for all that, willing to let a single season pass without writing to say so.
