# Of the Reverent Centuries

*FE 422 – 542*

> *Chan eil fearann aig neach nach eil aig a shliochd.*
> No one holds land who does not hold it for those who come after.
>
> — said of the granaries at Dùn dhearg, in the reverent centuries

## I. Of the Granaries and the Debt of Bread

Coinneach Mòr mac Tormoid was two-and-twenty, and still counted among the young men of Seann Dunn who had not yet been asked to speak at a council, the year the storm season broke early on the lowland fields and Sliochd Ìomhair opened the doors of the new granaries at Dùn dhearg for the first time to households that were not its own. He rode down with his father to see it, more from curiosity than need, for the house of Tormod held grain enough of its own that season, and what he remembered after, more than the stone walls or the measured piles of barley within them, was the queue: three hundred people or more standing in a line that bent twice round the yard, each with a sack or a basket, each waiting a turn that the keepers of the granary gave out with no more ceremony than a nod and a mark scratched on a tally, and none of them saying much to the ones ahead or behind, as people do not talk much while they wait on a thing they are glad of and ashamed of both at once.

His father, Tormod Bàn, had held no love for the house of Ìomhar and said so plainly enough at his own hearth, but standing at the edge of that yard he only watched the queue a long while and said, at last, that a house which filled its granaries against the storm season and then opened them to whoever came short had done what the old custom of sharing always asked and no lord before it had troubled to do at this scale, and that a debt of bread was a debt like any other, save that it did not always feel like one to the man who owed it, not until the day his own field failed and he found he had nowhere else to go but Dùn dhearg's gate. Coinneach asked him whether that made the house of Ìomhar the better lord for it, and his father said only that it made the house of Ìomhar richer in people who owed it something, which is not the same question, and that a young man who could not tell the two questions apart yet would learn to before he was much older.

He did learn it, in time, though not from any single lesson. The granaries filled again the next storm season, and the one after, and within Coinneach's own lifetime it had become the ordinary shape of things along that stretch of the lowland, so ordinary that the households who came to the gate no longer thought of it as owing anything at all, only as the way grain was had in a bad year, the way water was had from a well that happened to stand on another man's land. Sliochd Ìomhair never called in the debt in words. It did not need to. A house that had filled every belly on the plain through three bad harvests running had no need to remind anyone of it at the fourth, and the households themselves, when the matter came up at all, spoke of Dùn dhearg the way a man speaks of the ford he crosses without paying it any particular gratitude, save that he would notice keenly enough if it were taken away.

At the foot of the high pass to the north, in those same years, a different debt was being built more quietly still. Carriers who took the Hall's baskets over the pass had long rested their beasts at the last flat ground before the climb began, a stretch of machair with a spring on it and little else to recommend it, and by the time Coinneach was a grown man there was a settled place there with byres and a smithy and an inn that never had an empty bed in the season the pass was open, called by the carriers themselves, before it had any other name, simply the resting-place, and afterward Baile Mòr dhomhain, the great deep town, though it stood on no deep water and had no claim to greatness beyond the beasts it fed and the coin it took for feeding them. It lived by the pass and little else, as the carriers said of it without unkindness, the way a man says of his own trade that it is what he has rather than what he chose.

## II. Of the Keeping of the Flame

Coinneach came to the flame in the month of Giblean, in his five-and-twentieth year, the first Keeper of Sliochd Thormoid, chosen by the four houses in the hall at Seann Dunn on a morning cold enough that the breath of every man who spoke his name for the office hung visible in the air of the chamber before it reached the ears it was meant for. He had not sought the office with any great heat, being by his own account and everyone else's a young man of more patience than ambition, and it was that same patience, more than any gift for speech or arms, that the houses named when they chose him: a Keeper, they said, did not need to be clever, only steady, for the flame asked nothing of a man but that he keep it, and a man who kept it steadily for sixty years was worth ten who kept it brilliantly for five.

He kept it for two-and-sixty years, longer than any Keeper of any of the four houses before him, and it was said afterward, by those who lived to see the length of his keeping laid out whole, that he had changed nothing in all that time, which was meant, and taken, as the highest praise the office could earn rather than the smallest. The flame at Seann Dunn burned the year he came to it exactly as it had burned the year before, and the year he died it burned the same still, fed the same measure, tended by the same order of hands, answering to no different rule than it had answered to when Sìne nan Snaidhm first tied the knots at Dùn ìseal, generations beyond counting before him. A visitor from a coast that changed its harbour-master every ten years and thought nothing of it once asked Coinneach, late in his keeping, whether it did not weary a man to hold one office so long without altering it, and Coinneach answered that a flame altered was a flame gone out under a different name, and that he had never yet met the man wise enough to alter a fire and be sure, afterward, that it was still the same fire he had lit. The visitor pressed him once more, asking whether he had never in sixty years wished to leave some mark of his own upon the office he had held so long, and Coinneach said that a Keeper's mark, if he left one worth leaving, was that no one after him could point to any year of his keeping and say the flame had burned differently in it than in the year before, and that he counted himself fortunate to have found, in his own nature, so little wish to be remembered any other way.

It was in those same generations, the rolls said long after, in words Coinneach himself might have used of his own keeping without changing one of them, that the people of the lowland fields neither grew greatly in number nor dwindled, mined the same small measure of coal and burned the same small measure, whichever lord's stones a household happened to live between, and were called by the rolls, in time, the reverent centuries: not for any reverence the people themselves professed, which was ordinary enough, but for the stillness of the years themselves, laid one on another like courses of a wall that never rose any higher, a wall built, if it was built for anything, only to keep standing.

## III. Of the Headman of Achadh chaol

Not every household under Coinneach's keeping was so settled as the tellings of the reverent centuries later made it sound, and the first council he sat through in the year after his choosing gave him a lesson in how much settling a household still had to do before the rolls would call it ordinary. Shepherds out of the eastern hills had taken up the upland fields at Achadh chaol some years before, sending their wool down to the harbours along tracks no cart had cut before them, and their headman, a broad, slow-spoken man named Griogair Ruadh who had never in his life sat under a roof he did not himself thatch, came down to the council at Dùn ìseal that season to ask, on behalf of the whole settlement, for a seat among the houses that governed the plain.

He asked it plainly and without much art, standing before the four Keepers with his hat in both hands and his feet planted the way a man plants them on a hillside rather than a hall floor, and said that his people had held the upland fields long enough now to be called a share like any other share, and that a share which paid its dues as faithfully as Achadh chaol paid its had earned some voice in how those dues were spent. The houses heard him out, as the custom required, and then asked him the one question the custom also required, which was whether any household of Achadh chaol held hearth-right under the old grant, the right cut into stone at the Roinn, without which no settlement, however long-standing, could claim a seat at the council rather than merely a place in its queue.

Griogair Ruadh had no answer to that question beyond the true one, which was that his people's grant was newer than the Roinn by some thousand years and carried no such right written anywhere. He did not pretend otherwise, nor try, as a lesser man might have, to talk the council past the question rather than answer it; he only stood a while longer with his hat still in his hands, looked once round the chamber at the four Keepers seated above him, and said that he had walked five days down off the hill to hear a thing he had half known before he set out, and would walk five days back up having heard it said plainly rather than merely feared, which was, in its own way, worth the walking. Having given that true answer he sat a single season on the council as courtesy allowed, said little in it that any teller thought worth keeping, and went home again to Achadh chaol at the season's end with no seat and no promise of one, only the courtesy he had been given and the dues he still owed. He bore it, by every account that reached Seann Dunn afterward, without bitterness, telling his own people only that a right cut in stone outlasted a right spoken aloud, however fair the speaking, and that a household wanting a seat at that council had best begin, if it could, by finding some stone of its own to cut. Coinneach remembered the sound of Griogair Ruadh's voice longer than he remembered the substance of what either side had said, a man asking plainly for a thing plainly refused, and thought, more than once in later years when some newer household came asking the same question in gentler words, that the answer never changed because the stone never changed, and that a Keeper's whole use, in such a matter, was only to say so without pretending the saying cost the asker nothing.

## IV. Of the Walkers and the List of Ruins

It was in his own fortieth year of keeping, when the flame had settled into him as thoroughly as he had settled into the flame, that Coinneach sent out the walkers, four men and two women chosen for legs that had carried them the length of the plain more than once and tongues that did not run ahead of what their eyes had actually seen, with instructions to list every ruin standing on ground the four houses claimed and to say, of each, only who did not own it, and never to guess who had built it or why. It was not a grand undertaking as the plain reckoned such things, and Coinneach himself, asked why he troubled with it at all, said only that a lord who did not know what stood on his own ground could not in honesty claim the ground, and that the plain had grown thick enough with old walls and older stones that no house any longer knew, without asking, which of them belonged to anyone living.

The walkers were gone the better part of two years, and came back with a list longer than any of them had expected to make. They brought Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, a walled outpost on a spur above Cathair mhòr with stones dressed smooth on the outward face only and a single gate blocked from within by rubble no living hand had put there; they brought Làrach an Dùin, three rings of broken wall and a cistern cut into the bedrock with room, by the walkers' own reckoning, for four hundred souls and their beasts, though what four hundred souls had ever sheltered there none of the households nearby could say; they brought Làrach an Teampaill, a stepped platform of fitted stone whose altar, they noted with the same flat honesty they gave to everything else, faced the central mountains and not the sea, as no altar raised in living memory ever faced; and they brought word, too, of standing stones scattered singly across the plain that answered to no name at all, only a description each, a long stone leaning east, three stones in a broken ring, one stone alone on a rise with a hollow worn at its foot by nothing the walkers could account for.

Of not one of these did the list say who had raised it. Coinneach ruled, when the walkers laid their tally before him, that it should not, and held to the ruling against more than one house that pressed him afterward for a guess dressed up as a finding, saying that a Keeper who let his walkers guess once would have to let them guess every time after, and that a list which told the plain only who did not own a ruin was worth more, in the end, than a list which told it a comforting lie about who had. The list stood, unaltered, for the whole length of his keeping and beyond it, and it was from that list, more than from any single ruin in it, that the plain first learned to speak of the builders of such places, whoever they had been, as simply the old people, with no house's name attached to the phrase and no house troubled, thereafter, to attach one.

## V. Of the Ring of Stakes at Cnoc bheag

The standing stone above Cnoc bheag was the one ruin on the walkers' list that did not stay untended once it was named. It stood alone on rising ground just outside the town, taller than a man and cut, on its upper face, in a script none living could read, and beneath that, cut later and by a plainer hand with a narrower tool, four words of the tongue every household on the plain still spoke: bha teine sa chloich, there was fire in the stone. No one at Cnoc bheag claimed to know which of their own forebears had cut those four words, nor when, only that the cutting was old enough that even the oldest woman in the town, asked about it at her own hearth, could say only that her grandmother had called it old already.

It was the cattle grazing the rise around it that moved the people of Cnoc bheag to act, in the year the walkers' list reached them, more than any thought for the stone's age: they had taken, over some seasons, to rubbing themselves against its lower face the way cattle rub against any standing thing in a field with few trees, until the lower cut letters had begun to wear soft at their edges under hide and horn. A household whose byre stood nearest the rise raised the first ring of stakes about the stone that same season, driving them by hand into ground too stony for a proper post-hole, and the ring held the cattle off well enough that the household beside it raised a second length the year after, and within Coinneach's own lifetime the whole rise had a fence about it that no single household could claim to have built, only to have added to, each generation renewing the stakes its own grandparents had first driven and never once moving the stone itself so much as a hand's breadth from where the old people, whoever they were, had set it. It stands so yet, ringed and unmoved, the fence renewed oftener than any hall on the plain has been rebuilt, which Coinneach, hearing of it in his own old age from a Cnoc bheag man come to Seann Dunn on other business, allowed was as fitting a monument as any stone the old people themselves had ever cut, for it asked nothing of the plain but that the plain go on doing, every generation, the one small thing it had already learned to do.

## VI. Of the Bath-House at Muileann ruadh

The warm springs below Muileann ruadh had been known to the plain longer than any hall now standing on it, a handful of pools where the ground ran hot enough to steam on a cold morning, and for as long as anyone could tell it they had been used the way a household uses any good thing it finds unclaimed, by whoever walked far enough to reach them and troubled to strip and wade in. It was in Coinneach's sixtieth year, when a cousin of his own house fell sick of a wasting cough that the ordinary remedies had failed to touch and was carried the two days' journey to the springs on a litter more in desperation than in any settled hope, that the plain first saw what a roof over those pools might do that the open pools themselves had never managed, for the cousin lay in the steam of the covered spring through a hard winter that would have killed him lying anywhere colder, and came home in the spring thinner but breathing, and lived nine years more.

Coinneach, hearing the whole of it from the cousin's own mouth, gave the order that a proper bath-house be raised over the largest of the pools, roofed and walled well enough to hold the steam through a winter storm, with sleeping-places built along one side for the sick and rough benches along the other for whoever had carried them there and meant to wait out the cure with them. He named a keeper of the springs to tend the place, a task he gave, deliberately and against the grumbling of more than one house that thought the honour beneath a hearth-holder, to a widow of no great name who had nursed her own husband through a like wasting and knew, she said, more of steam and fever than any council was likely to teach her, and he set her office in the name of Dian Cècht, the healer, the first office the flame at Seann Dunn had ever made that answered to no measure of coal at all. It was, by his own accounting late in life, the ruling he was proudest of among all he had made or left unmade in two-and-sixty years, not because it altered anything about the flame or the granaries or the reverent stillness of the plain's own years, but because it was the one thing in the whole of his keeping that had cost the house of Tormod nothing and given a sick man breath he would not otherwise have drawn, and a Keeper, he said once to the widow herself, might do worse with sixty years than to find, somewhere in the middle of them, one such ruling. She kept the springs for four-and-thirty years after, and trained, before she died, two of her own grandchildren to the same office, so that the keeping of Muileann ruadh's steam passed from hand to hand within one household long after the household that had first carried a dying man there had any other claim on the place at all, and Coinneach lived long enough to see it pass a second time and to say, of that alone among his rulings, that it had outgrown him in exactly the way he had hoped it might.

## VII. Of the Weights of Muileann chiar

The silversmiths of Muileann chiar cast their set of weights in the same decade, though not at Coinneach's asking nor at any house's, being a matter the smiths took up among themselves after one too many disputes at their own market over whether a given lump of silver weighed what the seller claimed for it. They cut the least of the set to the weight of a single barley-corn and the greatest to the weight of a newborn lamb, with a dozen steps between carefully checked one against another until the smiths themselves would stake their own trade's name on the set's honesty, and set the whole of it out for any trader on the carriers' roads to use, asking nothing for the use beyond the courtesy of returning the weights to their proper rack when the weighing was done.

It spread the way an honest thing spreads among people grown weary of dishonest ones: no ruling of any house sent it abroad, only traders themselves carrying word of it from market to market, until within Coinneach's own lifetime a thing weighed at Muileann chiar was spoken of on every stretch of the carriers' roads as weighed fairly, a phrase that outlived, in the plain's own mouth, every other thing the smiths of that town were known for. Coinneach, who governed no market and claimed no credit for the weights, said of them only, when a visiting Keeper of another house asked him how his own flame had managed so useful a thing, that the flame had managed nothing, that the smiths of Muileann chiar had managed it themselves out of their own weariness with liars, and that the plain would do well to remember, watching the weights spread unbidden from market to market, that not every good thing on it had needed a Keeper's hand to make it good.

## VIII. Of the Marriage and the Double-Sworn Fields

The dark thread of Coinneach's later years began, as such threads so often do, with a match that pleased everyone who arranged it. Torcall Donn, great-grandson of Eithne nic Ailein and a landed man of the lowland plain about Cathair mhòr, was five-and-eighty and long past the age most men on the plain married a second time, when he took to wife Muireall nic Ìomhair, a young kinswoman of the house of Ìomhar not yet four-and-twenty, in a match both houses spoke of afterward as having cost neither side anything and gained both a great deal: Torcall a young wife and heirs still to come of her, and Muireall's own kin a tie of blood into the line that held Cathair mhòr's great share, which no marriage before had managed. The wedding was held at Dùn dhearg, by Muireall's own family's wish, and Coinneach himself, though the match was no business of his flame, travelled down to bless it at the asking of both houses, and said afterward to no one in particular that he had rarely seen two houses so pleased with an arrangement that neither of them, strictly, needed.

Muireall died in her first winter as a wife, of a fever that took hold in the space of nine days and gave the physicians of Cathair mhòr no chance to do more than watch it run its course, and she left behind no child, only the lands that had come to her at her marriage and the lands she had brought into it, held under both a grant from the house of Ìomhar and a grant from the line of Eithne, as her marriage-portion had been drawn up to satisfy both sides at once and had satisfied, in the drawing, no one's question of what should happen were she to die without an heir of her own body. Within the year, the house of Ìomhar claimed her lands as returning, on a childless death, to the kin that had granted them, and the line of Eithne claimed the same lands as passing, by the marriage, into the husband's holding and thence to his own kin at Cathair mhòr, and both claims were argued with equal heat and equal justice by men who had never troubled, while Muireall lived, to ask which grant would prevail should she die too soon, because no one had thought a woman not yet four-and-twenty would die too soon.

What began as an argument over parchment did not stay an argument over parchment. Within two years of Muireall's death, households on ground her marriage-portion had touched found themselves claimed by both houses at once, sworn, or made to swear, to Dùn dhearg on one hand and to Cathair mhòr on the other, and a holding sworn twice over was a holding neither side would leave standing for the other's use. The first of the double-sworn fields to burn belonged to a household that had held the same stretch of barley ground for four generations without either house troubling to ask it whose grant it lived under, and the burning, when it came, came in a single night from men its own household could name by voice if not by face, the byre fired first and the standing barley after, so that by morning there was nothing left on that stretch worth either house's claiming. The household that had held it went, as the plunderers' households had once gone, down to whatever kin would have them, and the ground lay unsown the season after, claimed loudly by both houses and worked by neither, which those who saw it called a stranger sight than the fire had been: land that a house of Ìomhar's blood and a house of Eithne's blood both wanted badly enough to burn a neighbour for, and neither wanted enough to plant. The men who fired it were no strangers to the household that lost it, but its own near neighbours, men who had shared a boundary stone with the burned household the season before and would share nothing with it now but the smoke. Coinneach, growing old by then and troubled more by the burnings than by anything else two-and-sixty years of keeping had shown him, called it, the one time he was heard to name it plainly, the war neither house had declared and both houses were fighting, and said that a plain which had gone three centuries scarcely changing had found, in one woman's early death, a wound that changing might not close again in his own lifetime nor in the lifetime of whoever kept the flame after him.

## IX. Of the Death of Coinneach Mòr

He died in his eighty-seventh year, in the month of the year's turning, in the same chamber at Seann Dunn where he had been chosen sixty-two years before, with the flame he had kept so steadily burning in its accustomed place not twenty paces from where he lay and three of the widow's own grandchildren, grown now to tending the bath-house at Muileann ruadh in her stead, among those who sat the watch with him at the end. In his last days he asked after the walkers' list, whether it still said only who did not own each ruin and had not been made, in his absence, to say more, and was told that it stood as he had left it, and seemed satisfied with that answer beyond any other comfort offered him.

He asked, too, after the fields his own Keeping had failed to keep whole, and was told, because those who sat with him judged an old man's asking better answered than left to guess at, that three more holdings on the marriage lands had burned that same season, the smoke of the nearest of them visible, on a still day, from the rise where the fence still stood about the stone at Cnoc bheag. A long silence answered that news, such as those who had known him through his whole keeping recognised as his particular way of receiving a thing he had no ruling left to give against, and he died before the season turned again, leaving the flame of Sliochd Thormoid to a nephew chosen in his stead and leaving the plain, in the words the rolls kept afterward of his going, exactly as reverent and exactly as centuries-still as he had found it, save for the smoke that stood, some mornings, on the horizon south of Dùn dhearg, where no smoke of that kind had stood in living memory before Muireall nic Ìomhair died too young to settle which of two houses her lands belonged to.
