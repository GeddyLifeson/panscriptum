# Of Goraidh mac Ìomhair

*FE 635 – 753*

*Part Four: The Gathering of the Shares*

## I. Of the Red Share Whole

By the time the oak props at Dùn ìseal had stopped smelling of the axe, the island had begun to fill out again, the way a field fills with barley once the birds have had their share and stopped coming. Cathair mhòr had its wall now, and would not say what it had built the wall against, though the reapers of Achadh mhòr still said, whenever anyone asked, that the town had not forgotten the smoke. Cnoc leathan had a harbour-master keeping notched sticks that were already the oldest counted things on the island, and the Keeper Seonaid Chiar, before she died, had let the stag's cutters back into the galleries of the Hall on oath to ask nothing of the vein aloud, which the Hall called a reconciling and the school still called a truce. The Peace of the Threshold was eighty years old and had not been broken once, not at a death, not at a boundary stone, not even at Cathair dhomhain, whose people were proud of keeping every measure to the grain and were forever finding some new law to keep strictly. The shares no longer shrank at every burial. Here and there, slowly, without anyone marking the day it began, they had started instead to grow.

Of no line was this truer than the line of Ìomhar.

Goraidh mac Ìomhair was born in the Red Share in the spring of the Hall's seventh century, the year his grandmother's roof at Dùn dhearg was new-thatched, and he grew up under a roof that had never in his father's memory, or his father's father's, been parted from a neighbour's spear. The Red Share had been won with a sword, not given at the Roinn, and it had never been cut since; whatever quarrels the rest of the island's heirs had over their boundary stones, the holders of Dùn dhearg had only to hold what they already had, and they had held it with a plainness that other lines, forever arguing a grazing-right through three generations of cousins, found almost insulting.

He came into it young, as his line always had. His father died at a hundred and ten, an old age for most households and no age at all for one of Ailean's blood, thrown from a horse on the low ground below Dùn dhearg in a spring so wet the ground never firmed under the beast's feet. Goraidh stood at the grave with his mother's people and his father's stewards, twenty-six years old and already taller than any man in either line, and he said nothing, because there was nothing that wanted saying. He took up the household the next morning. The granaries at Dùn dhearg were full that year, as they generally were; the Red Share paid its own way and had done since Ìomhar Dearg burned the halls within the double bank six centuries before, and Goraidh's first act as its head was no more than to walk the granaries with the steward and check the thatch.

In those years he thought of himself as no more than a holder of a hall. He married early, a woman of a small house on the coast whose name the Red Share's own reciters kept and no one else troubled to, and she gave him a daughter before the century was out, a quick, restless child who could not be got to sit still at a hearth and was forever down at the ford watching the carriers. Goraidh called her Mòrag, for his mother, and thought no more of the succession than any lord of a whole share needs to. The girl would have the hall when he was done with it, as her grandmother had had it before him, only smaller than most, because the Red Share had never been the kind of thing that grew.

Then, within the same ten years, both his mother's brother's sons died without children of their bodies.

They were cousins he had known well enough as a boy, before his own father's death and the long duties after it had cut down his visiting. Cailean had gone under a wall he was mending at his own hall on a night of high wind, and Donnchadh, the younger, had wasted two winters with a cough the hearth-women could not cut and died in the third with his hand in his sister's. Between them they had held two of the old counties east of Dùn dhearg, parted from the Roinn's great share of the north four centuries before and parted again at every death since, the way the north had gone on parting itself until, as the tellers of Dùn ìseal like to say, it could not raise twenty spears from the whole of it. There was no one of either dead man's body to take up what was left. By the custom that had held since the Peace, a share with no heir named went to the Keeper to give as she saw fit, unless some other of the blood could show a claim before her.

Goraidh could show one, through his mother, and so could three other households, more distantly. He did not go to Dùn ìseal to argue it. He went instead, the winter after Donnchadh's burial, to the third of his mother's kin who was still living unmarried and held the third of the three old counties in her own right, a widow some four hundred years old named Mairead nic Dhonnchaidh, whose county marched with both the dead men's and who had buried two husbands of her own without a child of either surviving her.

He did not pretend to himself, nor to her, that it was a love match. She was old enough to have carried the Second Book's own tellers in her lap, and he was not far behind her. But she had held her county alone for eighty years with no one to leave it to, and had watched her cousins' lands sit empty of a lord while three strangers' stewards argued over them at Dùn ìseal, and when Goraidh rode over in the worst of the winter with six men and no threat in him at all, she heard him out at her own hearth and told him she had been waiting for someone to ask her a sensible question, and that he was the first.

"You have not asked me what I want kept in the marrying," she said to him, the second evening he sat at her hearth, before he had so much as named the thing he had ridden over to ask.

"Tell me, then," said Goraidh, "and I'll write it before I write the rest."

She told him. Her own reciter would keep his place and his fire, whatever the Red Share's own reciters thought of sharing a hearth with a stranger's teller; the debts her two husbands had left the county's smiths were his to carry, and not her name's to carry after her; and she would be buried, when it came to that, among her own dead and not among his. He agreed to all three before she had finished the third, and she looked at him a while, the way a woman looks at a tally that has come out even for once, and said that he was the first of his line she had ever heard of who did not need a thing explained to him twice.

They were married at Dùn dhearg in the spring, before the roads had properly dried, with the Red Share's own reciters standing witness because no others could get there in time. By the terms she brought to it, her county came into the marriage whole, as the Peace allowed; and by the same terms, made before the Keeper's own clerk so that no line could later say the marriage was got up only for the land, Goraidh took on himself the debts her two dead husbands had left the county's smiths, which were not small.

So it was that within a single winter's dealing, and by nothing more than two deaths he had not caused and one marriage he had gone looking for, Goraidh mac Ìomhair held in one hand the Red Share, whole as his fathers had kept it, and three of the old counties of the east besides, parted since the Roinn and gathered now, for the first time in four hundred years, into a single household's granaries.

## II. Of the Third County and What It Cost

The stewards of the three counties came to Dùn dhearg that first summer to be sworn, and they were not, on the whole, glad men. Two of the counties had been managed for a generation by men who had grown used to answering to no one nearer than a dead cousin's memory, and the third had been Mairead's alone, run her own close way, with her own notches on her own sticks, and she had never once in eighty years asked any of them to account to a husband. Now there was a hall at Dùn dhearg that wanted their tallies each autumn, and a lord in it younger than some of their own grandchildren, of the Red Share's blood but not, until that winter, of theirs.

Goraidh did not go about it the way his father would have. He rode the boundary of all three counties himself in the first year, on foot half the time, with Mairead beside him telling him which stream had moved its bed and which family had held a grazing since before the Roinn and would go to war over it if a new steward tried to measure it out again. He ate at the hearths and not the halls, and asked before he ruled. It cost him a full summer and most of the autumn, and it cost Mairead, who was not young even by the reckoning of her own line, more than she let him see; she died in the ninth year of the marriage, in her sleep, with the county's own reciter in the room, and Goraidh buried her among her own dead, not his, because she had asked it of him plainly the week before, and he had learned by then not to argue with her.

He did not marry again. He had his daughter, growing tall and impatient at Dùn dhearg, and he had four counties where his father had held one, and he found, in the years after Mairead's death, that the four together took more of his waking hours than the Red Share alone had ever asked of him, and that he did not mind it.

It was a hard decade for the low ground east of the mountain. Two summers running the rains came at the wrong end of the season and the barley stood in the field and rotted before the reapers could get to it, and in the second of those years three of the smaller households along the old boundary between Cailean's dead county and Donnchadh's went hungry enough that their children were sent to kin in the hills to be fed through the worst of the winter. Goraidh opened the granaries at Dùn dhearg that year, all four of them, the Red Share's own stores and the three counties' besides, and sold the grain at less than it had cost him to keep, and gave it outright to nine households that could not have paid even that.

It was not, by his own account of it, a generous act. He said so once to his daughter, late in his life, when she pressed him on it: that a lord who lets his own low ground starve has nothing left to be lord of, granary or no granary, and that he had done the arithmetic before he had done anything that could be mistaken for kindness. Mòrag did not believe him, and neither, in the end, did the households.

## III. Of the Granary Door

It began, as such things do, with no one meaning it.

A household at the eastern edge of what had been Donnchadh's county came to the granary door at Dùn dhearg in the third bad autumn to beg seed corn against the next year's sowing, and the steward at the door, new to the post and unsure of the forms for a thing that had never before crossed three counties' worth of custom at once, asked whose authority he should set against the debt.

"The rìgh's," said the old woman who had come for the corn, before the steward could finish the question, and went on into the yard as if she had said nothing remarkable at all.

The word went through the queue at the door faster than the corn did. No one on the island had been called that since Ailean Mòr fell at the ford six hundred years before; at the Roinn the seven hundred and twelve heirs had sworn, each with some private reservation, that none of them would take it, and in the centuries since, only the reciters of the Mason's line had troubled to remember that the gods had once given the word to Ailean in the grove, and even they had let it go mostly to dust. The heirs of Ailean's blood, all down every one of the nine great shares, had never once offered it to Goraidh, nor to any lord of the Red Share before him. It was the granaries that gave it to him, and the granaries did not ask anyone's leave.

Goraidh heard of it that same evening from the steward, half apologising, as though the man had let something get past him at the door that he should have stopped. Goraidh did not answer at once. He walked out to the granary himself, in the last of the light, and stood by the door a while looking at the grain-dust still hanging in the air of the yard, and when the steward asked him at last whether he wanted the word stopped at the door in future, Goraidh said only that he did not see how a man was to stop a word once nine households had already said it to each other on the road home.

It spread the way such words do among people who have use for them and none for the thing they properly name. By the fourth bad harvest it was not only the hungry who said it at the door; the smiths of the third county said it, and the carriers on the road to Cathair dhomhain said it of him without ever having stood in his hall, and within a generation the lowland households from the Abhainn dhomhain to the old bounds of Donnchadh's county called Dùn dhearg the rìgh's hall as plainly as they called Talla na Lasrach the Hall.

Goraidh never once used the word of himself. He signed his rolls as he always had, Goraidh mac Ìomhair of the Red Share, and when the reciters of neighbouring counties tried it on him to his face, the way a man tries a new coat on another man to see how he wears it, he let it pass without either taking it up or putting it down, which some of his own stewards thought a kind of cunning and others thought a kind of honesty, and were probably both right.

## IV. Of the Climb

He was a hundred and fifty-three years old, by the granaries' own count, though there were men half that age in the hill counties who looked older than he did, when word came down from Dùn ìseal that the council wished to speak with the lord the lowlands had taken to calling their king.

It was not a summons in the way the Keepers had ever summoned anyone before. The council did not raise its voice; it had never needed to, in six centuries of holding the one thing every share on the island had sworn to hold in common, and it did not raise it now. But the word went east plainly enough for all its quiet, and Goraidh, who had by then held four counties for the better part of a century and been called a king by lowland households for the better part of two generations, understood well enough what was being asked of him, and rode west that spring with six men, as far as the last waypoint house on the mountain road.

There he stopped, and gave his spear to the man riding beside him, and told him to wait with the horses.

He had thought about it for three nights before he set out, lying awake in the hall his father and his father's father had held without a stranger's spear ever crossing its threshold in peace, turning the thing over the way his stewards turned a tally-stick to catch the light on a notch. A man who came to the Hall armed had already said, before he opened his mouth, what he wanted; and Goraidh, walking up the last mile of the mountain road on his own two feet with the wind coming down cold off the passes and his breath going short in a way it had not gone short in eighty years, hoped that the council would read the other thing in his empty hands, because he did not entirely trust himself to say it as plainly as he meant it.

The grey roof of Talla na Lasrach came in sight over the shoulder of the hill while he was still catching that breath, and for a moment, an old man on a mountain road with his knees aching and his men and his spear left behind him at the last house, Goraidh mac Ìomhair looked very little like anyone's king at all.

The council sat outside on the stones before the door, for the day was fine, and the Keeper sat among them with the stone cup at her feet, the same cup that had stood on the threshold since Fionnlagh Dall's time, filled always to the two palms and no more. Goraidh stood before them in his travelling cloak with the wind in his beard, an old man by any low-ground reckoning and a young one by his own blood's, and he said the thing he had come up the mountain to say before any of them could ask it of him.

"I claim nothing under the mountain. Not a gallery, not a grain of coal. Let the Hall hear me say it."

The Hall heard him. Nobody answered at once; the wind took the words up over the roof and out along the ridge, and one of the council, an old woman of the Mason's line who had sat on that bench longer than Goraidh had been alive, looked at him a long while before she said anything at all, as if she were counting something on his face that he could not see himself.

"A man who holds four counties and is called a king by nine households in ten does not come up a mountain to tell us what he does not want," she said at last. "Tell us what you do."

So he told her, and the telling of it was most of the day. The roads he wanted plainly, because his granaries already fed the households that walked them, and it seemed to him a poor kind of ruling to feed a man and let some other hand toll him on the way home. The waypoint houses he wanted left to their own keepers, because he had learned enough from Mairead's eighty years of one county to know that a thing gathered by a stranger is gathered worse than a thing gathered by a neighbour with a share in it. Of the galleries, the flame and the cup he asked to have nothing said, in the writing or out of it, because he had watched what four hundred years of the Roinn's quarrelling over the vein had done to every line that tried it, and he had no wish to be the fifth line remembered for trying again.

Then they bargained, and they bargained until the light went.

It was not a quarrel, though it had the shape of one at times, voices raised and let fall again, a councilman getting up to walk the length of the terrace and sit down somewhere else before he spoke his next piece. It was a dividing, done slowly and in the open air because neither side wanted it done any other way, each line of it turned over from every side before it was set down: what a rìgh might hold and what a Keeper must, what roads belonged to which of them and what happened at a boundary between a granary and a gallery, what was owed a household that answered, as so many did along the old bounds, to both halls at once. Goraidh gave up more than he had meant to when he set out, and got less, in the way of standing, than the lowland households already believed he had; the council gave him the roads and did not pretend, by the end of it, that the roads were a small thing to give.

They swore it the next morning, before the council had had time to sleep on second thoughts, on the stone cup itself: the Keeper to keep the flame and the galleries, the rìgh to keep the roads, the granaries and the peace of the lowlands; the rìgh never to set foot in a working gallery, the Keeper never to raise a spear. *Roinn a' Ghleidhidh*, the division of keeping, the council called it before the ink of the roll was dry, and by it, more than by any granary door or any word a hungry household chose to use of him, Goraidh mac Ìomhair became the first ruler since Ailean Mòr that the Hall itself would answer to as a ruler, on the one narrow ground the Hall had left him to be one.

He walked back down the mountain the way he had come, on his own feet, and took up his spear again at the last house without a word, and one of his own men said afterward that the old man had looked, coming down, twenty years younger than he had looked going up, though nobody could say for certain that it was the bargain and not simply the road being downhill.

## V. Of the Timber Hall and the Fish in Twenty

He built the hall the council had, in effect if not in any clause of the writing, told him he had a right to build. It went up on the same rise at Dùn dhearg where his father's turf-and-timber house had stood, a long hall of squared oak with a granary set at each of its four corners, so that a man riding in from any of the four counties saw first, before he saw the door, the thing the hall was actually for. The Red Share's own carpenters cut it, and the three gathered counties' carpenters raised it, working side by side in a way their stewards would not have thought possible ten years before, and when it was roofed Goraidh had the granaries filled from all four counties at once, in the same season, so that no household along any boundary could say its grain had gone first into a stranger's store.

Within the next handful of years, with the roads under his keeping and the Keeper's word behind the division that had put them there, he set the first toll the island had ever known on the carriers who used them: one fish in twenty, or its worth in whatever a carrier's basket held, to pay the guards who kept the road-peace and to mend the bridges the floods still took every few years wherever a crossing had been built too near the water. He did not set the toll himself, at every crossing, with his own hand; he had learned that much from Mairead's eighty years of running one county alone. He gave the gathering of it to the keepers of the waypoint houses along each road, the same households that had kept a bed and a fire for carriers since before his grandfather's day, and let each of them keep a share of what they took, on the plain reckoning that a keeper with something of his own in the toll would gather it more honestly than one gathering only for a stranger at Dùn dhearg. The rolls said so themselves, in their dry way: that the waypoint-keepers kept their share, and the rest went to the guards and the bridges, and the sums, allowing for the ordinary dishonesty of the world, came out close enough to be believed.

At one of the waypoint houses on the northern road, a woman who had kept its fire for thirty years and her mother before her tallied her first season's toll before Goraidh's own steward, sack by sack, and when the count came out a full tenth short of what the steward's own reckoning of the traffic said it ought to, she did not flinch from it. "The carriers who go by night pay nothing," she said, "because there is no one at the door to ask them, and I am not walking a night road at my age to catch a man carrying salt fish." The steward wrote it down as she said it, and took it to Dùn dhearg, and Goraidh, hearing it read out, laughed for the first time in some weeks and said only that a rìgh who wanted every fish in every twenty counted true had better learn to walk a night road himself, and let the matter drop. It was the kind of ruling the lowland households came, in time, to expect of him, and the kind the hill counties, who had never had a rìgh to expect anything of, found faintly ridiculous.

The years after that went the way years go for a man who has already done the one or two things his life was going to be remembered for. He grew old past a hundred and fifty and stayed hale a long while past it, the long years of his blood fading in him the way they had been fading in every line since the Roinn, century by century, so that where Murchadh Mòr had lived near three thousand years and Ìomhar Dearg fourteen hundred, Goraidh mac Ìomhair, seven generations on from the Roinn, counted himself old at two hundred and was right to. His daughter Mòrag, who had grown from the restless child at the ford into a woman who could not sit still in a hall any better than she had ever been able to sit still at a hearth, took over the walking of the roads long before he was willing to call it a handing-over, and he let her, because he had never much cared, himself, for the roads once they were built, only for the fact of their being kept.

He died in his own hall at Dùn dhearg in his two hundred and thirteenth year, in the granary month, with the smell of the new grain coming in through the door he had insisted be left open. Mòrag was with him, and two of the waypoint-keepers who had come in that week to render their tallies and stayed on when they saw how it was going, because a man does not like to walk out on a dying king over a fish in twenty. He said little at the end that the tellers thought worth the keeping; what they did keep was that he asked, the last evening, whether the toll-sticks for the western road had been notched that season, and was told that they had, and said that was well, and did not speak again.

Some days after, at the old waypoint house on the western road that had been Mòr nic Dhùghaill's in the Small-Burning Law's own time and was a waypoint-keeper's still, the keeper of that house sat at her door in the last of the light with a fresh-cut stick across her knees, and notched it once for a carrier's toll paid in fish and once for a toll paid in wool, exactly as she had notched it the week before, and would notch the next one the week after that, the granaries at Dùn dhearg being full or empty, a rìgh being alive in the timber hall or laid in the ground beside it, the road going on being a road either way.
