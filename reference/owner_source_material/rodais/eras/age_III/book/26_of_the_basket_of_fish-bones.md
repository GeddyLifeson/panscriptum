# Of the Basket of Fish-Bones

*FE 915 – 1,009*

## I. Of Sìleas Dhonn's Reckoning

Iain, son of Sìleas Dhonn, was born at Dùn dhearg in the fifteenth year of his mother's long watch over the waypoint-keepers' tallies, and he grew up under a roof where a column of figures was looked at with the same care other households gave a wound. His mother had found, going back through the Ceann mhin rolls in her girlhood, three years in which the keepers' own count did not match what had passed through their gates, and she had never afterward let a tally lie unchecked a season. By the time Iain could walk the length of the hall without a hand to steady him, she was ruler in all but the chair itself, her uncle Dòmhnall Crùbach old and content to let her carry what his leg would not, and the boy grew up at her knee hearing her read out a keeper's account the way other boys heard the old tales.

He was not a quick child with the reckoning itself. What he had, his mother said, laughing at him more than once across the ledgers, was a memory for a face that had lied to her once, and he never forgot one. She set him, from the age of ten, to sit beside her when the keepers came in from the road to give their tallies in person, watching the men who gave them rather than the columns they gave, and more than once it was Iain who said afterward, low, that the keeper of Muileann shean had not looked at her when he named his season's toll, and more than once his mother found, on going back through the man's figures with a harder eye, that he had been right.

He was not gentle with what he found. He had his father's build, broad through the shoulder before he was fifteen, and a laugh that carried the length of a hall and did not always come at the moment a laugh was looked for; the household called him Iain Garbh, Iain the Rough, half in fondness and half in caution, and the name stayed on him the rest of his life. His mother did not check the name. "A rough hand keeps a straight column as well as a soft one," she said, "and better, in a season when a keeper thinks a soft hand can be talked round."

Dòmhnall Crùbach died in the nine hundred and thirtieth year of the Fire, and the household stood the customary night and day with the chair empty before Sìleas Dhonn went in and sat in it, and did not get up again for the better part of a year. She was already past her hundredth year and would rule sixty-four more, and in all that reign, which the keepers came to call, among themselves and not to her face, the long counting, she broke not one column that she found honest and forgave not one that she found false. She widened the toll on nothing and lightened it on nothing; she only made certain that what was owed came in whole, and that what came in whole was written down where a later hand could not soften it. Under her the granaries at Dùn dhearg filled steadier than they had filled under any ruler since Goraidh Mòr first raised the timber hall over the granary door, and the roads Mòrag nan Rathad had cut through the mud two centuries before were kept, by Sìleas Dhonn's order and at her cost, in better repair than the old woman herself might have troubled to keep them once the far stretches stopped catching her eye.

She sent Iain out on the roads himself from his twentieth year, first with an older keeper to mind him and then alone, and he came back from those years with a sunburn that never wholly left his face and a habit, which stayed with him the rest of his days, of counting a granary's sacks with his own hands before he would take any steward's word for the number. He learned the shares that paid their tolls whole without being asked and the ones that paid only when a rider from Dùn dhearg stood at the gate to watch them do it, and among the second kind, in the years his mother still ruled, none was worse than the harbour town in the south-west called Caol leathan.

Caol leathan sat where the western fishing came in thickest and where the carriers' road that ran the length of the island met the sea, and it had grown fat on both, fatter than any town of its size on the coast, under the leading family of the place, whose head in Iain's young manhood was a broad, quiet man named Lachlann Ciar. The town's toll on the road's southern end had been Dùn dhearg's by right since the road-peace was first sworn, but it reached Dùn dhearg thinner every season Iain rode down to collect it, and thinner still the seasons he did not, and Lachlann Ciar's answers, when pressed, were always the same: a poor season on the boats, a poor season on the road, the toll-house roof wanting mending before the toll itself could be counted properly. Iain rode home from his third visit at twenty-six years old and told his mother, plainly, that the man was lying to her face and smiling while he did it, and that he had counted the boats in the harbour himself and there had been no poor season in it that any eye but a liar's could see.

"Then you know him," said Sìleas Dhonn, "and knowing a liar is worth more than catching him once. Wait. He is not the one who will answer for it; his account is not run out yet, and mine has years left in it either way."

Her account had thirty more years in it, and Iain waited them out at her side, counting granaries and watching a man in the far south-west grow fatter on a toll that was not his, and he did not forget the man's face, nor the smiling poverty of his excuses, nor the boats that had not once, in all Iain's visits, been fewer than the harbour could hold.

There was one keeper, in those waiting years, whom Iain never forgot either, though for a gentler reason. The man kept the toll-house at Cnoc dhubh on the eastern stretch of the road, an old fellow with a stammer that grew worse the longer Iain sat across the table from him, and on Iain's second visit the tally came out a full third short of the traffic Iain had himself counted passing the gate that week. He said nothing while the man stammered through his reckoning, and said nothing after, only asked, mildly, whether the keeper's daughter was still down with the fever that had kept the household from the last two fairs. The keeper, startled into honesty by the question, said she was, and that the physician at Muileann shean wanted paying, and that he had thought, God forgive him, to make up the difference out of the toll and put it back before the reckoning was due. Iain rode home and told his mother the whole of it, expecting, he said afterward, to see the man's name struck from the rolls before the month was out. Sìleas Dhonn sent instead a purse for the physician and a letter that named no theft at all, only a debt owed and now discharged, and told her son that a rough hand did well enough on a column that lied for greed, but wanted a softer one for a column that lied for love, and that he had best learn to tell the two apart before he sat in the chair himself. Iain kept that lesson beside the other; it was, he said in his own old age, the harder of the two to hold onto, and the one he was proudest of holding.

## II. Of the Basket of Fish-Bones

Sìleas Dhonn died in her hundred and ninety-fourth year, in the nine hundred and ninety-fourth year of the Fire, sitting upright in the chair with a keeper's tally still open on her knee, and the household found her there in the grey of the morning with the ink not yet dry on the line she had been reading. She had asked, near the end, that the chair stand its customary night and day and no longer, and that whoever came after her not waste a season getting the feel of it, having had, she said, forty years to get the feel of it already at her side.

Iain Garbh was ninety-four years old the night the chair stood open, and he did not stand long in the doorway looking at it the way, it was told, his grandmother's grandmother once had. He went in at first light, sat down, and sent for the year's tallies before the household had finished the mourning-meal.

Caol leathan's account that year showed the same figure it had shown for a decade: a toll paid thin, and an excuse to cover the thinness. Iain read it once, set it down, and sent a rider south with a plain demand, in his own hand and no steward's: the toll owed in full, counted before Dùn dhearg's own man at the harbour, or an answer for why it should not be paid at all.

The rider was gone the better part of a month, longer than the road alone accounted for, and came back at last white about the mouth and would not meet Iain's eye when he set what he carried on the floor of the hall. It was a basket, covered, of a size a fisherman's wife might carry to market, and it had been carried the length of the island roped shut, and the household backed a step from it before Iain had so much as touched the lid.

He lifted it himself. The smell came up first, old and gone dry, and under the smell, packed in rows the way a careful hand packs herring for the keeping, was a basket entirely full of fish-bones, picked clean, not one scrap of flesh left on any of them, and no letter, no word, no answer at all beyond the basket itself and the silence of the man who had sent it.

Iain looked at it a long while. Then he laughed, once, short and hard, a sound that frightened the household a good deal more than shouting would have done, because they had heard him shout before and knew the shape of it, and had never heard this. He called for the road-guards to be summoned from every waypoint within three days' ride, and for the lowland households to be told their levy stood, and he did not raise his voice again the whole of that week, which those who had known him longest found harder to bear than any roaring would have been.

"He has sent me an answer," Iain said, to the steward who asked what the basket meant. "There is nothing left in Caol leathan worth the honesty of a letter, and I believe him."

## III. Of the Flame That Would Not Come

He marched west before the month was out, with the road-guards of the southern waypoints and the lowland levies behind him, and he took the toll-house at Muileann òg that Lachlann Ciar's people had seized from Dùn dhearg's own keeper two winters before, and burned it, standing with his arms folded to watch the thatch go up, in a silence the household about him found harder to read than any order he might have given. It was the first war fought by Dia-thìreach against Dia-thìreach since the Peace of the Threshold had been sworn, and the first, of all the wars the reciters could name before it, fought over a road and not a share; every quarrel before this one had been a quarrel over land, and this was a quarrel over the right to carry a thing across land that both sides agreed belonged to neither of them.

Before he marched further Iain sent to Dùn ìseal for the Hall's blessing on the warband's hearths, as every rìgh before him had done at the raising of a levy, asking no more than the customary handful to light the first night's fires on the road. The messengers came back on the third day with their hands empty and a message they had been made, by the Keeper himself, to repeat word for word before they were let go.

*Cha bhlàthaich an lasair fir a tha a' dol a mharbhadh.* The flame will not warm men who are going to kill.

Uisdean mac Ruairidh had held the flame at Dùn ìseal eleven years by then, a spare, grey-eyed man who had never once, in all that time, refused a rìgh's messenger anything the Hall was able to give, and Iain heard the answer read out twice before he was certain he had heard it whole. He did not send a second messenger to argue it. He stood a while with the paper the first had carried, on which the Keeper's words had been set down by a clerk at Dùn ìseal so there could be no mistaking them later, and then he folded it once and put it inside his coat, and it is told that he carried that same paper, worn soft at the folds, the rest of his life, and would show it, in his old age, to anyone fool enough to ask him why he never once, in fifty years, set foot inside the Hall's own ground.

"Then we'll burn peat," he said, and the warband wintered on peat that year at Muileann òg, coughing through the cold months on smoke that lay low and sour over the camp and would not draw clean the way the Hall's flame drew, and men who had marched south whole came home in the spring with a cough that some of them carried the rest of their days, so that the older road-guards, decades on, could be told from the younger by the sound of a hard winter's night. Iain coughed with the rest of them and never once said, that whole winter or after, that he wished he had swallowed his pride and sent a gentler messenger; and it was in that camp, watching the sour smoke lie over his own men, that the division of keeping was first spoken aloud and understood for what it was: that the rìgh held the spears and the Keeper held the flame, and that neither, whatever the provocation, could any longer lay a hand on the other's.

The camp at Muileann òg was a poor thing to winter in, a scatter of turf shelters thrown up around the burned toll-house with the black timbers still standing over them like a warning nobody had asked for, and Iain walked it every evening of that season with a road-guard named Dùghall at his shoulder, a young man from the eastern waypoints who had never before been further from home than a day's ride and said so, more than once, in the flat voice of a man too cold to be ashamed of complaining. "The Hall would have kept us dry," Dùghall said one such evening, not quite meaning it as a challenge, and Iain, who had not expected to be answered for the choice by anyone under his own command, found he had no readier answer than the one he had already given the steward. "The Hall keeps whoever it likes dry," he said, "and it does not like us this season. Wrap the coat tighter and mind the peat doesn't smother; a smothered fire coughs worse than a good one." Dùghall did as he was told, and lived to be an old man himself, and told the story of that winter, in his own age, to grandchildren who found it hard to credit that a rìgh had once stood over a peat fire at midnight showing a common guardsman how to keep it drawing.

## IV. Of the Ford of the Forty

In the spring the men of Caol leathan met the levies of Iain Garbh at the ford of Àth fhiadhaich, where the road crossed the last river before the harbour town, and held the water against them a full day before they gave way. They had chosen the ground with more care than Iain expected of them, forming along the far bank where the current ran hardest and a man had to fight the water before he could fight anything else, and he heard, before ever he saw the far rank clearly, Lachlann Ciar's own voice carrying across the noise, hoarse already, telling his people to hold the deep water and let the river do half the work for them. It did. The first men across went down as much to the current as to any blade, and Iain, standing in reserve on the near bank with the second rank around him, watched the water take three before a single spear had crossed it, and understood, watching, why his mother had always said a fool fights the ground before he fights the man on it.

Iain went in with the second rank himself, when the first had thinned enough to want him, and he remembered it afterward, in the few tellings he ever gave of that day, less as a battle than as a string of particular things: the cold that took the legs at the first step into the current, worse than any cold he had felt on the winter roads; the noise of it, which was not the noise he had expected, less shouting than a kind of grunting labour, men working at killing the way other men worked at a stubborn stump; and one man of Caol leathan in particular, standing waist-deep with a shield split clean through and still holding it up before him with no sense left in the holding, until a spear thrown from somewhere behind Iain's own shoulder found him and he went down into the water and did not come up where Iain could see.

By evening the men of Caol leathan had given the ford, falling back toward the harbour in good order, and the carriers who came that way named it the ford of the forty, for the number of dead the two sides left in the shallows and on the near bank, and said, in the tellings that grew up around it in the waypoint houses, that the Morrìgan's own crows were down on the ford before the last light left the water, walking among the dead as if the field were theirs before either side had finished claiming it.

Iain crossed the water that night with the rest of the levy and did not look for the man with the split shield among the drowned, though it is told he asked, once, months later, whether any name had been kept for him, and was told none had.

## V. Of Baile ghlas and Baile chiar

Falling back on Caol leathan, Lachlann Ciar's men burned the granaries and the standing grain at Baile ghlas, a lowland town that had sent Iain neither men nor grudge in the whole of the war, so that the levies coming after would find nothing there to feed themselves on. Iain rode through the warm ash the next morning, the roofs still smoking, and found no one left in the place to ask where its people had gone; they had walked north to Cathair dhomhain in the night, most of them, and a good number of those, weakened on the road or taken by the cold that followed, never came home again to see what little was left standing.

South of the bridge-foot, the town of Baile chiar shut its gate against both hosts that same season and would let neither the levies of Dùn dhearg nor the men of Caol leathan inside its walls, and instead opened its own granaries to the burned-out people of Baile ghlas and fed them through the whole of that hard winter on grain the town had meant for its own year. Iain rode up to that gate himself, meaning to ask lodging for a night for no more than himself and a dozen men, and was met on the wall by the town's own reeve, a broad woman named Beathag who called down that the gate opened for the hungry and for no one carrying a spear, rìgh or otherwise, and that if he wanted a roof he could leave the spear on the road below and come up with empty hands like any other man asking shelter of a stranger. Iain looked at the wall a while, and at the smoke of Baile ghlas still faint behind him on the wind, and left the spear where she had told him to leave it, and slept that night on a floor among refugees from a town he had ridden through the ashes of two days before, and never afterward spoke a hard word of Baile chiar in any household where the tale might carry back to it. Neither side forgave the shut gate, each reading it as favour shown to the other, and neither, for all that, ever brought a spear against it; and in the years after, when the carriers spoke of the towns that had kept faith in the War of the Roads, they set Baile chiar's name beside no other town's, because it had kept faith with neither army and with every hungry mouth that came to its gate.

Iain closed Caol leathan by land before that winter was out, ringing the town on every road with his levies, but he could not close what the town's own boats still brought it from the sea. From the camp on the hill above the harbour he watched, night after night, the low dark shapes slip in past his lines at dusk, heavy in the water with fish, and there was nothing a road-guard on a hillside could do about a harbour that opened onto open sea. His levies, drawn from households that had their own harvest waiting, began going home first by ones and then by whole villages as the season turned, and the siege that could not close a harbour ended without an assault, dissolved by an army that simply was no longer there to press it.

## VI. Of the Peace at Mòr nic Dhùghaill's House

The war ended, as every long quarrel on the island's roads had come, in the end, to end, under the roof of a waypoint house bound by the road-peace, where no man might draw a blade on another whatever stood between them outside its door. Iain Garbh and Lachlann Ciar met that autumn at the house that had once been Mòr nic Dhùghaill's, on the northern stretch of the road where the road-peace had first been sworn generations before, and sat down at one board because the roof over them allowed nothing else.

Iain had thought, riding to it, that he would find the fat, smiling liar of his younger years, the man who had sent boats out full while he swore the season poor. He found instead an old man with a cough of his own, thinner than the harbour's wealth should have left him, who ate the jugged buffalo the house was known for without much appetite and did not, for a long while, say anything at all. It was Lachlann Ciar who broke the silence first, saying only that the fish-bones had been his son's notion and not his own, and that he had let it go out under his own name because a father does not send a son's foolishness back to be answered for by the son; and Iain, hearing it, found he no longer much wanted the argument he had ridden south to have.

They talked the rest of that evening as two old men talk who have spent thirty years disliking each other from a distance and find, sitting across one board, that the distance had done most of the disliking's work. Caol leathan kept half its road-toll after that and sent half to Dùn dhearg, and Lachlann Ciar swore to the Small-Burning Law before the witness of a keeper sent down from the Hall for the purpose, the first oath the Hall's own witness had taken from him in twenty years, though Uisdean mac Ruairidh did not come himself, and neither man asked him to.

It left three things behind it, that war fought over a road and ended in a house built to feed the carriers of one. Iain rode south the following spring, at Lachlann Ciar's own asking, to stand at the town gate of Caol leathan while the men who had fought the siege that could not close a harbour swore their new watch upon it: a white stone cut, by a mason of the town, in the rough shape of a wolf standing over what might have been a fish or might have been a lamb, no two tellers agreeing which, and set into the gatepost where every traveller on the road would pass under its eye. The men knelt to it one by one, a hand laid flat on the stone's cold flank, and swore to count a toll true and carry a warning fast and never again send a covered basket in place of an honest word, and called themselves, from that swearing on, the wolf-sworn; and Iain, watching from horseback with Lachlann Ciar's son at his stirrup, said to no one in particular that he would have given a fair sum, in his younger years, to see the day a road-guard of Caol leathan swore anything at all before an honest witness. No waypoint-keeper on the southern road ever again found reason to doubt a toll that a wolf-sworn guard had counted. The harbour town of Caol fhiadhaich, whose boats had fed the besieged town through the whole hard winter Iain could not starve it out, was given by Lachlann Ciar a share of the western tolls in payment, and built with it, in the years after, a harbour wall of its own. And Iain Garbh, riding home past Dùn ìseal for the last time in his life, did not turn in at the Hall's gate, nor ever would again; the folded paper stayed inside his coat, and the flame that would not warm him stayed, by his own choosing, a flame he never again asked to be warmed by.

Lachlann Ciar died eleven years after the peace at Mòr nic Dhùghaill's house, in his ninetieth year, the cough that had sat with him at that last board having worn him down by slow degrees the way it had worn down half the men who wintered on peat smoke at Muileann òg; and word came to Dùn dhearg that his household had buried him within sight of the harbour he had grown fat and then thin defending, with his son standing where he had stood at the peace-board, ready, if the toll ever thinned again, to send an honester answer south than a basket of bones. Iain read the word of it at his own hearth, said nothing for a while, and then went out into a cold rain that had been falling since morning and walked the length of the road above the ford without his staff or his steward, in no hurry at all to come back in.
