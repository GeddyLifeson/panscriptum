# Of the Binding of the First Flame

*FE 1*

> *Cha tèid an fhèith a roinn. Gearr i, agus silidh i fuil.*
> The vein will not be parted. Cut it, and it bleeds.

## I. Of the Keepers of the Slab

*This is told at Tobar dhìreach, where the slab lies, by the households whose turn it is to sit by it.*

Under the king the hill-folk of Dia-thìr had burned an gual dathach by the handful and shared their grain before the storms, and at every gathering they said that they had always been on the island and had come from nowhere. Nine keepers, one drawn by lot for each district of the hills, watched the slab over the crack at Tobar dhìreach where the mason had broken the vein at the beginning of the count. All of it was custom. None of it was law, for there had been no law but the king's word, and the king was in the earth.

Aodh Crom had been drawn for his district so many times that the households had stopped drawing and simply sent him. He was the eldest of the nine that year, and the eldest keeper there had ever been. His people said that some grandmother of his, far back in the long reign, had married a grandson of the king whose name the family did not say, and that a little of na Bliadhnachan Fada had come down to them in the blood, thinned out like a dye in a great vat of water. Aodh had lived a hundred and eighty years and walked with a stick. He had buried three wives and four of his seven children. He was older than any keeper had been before him, and younger than the youngest of the king's sons in the valley, who were none of them less than some centuries old; and he knew both things about himself, and thought about the second more than the first.

When the runners had gone out to every coast, the nine keepers came down from Tobar dhìreach to Dùn ìseal together, walking, with the fire of their hut carried before them in a covered pot.

That fire was the only thing in their charge besides the slab. It had burned in the keepers' hut beside the slab for as long as anyone could remember, fed with a little of an gual dathach each night and never let go out, and the keepers passed it from hand to hand at the change of the lot as they passed the slab. The reciters of Clann a' Chlachair held that it had been lit from the mason's own fire, the fire the lightning had kindled in the broken vein, and carried from hearth to hearth down all the generations until it came to the keepers. The keepers did not say that. They said only that it was old, and that it was not allowed to go out, and they did not quarrel with the reciters, who were welcome to their telling.

They brought it down to Dùn ìseal in the pot because Aodh Crom had told them to. The others had asked him why.

"Because they are going to fight over the hill," he had said, "and I want something in the room that is older than they are."

On the road down the nine keepers passed a great many carriers going up and down between the slab country and the coast, as they always did at that season, with their baskets on their backs; and every carrier stopped and asked them the same thing: whether the handful would still be given out at Samhain. None of them asked who would hold the hill. Aodh Crom told each of them that it would. He did not know it. He said afterward that it was the first promise he made in the matter of An Ceangal, and that he made it to people who had no knot on anyone's cord and no voice in the gathering-house, and that it was the one he minded most about keeping.

## II. Of the First Quarrel

The heirs' first quarrel was over the hill, and it came before any other, because the hill stands over the vein.

They met for it in the long gathering-house below Dùn ìseal on the twentieth day of the month, when the nearer coasts had come in and the farther were on the roads. The house was built for the gatherings of the valley and held some hundreds at a pinch, and that day it held more than it could, and the rest stood outside the doors in the drizzle and passed back what was said. The kin sat on the benches along the walls by the age of their lines, as near as they could agree on it, which was not near. The nine keepers of the slab sat at the back of the house on a bench of their own by the hearth, with their covered pot on the floor between their feet, and nobody paid them any heed.

The king had held the hill of Dùn ìseal in his own hand at the last, and had given it to no one. Every line in the house claimed it, and every line by a different rule.

Murchadh Mòr claimed it by the rule of the eldest. He was the king's eldest living son, close on two thousand nine hundred years old, a heavy slow man with a white beard to his belt and a voice like a door on a cold hinge, and he did not argue his claim so much as stand in it. He said that the eldest son took the father's hearth, as every household in the hills knew, and that the hill was the king's hearth, and he was the eldest son, and there was an end of it.

Eithne nic Ailein rose before he had sat down. She was a daughter of the king, some nineteen hundred years old, small and quick, with a sharp chin and a sharper tongue, and she held the lowland barley-country toward the south sea in all but name. She said that if the hill went to the eldest, then the eldest had only to die for the hill to be parted among his eleven sons, and each of those among his, and that she for one would sooner not live to see it.

"You are not likely to," said Murchadh Mòr.

Eithne's hand went to her belt, and so did the hands of the men behind her; and the men behind Murchadh stood up; and there was very nearly blood on the floor of the gathering-house on the first day. It was Niall Òg, the laughing grandson, who got between them, with his hands open and spread, saying that he had not come all this way to see his aunt and his uncle kill each other before he had been given anything to be killed for. Some laughed. Enough of them sat down.

Then the others began. There were those who claimed the hill by the rule of the living: that the king's children who were alive, and there were more than a score of them, stood before the children of his dead children, whose lines were only grandchildren. The lines of the dead answered that the king had loved their fathers and mothers as well as he had loved anyone in the house, and better than some, and that a grandson of a firstborn stood higher than a fifth daughter. There were those who said that the hill should go to whoever the king would have chosen, and each of those had a different notion of whom the king would have chosen, and a story to prove it. A cousin from the western shore, whose name the tellers have let go, stood up and said that there was no call for all this. The hill could be cut like any other ground. Give each of the great lines a gallery, he said, and let each dig its own, and the one that dug deepest would be the one the vein loved best.

There was a noise in the house at that, half laughter and half a growl. Aodh Crom, on the keepers' bench at the back, looked down at the pot between his feet.

## III. Of What the Old Keeper Said

The heirs argued through that day and into the night, and on the second day, and into the third.

Aodh Crom listened to all of it. He heard the rule of the eldest put twenty times and the rule of the living thirty. He heard the western cousin's galleries taken up by a dozen young heirs who liked the sound of digging for themselves, and heard their fathers shout them down. Once a great-grandson of the king explained at length that he had a better claim than anybody present through a marriage in the second millennium of the reign, and the whole house groaned. Once, late on the second night, when the lamps were low and most of the old heirs were asleep on their benches, he heard a young woman near the door say quite quietly to her neighbour that the king had been dead eighteen days and nobody in the house had yet said his name.

On the third day, toward evening, he got up.

He did it slowly, because of his knees and his stick, and at first nobody noticed. Then the keepers on either side of him stood up too, to help him, and then the whole bench stood, and the heirs nearest the back turned round to see what the old men were doing, and so it went up the house, until the house was looking at him and had fallen quiet, more from surprise than respect.

He was a small man, bent almost double, and his voice was thin. He did not raise it. The heirs at the far end of the house had to lean forward, and they did.

"Cut the vein," he said, "and it bleeds."

He waited, and then he said the rest of it. "You will be parting it for as long as there is any of it left to part. Every one of your children will want a gallery, and every one of theirs. You will be cutting the mountain into slices for the rest of your lives, and your lives are long. And when there is nothing left in it you will part the hole."

Nobody laughed.

Then he reminded them what the keepers of the slab had asked of Catrìona Mhòr in her time, when she held the hill under the king: that the keepers' charge be declared before a whole gathering, so that it should stand on something firmer than custom. Catrìona Mhòr had told them to ask again when the king was gone, since while he lived his word was all the declaring anyone needed. The king was gone. He was asking again.

"And I ask more," said Aodh Crom. "I ask that the vein, and the hill over it, be put into no share at all. Let no one of the king's blood hold it. Not the eldest, not the living, not the dead. Give it to the keepers, who are nobody's, and let the keepers answer for it to the gods."

He sat down. The keepers on either side of him sat down with him.

The heirs were quiet a long while. It was the longest quiet in the gathering-house in three days, and Aodh Crom said afterward that he had counted his own breaths through it, and got to forty.

Then Eithne nic Ailein laughed. It was a short laugh, and not a kind one. "I would sooner nobody had it," she said, "than Murchadh."

Murchadh Mòr turned his great head and looked at her. "I would sooner nobody had it," he said, "than Eithne."

So it went down the house, line by line. The lines of the living would sooner nobody had it than the lines of the dead, and the lines of the dead than the lines of the living. The western cousin would sooner nobody had it than whichever of his uncles would have taken it from him. Ìomhar Dearg, who had sat three days on his stool apart from the others and said nothing, stood up when it came near him and said that he held Dùn dhearg, and that the hill was nothing to him either way; but that he would sooner it went to the old man's pot than to any of his brothers; and sat down. Niall Òg said he would sooner nobody had it than anybody, since he was plainly never going to be given it himself, and at least this way he would not have to watch someone else enjoy it.

Each of them was willing to give the thing away sooner than see a cousin get it. The tellers of Dùn ìseal, who love An Ceangal more than any other tale they tell, admit that this is how it was won.

## IV. Of Murchadh at the Hut

That night, the night between the old keeper's words and the swearing, Murchadh Mòr came up the slope to the keepers' hut alone.

Aodh Crom was sitting by the pot with the lid off, feeding the fire. He heard the heavy step outside and the door-skin pushed aside, and the king's eldest son came in stooping, for the door was low and he was a very big man, and stood a moment getting his breath. Then he sat down on the ground across the fire from the keeper, as a carrier sits, with his knees up.

"You'll not have the hill for yourself," said Murchadh Mòr. "You understand that. None of you. It goes to the fire and not to the man who feeds the fire."

"I understand it," said Aodh Crom.

"Then what do you want?"

The old keeper thought about it. "To die before I see you cut the mountain," he said. "And I am a hundred and eighty, and you are nearly three thousand, so I have the better chance."

Murchadh looked at him. Then, to Aodh's surprise, he laughed, a short creaking laugh like a gate, and put out his hands to the fire.

They sat so a long while. The king's son said nothing for a time, and then he began to talk about his father: how he had ridden with him to Tràigh an t-Salainn in the first thousand years of the reign, when the salt-folk would not kneel, and how the king had gone down alone to the edge of the water and stood there until they came out to him. He said that his father had never told him anything he did not need to know, in two thousand nine hundred years, and that he had thought there would be time.

"I was on my way," he said. "I want you to know it. I was on my way to the ford. I had the spears called up. I was going in the morning, when I had the salt-rights settled with my sister." He looked into the fire. "They are still not settled."

Aodh Crom said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would help, and the king's eldest son knew it. After a while Murchadh got up, stooping, and went to the door, and turned there.

"Tomorrow I swear first," he said. "Because I am the eldest. You will see to it."

"You will swear first," said Aodh Crom.

When he had gone the old keeper sat a long while looking at the door-skin, which was still swinging. The young woman by the gathering-house door had been wrong, he thought. Somebody in the valley had said the king's name at last. It had taken eighteen days, and it had been said in the dark to an old man of no account, where no one else would hear it.

## V. Of the Swearing

They swore it the next day, the twenty-third of the month, three weeks after the king fell. It was the first oath the heirs took, before they had agreed on anything else; and it was the one oath all of them could take, then or afterward.

The keepers had asked for it to be done out of doors, on the slope above the gathering-house where the ground runs up toward the mountain, and the heirs had agreed, since the house would not hold them all and nobody wanted to swear in a room where somebody else had been given the best seat. They stood on the wet slope in the order of their lines, as near as they could agree on it, with their households below them, and the valley people below the households, and the carriers who had come up from the coast with fish and found the whole kingdom on a hillside standing among the valley people with their baskets on their backs.

The nine keepers stood at the top of the slope with the pot. Aodh Crom took the lid off it with his own hands, and lifted out the fire in its dish of clay, and held it up so that the whole slope could see it: a small red fire of an gual dathach, burning low, with a thread of smoke going straight up in the still air.

He said whose it was from then on. He said it was Brìde's, who keeps the hearth and the undying flame, and that it would not be let go out while there was anyone on the island to feed it. He said that the vein under the mountain, and the slab over the first break in it, belonged from that day to Crom Cruaich, the bent one of the mound, whose price is measured, and that what was taken from the vein would be answered for to Crom. He said that the keepers of the slab would keep both, the flame for Brìde and the vein for Crom, and that they would keep them for no one's share and in no one's name; and that no one of the king's blood would ever hold either, though every one of the king's blood might warm his hands.

The reciters of Clann a' Chlachair, standing among the valley people, say that the flame Aodh Crom held up that day had come down from Teine a' Chlachair. The keepers never said so, and they kept the two apart after, as the reciters did too, at the last. Teine a' Chlachair is Teine a' Chlachair, which the lightning lit in the broken vein at the beginning of the count. A' Chiad Lasair is the flame that was sworn to on the slope below Dùn ìseal, and it is from that swearing that An Aois Naomh has its name.

Then the heirs came up the slope one by one to swear, and it took until dark.

Murchadh Mòr came first, as the eldest, and he put his great hand over the fire in the dish and held it there till the heat made him take it away, and said that he would never hold the vein, and went down again without looking at anyone. Eithne came after him and did the same, quickly, and said something to Aodh Crom under her breath that the old man would never repeat. Ìomhar Dearg came in his brown cloak of the east and swore with his left hand, since his right was cut, and nobody asked how. Niall Òg swore, and grinned at the old keeper, and said, "There. Now I've given away something."

The last of the king's blood to come up the slope that day was nobody the heirs knew.

He was a man of middle height, weathered, in a plain hill cloak, and he had walked down out of the high valleys to the west of Tobar dhìreach when he heard the king was dead. His name was Gille-Brìde. He was of the line of Somhairle, the king's son who had lived among Clann na Ceiste by the slab and died long before his father, and who had taken no share of anything, and whose children had kept to the high valleys ever since and asked for nothing. Gille-Brìde was a man of a hundred and seventy-two, which was young for a man of the king's blood and old for anyone else, and he had na Bliadhnachan Fada in him, though they were running out.

He put his hand over the fire and swore that he would never hold the vein. Then he asked Aodh Crom, very courteously, whether he might take a coal of it home.

The keepers looked at one another. Nobody had asked that. Aodh Crom said that he might, since the flame was sworn to no one and so was the one thing on the island that anyone might have a little of. Gille-Brìde took a coal from the dish on the end of a stick, and dropped it into a small clay pot he had carried at his belt for the purpose, and put the lid on it, and went down the slope past the heirs, who stared at him.

"And your share?" said one of them. "You're of the blood. You'll want your knot."

"I've got what I came for," said Gille-Brìde, and he walked back up into the high valleys with the pot under his arm that same night; and no knot was ever tied for him on anyone's cord.

## VI. Of the Night after the Binding

Aodh Crom sat that night in the keepers' hut on the slope above the gathering-house, where they had carried the dish, and fed the flame.

It was a small hut, of stone and turf, with one low door. The flame burned on a flat stone in the middle of it, in its dish, and the old man sat on the ground beside it with a basket of coal and put a little on from time to time with his fingers, as the keepers had always done, and listened to the valley.

The valley was loud. The heirs had gone back to their fires in a good humour for the first time since the king fell, pleased with themselves for having given a thing away, and they were drinking to it. He could hear singing from the upper camps. He could hear Niall Òg's laugh, which carried. Below all of it, down on the valley floor, he could hear the carriers, who had stood all day on the slope with their baskets and now had to go back down to the coast in the dark with them, calling to one another on the road.

After a while another of the keepers came in and sat down across the flame from him. She was the youngest of the nine that year, a woman of the southern districts named in no telling, and she had not said a word all day.

"They'll go back to the hill tomorrow," she said.

"They will," said Aodh Crom. "Tomorrow they'll start on the rest of it. The shares. And they'll count each other, and they'll cut each other, and they'll hate each other for a thousand years, some of them, for they have the years to do it in." He put a coal on the flame. "But not over this."

"And who holds this?" said the woman. "When you are gone? And the rest of us?"

For a while he gave her no answer. He was thinking of the young woman by the door of the gathering-house, on the second night, who had said that the king was eighteen days dead and nobody had said his name.

"Somebody of nobody's blood," he said at last. "They will need to find somebody of nobody's blood. That will be the hardest thing they ever do." And he put another coal on, and the flame took it, and the smoke went up straight through the hole in the roof into the dark over Dùn ìseal.
