# Of the Peace of the Threshold

*FE 556*

*Niall Òg told this at more hearths than any other tale of his long life, and he always ended it the same way: "I have carried a great many things up that path. That was the heaviest, and she weighed nothing at all."*

## I. Of the Hungry Winter

The smoke of Achadh mhòr had not gone out of the sky before the middle of the island understood what it had lost. The two hosts that came home from the burned fields that Lùnastal brought back no grain, for there had been none to bring, and every granary between Dùn dhearg and Cathair mhòr that had counted on Fearghas's short sheaves to see it through the dark months now counted its own stores twice and found them wanting both times. An Earrann Dhearg's granaries were full enough from the east, but the east had spent forty years feeding a war and had little to spare beyond its own households, and Cathair mhòr's plain, which grew less grain than it ate even in a fat year, had leaned on Achadh mhòr's harvest longer than anyone at either hall had cared to say aloud.

By the turn of the year the households of the middle plain were boiling bark with their oats to make the sacks last, and the waypoint-keepers on the road between the two shares had begun to ask carriers for news of grain before they asked for news of anything else. Old men said it was the worst want since Na Linntean Urramach, when the same measure had been mined and burned generation after generation and nobody had thought to ask whether it was still enough for the mouths it fed. Younger men, who had grown up knowing nothing but the burnings, said only that a war fought over granary fields had done what neither side's spears could, and burned the granary itself.

Talla na Lasrach's own rolls, kept on Luchd nan Snaidhm' cords since Sìne nan Snaidhm's day, counted that winter's dead among the dead of the war, not apart from them, for the cold took the old and the very young in numbers no one troubled to count separately from the numbers the burnings had already cost, and there was no one left with the heart to draw so fine a line between a death by the sword and a death by an empty sack. Those who lived through it gave it a plain name and kept to it afterward: the hungry winter, said the same way in every hearth between Dùn dhearg and Cathair mhòr, and no other name was ever offered for it.

At the waypoint house on the road below Muileann chiar, where carriers had once asked first for news of the war, the keeper that season began asking first for news of grain, and kept a slate by his door on which he chalked, and rubbed out, and chalked again, which granaries along the road still had anything to sell and at what the toll-house on either side would let it pass for. A household two days' walk from Achadh mhòr's black fields sent its youngest son round every kinsman it had, near and distant, sworn or unsworn to either share, asking only for seed corn and not for grain to eat, on the reckoning that a family that ate its seed that winter would have nothing to sow when the ground opened again; and it is remembered, in that country, that the boy came home with less seed than he asked for but more than he had feared, gathered a handful at a time from households no better off than his own. It was the kind of winter, the old at Cathair mhòr said afterward, in which a neighbour's door mattered more than either lord's, and in which a good many people who had spent forty years choosing between two halls learned, in a single cold season, how little either hall could do for them once the choosing was over.

## II. Of the Call to the Threshold

It was Ruairidh Glas who sent the first word. He held an Earrann Dhearg at Dùn dhearg by then, an old man himself, though nothing to the age of the woman he was sending to, and the word his runner carried to Cathair mhòr was short: that a war fought for a granary which no longer stood profited no one, that his own people were as hungry as hers, and that he would meet her to say so, if she would name a place neither hall need be ashamed to walk from.

Eithne sent back that she set foot in no hall of an Earrann Dhearg's, as he set foot in none of hers, and that this had been true since before either of them held anything, and was not like to stop being true now. But she named a place all the same, by the same runner, who had to say it back to her twice before she would let him go. There was ground on the island that belonged to no share and never had, ground that every line of the old heirs had sworn to on the same day, before any of them had learned to hate the others: the threshold of Talla na Lasrach at Dùn ìseal, where the stone cup stood, and where seven hundred and twelve of them, in another age, had agreed on nothing except that the vein under the hill should belong to none of them. If the lords of the shares could not go under one another's roofs, she sent, let them stand under no roof at all.

Ruairidh Glas agreed to it, and so, in the weeks after, did every lord and lady left holding a share worth calling one: An Earrann Dhearg out of Dùn dhearg; Cathair mhòr's plain under Eithne; the lesser holdings that had come through the burnings still whole, whose lords had watched the war from the edges and had no wish to be caught in the next one; and the double-sworn, those hundreds of households that had chosen a side, or refused to, and been burned by one or both for it, whose headmen came to Dùn ìseal with nothing left to lose by standing on ground that owed allegiance to neither of the powers that had ruined them.

Not every lord who was asked came willingly. The steward of one of the lesser holdings north of Achadh mhòr sent back word that his lord saw no profit in standing bareheaded before an old woman and an older man to be told what he already knew, that a burned field feeds nobody; and Eithne, when the answer was brought to her, said only that the man could stand at home then, and explain to his own hungry households why the peace that fed everyone else's had passed his door by. He came, in the end, with the rest, a season later than the first runners, and swore the same words as any other, and no one at the threshold troubled to remind him aloud that he had once thought better of it.

The day set was Samhain, the dark of the year, when every hearth on the island burned its own small handful and remembered the night the seven hundred and twelve had first sworn An Ceangal. It was Coimheadaiche na Lasrach who pressed for that day above any other, arguing to both halls, by runners whose words Eithne and Ruairidh Glas each repeated afterward in much the same form, that a peace sworn on the one night every household on the island already kept the same rite would need no explaining to the households themselves; they would understand it the way they understood their own hearths, without a herald's telling. No one troubled to say aloud that this was also the night An Ceangal itself had first been sworn, on that same hill, by heirs who had agreed on nothing but that the vein should belong to none of them. It did not need saying, and by the time the runners had gone the length of the island and back, everyone who mattered to the swearing had worked it out for themselves.

## III. Of the Carrying of Eithne

She had ridden to Dùn ìseal once before, in the first year of Roinn na Rìoghachd, on her own feet and at her own pace, quick and sharp and impatient of the ceremony that had kept seven hundred and twelve heirs sitting in a gathering-house through a whole winter and the summer after it. She did not ride there now. Her legs, which had carried her the length and breadth of the plain for the better part of two thousand years, had stopped carrying her some decades before, quietly, the way a well runs low before it runs dry, and she had accepted the chair the way she accepted most things by then, without complaint and without pretending it was anything other than what it was.

Two of her household carried the poles, one at the front and one at the behind, and the one at the front was Niall Òg. He had come to Cathair mhòr a season before, as he did most seasons by then, for he had outlived nearly everyone else who remembered the valley under Dùn ìseal in the days the tents stood there, and Eithne was one of the last who could still finish his own stories for him when his memory faltered on a name. He was some fourteen hundred and fifty years old that winter, a grandson of Ailean beside a daughter, and young enough beside her that she still called him the boy, as she had at Roinn na Rìoghachd, though his own hair had long since gone the colour of hers.

"You could have sent a chair-man," she said to him, on the second day of the climb up from the plain, when the path had begun to steepen toward the hill. "You could have sent two chair-men and stayed by your own fire."

"I could have," Niall Òg said, and did not slow his pace, and did not answer the rest of it, because they both knew the rest of it, and neither of them had ever found much use in saying aloud what a silence already carried.

They went by the old carriers' road rather than the shorter track over the hill-shoulder, for a chair could keep the road where it could not keep the track, and the going was slow enough in any case that the second bearer, a grandson of one of Eithne's own household some three generations removed and young enough beside the other two that neither of them thought to ask his opinion of anything, had time to point out to her, at every mile, some boundary stone or byre or burned strip of ground he judged she might not have seen from a chair before. She had seen most of it a great many times, from a horse and from her own two feet, in centuries he had no memory of and no grandmother old enough to have told him of either, and she let him point it out to her all the same, because a young man's pleasure in showing an old woman his own country cost her nothing and seemed, on a road with so little else to recommend it, worth the having.

At the first night's stopping-place, a waypoint house on the old road where the keeper turned out half his own stores to feed a party he had not been told to expect, Niall Òg sat by her fire after the household had gone to their own beds and asked her, as he had not troubled to ask in some decades, whether she remembered the valley under Dùn ìseal the first time, when the tents had stood there and the cord had not yet been tied. She said that she remembered the smell of it before anything else, woodsmoke and wet canvas and something under both that she had never found a word for and had stopped trying to, and that she remembered Murchadh's voice before she remembered his face, a door on a cold hinge, he had, and had kept it to the end of his own long life. Niall Òg said that he remembered being called a boy for the first time in his life at that gathering, by men younger than his own father who had somehow contrived to be older than he was in years the island still counted, and that it had taken him the better part of nine hundred years to stop minding it and rather longer than that to be glad of it, since every one of the men who had called him it was dead now, and he was not.

"You are not a boy," Eithne said. "You are the last man on this island who remembers what I remember, and when I am gone you will be the only one, and there is nothing boyish left in that."

He said nothing to that for a while, and mended the fire instead, and when he spoke again it was only to ask whether she wanted another blanket against the cold, which she took as his own way of not saying aloud what her silence, in its turn, had already told him.

She watched the country go by from the chair, the burned black stretch where Achadh mhòr had stood still bare between the rebuilt eastern boundary and the rebuilt western, Clachan-crìche of a hundred small holdings along the road, cut with the marks of shares that were no longer as whole as they had been when the marks were first struck. Two thousand four hundred and fifty-five years she had lived by then, more than any soul then living on the island, and she had watched every one of those stones go up, and the wars that moved them, and she thought, watching the road unwind behind her, that she was tired in a way sleep did not answer, and that she meant this to be the last long road she asked her own legs, or anyone else's, to walk for her sake.

She said none of that to Niall Òg. She had never been a woman who told a man behind her what she was thinking before she had decided what to do about it, and she had not changed that habit for dying, any more than she meant to change it for living a while longer yet.

## IV. Of the Swearing

They came up to the hill of Dùn ìseal on the evening before Samhain, and found the stones before Talla na Lasrach already crowded, for word of the meeting had run ahead of every party that came to keep it, and no lord wished to be the last to arrive at a peace he had asked for himself. Ruairidh Glas was there before her, an old man in a plain cloak with no more men about him than courtesy required, and when her chair was set down he came across the stones himself, without waiting to be sent for, and gave her his arm to stand on, which she took, because her own legs by then needed it and there was no profit in pretending otherwise in front of the one man on the island old enough to remember why they had once been enemies.

"I have not stood this close to you since the ford," he said. He did not say which ford. There had been more than one in their two long lives, but she knew, because she had asked him once, decades before this, whether he still thought of the boy he had killed at Dùn thais, and he had said that he thought of little else on a cold morning, and had never asked her not to mention it again, which she had taken, from a man of his kind, for as near to an invitation as he was able to give.

"Nor I you," she said. "It has taken a burned granary to do it."

"Less than that, in the end," he said. "A winter with nothing in the sacks did it. I have buried more of my own people to this last hunger than to forty years of war, and I expect you can say the same of yours."

She could, and told him so, and they said nothing further to each other that evening, for there was a Keeper to greet, and lesser lords waiting their turn to be seen greeting the two eldest, and the ordinary business of a great meeting that neither of them, after so many centuries of great meetings, had any taste left for hurrying.

Coimheadaiche na Lasrach that year was neither of the two names Talla na Lasrach's own roll remembers best from either side of her, an office between offices, holding the flame in the years after Coinneach Mòr and before the woman who would take it from her; but she held the stone cup at her feet all the same, as every Keeper before and since had held it, and when the lords of the shares gathered on the stones the next morning, in the grey light before Samhain's dark truly closed on the day, it was she who set it down on the worn sill of Talla na Lasrach's threshold and stood back from it, so that no one might say Talla na Lasrach itself had asked for anything from either side.

She had shaped the terms herself, at both halls' asking, for neither Eithne nor Ruairidh Glas had trusted the other's stewards to shape them and neither trusted his own stewards not to shape them a little in his own favour; and it was Coimheadaiche na Lasrach who said them aloud to the gathered stones before any lord was asked to swear to a word of them, so that the crowd standing back beyond the lords themselves, headmen and stewards and a good many households who had walked up from the plain on no better claim than curiosity and hunger for news, might hear the whole of it plainly and carry it home in their own words rather than whatever a lord's own steward chose to tell them afterward. She said them slowly, in the flat voice the office asked of her rather than any voice of her own, and the crowd was quiet enough, in the cold of that morning, that a man at the back of it said afterward he had heard every word of it clear across the stones, though he stood further from the threshold than any lord standing to swear.

Eithne swore first. It had been agreed beforehand, by nobody's authority but the plain fact that she was the last living of the nine who had cut the cord at Roinn na Rìoghachd, and no one at the gathering, not even Murchadh's own great-grandsons who still held the northern share in his name, thought to dispute it. She came forward on Niall Òg's arm rather than the chair, for she judged, and said so afterward to no one but him, that a peace sworn from a chair looked too much like an old woman being carried to her grave, and she meant this peace, if it held, to look like something a living woman had chosen. She laid her palm across the cup's mouth, as the custom asked, and said the words that had been agreed among the lords in the weeks before: that no share should be parted again at a lord's death, but pass whole to one heir named while the lord yet lived, or, failing a name, to whoever Coimheadaiche na Lasrach judged nearest and fittest; that no household should hold an oath to two lords, and any that did should give up one of the two before the spring sowing; and that Clachan-crìche should stand where they stood that morning, on every share and every holding, and whoever moved one after should answer for it at Dùn ìseal.

Ruairidh Glas swore next, and where Eithne had said the words plainly and been done, he stood a moment longer with his hand still on the cup after the words were out, and said one thing more of his own, which no one had agreed beforehand and no one afterward thought to strike from the telling: "I shed the first blood of this at Dùn thais, over cattle on a wet slope, when I was young enough to think a stone worth a life. I am old enough now to know what I bought with it, and I would not buy it again for twice the grazing on the island." He took his hand from the cup, and no one answered him, for there was nothing owed in answer, and the lords who came after him to swear, one by one down the line of the shares, great and small, said only the words that had been agreed, until the sun that had barely cleared the eastern ridge when Eithne began was well up and warm on the stones by the time the last of the double-sworn headmen had sworn his own turn and stepped back.

So, on the day the island still calls Sìth an Stairsnich, the lords of the shares ended Aimhreit nan Oighrean, some five hundred and fifty years after Ruairidh Glas's spear had gone into Aonghas Bàn on the wet slope above Dùn thais. No one who stood on the stones that morning troubled to reckon the years aloud. It was Niall Òg, telling it afterward at his own hearth and at every other that would have him, who did the reckoning, and who first said the number to anyone who had not already worked it out for themselves.

## V. Of the Double-Sworn

The clause that had already remade the shape of the island, holding a share whole from one lord's death to the next heir's swearing, cost the shares themselves the least grief that winter. The clause that cost the most was the plainer one, that no household should answer to two lords, and that whoever did must choose before the spring. There were still some hundreds of such households scattered along the old boundary lines, most of them families who had married across a share's edge two or three generations back and never thought the double oath worth troubling over until the burnings taught them otherwise, and every one of them now had a winter to decide which hall it would keep faith with and which it would let go.

Some chose the way Achadh mhòr's neighbours had chosen years before, for the same reasons: nearness, an old debt of kindness, a steward who asked civilly rather than one who threatened. Others chose against their own nearest interest, out of a grief that was still raw enough to want no further dealing with whichever hall's riders had done the burning in their own valley, whatever the cost of refusing it. A few, it is told, chose by no reckoning at all, but by a knucklebone thrown at the hearth on the night before the deadline, judging, as one such headman is remembered saying, that a knucklebone at least bore no grudge and asked for no bribe, which was more than either hall's steward could truthfully claim.

One such household, a holding on the old boundary a half-day's walk east of Achadh mhòr's black fields, had been sworn twice since a grandmother's grandmother's marriage, and its headman that winter sat by his own hearth three nights running, saying nothing to his wife or his grown sons beyond that he was thinking, until the eldest son, who had lost a brother to the burnings and had no patience left for his father's thinking, asked him outright which hall had ever once sent grain to their door in the whole of the hungry winter just past. The old man said neither had, and the son said that this, then, was the whole of the answer, and by the son's own account, given many years after at his own hearth in his turn, that was the reckoning that decided it: the plain memory of which door nobody's carrier had knocked on when the sacks ran low. The household kept the oath that had cost it least, and let the other go before the first thaw, and was neither the first nor the last on the island to reckon its loyalty that way that winter.

What none of them did, that whole winter, was burn a neighbour's roof for choosing wrong. Sìth an Stairsnich held that first test better than either the lords who had sworn it or the households bound by it had quite dared to expect, and by the time the spring sowing came the boundary lines that had shifted and blurred through forty years of burning had settled again into a shape that would hold, with only small mendings, for longer than any lord who swore that Samhain morning would live to see.

## VI. Of the First Market

In the spring after Sìth an Stairsnich, Talla na Lasrach opened its ground under Talla na Lasrach to a market on the four quarter days, Samhain, Là Fhèill Brìde, Bealltainn and Lùnastal, when the households of the eastern hills came up to pay their dues and found, waiting for them under Talla na Lasrach's own shadow, traders from both the shares that had so lately burned each other's granaries, laying out hides and venison and charcoal and the silversmiths' new weights from Muileann chiar side by side on the same trampled grass. The first of those markets was a thin one, for the hungry winter had left little enough to trade, and more than one household came up the hill with nothing to sell and stood about the edges only to watch whether a trader of an Earrann Dhearg and a trader of Cathair mhòr's plain could truly stand at the same stall without a steward from either hall coming to part them. None came. Coimheadaichean na Lasrach allowed no coal to be sold on Talla na Lasrach's own ground, then or ever after, and none was; but everything else the island grew or made or dug changed hands there on the fair weights, and by the third quarter day the households who had only watched the first were bringing their own baskets, and within a few years no one thought it worth remarking that a trader of one share should haggle over the same measure of hides with a trader of the other within a spear's throw of the cup that had lately parted their two hosts.

Eithne lived to be carried up the hill to a great many more of those markets than her household thought wise; Ruairidh Glas did not, whose own years, long as they were beside any ordinary man's, ran out within two generations of the swearing. But the stones they had sworn to leave standing stood, boundary after boundary, along every road a carrier walked between the shares, cut with the marks of lines that had once counted their honour in spears and now counted it, more quietly, in whether a stone had moved. In time the carriers who passed them going to and from the quarter-day market stopped troubling to read the signs cut on their faces at all, for the stones had stood so long, and the peace they marked had held so plainly, that a boundary no longer needed reading to be believed.
