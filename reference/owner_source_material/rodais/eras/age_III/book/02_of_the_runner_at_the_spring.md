# Of the Runner at the Spring

*FE 1*

## I. Of Sìne and the Cord of the Households

*At Dùn ìseal the knot-keepers tell this, and they tell it with a cord across their knees.*

Sìne nan Snaidhm kept the cord at Dùn ìseal in the year the king fell. It was the cord of the households, of the kind Catrìona Mhòr's people had first made: a long rope of twisted flax, grey with handling, with a knot in it for every hearth in the valleys about the hill. A birth was a small knot tied beside its hearth's knot, a death was a small knot untied, and a household that went away over the hills was cut out and the two ends spliced. Sìne could run it through her fingers in the dark and tell you who lived at the head of every glen from the slab to the salt road, and how many mouths they fed, and which of them had burned their measure early the winter before.

She was of no one's blood that mattered. Her mother had kept the cord before her, and her mother's mother, and nobody could remember why it had gone down that way and not through some better family. She had the tying of it because her fingers were quick, her memory was good, and she could not be bribed, having nothing that she wanted. She had a house of turf and stone below the spring, a husband who cut peat, two daughters who were learning the knots, a goat, and the cord. The cord lived in a basket by the hearth with a lid on it, and when there was news in the valley people came to her door to see it written.

That summer the valley was full of the king's kin. They had been coming in since the spring, from every coast of the island, to wait about the king for their shares, and there were so many of them now that the cord of the households had nothing to say about them. Each of them was a fire and no household: there was a fire for every son and daughter of the king who had come, and every grandchild, and every great-grandchild, and all the fires down the length of the valley had stewards to argue for them and servants to fetch their water and grievances against the fire next door. Sìne fetched her own water, and on most evenings she was the only woman at the spring who did.

The king had gone down to the ford seven days before the runner came. Everybody knew he had gone. The talk at the spring was that the spears of Dùn dhearg were out, and that the old man had taken a handful of his own people and gone to meet them, and that the kin would follow him in a day or two when the grazing and the salt and the shore-rights were settled. Sìne had heard it every evening. On the second day the smoke of many fires had stood over the low ground to the east, and nobody moved. Two days after the fighting stopped, a great smoke went up over Dùn dhearg itself, a black smoke that leaned north in the wind, and the fires in the valley took it for the host of the east coming on at last, and there was a great running about and arming, and stewards shouting at each other about whose spears should stand at the head of the road. Then nothing came, and the smoke thinned, and the kin put their spears away again and went back to arguing.

## II. Of the Herd-Boy

She was at the spring with her pails on the fourth evening after the battle when the runner came up the valley road.

He was a herd-boy of the eastern hills, barefoot, in a shirt that had been white at some time and was now the colour of the heather. He came up the road at a run that had gone past running, the run of a body that has forgotten how to do anything else, and he had run so far that he could not stop when he reached the spring. He went on past it three or four steps, and then his legs folded and he sat down in the mud with a grunt, as if someone had pushed him.

Sìne put her pail down beside him and gave him water in her cupped hands, because the pail was too heavy for him to lift. He drank half and was sick, and drank the rest.

"Where is the camp of the king's son?" he said. "Any of them. I was told to tell the king's sons."

She looked down the valley at the fires, which were being lit for the evening, one after another, all the way to the bend of the road and past it.

"There are sixty camps in this valley that call themselves the king's sons," said Sìne. "Tell me, and I'll tell them."

His name was Donnan, and he came from the high grazings north of the ford, where his people had summer shielings. He had been with the cattle on the ridge above the river when the host of Dùn dhearg came down the valley, and he had stayed there, because the cattle could not be left and because he wanted to see. He had seen all of it. For three days he had lain in the heather on the ridge with a crust in his shirt and watched.

He told her, sitting in the mud, in the plain words of a herd-boy, with his teeth going. The king had held the ford alone, three days and three nights. The host had come at him through the water and across the deep channel above it, all the first day and all the second, and the dead had piled at the shallows until the river backed up behind them and spread out over the meadow. On the third day every spear of the east had gone into the river together, and had kept going in, and there had been a light round the king in the afternoon that the boy could not describe, and did not try to twice. On the third night the king went down. No one man had done it. They had come at him until there were no more of him to come at.

"I would have come the straight road," he said, "but they are all over it. The host. Those that are left of them. And then there were other spears coming down off the hill in the morning, the day after, and I didn't know whose they were, so I went round. Over the tops. I have been two days coming over the tops."

"Whose were they?" said Sìne. "The spears in the morning?"

"I don't know. They came from this side. They killed the rest." He looked past her at the tents, all down the valley, and the smoke of the cooking fires going up straight in the still evening, and a woman laughing somewhere, and a dog. He had come a long way. "Where were his kin?" he said. "The king's kin. Where were they?"

Sìne did not answer that. She picked up the boy by the arm, and left her pails at the spring, and took him up to the tents.

## III. Of the Question in the Valley

She remembered afterward, and told it to her daughters, and they to theirs, that the first thing said when the news went into the camps was a question about the hill.

The nearest fire-circle was where she took him, because it was the nearest, and because she did not know which of the sixty was the right one and did not believe that any of them was. A steward in a blue cloak came out to see what the knot-woman wanted, and she told him to hear the boy, and he heard him through. He was a man of middle years, clean-shaven, with good shoes, and he heard the whole of it without a word, looking at the boy's feet. When it was finished he turned round to the men behind him at the fire, and he said:

"Who holds the hill now?"

Sìne watched that question go down the valley faster than the grief did. It went from fire to fire as a spark goes along dry heather. She saw men get up from their suppers and walk quickly to the next fire, and men come out from the next fire to meet them; she saw stewards running, and a boy sent up the slope toward the upper camps at a gallop on a pony; she heard the question asked at a dozen fires before she heard anybody weep. For a while nobody keened at all. The valley was full of voices, and all the voices were talking about the hill.

Then a woman in one of the upper camps began to keen alone.

She was a daughter of the king, and her name was Mairsile. She was so old that her hair had gone white and then gone thin, and she had come in from the north coast in a litter that spring because she could no longer ride, and she had sat all summer at her fire in the upper valley with a blanket over her knees and taken no part in the arguing. Her keen was thin and cracked, like the voice of a very old bird, and at first it only made the voices below it louder. But it did not stop. After a while the fire nearest hers took it up, and then the next; and it came down the valley from fire to fire as the question had gone up it, slowly, until the whole of the valley was crying in the dark, the old and the young, the stewards and the servants, the sons and daughters and grandchildren of the king; and the herd-boy with it, sitting on the ground by the fire of the blue-cloaked steward with his face in his arms.

Sìne did not keen. She stood a while and listened, and then she went back down to the spring, where her pails were still standing in the dark, and carried them home, and put the lid more firmly on the basket where the cord was. Her husband asked her from the bed what the noise was. She told him the king was dead, and that the kin were asking who had the hill. He said nothing for a while, and then he said that the peat would want cutting the same, whoever had it; and she lay down beside him and did not sleep.

## IV. Of the Bier Coming Up

The bier came up the valley road three days after the boy.

Sìne was at the spring again, because the water had to be fetched whatever happened, and she saw it come round the bend of the road below, slowly, with the evening sun behind it. It was a strange long thing, black and bristling, and at first she did not know what it was. Then she saw that it was made of spears: two long spears for the poles and a lattice of shorter ones lashed across them, with cloaks laid over the lattice, and on the cloaks a man lying.

Twelve men carried it, men of the east and none of the valley, in the rough brown cloaks of the eastern barley-country, and some of them had cuts on their arms and faces that had not healed. Before them walked a few men with green branches, as the old custom was for a death on the road, and behind them walked two old men, tall, with grey heads, who she knew were sons of the king because she had seen them at the gathering-house door; and behind those came the spears of their households, who had ridden out that morning to meet the bier and were now walking home behind it.

The heirs came out of the tents on both sides of the road to see it pass. Sìne stood among them with her pail.

She had seen the king three times in her life, and each time far off: a tall man on a horse at the edge of a gathering, with people falling back from him on either side. She saw him now close to, for the first time and the last. He had not grown old. His hair was grey, as it had been since before anyone in the valley could remember, but his face was a young man's face, and his eyes had been closed. The wounds were all in front. There were a great many of them, and none of them, to look at, seemed enough.

The bearers were thirsty. She could see it in their mouths. None of the heirs had thought to give them anything, and the bearers would not ask. When the bier came level with the spring she stepped out into the road and gave each of the twelve water from her pail, in a cup she carried at her belt, one after another as they walked, without stopping them. None of them looked at her. The last of them, a grey-bearded man with a bad hand, said "Thank you," very low, in the speech of the east. She did not ask who they were. She thought that she knew.

The keening began again as the king went by, at the upper camps first, where Mairsile's fire was, and ran down the valley to the bottom of the road. Sìne walked beside the bier as far as the long gathering-house below the hill. There the old keepers of the slab were waiting, nine of them, on the bench before the door, and they stood up and took the king from the twelve men of the east, and the twelve were given bread and sent away.

As they went a young man of the kin, standing at the gathering-house door with his thumbs in his belt, asked loudly whether Ìomhar Dearg had come up with the bier. When he heard that Ìomhar had not, he said, "He has found something to hold, then," and laughed. Some of the kin about him laughed too. Sìne knew him. He was a grandson of the king, some nine hundred years old, who laughed at most things, and whom the kin called Niall Òg, Niall the Young; and she saw that when the others had stopped laughing he went on looking after the twelve men of the east, down the road, and did not laugh any more.

## V. Of the Two Mounds

Where they laid the king, the tellers of Dùn ìseal and the tellers of the Mason's line do not agree, and each has a mound to show.

This much all agree on. He lay for three nights in the gathering-house, on the lattice of spears, with the keepers of the slab sitting by him in turn and the kin coming in to look at him and going out again. On the fourth morning he was carried out before dawn, with the nine keepers walking before and his two grey sons behind, and a few of the eldest of the kin; and he was laid in the earth that same day, and a mound raised over him, and nobody who was not there saw it done.

The tellers of Dùn ìseal say that they laid him on the slope of the hill, on the side that faces the vein, with his face toward the mountain, as the keepers of the slab were laid. There is a long low mound on that slope, grown over with heather, and the knot-keepers of Dùn ìseal will take you to it if you ask.

The tellers of the Mason's line say otherwise. There was a man of Clann na Ceiste in the valley that year, a reciter from Tobar dhìreach named Seathan Crom, who had walked down to Dùn ìseal at midsummer to see the king once more before he died, for he was an old man and the king had been kind to his father. He was lame in one leg, which is why they called him Crom, and he was at the gathering-house when the bier came in. The reciters of the Mason's line say that it was Seathan who went to the grey sons on the third night and reminded them of what the king was: that he had been crowned in a grove by the gods themselves and was the heir of the mason's fire, and that he should lie where the fire had first come to his people, and not on a hillside among keepers. They say the grey sons heard him, and that the bier went out before dawn by the north road and not by the slope; and the reciters of the Mason's line have a mound of their own to show, older-looking, in a place they will name only to their own.

Sìne was not there, and she said so to her daughters. She said that she had seen the bier go out of the gathering-house in the dark, and the lamps go with it, and she had not followed. She said that when the grey sons came back in the evening they were very tired and their shoes were wet, and that the shoes might have got wet on either road.

The knot-keepers who tell this tale after her leave the matter there.

## VI. Of the Lord of Dùn dhearg

On the day after the burial, Ìomhar Dearg came up the valley road.

He came riding, which nobody did in that valley any more except the king, and he came with his household behind him, two hundred spears or more, all in the rough brown cloaks of the east. The valley had heard by then whose spears had come down off the hill on the morning after the ford, and what they had done, and what they had done after that at Dùn dhearg. The heirs came out of their tents to see him pass as they had come out to see the bier, and they did not keen this time. They stood in silence on both sides of the road, and Ìomhar Dearg rode up between them, looking at nobody, a big red-bearded man on a big horse, and got down at the gathering-house door.

Sìne was inside, because she had been sent for. The keepers of the slab had asked her to sit at the back of the house with the cord of the households, in case there was need to say who lived where. She saw him stand in the door with the evening light behind him, and she saw the talk in the house stop as if a knife had cut it.

"I have come," said Ìomhar Dearg, "as the lord of Dùn dhearg."

Nobody said anything. It was the eldest of the keepers of the slab, an old bent man named Aodh Crom, who stood up at last from the bench by the hearth and said that the lord of Dùn dhearg was welcome under that roof, as anyone of the king's blood was welcome, and that he might sit.

Ìomhar Dearg sat. He did not take off his cloak. He sat a little apart from his brothers and sisters, on a stool his own steward carried in for him, and he said nothing more that evening. But Sìne, who watched him from the back of the house with the cord in her lap, saw that the others kept looking at him when they thought he did not see; and that when they spoke of the shares, as they were already speaking of them, they spoke of them around him, as a stream goes round a stone.

## VII. Of the Runners to Every Coast

That night the heirs sent to every coast.

There were kin of the king who had not come in that summer: some who were too old, some who had quarrelled with the others, some who had held that there was no hurry, since the king had lived four thousand years and would plainly live four thousand more. Now there was a hurry. Runners went out of the valley before dawn by every road, north over the high passes, west toward the lake shore and the salt, south to the barley-country and the southern sea. They went in pairs, one from each of two fires that did not trust each other, so that neither fire could send a message the other had not heard. There were so many pairs of runners on the roads in the week after that the carriers coming up from the coast to the slab with their baskets of fish thought there had been a war.

The kin who had come in waited. They buried nobody else, and they did not go home. They sat at their fires and argued about who held the hill, and who should, and by what rule, and they began to count one another.

The counting was the trouble. Every fire in the valley had its own reckoning of how many of the king's blood there were, and every reckoning left out somebody that another fire said was owed, and put in somebody that another fire said was nobody. The stewards went from fire to fire with notched sticks and came back with different numbers. Nobody believed anybody else's sticks. A steward of the salt shore came to blows with a steward of the western lake over a great-grandchild whom one of them counted twice and the other not at all, and the child's mother, when they asked her, said that she had four sons and had not come all this way to be counted as half of one.

Three nights after Ìomhar Dearg came up, there was a knock at Sìne's door, late, when the fire was banked and the children asleep. She opened it and found Aodh Crom there, the eldest of the keepers of the slab, leaning on his staff in the dark, with the rain running off his hood.

He was older than any keeper of the slab had been before him, and he looked it. His people said that there was a thin strain of the king's long years in them somewhere far back, through some marriage in the old days that nobody would name, and he had outlived three wives and most of his grandchildren. He had sat on the bench by the slab at Tobar dhìreach for longer than Sìne's mother had been alive. She had never had him at her door before.

"I want a new cord," he said.

"The households are counted," said Sìne. "I have them all. You saw them tonight."

"Not the households," said the keeper. "The heirs. They will want to know how many there are before they start. And they will not believe each other."

She looked at him a long time in the dark doorway, with the rain coming down between them. Then she said that she would need flax, a good deal of it, and a place to sit where every one of them would have to come to her, and not she to them.

"The door of the gathering-house," said Aodh Crom. "They will all come through that."

"And who will they believe," said Sìne, "when they won't believe each other?"

"You," said the old man. "Because you want nothing." He pulled his hood forward over his face. "And because none of them will have thought yet that you are worth the trouble of buying."

He went away up the path in the rain. Sìne shut the door and stood a while in the dark by the banked fire. Then she took the lid off the basket, and took out the cord of the households, and began to run it through her fingers, knot by knot, from the slab to the salt road; and she was still at it when the grey came into the smoke-hole at dawn.
