# Of the Stones and the First Blood

*FE 2 – 4*

> *Mharbh mi mìle bliadhna.*
> I have killed a thousand years.
>
> — said by Ruairidh Glas of the line of Ìomhar, kneeling in the mud at Dùn thais

## I. Of the Stone on Each Bank

Ruairidh Glas was a grandson of Ìomhar Dearg, some three hundred years old that autumn, and he had never in his life killed a man.

He had not been at the ford the autumn his grandfather took Dùn dhearg; he had been in the western grazings with an Earrann Dhearg's cattle, and what he knew of that morning he knew from other men's mouths. Since then he had grown up in the granaries and the cattle-yards of an Earrann Dhearg, learning what a grandson of the man who held it by the sword was supposed to know, which was mostly where the boundary of a thing ran and how to hold it, and he had come to think of himself, without ever quite saying so, as a man who held ground rather than a man who fought for it. He was wrong about that, though he did not yet know it, any more than Niall Òg on Oidhche an Dòrlaich had known he was wrong to think the cutting of the cord was the end of the trouble rather than its beginning.

The cord was cut, and had been cut for a year by the time Clachan-crìche went up. It had taken that long for the nine lines to agree even roughly where their shares began and ended on the ground, because a line drawn on a cord with a knot is one thing and a line drawn across a hillside with cattle on it is another, and the two did not always answer to each other as neatly as the heirs at the gathering-house had supposed they would. Through the winter after the handful the stewards of the nine lines rode the boundaries with the reciters who had carried the tale of the cutting in their heads, arguing over fords and ridgelines and the exact course of streams that moved their own banks between one flood and the next, and where they could agree a mason cut a stone with the sign of the share that looked into it and set it up on the line.

They called them *clachan-crìche*, Clachan-crìche, and there came to be a great many of them before the winter was out, more than the stewards themselves could always account for, because a lord's man who did not like where a stone stood would sometimes move it in the night, a hand's breadth or a field's width, and set it again facing the same way as before, so that in the morning it looked as though it had always stood there. The keepers of the slab, who had no share and wanted none, said nothing about this, because it was no business of Talla na Lasrach's what the heirs did with their own boundaries, so long as none of it came near the ground under the mountain. Aodh Crom, when a steward once asked him to witness where a stone had truly stood the week before, said only that he had not been watching, and that he did not think any man alive had, which was as near as the old keeper ever came to naming what everyone already knew.

Dùn thais stood on its hill north of Dùn dhearg, the first fort the island's people had ever banked away from the mountain and An Cnoc Ruadh both, in years so far back that the reciters could put no king's name to the raising of it. It sat close by the line the cord had drawn between Earrann Mhurchaidh, which was Murchadh Mòr's, and an Earrann Dhearg of Ìomhar Dearg, and it sat close enough that nobody could say with any confidence on which side of the line it truly fell. The stewards who rode that stretch that winter argued it for the better part of a month and came away no nearer agreement than when they had started, and in the end, because neither side would give way and neither side would let the fort stand for the other, the masons of both lines did the one thing that let each of them go home believing he had lost nothing: they set a stone on the north bank of the burn below the hill, cut with the sign of Murchadh's share, and another on the south bank, cut with the sign of an Earrann Dhearg, and left the fort, and the slope above it, lying between the two.

Ruairidh had ridden that ground twice that winter with his grandfather's steward, an old man named Lachlann Glas who had held the same office under Ìomhar since before the ford and had grown grey in it, and both times Lachlann had stood a long while looking at the two stones and said nothing worth remembering. The third time Ruairidh rode it, in the wet spring of the third year of An Aois Naomh, he rode it alone, with twenty men of an Earrann Dhearg behind him and the cattle of the northern slope in front, because his grandfather had said, over supper the week before, in the flat voice he used for things he did not expect to be argued with, that the grazing above Dùn thais was theirs and had always been theirs, whatever a stone on the wrong bank of a burn might say, and that it was time somebody drove the beasts down off it before another season's grass went to a share that had never paid for it.

## II. Of the Rain at Dùn thais

It rained the whole of that day, the low, steady rain of early summer that does not so much fall as hang in the air and soak a man through by evening whether he has stood still or ridden hard, and by the time Ruairidh came up under the hill of Dùn thais the ground on the northern slope had gone to mud past the fetlock, and the cattle he had come to move were bawling and slipping and turning back on themselves faster than his twenty men could turn them again.

Aonghas Bàn mac Mhurchaidh was on the bank above with twenty men of his own, and Ruairidh did not at first know why. He had ridden that slope twice before without meeting a soul of the northern share on it, and it did not occur to him, coming up through the rain with his mind on the cattle, that word of his grandfather's supper-table decision could have crossed the boundary faster than he had. It had. Somebody in Ruairidh's own household, or in Lachlann's, had said something to somebody who ought not to have heard it, the way such things always went in a country that had nine great households and every one of them full of cousins married into every other, and Aonghas Bàn, a grandson of Murchadh Mòr some four hundred years old, had come up with his own herdsmen the night before to hold the ground his own grandfather had said was theirs.

He stood above the beasts with a herding-stick in his hand, not a spear, because he had come to move cattle, not to fight for them, and Ruairidh saw him first as a shape on the bank in the rain, grey against grey, and did not know for a moment whose man he was. Then Aonghas Bàn called down, not unkindly, that the grass under Ruairidh's cattle belonged to the north, and that he would be glad to help drive them back across the burn if Ruairidh's men were finding the going hard in the wet.

It might have ended there. Ruairidh said afterward, many times, to anyone who asked him and to a good many who did not, that it might have ended exactly there, with two grandsons of two brothers standing in the rain arguing amiably enough over a hillside neither of them had ever slept on, and going home each to tell his own household that the other side had been reasonable, for northerners, or for an Earrann Dhearg, whichever way the telling ran. It did not end there, and the reason it did not, as near as Ruairidh could ever afterward make out, had less to do with the grass than with the twenty men behind each of them, none of whom had ridden up that hill expecting to be reasonable, all of whom had grown, in the year and a half since the cutting of the cord, thoroughly used to hearing that the other lines were thieves and liars and worse, and none of whom, when the shouting started somewhere down among the cattle, over whose beast had trodden whose man's foot, waited to hear whether their own lord meant to answer it with words.

Ruairidh's spear was up before he had decided to raise it. He said that afterward too, more than once, in the years he had left to say it in: that his hand had done it before the rest of him had caught up, the way a man's hand closes on a hot stone before his mind has finished telling him it is hot. He did not remember aiming. What stayed with him was the shape on the bank turning toward the shouting below, the herding-stick still in his hand and not a weapon raised anywhere near him, and the spear going, and then, after it had gone, the knowledge that there was nothing in the world he wanted less than to have thrown it.

Aonghas Bàn went down into the mud without a sound that carried over the rain and the cattle and the shouting, and for a moment neither side seemed to understand what had happened, so that the noise of the quarrel went on a little while over his body before it died away into something worse than quiet, a kind of ragged stillness broken by the bawling of beasts that did not know or care what the men around them had just done.

Ruairidh came down off his own horse and knelt by him in the mud. Aonghas Bàn had the face of a man of thirty, though he was four hundred years old and had, by na Bliadhnachan Fada of his own line, perhaps a thousand more before him that he would never now come to; and Ruairidh, kneeling there with the rain running down both their faces, one living and one not, said the same six words over and over to the men who came to lift the body, because it was the only true thing he could find in himself to say and no other words would come in its place.

"I have killed a thousand years," he said. "I have killed a thousand years."

## III. Of Eachann Ruadh's Oath

They carried Aonghas Bàn down off the hill in a cloak between two spear-shafts, the rain not yet finished with them, and word went ahead of the party faster than the men carrying him could travel, the way it always does when a man dies who was not expected to, so that by the time they came within sight of his own hall the household was already out at the gate, and his brother Eachann Ruadh was already standing at the front of it, having heard, and not yet having been told, and already knowing from the shape of the party coming down the road what he was about to be told.

Eachann Ruadh was a year or two the younger of the two brothers, red-haired where Aonghas had been fair, and he had been, until that afternoon, the quieter of the pair by a wide margin, the one who let Aonghas do the talking at councils and the deciding at boundaries and was glad enough to let him, having, as he said of himself more than once, no great appetite for either. He stood at the gate while the party came up, and he did not go to meet it, and he did not ask the question everyone standing near him could see forming on his face, because by the time the two spear-shafts were near enough for him to see what lay between them, there was no longer any question left to ask.

He knelt by his brother's body in the yard of their own hall, in front of the whole household and in front of Ruairidh's men, who had carried it down themselves and would not have trusted the carrying to anyone else, and he put his hand flat on the dead man's chest, over the wound the spear had made, and swore an oath there that the reciters of both lines kept afterward, word for word, though they never agreed between them on quite what it meant to keep an oath of that kind. The blood on the grass at Dùn thais, he swore, would be answered, blood for blood, by his own hand or his sons' or theirs after them, for as long as there was a man of Murchadh's line living to remember whose spear had thrown it; and he did not name Ruairidh Glas in the oath, though everyone standing in that yard understood well enough whom it was aimed at, because to name a man in a blood-oath was, by the old custom of the households, to close the door on any settling of it that did not end in a second death, and Eachann Ruadh, red-haired and quiet and not yet altogether sure, in that first grey hour, what kind of man his brother's dying had made of him, left the door open that far and no further.

Ruairidh did not go back to Dùn thais. He rode home to his grandfather's hall with his twenty men in a silence none of them broke the whole way, and he told Ìomhar Dearg what had happened in as few words as Eachann Ruadh had used to swear his oath, and his grandfather, who had taken a fort by the sword and burned the halls inside its double bank and thought little enough of a single death on a wet hillside, told him only that he had done what a man does when his own are threatened and that he was not to trouble himself with an oath sworn by a boy over his brother's body. Ruairidh did not answer that either way. He was three hundred years old and had, by his own line's long blood, some centuries yet before him in which to find out what it meant to have killed a man with a thousand more coming to him than Ruairidh himself was ever likely to see, and he spent a good many of the years he did have turning the six words he had said in the mud over in his mind, and found, each time he turned them, that they had not worn any smoother.

With that death began *Aimhreit nan Oighrean*, Aimhreit nan Oighrean. It was a feud first between the line of Murchadh and the line of Ìomhar only, a matter of raided byres and burned haystacks and the odd broken head at a boundary market, the kind of quarrel two great households might, in another age, have let cool over a generation or two. It did not cool. As the years went by and the lines married into each other, as they had married for a hundred years before Roinn na Rìoghachd and went on marrying after it because there was no one else on the island of a rank fit to marry, the quarrel married too, carried into new households on both sides by every wedding that was meant to end it, until there was scarcely a line of na h-Earrannan Mòra that did not, sooner or later, find some grievance of its own tangled into the old one from Dùn thais, and the strife that had begun with one spear on one wet hillside went on, by fits and lulls and fresh grievances, for the better part of five hundred and fifty years. Every man who had stood on that slope in the rain was dead and past caring who had thrown first before it burned itself out.

## IV. Of the Shares of Shares

The stewards who had ridden the boundaries that winter had done the easy half of the work, and knew it. Nine great shares, agreed on a cord and marked, more or less, with stone, were the whole of what Roinn na Rìoghachd had settled; but under each of the nine lay the households that had knotted their own claims into it, some of them the direct line of an heir's own children, some of them cousins six times removed who had stood up at the gathering-house because a great-uncle's blood gave them a right to stand, and none of them, once the great sharing was done, willing to be governed as one undivided thing by a single lord when the cord had already recorded them as separate strands.

So the shares were parted again, and parted a second time under that, in the two years after the stones went up at Dùn thais, until what had been nine great shares on the cord had become countries under each of the nine, and provinces under the countries, and counties under the provinces, and under the counties holdings smaller than any of these, some no bigger than a single steading and its grazing, sworn by the household that held it to whichever county's steward stood nearest and could be bothered to ride out and take the oath. It was not done tidily, and it was not done once. A holding whose old boundary had run along a stream that changed its course in a wet winter was claimed the following spring by two counties at once, each steward certain the stream had always run where it now ran, and more than one such holding, rather than let the matter be settled by force, simply swore to both and paid both, and hoped the two counties would forget to compare their tallies, which for some years they generally did.

The mill on the Abhainn dhomhain, a little way below where three counties met at a bend in the river, came to be held by three lords at once in exactly this fashion, none of whom had ever laid a stone of it or ground a single sack of grain in it, and all three of whom claimed, with equal confidence and no writing at all to settle the matter, that the eels taken from the millpond in the autumn run were theirs by right of the ground the pond stood on. The miller, an old man who had built the wheel himself before Roinn na Rìoghachd was so much as thought of and cared nothing for whose ground it stood on so long as the water kept turning it, took to paying each of the three lords a third of the eel-catch every autumn without being asked, on the plain reasoning that a third paid three times over cost him less in the end than a whole season's catch seized by whichever lord's steward arrived first and angriest, and the three lords, who might otherwise have come to blows over it the way Ruairidh and Aonghas Bàn had come to blows over a hillside, found that a miller who paid all three punctually and complained to none of them was a peace easier to keep than a war worth fighting, and let the matter stand at that for longer than any of them lived to see changed.

It went on so, boundary by boundary, holding by holding, all through the two summers after the stones went up at Dùn thais, and Clachan-crìche themselves went on being set up and moved in the night and set up again, until it was said, by carriers who came to know that stretch of country better than any lord's steward ever did, that a man could walk from an Earrann Dhearg to Earrann Mhurchaidh in a single day and cross nine stones that all claimed to mark the same line.

## V. Of the Count That Gave Up

Luchd nan Snaidhm of Dùn ìseal, who had counted the seven hundred and twelve knots of Roinn na Rìoghachd on Sìne's own cord and never once lost the tale of it, tried in those same two years to keep some count of what the shares had become underneath, because it seemed to them, as it seemed to Aodh Crom and the other keepers of the slab, a foolish thing for a kingdom to be cut so fine that no one living could say how many pieces it had been cut into.

They began well enough. Nine great shares, no one disputed; and by careful asking, county steward by county steward, riding the boundaries themselves with cords of their own to notch a knot for every claim they were shown, Sìne's daughters and the women who had learned the craft from them came, in the second summer, to a count of forty-four countries under the nine, which held for a season and was believed, and then had to be unmade and made again when two of the forty-four turned out, on a closer asking, to have quietly divided themselves into three apiece without troubling to send word to Dùn ìseal that they had done so. Under the countries they counted a hundred and thirty-one provinces, and were reasonably confident of that number for the better part of a year, though even as they counted it two provinces of Earrann Caol shean fell to quarrelling over a fishing-weir and split into three between them before the ink of the notches, had there been ink, could have dried.

Below the provinces lay the counties, and there Luchd nan Snaidhm gave up.

It was not laziness, and none of the tellers who carried the story afterward ever said it was. There was, in those years, no writing on the whole of the island, no mark on hide or stone that could hold a name still while a man's memory went on to the next thing; the rolls of the kingdom lived nowhere but in the knotted cords and the notched sticks and the heads of the women who kept them, and a head, however well trained to the craft, could hold only so many counties before the eight hundredth or the nine hundredth began to crowd the first out. Anna nan Snaidhm, Sìne's eldest daughter, who had ridden more of those boundaries herself than any woman then living, told Aodh Crom plainly, the autumn the count of counties passed nine hundred and showed no sign of stopping, that she could keep na h-Earrannan Mòra in her head until she died and her daughter's daughter could keep them after her, but that the counties changed their own borders faster than any cord could be tied to hold them, and that a knot-keeper who tried to count them all would spend her whole life chasing a number that was never the same number twice.

Aodh Crom, when she told him this, said only that a kingdom too fine-cut to be counted was a kingdom that would have to be governed, in the end, by something other than counting, and that he did not envy whoever was given that task, because it had not yet been given to anyone, and would not be for a long while after his own death. He did not say what he thought that something might be. He was an old man by then, older than his years by rights should have made him, and he had carried the asking of An Ceangal and the counting of the first cord and now the setting of the stones, and he told Sìne once more, at her own door, much as he had told her once before, that he did not think a man his age was owed rest on top of everything else the valley wanted of him.

## VI. Of the Ground No Feud Might Follow

In the second summer after the stones went up, a household of an Earrann Dhearg that had suffered a raid from across the boundary at Dùn thais, a matter of burned hay and two calves driven off in the night, brought its steward to Talla na Lasrach's own ground under the central mountain to lay the complaint before the keepers, not because the keepers had any authority over a boundary quarrel between two lines of the king's blood, but because there was nowhere else on the whole of the island that both sides of the quarrel could be brought to stand together without one side or the other coming armed.

The steward of the northern line came too, when word reached him, and for a moment it seemed to the keepers watching from the door of their hut that the quarrel meant to follow the two men up the hill and finish on the very ground where An Ceangal had been sworn. Aodh Crom, old and slow on his stick by then but not too slow to see what was coming, came out and stood between the two stewards before either had raised a hand, and said what he had said once before on Oidhche an Dòrlaich, in much the same short way: that the ground under the mountain, from the slab to the boundary the keepers themselves had walked at An Ceangal, was in no share and never would be, sworn so before any cord was cut, and that no man's spear, thrown or carried, was to come onto it in anger, whatever quarrel had brought him there.

It was not a new law. It was the same word Aodh Crom had spoken over the flame three summers before, said again because a new kind of trouble had come along to test whether the old word still held, and it held. The two stewards laid their complaint before the keepers instead of before each other's spears, and went away no more satisfied than men in a boundary quarrel generally go away, but they went away on their own feet, and Talla na Lasrach's ground kept, that summer, the one thing it had always claimed: that whatever a man had done, or had had done to him, on the wrong side of whichever stone claimed to be the true one, he could stand under the mountain and not be answered for it there.

The heirs went home from the valley in the last of that summer, most of them for the first time in nearly two years without a boundary still open to argue, though a good many boundaries would open again before Aimhreit nan Oighrean was done, and Ruairidh Glas went home to an Earrann Dhearg with them, older than his three hundred years by more than the calendar could account for, and never, in all the centuries that were left to him, rode the ground above Dùn thais again. Carriers who came that way in the seasons after learned to walk the road between the two stones without troubling to read what was cut on either face, the way a man learns to step over a root in a path he has walked often enough not to need to look at it, and if any of them ever wondered, crossing the burn at the fort's foot, whose grass they were walking on that day, none of the tellers who carried their sayings afterward thought it worth setting down which of them asked, or what answer, if any, they were given.
