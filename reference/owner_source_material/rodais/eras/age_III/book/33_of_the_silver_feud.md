# Of the Silver Feud

*FE 1,736 – 1,781*

> *Cha do bhris an t-airgead a dhath riamh; carson a bhristeadh e a chumhachd?*
> Silver never broke its colour; why should it break its word?
>
> — said still at the smiths' hearth of Muileann chiar, of no ruler in particular

## I. Of the Grey Rìgh

Ruairidh Liath took the red hall at Cathair dhearg in his thirty-sixth year, five days after they had carried his mother the length of the island to lie among the house of Ìomhar, and the reeves who set the crown on him did it quietly, for a household still in its year of mourning is not a household that wants a feast made of a crowning. He had been grey since his cradle, as his mother's own household had first taken for an ill sign and then, watching him grow tall and steady and no sicklier than any other child, for nothing more than the colour a man is born with, and the byname stuck to him the way a byname sticks to a family that has run out of new ones: Ruairidh Liath, Ruairidh the Grey, the one jest the king-list ever allowed among its years of names.

He kept his mother's clerks, her stewards, her reeve at Dùn dhearg, and for the first years of his reign changed little that she had set in order, being, his own household said of him, a man who mistrusted a thing done only because it was new. The old reeve who had walked at Beathag Mhòr's stirrup was long dead by then, but his grandson kept the same tallies in much the same hand, and it was to him, in his first winter, that Ruairidh Liath said the one thing of his own that the granary household remembered afterward: that he had watched his mother spend her reign finding out what the island held, and meant to spend his own reign finding out only what it needed, which he judged the smaller and the harder task of the two.

What he did change, in his fourth year, was the guard he kept on the eastern road, doubling it against the toll-cheats who had grown bold in the years the census had kept every clerk in the island walking from house to house rather than watching the roads, and it was the guard's own pay, more than anything else in his reign, that brought him at last to the silver.

The silver of the island came from one place only, the workings at Muileann chiar, a small town of a few hundred souls a short ride north of Dùn ìseal, where the seam ran shallow enough to be worked with pick and fire-setting and had done, by Ruairidh Liath's day, for longer than any family there could count in generations rather than reigns. It was Muireach Liath himself, second Keeper of the flame, who had ruled in the age's first years that silver showed no colour when it broke and so was not the mountain's, and Muileann chiar had dug as deep as it liked ever since on the strength of those words, paying no toll to the Hall and none, until Ruairidh Liath's reign, to the crown either.

## II. Of Sliochd Fhearchair

The house that held the workings by that time, and had held them for sixty generations without any other house troubling to dispute it, was Sliochd Fhearchair, the line of Fearchar Airgeadach, the basket-carrier who had first hammered the ore into rings in the Hall's first century, and it was an old woman of that house, Màiri nic Fhearchair, who spoke for it in the years that followed, having outlived two husbands, three of her own children, and every other smith of her own generation who might have spoken instead.

She had been born in the first years of Beathag Mhòr's reign, and had lived to see her own grandmother's claim to the handful proved before the counters when the census came at last to Muileann chiar, so that Màiri nic Fhearchair knew, before she knew much else, what it cost a house to have its right questioned and what it was worth to have it settled beyond argument afterward. She came to speak for Sliochd Fhearchair the ordinary way a smith's house chooses its voice, by being the one whose word the others trusted furthest, and she held the place for twenty years before Ruairidh Liath ever asked anything of her that was hard to give.

Neither large nor loud, she was known to the traders who came up from Dùn ìseal to buy rings by the basket only for weighing a ring twice before she named its price and never once, in all the years they dealt with her, weighed it wrong. Her own hands, by the time Ruairidh Liath's reign was ten years old, had thickened at the knuckle from forty years at the fire, and she wore, summer and winter both, a single ring of her own house's silver on a cord at her throat rather than on her finger, saying, when anyone asked her why, that a ring worn where it could be seen was a ring a smith remembered making rather than a ring she had forgotten she owned.

Her own house kept, in those years, some thirty smiths and apprentices at the workings and the forge-yard beside them, a small enough number beside the shepherds and cutters of the greater shares but the only hands on the island that drew silver at all, and Muileann chiar had grown up around them a town of a few hundred souls who owed their bread, one way or another, to the ore beneath their own feet. Màiri knew every one of the thirty by name and by trade, the fire-setters who cracked the rock with heat and water before the pick ever touched it, the smelters who read a flame's colour the way a reciter read a knotted cord, the smiths proper who drew the wire and struck the rings, and she judged each of their disputes herself rather than let them go before the town's own reeve, saying that a smith's quarrel was a family matter until the day it was not, and she meant, by that, to keep it a family matter as long as she was able.

## III. Of the Ring in Five

The demand came in Ruairidh Liath's ninth year, carried up from Dùn dhearg by a steward who had ridden three days to deliver it and plainly wished, from the look of him, that someone else had been sent instead: that Sliochd Fhearchair should render one ring in every five it drew from the ore, the silver going to pay the guard whose doubling had emptied the crown's own purse faster than the eastern tolls could fill it again.

Màiri nic Fhearchair heard the steward out in the yard before her own forge, with the fire banked low behind her and her apprentices standing well back, and the steward, who had expected an old smith to hear a rìgh's asking with some show of trouble at least, said afterward that she heard it the way a woman hears news of weather she has already dressed against. She asked him only one question before she answered him at all: whether he had been told to wait for her answer or only to carry it away with him whatever it was, and when he said he had been told to wait, she told him he need not, for the answer would take her no longer to give than it had taken him to ask.

She answered him, then, with words she had not troubled to soften: that the tokens which paid a queen's guards were the queen's own business, but the silver that made them had never once, in sixty generations, been anybody's business but the hands that dug it and the fire that shaped it, and that Muireach Liath himself, second Keeper of the flame, had said as much before any road-guard on the island carried a spear. The steward asked her, before he left, whether she wished him to soften the words on the road back to Cathair dhearg, and she told him no, that a message softened in the carrying was a message that lied twice over, once to the one who sent it and once to the one who received it, and she would rather Ruairidh Liath heard her exact meaning and be angry at it than a kinder one and be satisfied with less than the truth.

She sent the steward home with no other answer, and within the month, before the crown's own clerks had finished drawing up the toll roll they meant to collect it by, the workings at Muileann chiar stood shut, the fires out, the ore left in the ground.

It was not a thing any house on the island had done before, to close a working rather than pay what its ruler asked, and Sliochd Fhearchair did not do it lightly: the shutting cost the house its own trade as surely as it cost the crown its silver, and more than one of Màiri's own kin asked her, in the first weeks, whether a ring in five paid quietly would not have been the cheaper course in the end. Tormod himself, then a young man newly come into his own place at the forge, put the question to her most plainly of any of them, standing in the cooling yard with the last of the fire's light on his face, and asked whether a house that had never once been troubled for a toll in sixty generations might not simply pay the one toll and go back to being untroubled the generation after.

She told him that a house which paid the first asking without argument would be asked again within the year, and a third time within two, until it had nothing left to argue with, for a right unused, she said, was no different in the end from a right given away, only slower in the losing of it; and she told him besides that she had not shut the fires to save the house a single ring, since the house could well have spared the silver, but to make certain that whatever it paid afterward, it paid because a Keeper had judged it owed and not because a rìgh had asked for it once and found no one troubled to say him no. Her own certainty in the matter, Tormod said years afterward, was the only certain thing in Muileann chiar that whole first season, for even her own household went to their beds most nights that winter no surer than the rìgh himself how long a smith's house could go without its fires before it stopped being a smith's house at all.

## IV. Of the Three Seasons

The shutting held three seasons entire, the longest quiet the workings at Muileann chiar had known since they were first opened, and the island felt it before the year was out. The fair at Muileann òg, which had traded half its custom in silver rings for as long as the smiths of Sliochd Fhearchair had carried them there, went back to weighing its bargains in grain and cloth as it had done before the workings were dug at all, and the traders who had grown rich carrying rings down the eastern road found their own baskets empty and their own trade gone to whoever still had grain to sell.

## V. Of the Toll-House at Àth ghlas

The road-guards themselves, whose pay had begun the whole quarrel, went unpaid through the second season and the third, and it was at the toll-house of Àth ghlas, the first keeper the rìgh's hall had ever sent to stand a road, that the wanting was felt worst. Two of the four guards posted there left their spears against the toll-house wall in the same week of the second winter and walked home to their own villages rather than stand a further watch for wages that were themselves standing, unpaid, on a silver toll no one had yet agreed to gather, and the keeper who held the house, a woman named Oighrig nic Ailein who had kept it eleven years without missing a season's watch before that winter, sent no word of the desertion to Cathair dhearg at all, judging, she told the two who stayed, that a rìgh already short of silver had no need of a letter telling him he was short of guards as well.

She stood the toll-house herself through the worst of that season with the two guards who remained, turning back no traveller and collecting no toll from any of them either, for a toll-house with half its watch was no toll-house at all in her own reckoning, only a roof with a fire under it, and she told travellers who asked after the toll that the crown's business with the silversmiths was the crown's own to settle and none of theirs, an answer more than one carrier carried onward down the road and repeated at hearths from Àth ghlas to the western harbours until it had travelled, by the third season, further than the quarrel itself had yet been heard of in some quarters. The tolls of the eastern road went ungathered the whole three seasons through, for want of anyone willing to stand the road and collect them, and it was the emptiest the crown's own purse had stood since Beathag Mhòr's own reign, before the census had ever begun to fill it.

Ruairidh Liath, for his own part, did not send soldiers to the workings, though more than one of his own reeves urged it on him; his mother's household remembered him saying once, in those years, that a mine could be broken open by force but a smith's hand could not, and a crown that had only broken hands to show for its silver had made a poor bargain of it. He sent, instead, a second steward to Àth ghlas with grain enough to feed the toll-house through what remained of the winter, unasked and unannounced, much as his own mother had once sent grain to a hungry waypoint house without naming herself the sender, and Oighrig nic Ailein, who guessed well enough whose grain it was, never once told a traveller the story of it, though she kept, for the rest of her life, a fuller hearth at Àth ghlas than the crown's own wages alone would have bought her.

## VI. Of the Third Winter

He held to the toll, and waited, through the whole of that hungry season, and it was Màiri nic Fhearchair, in the end, who moved first, not because her own house's will had bent but because of what she saw, in the third winter, in her own forge-yard: two of her youngest apprentices, a brother and sister taken on the year the shutting began, going thinner at the wrist than any apprentice she had trained in forty years at the fire, their own family having nothing to send them from Muileann chiar's own thin fields, and no wage from the silver to buy what the fields could not give. She fed them from her own table rather than send them home, and told no one of it beyond her own household, but she judged, watching the boy's hands shake over a task that should not have troubled hands half so young, that a right worth keeping was not worth keeping at the cost of the hands that would keep it after her.

She said as much to her own kin, plainly, the same week she sent word to Dùn ìseal: that Sliochd Fhearchair had shut its fires to keep a principle and very nearly starved two children doing it, and a principle that cost a smith's own apprentices their strength before it cost the crown a single ring was a principle she had begun to hold for her own pride's sake rather than the house's, which was, she told them, the one thing she had sworn, on taking up her place at the forge twenty years before, that she would never let herself do.

## VII. Of the Journey to Dùn ìseal

She made the ride to Dùn ìseal herself, in the last month of the third season, in a rain that had not let up in four days and turned the road below Muileann chiar to a soup the horses baulked at twice before she got them through it, taking with her only her grandnephew Tormod and a single strongbox holding every ring Sliochd Fhearchair had struck in the reign of Fearchar Òg, that the Keeper might see, she said, exactly what silver looked like before anyone had thought to argue over who owned it.

She had never before asked the Hall for anything, her own house having stood, since Muireach Liath's ruling, entirely outside the Hall's reckoning of what belonged to the mountain and what did not, and it cost her something, Tormod said years afterward, to ride up to the Keeper's own door and ask a judgment of a man whose office existed to weigh coal, not silver. Dùghall mac Raghnaill received her all the same, being by then an old man of seventy-nine who had judged every manner of claim the island could bring him and had long since stopped thinking any of them beneath his hearing, and he sent word to Cathair dhearg the same week asking that Ruairidh Liath meet her there, at Dùn ìseal, on ground that belonged to neither of them outright.

## VIII. Of the Rain-Shut Hearing

The hearing was held in the Hall at Dùn ìseal on a day the rain shut every door in the town and kept the roof-gutters running loud enough that Dùghall mac Raghnaill twice had to ask both sides to speak again, and it was there, before the cup and the Keeper and Ruairidh Liath, grey and forty-nine and no longer young enough to think a quarrel would settle on its own, that Màiri nic Fhearchair said Muireach Liath's sentence for the first time: that silver showed no colour when it broke, and so was not the mountain's.

She said it standing, her strongbox open on the floor before the cup so that any who wished might look into it and see the rings themselves, struck before the crown had ever thought to ask anything of them, and she added to Muireach Liath's own words only a handful of her own: that she had not come to Dùn ìseal to ask whether the crown might be poor, for every house on the island had known a poor season and none begrudged its ruler one, but to ask whether a right sixty generations old could be unmade by a single asking from a man who had held the hall thirteen years, and if it could, she wished to be told so plainly, that Sliochd Fhearchair might know from that day what its rights were worth.

Ruairidh Liath answered that he did not dispute the mountain's claim or lack of one; he disputed only whether a crown might ask a toll of a trade that used its roads, wore its peace and sold its wares under laws his own house had written, silver or no silver, mountain's or no mountain's. He said, further, that he had not shut her workings; she had shut them herself rather than argue the matter with him first, and a house that answered an asking with a closed door had made the quarrel larger than the asking ever was, a charge Màiri nic Fhearchair did not trouble to deny, saying only that a door closed in argument was easier to open again than a right given away was to take back.

Dùghall mac Raghnaill let them argue it the better part of a day, the rain never once slackening, and when he judged at last he had Màiri nic Fhearchair say the sentence a second time, word for word as she had said it the first, before he gave his own ruling on it.

He ruled that the words of Muireach Liath stood as they had always stood: silver was not the mountain's, and no Keeper after him would say otherwise. But he ruled, in the same breath, that the crown kept the roads the silver travelled whether the silver itself was the crown's or not, and that a house which used those roads owed something toward their keeping, though nothing toward the ore beneath its own feet. Each of them was right, he said, and each of them had asked the wrong question first, Ruairidh Liath for reaching past the silver to the roads without saying so and Màiri nic Fhearchair for reaching past the roads to the silver in her own defence of it, and a ring in five had never been the toll he would have set had either of them asked him the plainer question at the start.

He set it, in the end, at one ring in ten, owed not on the ore drawn from the ground but on every ring that left Muileann chiar by the roads the crown kept, and both sides, Tormod said afterward, rode home from Dùn ìseal calling it a victory, though neither, in truth, had won anything but the certainty of knowing what it would cost them from that year on.

## IX. Of the Weight Made Law

The workings opened again within the month, the fires lit and the ore drawn as it had been for sixty generations, but the settlement left one thing behind it that outlasted the quarrel's own memory: for the toll to be reckoned fairly, ring against ring, the weights of Muileann chiar, kept by Sliochd Fhearchair's own hand since the Peace of the Threshold and trusted nowhere beyond the workings themselves, were declared by the Keeper's own judgment the only lawful weights on the roads of the island, and copies in bronze were ordered sent to every waypoint house between Dùn ìseal and the western harbours, that a ring weighed at one hearth might be trusted at the next without argument.

Màiri nic Fhearchair was paid, by the crown's own clerks, for every copy cast from her house's own weights, the first silver Sliochd Fhearchair had ever taken from Cathair dhearg rather than given it, and she remarked once to Tormod, counting the payment into the strongbox that had ridden to Dùn ìseal and back, that she had gone to the Hall meaning to keep the crown's hand off her house's silver and had come home, instead, with the crown's own silver in her own strongbox, a different victory than the one she had set out to win, and, she judged, no poor one to settle for.

## X. Of the Bronze-Casters

The copies themselves were cast at Muileann dhearg, a market town roughly midway along the road between Cathair dhearg and Dùn ìseal, by a family of casters named Sliochd Aonghais who had learned the trade from the smiths of Muileann chiar two generations before and had never yet found a market for it beyond a few pins and buckles a season. The weight-copies gave them, for the first time, a craft the whole island wanted, and they went on, in the years after, to cast bells for the waypoint houses and lamp-bowls for the Hall's own use, each lamp-bowl holding, by the founder's own boast, exactly the measure the Small-Burning Law required and not a hair's breadth more or less.

The founder of that trade, a broad woman named Beitris nic Aonghais, kept for the rest of her life the first weight-copy she had ever cast, a poor one, cracked along its rim from cooling too fast, and showed it to every apprentice she took on afterward before she let them near the good bronze, telling them that a caster who forgot her own worst work was a caster likely to repeat it.

She sent word to Màiri nic Fhearchair, the season the good copies first went out along the road, asking leave to cast the smiths' own hearth-sign into each weight's underside, that any waypoint keeper who turned one over might know at a glance whose measure it stood for, and Màiri gave the leave gladly, saying it cost Sliochd Fhearchair nothing and might, in some quarrel neither of them could yet foresee, save a keeper an argument over whether the weight in his own hand was the true one. It was a small mark, cut no bigger than a thumbnail, and the two houses that had never traded more than a few pins between them before the settlement went on trading weights and bells and lamp-bowls for years after both women who had first agreed to it were dead, every weight cast at Muileann dhearg still carrying, to the close of Ruairidh Liath's reign and past it, the same small sign struck at Muileann chiar and cast at Muileann dhearg together.

## XI. Of Màiri's Last Years

Màiri nic Fhearchair lived eleven years past the settlement, long enough to see the bronze weights reach every waypoint house from Dùn ìseal to the western harbours and long enough to see her own grandnephew Tormod take up her place at the forge-yard, speaking for Sliochd Fhearchair in his turn with a plainness she had taught him without ever once calling it teaching. She wore the same ring on its cord at her throat to the end of her days, and when she died, in the eightieth year of her life, the household of Sliochd Fhearchair buried her, at her own long-standing word, with the strongbox itself rather than the ring on the cord, empty by then of everything but the single weight-copy she had carried to Dùn ìseal in the rain, saying she had carried enough silver in her life without needing to carry any of it into the ground.

Dùghall mac Raghnaill died the same year, in his ninetieth, an old man who had held the flame near as long as Coinneach Mòr before him and had judged, by his own late count, more disputes over the handful and over the silver both than he could any longer put a number to; the reciters of Dùn ìseal remembered him afterward less for the census that had made his name than for the rain-shut hearing at which he had told two sides they were each right and each wrong, a saying more than one Keeper after him borrowed without troubling to say whose it had first been.

## XII. Of Seonag Chrom and the List Kept Yearly

Ruairidh Liath reigned one-and-thirty years more, growing no greyer than he had been at his crowning, for a man born grey has little further to grow into, and died in the red hall at Cathair dhearg in his eightieth year, in the same small chamber where his mother had died before him, mourned by a household that remembered him, in the reeve's own words to his successor, as a rìgh who had waited where waiting served and moved where nothing else would.

His granddaughter Seonag Chrom took the hall the year after, in her twenty-first year, the crown set on her without argument from any of the houses that might once have disputed a young ruler's age, for the island had by then buried enough young rulers grown old in office to have stopped counting a crowned head's years against her. She had grown up, her own household said, on stories of her grandfather's silver quarrel told at the granary fire by clerks who still kept, among the hall's own rolls, the roll of weights sent out that year to every waypoint house, and she asked, in the first month of her reign, to see that roll for herself before she asked to see any other roll in the hall's keeping.

What she found, turning it over by lamplight with the reeve who had kept it since his own father's day, was a record set down on whatever scrap of hide or bark had come to hand at the time, one clerk's tally crossed through and corrected in another clerk's hand, a third page missing its corner where damp had eaten it, and she judged, closing it at last, that a crown which meant to keep such a roll at all owed the keeping of it more care than a corner of the granary stores had ever given it. She gave the Hall's clerks, in her first season, a room of their own within the red hall, the first they had ever had that was not a corner of the granary stores or a table borrowed from the kitchens, and a wage in silver besides, the same silver the settlement at Dùn ìseal had first taught the crown to reckon by the ring rather than by the sack.

It was from her reign, the clerks of Cathair dhearg said afterward, that the king-list ceased to be kept reign by reign, as it had been kept since Tormod first set it down, and began instead to be kept year by year, so that no year of the island's telling, however quiet, would ever again go unwritten for want of a reign large enough to hold it.
