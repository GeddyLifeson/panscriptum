# Of the Smoke and the Stone Cup

*FE 126 – 148*

> *Cha do dh'iarr mi mo shùilean air ais. Dh'iarr mi làmhan a b' fheàrr na sùilean.*
> I did not ask for my eyes back. I asked for hands better than eyes.
>
> — said of Fionnlagh Dall, called the Blind Keeper

## I. Of the Death of Gormshuil

Fionnlagh had been three days without sleep worth the name before anyone thought to tell him that Gormshuil nic Mhuirich was dying, and when the word came at last it came by a boy's hand on his sleeve rather than by any voice raised for him, since the household at Dùn ìseal had fallen into the habit, over the years of his sitting with the flame, of forgetting that a thing said across a room was no use to him at all unless someone thought to walk it over.

He had sat with the flame himself on and off since he was a boy of fourteen, one of a dozen or more the nine hearths sent up in their turns to learn the slab and the galleries under the Keeper's eye, and in all that time he had never once seen Gormshuil's face, having lost the last of his sight to a fever in his childhood, long before either of them came near Talla na Lasrach. What he had of her he had built the way he built everything, out of what came to him by other roads: the particular dryness of her voice when a household pressed her too hard for a ruling she had not yet made up her mind to give; the way she paused, always, a full breath before she said a hard thing, as if weighing whether it still wanted saying; the smell of the oil she rubbed into her hands against the cold of the galleries, which he could pick out of a crowded room before anyone spoke a word. He built her out of these the way a man born blind builds a mountain out of the wind that comes off it, and he had come, over eighty-seven years of her life and near forty of his own, to trust the picture he had built better than he trusted most things he was told he could see.

She died on the ninth night of the second month of the hundred and twenty-seventh year, in the small room off the Hall where the Keepers slept, with her cutter's daughter holding one hand and Fionnlagh, sent for late, holding the other, and the last thing she said to him, in a voice gone thin as the last of a coal's colour, was a complaint, plainly meant to be his last memory of her and, by every account he was given afterward, exactly the thing she would have wanted it to be: that the room had always been colder on his side of the bed than on hers, and no one in eighty-seven years had troubled to find out why.

He sat with her body until the household came to wash it, and when they had gone he sat a while longer with the empty bed, and it was there, by his own telling of it in the years that followed, that he first let himself think what half of Dùn ìseal had already begun to think for him, which was that the nine hearths would meet within the week to choose who carried the flame after her, and that his own name, sitter of more seasons than any man living who was not already too old to be chosen, could not honestly be left off whatever list the hearths drew up, blindness or no blindness.

## II. Of the Nine Hearths and the Boy who Led Him

The nine hearths met at Dùn ìseal on the appointed day, as they had met to choose Gormshuil after Muireach Liath and Beathag Bhàn before either of them, and heard the households argue for their own candidates through the better part of an afternoon: a strong young cutter of the fourth hearth who had never once, in six seasons, let a lamp gutter in a gallery; a niece of Gormshuil's own who carried her aunt's dry manner and, some said, a fair measure of her judgement besides; and Fionnlagh, put forward by three keepers of the slab together, no household of his own eager to claim him, on the plain ground that a man who has read the mountain by touch and hearing for twenty years knows it in ways a man who reads it by sight has never had cause to learn.

It was the niece's own household, in the end, that settled it, standing up before the hearths were done arguing to say that a Keeper's hands mattered more to the measure than a Keeper's eyes, and that they had watched Fionnlagh's hands fill a lamp steadier than any two of the other candidates' together, blind hands having no choice, as sighted hands sometimes did, but to feel exactly what they held. The nine hearths chose him before the light failed that day, the first blind man ever set to keep the flame, and it is told that when the choosing was carried to him where he sat waiting in the passage, he asked only one question of the elder who brought it, which was whether anyone had thought to ask who would walk him from the Hall to the slab and back on the mornings his own feet did not yet know the way, the galleries having grown, in Gormshuil's years, a good deal further than they had been in his own boyhood.

The answer to that came from an unlooked-for quarter within the week, in the shape of a boy of ten named Calum Beag, a grandson of one of the nine-hearth households who had taken to following Fionnlagh about the Hall since he was old enough to walk, out of nothing more than a child's plain curiosity about a man who moved through rooms he had never seen without once putting a hand wrong. The boy's own mother brought him to Fionnlagh on the third morning of his keeping and said, more or less, that the child would follow him whether she gave leave or not, and that she would rather he did it with her knowledge than without it. Fionnlagh, by his own later telling, asked the boy a single question before he agreed to anything, which was whether he could hold his tongue about what he saw in the galleries, since a Keeper's eyes, borrowed or his own, owed the vein the same silence either way; and Calum Beag, who had clearly given the matter no thought whatever before that moment, said yes so quickly and so solemnly that Fionnlagh laughed for the first time anyone at Dùn ìseal had heard him laugh since Gormshuil's death, and took the boy's shoulder in his hand, and from that day the two of them went about the Hall together, the blind Keeper and the boy who told him, in a voice pitched low enough for no one else to hear, what step came next and which face of which gallery he was standing before.

## III. Of the Smoke at Cnoc dhubh

The winter that came on within the year was harder than any the Hall's own memory reached back to plainly, and it fell hardest of all on the households of the high ground above Cnoc dhubh, a scatter of turf-roofed houses a few miles from Dùn ìseal where the wind off the mountain ran cold enough, in a bad season, to freeze the milk in the pail before a woman could carry it from byre to hearth. One household there, whose name the Hall's own roll kept afterward with a plainness that spared no one, had burned its measured handful through the early cold and found, by the turn of the year, that the handful had not been enough to keep three small children and an old woman through the worst of the nights, and so had begun, in the desperation of a house going cold around people it could not warm any other way, to burn the coal by the basketful instead, a measure no household on the island had ever burned before, since no household before that winter had ever needed to find out what a basketful would do.

What it did, over three nights of steady burning in a turf house with no chimney worth the name and a wind outside too fierce to let a door stand open, was lie down in the valley the way smoke from a green fire lies down on a still evening, thick and low and unwilling to lift, filling first the house that made it and then, before the wind changed, the two houses nearest it as well. Calum Beag was the one who brought Fionnlagh the word, three days after, having heard it first from a carrier who had smelled the valley from half a mile off and turned aside to see what burned; and it was Calum's own voice, cracking somewhere in the middle of the telling in a way that had nothing to do with a boy's changing years, that told Fionnlagh before the carrier's own account did that this was worse than any winter's ordinary grief. Five were dead of it in the end: three of the burning household, a grandmother of the house beside it who had gone in to see why no smoke rose properly from either chimney and never came out again on her own feet, and a neighbour's infant carried in its mother's arms through air that should have been safe a full house-length from the fire and was not.

Fionnlagh went up to Cnoc dhubh himself before the case was ever argued at Dùn ìseal, led by Calum Beag over ground neither of them knew well, and stood in the burned household's own doorway while a neighbour described to him, in the flat voice of a man too tired for grief to sound like grief any longer, what five bodies carried out of two houses in a single morning had looked like laid side by side on the frozen ground before anyone could dig a grave for them. He asked to touch the hearth before he left, and knelt with his hand flat against the cold stone a long while, Calum Beag said afterward, longer than seemed needed for any purpose the boy could name, until an old man of the third house, watching him, said aloud that a hearth-stone could tell a blind man nothing a burned house had not already told the whole valley. Fionnlagh, rising, answered that he had come to know what his own hands had been set over, and the stone was the surest way he had of learning it.

## IV. Of the Season of Argument

The case was carried to Dùn ìseal within the week and argued there, on and off, for the better part of the season that followed, and Fionnlagh, new to the flame and new to judgement of any kind that mattered beyond a lamp's filling, found himself sitting through arguments that circled the same ground again and again without ever quite landing on it. The dead household's kin said plainly that a house with three children and an old woman in it and only a handful of coal to warm them through the worst nights the Hall's own memory could name had no choice at all, and that a law which let people freeze rather than burn what the mountain held in more than enough for every household living was no law worth keeping. The households of the other eight hearths, frightened by what a single family's basketful had done to two houses that had burned nothing at all, said with equal plainness that the handful had held the island's peace since before any of them was born precisely because it was never argued with, and that the day a household could burn what it judged it needed rather than what the Hall measured out was the day the measure meant nothing to anyone.

Fionnlagh listened to both sides argued past each other for the better part of two months without once giving a ruling, which some of the households took, in those early days of his keeping, for a blind man's slowness and others, more rightly, for a new Keeper's unwillingness to answer a hard question before he had turned it over as many ways as it would turn. What he came to, sitting alone with it through the nights Calum Beag had gone home to his own mother's hearth, was neither of the two things argued at him: not that the measure should be loosened, since he had heard enough that season of what five deaths from too little coal could look like beside five deaths from too much of it to know the mountain's own patience was not the question, and not that the law needed no change at all, since a law no one could be certain of, held only by custom and the memory of what Beathag Bhàn and Muireach Liath had once ruled by word of mouth, had let one frightened household burn its way past the edge of what any hearth could survive before anyone stopped it.

What the mountain wanted, he came at last to think, turning the question over the way his own fingers turned a lamp's wick when he could not yet tell by touch alone whether it was trimmed even, was a measure no household could argue with, because no household could argue with its own two hands.

## V. Of the Grey Stone Re-cut

There had been a stone cup at Dùn ìseal since the Age of Ailean, a plain thing of soft pale rock that the earliest keepers of the slab had used, by tradition rather than by any law, to measure out the coal each household carried home from the galleries; but it had cracked across one side in Muireach Liath's day and been mended with clay so many times since that no two households, filling it in turn, ever quite agreed how full it ought to sit before the measure was met. Fionnlagh sent Calum Beag up into the hills above Dùn ìseal in the hundred and thirty-eighth year to find him a stone the boy judged hard and fine-grained and free of any crack a mason's eye could find, and when the boy came back three days later leading a cutter of the fourth hearth and a slab of grey stone slung between two poles, Fionnlagh spent the better part of a month with his own hands upon it before he would let the cutter's chisel near it at all.

He had the mason cut it hollow to the exact measure of his own two hands cupped together, palm to palm, the fingers curled just short of touching, since his own hands were the one instrument he trusted absolutely to be the same hands every time; and when the cutting was done he tested it himself, again and again, filling it by feel alone until he could say, blind, with his fingers alone against the stone's cold lip, whether a fill sat a hair short of the measure or a hair over it, the way another man might have tested it by eye. Calum Beag, watching him do this through most of an autumn, asked once why he did not simply mark a line on the stone's inner face for a sighted keeper to judge by, and Fionnlagh's answer, which the boy carried about the Hall afterward as though it were a thing worth keeping, was that a line could be read generously by a friend and meanly by an enemy, and a blind man's fingers, having no friends and no enemies among the coal, could not.

## VI. Of the Threshold Found by the Foot

He set the finished cup, when it was done, on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach, the worn stone slab between the Hall's own ground and the path that every household walked to reach it, rather than inside the Hall where the old one had always stood within reach of whichever keeper the council favoured that season. There any man or woman with a claim to the coal could see, or be told by a neighbour who could see, that the cup sat in the open where no keeper's favour could shield it from any eye.

He found the threshold's exact centre himself, on the day he set the cup, by walking the width of the doorway with his own bare feet until he felt the worn place where centuries of households had crossed it evenest, the stone smoothed to a hollow no deeper than a fingernail's width by feet that had none of them meant to wear it so, and it was there, kneeling with the cup before him and Calum Beag standing silent at his shoulder, that he filled it for the first council to come and ask.

The household of the fourth hearth that came first that morning was a poor one, its elder an old woman whose hands shook with a palsy the cold made worse, and Fionnlagh, hearing the shake in her own voice before he ever touched her hand to guide it, filled the cup by touch alone and held it out to her level, not favouring the low reach an old woman's trembling arms might have asked for nor demanding the steady one a young man's would have given without thought. "Give me the coal," he said, and she gave it into the cup's mouth handful by handful from the household's own store until his fingers, resting light along the stone's rim, told him the measure was met; and when it was met he said only "Enough," and closed his own hand over the cup's lip so that no more could follow, whether the old woman's shaking hand meant to give more or not. It is told, and the households of the fourth hearth kept the telling a long while, that she wept a little at the fairness of it, having gone to every council for three years certain that a sighted keeper favoured whichever household stood nearest his own eye, and having found in a blind one's hands the first measure she had ever trusted absolutely.

## VII. Of the Law Sworn at Samhain

Word of the cup on the threshold ran ahead of Fionnlagh through that autumn faster than any ruling he had yet given in council, and by the time the lords of the shares gathered at Dùn ìseal for the Samhain gathering of the hundred and forty-second year, most of the island's households had already heard, from a neighbour or a neighbour's neighbour, that a blind man's hands had found a measure no one could quarrel with. Fionnlagh put the custom to the lords plainly that Samhain, standing before the assembled council with Calum Beag's hand light at his elbow: that the measured handful, held till then by nothing firmer than a Keeper's word and the memory of two dead Keepers before him, should be sworn as a law binding on every share alike, north and south, the Red Share and the lowland plain together, so that no household anywhere on the island might burn more than the cup on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach would give it in a season.

The lords grumbled, as lords of every share had grumbled at every measure laid on them since the Roinn, several rising to say that a law was a poor thing to bind on people who had kept the custom faithfully enough without one, and that the Hall had never before told a share what it might or might not burn on its own hearths. But the five who had died at Cnoc dhubh were not so far behind any of them that the argument could be pressed very hard, and in the end it was not Fionnlagh's own words that carried the council so much as the memory, fresh in every lord's mind that autumn, of the old woman of the fourth hearth weeping over a cup that had measured her fairly for the first time in three years. The lords swore to the law that Samhain, one by one, each laying a hand on the stone cup where it sat on the threshold; and when the last of them had sworn, Fionnlagh, who could not see a single one of the hands that had touched the stone that day, said only that he trusted the measure to hold them to it better than any oath would, since a cup, unlike a lord, could not be argued out of its own fullness. So the custom of the handful hardened that day into the Small-Burning Law, the first law that ran the same in every share the island held, made by no lord of any share and grumbled at by all of them, and it held, unaltered by a word, for longer than any man living that Samhain would ever have thought to guess.

## VIII. Of the First Sentence

It was a household of Seann Tarr, a fishing town on the north-eastern coast that had never before troubled the Hall's roll for anything worse than a boundary dispute over drying-racks, that gave the new law its first sentence, in the hundred and forty-fifth year, having burned twice its measure in a single season against a run of wet weather that had kept its boats ashore and its store of dried fish thinner than any winter before it. The household's elder came to Dùn ìseal himself to argue the case before the council, laying out plainly that a fisher's household without fish to trade for grain had needed the extra coal to smoke what little catch the weather allowed rather than lose it to the damp entirely, and that a law meant to spare lives had very nearly cost his own.

Fionnlagh heard him out at length, and Calum Beag, a man of seven-and-twenty by then and still standing beside the Keeper through a whole council's sitting without his attention wandering, told him afterward that the old man's hands had shaken through the whole of his telling in a way that had nothing to do with cold. But the law Fionnlagh had sworn at Samhain three years before allowed no household's need, however honest, to loosen a measure sworn the same for every hearth on the island, and he ruled at last, with what Calum Beag remembered as real reluctance in the set of his shoulders, that the household of Seann Tarr should lose its right to the coal for the season following, its name set upon the roll with the offence beside it. It was the harshest thing Fionnlagh had yet done as Keeper, and the household's own descendants, the tellers of Seann Tarr say still, petitioned Dùn ìseal for generation after generation afterward to have the offence struck from the roll, and were refused every time, the roll itself having become, by then, a thing the Hall would sooner let stand unaltered than see loosened for any household's asking, however old the wrong or however honest the need that had first caused it.

## IX. Of the Fires that Answer to Nobody

The last question the law raised that Fionnlagh had to answer came from the headland at Tobar dhearg, from no household's own hearth, where a beacon had burned, time out of mind, to warn boats off the rocks on a black night, fed by whatever coal the households nearest it could spare from their own measured store. A fisherman of that coast asked the council, in the hundred and forty-eighth year, whether a beacon lit for the safety of strangers who might never set foot on the island at all ought to be counted against a household's handful the same as a fire lit for its own hearth, since a light that answered to no one household's need seemed to him a different thing from a fire that warmed a family's own children.

Fionnlagh turned the question over for the better part of a day, Calum Beag told it afterward, sitting alone with the stone cup at his own hand the way he had sat with it through the season of the smoke at Cnoc dhubh, and the ruling he gave in the end went further than the fisherman had asked for. No coal at all, he ruled, might feed a beacon meant to warn strangers off the rocks, since the vein's fire was the island's own and owed nothing to a boat that might never touch the shore it warned away from; and from that ruling the beacon at Tobar dhearg was kept after with driftwood gathered off the tideline and seal fat rendered down for the purpose, a poorer light than coal would have given and, by every account the fishermen of that coast kept afterward, a steadier one, since driftwood and seal fat answered to no measure at all and never once, in all the years that followed, ran short at the one hour a black night made it matter. Every sea-light raised on the island after Tobar dhearg's was kept the same way, on the same ruling, and the households of that coast, telling of it to their own children afterward, said that the blind Keeper had judged rightly what a fire owed to its own people and what it owed to no one at all, which was, they said, a harder thing to see plainly than it sounds, and a thing a man with his eyes about him might easily have judged the other way.

Fionnlagh himself, asked once in his old age by a stag's teacher passing through Dùn ìseal why he had ruled the beacon apart from every other fire on the island, gave an answer Calum Beag, by then long grown and still at the old man's side, remembered word for word to the end of his own life: that a man who cannot see a fire learns early to judge it only by what it is for, and that the coal under the mountain, whatever else it was, had never once in all the island's telling been for a stranger.
