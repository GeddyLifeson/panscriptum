# Appendix E — Words and the Reckoning of the Dubhan Era

The age changed how the island counted its years, and it changed some of its words, and it made a few. Most of the new words came up out of kitchens and markets and inn yards and were in every mouth before any clerk wrote them down; two were made in small rooms and sent to the library to be kept; one was proclaimed. This appendix gives the reckoning of Linn an Dubhain as the council proclaimed it and Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd took it up, and then the words of the age that the record and Sceulachdan nan Tuathach keep, as they were used at its close.

## The proclamation and the re-heading

On {{date:VI-0049a}}, at the sitting that pegged the coin to the making of dubhan, the council proclaimed that An Aois Rìoghachd had ended with the first working dubhan, and that the kingdom's years were counted anew from that day. The first working fuel had been made fourteen years and some months before, at midsummer; the year of the proclamation was entered as the fifteenth of Linn an Dubhain, and the years before it as the first to the fourteenth. The printed proclamation was pinned to the market posts of every chartered town and read by the criers; at the harbour of Cuan shean the fishing crews asked whether it changed the day of the fish-market, and it did not.

Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd had been kept for those fourteen years under the count of Linn an Dealachaidh. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh re-headed it by lamplight, year by year, setting the new years beside the old headings and striking none of them out, and sent the re-headed pages to the copyists. At the head of the work she wrote the line the library keeps: *Cha robh agam riamh ri bliadhna a ghluasad a bha mi beò innte*, never before have I had to move a year I was living in. The market-clerks of the towns kept both counts side by side for a year or more, and some kept them longer. The count of Linn an Dealachaidh is still used in the rolls for the births of those born before the fuel, and in this volume for the years before the first working dubhan; the count of Linn an Dubhain for everything after.

## The words of the fuel

**dubhan.** The fuel's name was a man's before it was a thing's. For some years men said Dubhan's fuel, or the northern fuel, or the black cake; then, about the eleventh year, they began to say dubhan of the fuel itself, years before the trade built on it was whole. Sìleas nic Rath's slip-box at the library found it spelled dubhan, dùbhan and dùban in the council's own letters, and once, in Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's minute-book for a fortnight only, An Dubhan, with the article and a capital letter, as though there were only one. By the forty-first of the sixty hands that wrote regularly to the library it had turned, each hand at its own month. The rhyme of the north sets the two words side by side: *Dubh an gual, 's dubh an dubhan*, black the coal, and black the dubhan.

**dubhan-tùsail.** A word made by the masters of Scoiltean nan Draoidhean at Caol shean and first found in their letter to the library of {{year:VI-0036}}: a first attempt that looked, when it was made, like one more failure among many, and proved afterwards to have been the one that mattered. They made it from the old root for a first working and the north's word for the fuel, and used it of their own school's revival, not yet knowing whether that would hold. They sent it to the library for one reason alone, that a word made in a small room ought to be sent somewhere it might be kept even if it was never used. The library entered it. It has not been used.

**Leòmhann.** At An Leòmhann Buidhe, on the north-east road, the landlord kept one wick lamp burning over the door beside the new dubhan lamps in {{year:VI-0026}}. The custom spread along the roads, and a wick lamp over an inn door is still called a Leòmhann, whether or not the inn is a lion.

**bus, tacsaidh.** When the dubhan buses began to run between Cathair dhearg, Inis mhòr and Cathair mhòr in {{year:VI-0041a}}, and hired cars waited at the bridge in the capital, Dia-thìris took the names of both from the humans' tongue as it found them, and nobody coined others. The coal caste, whose own words for the trade were going out of use that year, said nothing of it.

**an t-slighe.** Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's timetables called its main line Slighe a' Ghuail, from its laying until {{year:VI-0037}}, when they began to print it only as an t-slighe, the line. Everyone else still calls it Slighe a' Ghuail.

## The words of the war

**Muinntir gun Siorrachd.** An Clàr Foscailte names the holdouts of An Cogadh Fada, as it names those who went onto an Oirthir Ghaothach beyond the edge of the shire of Seann Vell, only as Muinntir gun Siorrachd. Neither the Board nor the council uses any other name for them. The Tuathaich call them by their towns.

**chaidh am fadachadh.** They got long-warred. The saying began among Tuathaich speakers on the day of Muileann ghlas, {{date:VI-0119}}, of the defeat in the west, and then of any defeat after holding out too long. It is said as a jest, and those who say it do not laugh. A Tuathach of Caol mhòr is said to have first said it at the harbour steps.

**Cead an Rathaid.** The pass that the Tuathaich show at An siathamh rèisimeid's posts on the northern road, printed on grey card with a space for a name and none for a trade, and good, the card says, for a time only.

**An Clàr Foscailte.** An Clàr Foscailte of the war years: Sìleas nic Rath's list of places, the regiments present, and the counts she was allowed, which is all of Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd for the war that stayed open when the rest was sealed in {{year:VI-0128}}.

**Freiceadan Àth leathan.** An seachdamh rèisimeid's post on the road below Àth leathan, so called in the regimental lists. The town calls it something else, which the lists do not give and this volume does not know.

## The old reckoning

**seann-chunntas.** An old reckoning: a debt that none in power will settle and none has cancelled. From {{date:VI-0143}} Tuathaich speakers began to name together by this word the request that Sceulachdan nan Tuathach be kept in the library, which was laid on the table in the seventeenth year and never refused, and Tagradh na h-Èirig, which was let die in the twenty-fifth without a vote. The Dia-thìrich in office do not use the word. Tional nan Tuathach's minute for the motion runs to one line, *Allowed to die*, and Tional nan Tuathach has entered nothing against the word since.

## The two Seann Skells and the two Tobar dheargs

Two towns of the kingdom are called Seann Skell and two Tobar dhearg, and the age, which sent regiments and letters to all four, had to tell them apart.

**Seann Skell** on the river, in the west, is the great western river port, the seat of the western shire, where the Board's enforcers built their depot and An seachdamh rèisimeid was raised. **Seann Skell** on the coast, in the south, is the fishing harbour of the shipmasters' works and Brìde's house, where Comann nan Goibhnean-ceangail were chartered, An dàrna cabhlach and An naoidheamh rèisimeid were raised, and the altar-coal is drawn. The regimental clerks tell them apart by river and coast; the Board's letters say the western and the southern; Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, which has only one house of note at either, says Seann Skell and means the southern.

**Tobar dhearg** on the south coast is the town of the southern lighthouse and the red well, from whose light the last coal flame was carried to Brìde's altar. **Tobar dhearg** of the hills is the least of the five western pit towns, with one pit and forty hewers, which sent its coal to Cathair gheal for fish. Sceulachdan nan Tuathach call the second simply Tobar dhearg, having no need of the first; An Clàr Foscailte says of the hills.

## The months and the measures

The months are counted as before, from am Faoilleach to an Dùbhlachd, and the feasts of the year are kept on their old days: Lùnastal, at which Cathal was crowned; an t-Samhain; an Nollaig, on whose day Martha Greaves carried Athchuinge Dheireannach an Tionail to the council. The fuel is measured by the barrel, and An t-Ionmhas's measure is fixed on every barrel loaded under Cairt na h-Às-mhalairt. The coin is reckoned in purses. Distance on the roads is reckoned in miles, as the humans left them.
