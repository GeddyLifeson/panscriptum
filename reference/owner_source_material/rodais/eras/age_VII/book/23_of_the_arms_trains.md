What is told here of the war comes from the open record, the towns' reports and the Tuathaich tellings; the rest is sealed. What follows is what was seen and what was written down at the time: from a footplate on the coal line's western branch, and from a trestle table above a pit village where a woman kept a list.

## I. Of the Trains That Ran by Night

Eachann mac Ruairidh had driven on the coal line for three-and-thirty years and the dubhan trains for thirteen of them, and in all that time he had never once been asked to carry a crate he was not told the weight of. That changed on the second night of am Faoilleach, when the yard foreman at Muileann chrom came down the line with a paper in his hand and a lantern that threw more shadow than light and told him his engine would take on eight wagons before dawn, loaded by the Rail Board's own men, and that he was not, this once nor any night after, to ask what was in them.

"Weight's on the manifest," the foreman said, holding the paper up rather than handing it across, as though the handing across were itself a thing to be careful of. "That's all the manifest's for, this trip. You'll sign for the weight and not the goods."

Eachann had signed a good many papers in his time on the footplate without reading past the figure at the bottom, and he signed this one the same way, though he noted, as a man notes weather he means to remember, that the foreman did not meet his eye while he did it. The eight wagons came on through the small hours behind a screen of tarpaulin that the Rail Board's men kept up even inside the shed, working by shuttered lamps and speaking to one another, when they spoke at all, in the flat short sentences of men who have been told exactly how much to say and have decided to say less than that. Eachann's own second man, a broad quiet man of Muileann chrom who had ridden with him three years and asked him nothing that night either, watched the gauges in silence and once, when a case slipped an inch on its rollers and struck the wagon's floor with a sound too dull and too heavy for tools, looked across at Eachann in the lamplight and looked away again without a word passing between them.

They ran west before first light, the coal line proper past Muileann mhin and the goods-sheds at Cathair dhearg, and beyond the capital onto the western branch, single-tracked and slower, that the Rail Board had laid twenty years before to carry lime and slate down to the coast and had carried little enough of either since. It was dubhan in the tank now as it had been for thirteen years, and Eachann had long since stopped missing the coal's particular heat, though he still, on a cold footplate in the first mile, found himself listening for the crackle of a fire that was not there and hearing instead the flat, steady beat of the engine. The branch ran through country he had known since he was a boy riding with his own father on the coal trains, low hills going up into the higher ones, farms and a scatter of pit villages whose lamps, this deep in winter, were most of them out before his engine passed, and he drove it that first night, and every night after through the rest of the month, at a speed the branch's condition did not properly allow, because the manifest that rode with him now, whatever else it did not say, said plainly enough that speed mattered more than the branch's rails did.

By the middle of the month it had become his run and no other's, given him, he understood without being told outright, because the yard's masters had found him steady and asked no questions of him twice, which in that season had come to count for more than the years he had spent learning every gradient of the line by feel. He ran the double shift the yard had gone to that winter, out on the down run with the wagons and back light before noon, and slept what hours he could in the drivers' room at Muileann chrom with the other men of the arms trains, none of whom spoke much of the work either, though Eachann noticed that they had all, every one of them, taken to a particular silence about the eight wagons that was not quite the silence they kept about anything else, a silence with a kind of care folded into it, the way a man is careful crossing ice he has been told will bear him and has not tested for himself.

He did not open the crates. That, he told himself those first weeks, was no more than the ordinary discipline of the line, the same discipline that had him signing for coal tonnage he never counted sack by sack; and it was only later, well into the month, hauling the same eight wagons west through a night of driving sleet with the branch's rails slick under him and the manifest folded in his coat where it had ridden since the foreman first handed it across, that he let himself think plainly what any man on that footplate would have known from the weight alone and the care the Rail Board's men took loading it, that he was carrying arms west to Seann Bhral, and that a man does not load arms onto a train bound for a river port below the western hills unless he means, before long, to use them on the hills themselves.

At Seann Bhral, those first nights, he saw little of where the wagons' cargo went once it left his own charge. The quay there was new work, built out over the tidal mud in the two years since the river port had grown from a lime-boat's stopping-place into something the Board thought worth a covered shed and a length of proper stone, and the Rail Board's men unloaded by lantern into a lorry that always stood waiting with its engine already turning over, so that the whole business, from the last wagon's door swung open to the lorry's tail-lamp vanishing up the coast road, took less time than it took Eachann to turn his engine on the triangle below the shed and see it faced back east. He asked, the second week, where the lorry went, of a stevedore he had exchanged a civil word with more than once over a winter of shared cold mornings, and got for his answer only a look that told him plainly enough the question had been heard, weighed, and judged not worth the trouble of an answer, and he did not ask it a second time.

He thought of this the way he thought of the manifest, which was to say he thought of it and put it from him, because the alternative, the thing his thinking kept circling toward and would not quite land on, was to ask himself what he owed the men whose hills the line ran under, and he found, turning that question over on a cold footplate at three in the morning with thirty miles of bad rail still ahead of him, that he did not know the answer and was in no great hurry to find it before he had to. A driver of the Rail Board since he was nineteen, and his father before him a driver of coal for the same line under the same board, he had never in either man's time on the footplate known it to be any part of a driver's trade to know what his cargo was for. He told himself this on the second night, and again on the ninth, and by the seventeenth had stopped telling himself anything at all, and drove.

The eighteenth night brought him, an hour past the second lamp-post below the capital's edge, a company on the branch road below the line, marching in the dark with lanterns swung low against the wet and a corporal at their head whom Eachann, slowing for the level crossing at that point, recognised by the set of his shoulders before he made out his face: a steady, unremarkable man he had carried west once before on an ordinary passenger working, three years and more back, a man he had exchanged no more than a dozen words with in that whole journey and remembered chiefly for having asked him, once, an intelligent question about the line's gradients that most passengers never thought to ask. He did not know the man's name then and did not learn it now; he only lifted a hand from the regulator as the engine passed above the marching company, an old habit from lonelier runs, and the corporal, glancing up at the sound of the engine, lifted his own hand in answer without breaking step, and the company went on into the dark toward the west, and Eachann's train went on into the dark toward the west a little faster, since arms do not wait on marching men and the manifest in his coat had never once, in eighteen nights, allowed him to slow for anyone.

It was the night after that, the third-from-last night of the month by his own count, though he had by then stopped keeping the calendar with any care, that he came, an hour before the false dawn that time of year brings before the true one, upon the family walking north along the line.

He saw them a long way off, the way a man on a footplate sees anything on a straight run at night, as a paleness first and then a shape and only at the last a family: a man and a woman and, between them, two children small enough to be half-carried, walking the cinder path beside the rails because the branch road that season was deep in mud and the cinder path, packed hard by generations of platelayers, was not. They had a handcart with them, piled with what a family carries when it is carrying everything it owns at once, and they walked, Eachann saw as his lamp swept over them, with the particular steadiness of people who have already walked further than they meant to and have found that walking, once begun, is easier kept up than stopped. He knew them for Tuathaich before his lamp had fully passed over them, by the cut of the man's coat and by the way the woman carried the smaller child against her hip, a way of carrying he had seen at Caol mhòr's harbour a hundred times and never once, until that morning, seen on the coal line's western branch, going north, away from the hills the arms in his wagons were bound for.

He did not stop. In the half-second such things take a man on a footplate doing forty miles an hour with thirty more still to run and a manifest in his coat that named a time at Seann Bhral he could not miss, the reasons came to him ready-made: that a train running arms west did not slow for anyone on the line whatever the hour or whoever they were, that the family, whatever had put them on that path at that hour, were plainly bound the opposite way from wherever his own night's work would end, and that whatever had set a family walking north with a handcart in the dark of am Faoilleach was no business a driver could mend from a moving footplate in any case. All of this was true, and none of it, he found afterward, sitting in the drivers' room at Muileann chrom with a mug of something hot going cold in his two hands, was the reason he had not stopped, and the reason he had not stopped was one he did not put into words that night nor for a good while after, though he came, in the months the arms trains ran, to know it well enough by the feel of it, which was that stopping would have meant knowing something about the family that not stopping let him not know, and that a man who has agreed to sign for weight and not for goods finds, given the practice of a few weeks, that the same discipline serves for a good deal else besides.

He did not look back. The line ran straight enough there that he could have, in the lamp's last light before the curve, but he kept his eyes on the rail ahead and his hand on the regulator and let the family go by behind him into the dark, and drove on to Seann Bhral, where the Rail Board's men were waiting on the quay with lanterns and a covered lorry, exactly as they had waited every night that month, to take the eight wagons' cargo off his hands and into their own keeping, and ask him, as they always did, for nothing but his signature on the manifest, which he gave them, and turned his engine, and ran light back east before the true dawn came up grey over hills he had, by then, stopped letting himself look at from the footplate at all.

## II. Of the List at Àth leathan

Ruth Calder had kept lists her whole life, first her father's tally-books and then, these five years past, the Muileann àrsaidh agreement's own accounts, and she had thought, before the last week of am Faoilleach, that she knew the shape a list could take. She learned otherwise standing at the mouth of the road into Àth leathan on a morning so cold the ink in her pen thickened between names, watching the hewers of five towns come down out of the hills by paths no cart had ever used, with their pit-lamps lit at noon.

They came first from Cnoc thais, forty men and more under Tom Varley, down the drove track that ran along the ridge and had not carried so many feet at once, Ruth thought, since the Severance itself; then from Àth naomh, a smaller company but no less determined, the men walking two abreast where the path allowed it as though even now, arriving at another town's pit-head with nothing to defend but their own presence, they meant to be seen keeping order; then, through the afternoon and into a second cold dusk, the men of Muileann àrsaidh and Tobar dhearg of the hills in smaller parties, some of them boys of fourteen who had never cut a working face in their lives but whose fathers or brothers or, in two cases that Ruth wrote down and did not trust herself to think about further, whose whole households had already come, so that there was nobody left at home to keep them from it.

Sarah Dunmore sent down from Muileann àrsaidh, with the second of her town's parties, two cartloads of oatmeal and a barrel of salted fish that her own kitchen could ill spare, with a note pinned to the barrel's lid in a hand Ruth knew for hers, saying only that a company that had not eaten would not stand the walk home again whatever else the week brought, and asking no thanks and expecting, Ruth judged from the note's shortness, none given. Ruth set a boy to portioning it out at the pit-shed door, one bowl to a name checked off her own list, and found, doing the sum of oatmeal against mouths on the second evening, that Sarah's two cartloads would carry the gathering perhaps nine days at the ration she judged safe to set, and said nothing of that figure to anyone, since a number a company cannot mend by any labour of its own is not a number worth handing round a cold pit-shed at dusk.

Tobar dhearg's men, last of the five towns to arrive and fewest, came down a path so little used that two of the older hewers had to be sent ahead the day before to cut back the whin that had grown across it in the years since anyone had walked it in company; there were but eleven of them in all, every man the hill town's one pit could spare and still keep a face working, and their captain, a stooped man older than Tom Varley and older, Ruth thought, than the walk down should rightly have let him manage, set his lamp on her trestle table before he gave her his own name, as though the lamp itself, and not the man carrying it, were the thing that most wanted counting.

Why the lamps, lit and carried at noon when there was no working face at the end of any of these paths to light, Ruth did not ask any of them, because she did not need to. She had grown up among hewers and knew a hewer's habits the way she knew her own hands: a lit lamp carried openly, at noon, on a hill path, was not light a man needed to see by, it was a thing said without saying it, the way a company musters under its own colours rather than marching bare, and she understood, watching the first of Cnoc thais's lamps come down off the ridge in the grey early light, that the men carrying them meant the whole of the western hills to see, from every farmhouse window between here and the sea, that the pits' own men were coming to Àth leathan and coming as pit men, lamps lit, not as anything the council or the Board might afterward choose to call them.

She kept her list at a trestle table set up in the lee of the Àth leathan pit-shed, with Tom Varley beside her the first two days until Cnoc thais's own numbers were fully in and he judged his own presence better spent seeing to the byres, and she wrote down every name that came to her, town by town, with the age of each man or boy where she troubled to ask it and a mark against those who had brought their own tools, since tools, in the weeks that followed, proved harder to find than men willing to carry them. By the fourth day the pit-sheds at Àth leathan, three of them standing since before her father's father worked the seam, were full to their rafters with men sleeping on straw laid over the old floor timbers, and the byres behind the chapel, given over by families who had, most of them, already sent a son or a husband to be counted at her own table, held the overflow, cattle moved out into whatever shelter a hedge or a drystone wall could offer in a month that gave them precious little of either.

She did not, in all those days, let herself think overlong about what the list was for. It was, on its face, no more than a count: how many men, from which town, with what tools, so that whoever came after her with the harder questions, feeding them, arming what few of them could be armed, finding them somewhere to stand when the day came that everyone at Àth leathan now spoke of only as "when it comes" and never named further, would have a true number to work from rather than a guess. She told herself this each evening, going back over the day's names by lamplight in the small room above the pit-shed office that had been given her for the writing, and she believed it, mostly, and it was only on the sixth evening, with the list grown past four hundred names and the ink gone thick again in the cold, that Tom Varley came in from checking the last of the byres and told her, in the flat voice he had taken to using for anything he judged she needed to hear and would not thank him for, that the Board kept a list too.

"Whose is it," she asked, though she had already half guessed the answer from his voice before he gave it.

"Mine Board's, out of Cathair mhòr," he said. "A clerk of Seumas mac Eachainn's office let it slip to a carter at Seann Skell, and the carter told it to a man at Cnoc thais, and the man at Cnoc thais told me on the drove path coming down. Two names on it above every other, he said. Yours and mine."

She sat with the pen still in her hand and did not write anything for a moment, looking at the page of names she had taken all that week, four hundred and some men and boys of five towns who had walked to Àth leathan carrying lit lamps at noon because a lit lamp said what none of them had the words, or perhaps only the wish, to say outright; and the thought that had circled her all that winter came to her now fully formed, that a list is a strange kind of thing to be the author of, since the same discipline that let her set down four hundred names in a clean careful hand for the hills' own use was, on some other table in Cathair mhòr, being used to set down two names, hers and Tom's, for a use she did not need Tom to spell out further.

"Then they'll know where to look for us," she said, "when it comes."

"They knew that already," Tom said. "It's not as though we've been hiding at Àth leathan." He said it without much heat, the way a man states a fact he has already made his peace with, and went to see about the last of the straw for the newest arrivals, and Ruth sat on a while longer with her own list open before her, running her thumb down the column of names as though the touch of the page might tell her something the ink itself did not, before she closed the book and blew out the lamp and went down to see that the pit-shed fires, banked low against the cold, were not banked so low that a man might wake in the night and find himself colder than the walk down from his own hill farm had already left him.

Word of the regiments' setting out reached Àth leathan on the six-and-twentieth of am Faoilleach, carried up the western road by a carter who had watched them pass below Cathair dhearg on the three-and-twentieth and made the walk north as fast as a laden cart could manage once he had seen enough to know it was worth the telling. They had gone by the western road, he said, companies enough to fill it from verge to verge for the better part of an hour, their lorries running on dubhan the whole column's length, so that the road smelled, he told it, of a fuel the western hills had never burned a barrel of and never meant to, going west now ahead of the men who were to use it, to Seann Skell in the west where the enforcers had sat quartered these two years and more waiting for exactly this company to come and join them.

Ruth heard it standing at her trestle table with the day's last names not yet entered, and found, hearing it, that she felt none of the surprise she might have expected of herself a month before, only a kind of settling, the way a held breath settles once the thing waited for has at last, for good or ill, arrived. She looked down the pit-shed row at the lamps still burning here and there among the sleeping men, small steady flames against a cold that showed no sign yet of any spring; and she opened her book to a clean page, dipped her thickened pen, and began, because it was the only work she properly knew how to do and the only answer she had left to give a Board that had already, by its own list, given its answer to her, to count once more, town by town, how many men Àth leathan now held who had come to it carrying a lit lamp at noon and meant, whatever the road below Cathair dhearg was now carrying west to meet them, to be there still when it came.
