# Of the Leòid Ruling

*DE 18*

## I. Of the Wool Contract

The letter that began it was not addressed to Seumas mac Eachainn at all, and he read it only because the clerk whose table it belonged to was down with a chest cold and had asked him, as a favour between neighbours at the long table, to sort what came in until she was back on her feet.

It was from Tional nan Tuathach's wool mills at Doire ghlas, a contract of the plainest kind, three lines longer than it needed to be only because the mill's clerk had been taught to write a full sentence and could not bring himself to stop short of one: that the mill would take its winter coal from Cnoc thais and Muileann àrsaidh, at the price those towns asked, and no longer from Comhairle nam Mèinnean's yard at Cathair mhòr, Comhairle nam Mèinnean's price having risen twice in two years while the fires under the mill's own vats burned no hotter for it. Seumas read it twice, because the first time he did not believe the figure at the foot, and filed it in the tray marked for the master's own hand, because it plainly was not his to answer.

He was forty that spring, and had been four years Comhairle nam Mèinnean's clerk at Cathair mhòr, a post he had not asked for and had not thought, at four-and-twenty riding north with Iain Ruadh mac Nèill to watch a northern fire go out, that he would ever hold. Iain Ruadh sat two tables down still, grey now past red, half deaf in the ear nearer the door, and looked at the world through the coal he had spent his life measuring and found it, on the whole, unimproved by dubhan. He had not been north again since that first ride. He said the road was longer than it used to be, by which he meant that he was.

"There'll be more of those," Iain Ruadh said, when Seumas told him what the letter held, not looking up from the return he was totting. "The pit towns sell dear to us and cheap to their own kind. It's not a mystery. A man sells cheap where he must live after."

"It's the mills I'm thinking of. If Doire ghlas buys northern coal—"

"Then Cathair gheal will, and Cathair mhòr's own tanners after that, and in a twelvemonth every mill from here to the strait is burning coal that never paid An Crùn's fifth to anybody, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's yard stands full and nobody comes for it." Iain Ruadh set down his pen. "I've seen a seam go like that. Not all at once. A gallery here, a gallery there, until one quarter you look at the returns and the whole face is gone and you cannot say the day it went."

Seumas did not answer that, because there was no answer in it that would not sound as though he were arguing with a man thirty years his elder about a mountain the man had walked underground before Seumas was born. He went back to the tray, and by the time the regular clerk was back at her table the letter from Doire ghlas had three more like it behind it, from Cathair gheal, from a tannery at Ros gheal, from a brewer at Baile ìseal who wrote that he saw no shame in warming his own vats with whatever coal came cheapest and would take no lecture on the matter from a Board that had never once, in thirty years, carried coal north of the boundary stones for a Tuathach hearth to burn.

Seumas kept that last one on his own table longer than the others.

## II. Of the Quarter

The master of Comhairle nam Mèinnean at Cathair mhòr was Fearghas mac Aonghais, a broad, short-necked man of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own service who had risen to the mastership from the yard rather than from the mine office's tables, and who trusted a column of figures the way other men trusted a priest. He called for the northern returns himself in the third week of am Màrt, which he had not done in Seumas's four years at the table, and Seumas carried them in and stood while the master read.

It took him the better part of an hour to draw them properly: Comhairle nam Mèinnean's takings from every northern account, set against the same quarter of the year before, town by town, with the wool mills' new contract entered where it belonged and a note beside it in his own hand, because a note seemed to him owed. When the sum stood at the foot of the page it was down by a quarter on the year before, and by more than a quarter against the quarter before that, and no part of the fall lay anywhere but in the five holdout towns' own coal finding buyers that had once bought from Comhairle nam Mèinnean and now did not.

"A quarter," Fearghas said, when Seumas laid the page before him, and said nothing else for a while, turning the page as though a second look might find him a different sum on it. "In how long?"

"Eighteen months, master, near enough. It was steady until the wool mills went over, and it has moved faster from that month on."

"Since when has anyone in the north had coin enough to buy coal at all, that An Crùn never saw a fifth of?"

"Since the fuel came, master. They sell the dubhan they cannot use themselves, and buy each other's coal with the coin it brings." Seumas had thought about how to say this on the ride in, turning the sentence over as a man turns a coin to see it is not counterfeit, and it came out flatter than he had meant it, which he thought afterwards was as well. "It's an honest trade between them. I've no figures that say otherwise."

Fearghas mac Aonghais looked at him for a moment in a way Seumas had not been looked at before in that office, the way a man looks at a tool to see whether it will do the next job as well as it did the last.

"Nobody asked you whether it was honest," he said, and it was not said unkindly either. "You're asked what it costs Comhairle nam Mèinnean. You've told me. Go and find me Breith Leòid. The whole of it, not the digest. I want the words she used."

## III. Of the Ruling Read Again

Breith Leòid had lain in Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own press for eighty years, copied twice since, once when the press at Seann Bhral burned and once, later, for no reason the docket gave, and Seumas found the newer copy within the hour and carried it to the master's room himself rather than send it by a runner, because he wanted, without admitting it even to himself, to hear how it was read.

Eilidh nic Leòid had ruled, sitting at Seann Bhral in the eighth year of the old count, that the land the Tuathaich held under the humans' title passed to An Crùn, and that the right to work it stayed with the holders and their heirs. The rulings of the circuit that followed on it, copied into the margin of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's press copy in three different hands, had settled the rest: that the holders' right ran with the payment of An Crùn's fifth and failed with it; and that nothing in the ruling bound Comhairle nam Mèinnean to carry any holder's coal it did not choose to carry. It had been written, Seumas judged from the language, to settle where the north should stand when the humans' titles fell, and it had gone on being cited for eighty years in every land case the north had brought, because it gave the holders their right in one clause and, read with its margins, let Comhairle nam Mèinnean withdraw its custom and keep its claim both.

Fearghas mac Aonghais read it through once, slowly, moving his lips over the harder clauses as a man does who reads more figures than sentences, and set it down.

"Comhairle nam Mèinnean stopped carrying from the western pits when the branch to Àth leathan was closed," he said. "Before the fuel."

"Twenty years, master, near enough. The branch closed in the sixty-eighth year of the old count. They went on paying the fifth all the same, by cart and by boat, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean entered it as arrears, until the agreement."

"And since the agreement?"

"Not from the western towns. Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh, Tobar dhearg and Àth leathan have paid nothing to Comhairle nam Mèinnean's account since the agreement they signed among themselves. I have the ledger, if the master wants the figure."

"I don't want the figure. I know the figure." Fearghas mac Aonghais stood, which he did not often do at his own table, and went to the window, which looked out over the yard where a dubhan tank stood being filled from a cart, and said nothing for long enough that Seumas began to wonder whether he was dismissed. "Under her ruling, the right lapsed the day the fifth stopped, whatever the reason it stopped. It doesn't ask whether Comhairle nam Mèinnean's reason for not carrying was a good one. It only asks whether the fifth came in. It didn't."

"They would say Comhairle nam Mèinnean stopped carrying first, master, and the fifth was owed on carrying."

"They can say what they like. It isn't in the ruling. I've read it twice now and it isn't in it." He turned back from the window. "Write the report. The northern revenue down a quarter in eighteen months, the cause named plain: the holdout pits underselling Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own coal to Tuathaich buyers who were Comhairle nam Mèinnean's custom until they had somewhere cheaper to go. And the remedy, since the council will ask for one whether I give it or not: that under Breith Leòid the western seams are An Crùn's to take back, the holders having let the fifth lapse these two years, and that Comhairle nam Mèinnean declare them so and order the pits shut until such time as they are worked again under An Crùn's own hand."

Seumas wrote it that same evening, at the long table after the others had gone, with the lamp turned low to spare the oil, and every figure in it was true. He had drawn them himself and checked them twice, and he would have sworn to any of them before the council or before Manannan's own altar. It was only when the report was fair-copied and sealed, and he sat looking at his own name at the foot of it as the hand that drew the figures, that he let himself think, for the length of one candle burning a finger's breadth, about what a true figure could be made to carry that the man who drew it had never meant it to carry. He had written that the north bought its own coal with dubhan's coin. He had not written that Comhairle nam Mèinnean had itself stopped carrying that coal first, and taken the western towns' custom away before it complained of losing it; that sentence was in the ruling and not in his report, because the master had not asked for it, and a clerk's figures went where he was told to send them and no further.

He thought of the brewer's letter at Baile ìseal, still in his own tray, that no coal had gone north of the boundary stones in thirty years, and put the thought away, because there was nothing to be done with it that would not cost him his place and change nothing else at all.

## IV. Of the Order Carried North

Comhairle nam Mèinnean's decree went out under Fearghas mac Aonghais's seal on the twentieth of an Giblean: that the western seams, their right to work them lapsed under Breith Leòid for two years' unpaid fifth, were declared An Crùn's; that the pits at Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh, Tobar dhearg and Àth leathan were ordered shut from that day; and that any coal raised from them after the order was coal of An Crùn, taken without right, and answerable at law.

It went north by Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own courier to the maor of the western Seann Skell, who read it standing at his own door because the courier would not wait, and who sent for the town's clerk before he had finished reading it a second time, because he judged, rightly, that a paper of that weight wanted two witnesses to its arrival rather than one.

"They'll not shut," the clerk said, when he had read it in his turn. "Not for a paper."

"No," said the maor. "I don't suppose they will."

He was right in both halves of it. The word reached Muileann àrsaidh within the week, by a carrier who had it from a Cnoc thais man who had it from the maor's own clerk, and the five towns' answer, when it came, came not to Comhairle nam Mèinnean but to each other, in the form of a letter passed hand to hand and signed by none of them formally, that the pits would go on working until the seams themselves were spent, An Crùn having never carried their coal, never paid a wage in their pits and never, in all the years since the agreement, sent so much as a surveyor to say what it thought the seams were worth. They had stopped paying the fifth, the letter said, when Comhairle nam Mèinnean stopped earning it. If Comhairle nam Mèinnean wished the fifth restored, it might restore the carrying first, and the fifth would follow the same week.

Both were true, and both sides knew both were true, and neither the truth of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's claim nor the truth of the towns' answer moved either an inch from where it had stood since the wool mills of Doire ghlas first went over to northern coal. The letter never reached Cathair mhòr in any form Comhairle nam Mèinnean would call an answer; it was read at Muileann àrsaidh, at Cnoc thais and at Àth leathan, and its substance, rather than its words, made its way south by the same carriers' road the order had come north on, so that within a month it was generally known at Cathair mhòr, without ever having been formally received, that the western towns did not mean to obey.

The five towns met, as they had met before, in Sarah Dunmore's kitchen at Muileann àrsaidh and then in the pit-shed when the kitchen would not hold them all, and Ruth Calder read Comhairle nam Mèinnean's order aloud from the paper a carrier had brought, twice through, once fast and once slow, so that no one in the shed could afterwards say he had not heard every word of it. Tom Varley wanted the letter answered line for line, clause against clause, in the manner of a man who has read a great many contracts and does not trust a paper that goes unchallenged. Ruth said Comhairle nam Mèinnean had not asked for a clause-by-clause answer and would not read one if it were sent; Comhairle nam Mèinnean had asked for the pits shut, and the only answer that mattered was whether the coal came up on the morning after the order's date. It came up. Nobody at Muileann àrsaidh put down a tool, and the shed voted, without much debate and without a show of hands anyone troubled to count, to go on as before and let Comhairle nam Mèinnean make the next move, since it plainly meant to make one regardless of anything the towns wrote in reply.

"They'll send men," said Sarah Dunmore, who had said little through the meeting and was heard the more for it. "A paper that isn't obeyed gets men sent after it. It always has."

Nobody answered her, because nobody could say she was wrong, and the meeting broke up into the wet evening with the matter no further settled than it had been when it opened, except that every person in the shed now carried, alongside the day's ordinary work, the private certainty that the paper nailed to nothing yet would shortly be nailed to something.

## V. Of Somhairle mac Lachlainn

Somhairle mac Lachlainn had come up through Comhairle nam Mèinnean's enforcers by the same road most of them came, from a farm too small to keep a second son, into a company that had existed for forty years to see harbour-dues paid and smugglers' coal weighed at Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own scale. In eighteen years he had made himself the man other captains sent for when a thing wanted doing quietly, whether or not it stayed quiet once he had started on it. He was two-and-forty that autumn, broad through the shoulder in the way a farm makes a man rather than a drill-yard, with a scar through one eyebrow from a fight at Ros dhomhain's quay he never spoke of and a reputation, among the men under him, for giving an order once and expecting it obeyed without a second telling.

He was sent for at Cathair mhòr in the last week of an t-Sultain, called before Fearghas mac Aonghais and a councillor of the Rìoghachd he did not know by sight, and told, in fewer words than he had expected the matter to take, that a company of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's enforcers was to go north to the western Seann Skell before the winter closed the roads, and that he was to command it.

"How many men, master?"

"Forty. What Comhairle nam Mèinnean can spare from the southern stations without leaving them bare."

"And their orders, once we're there?"

"To see the shutting order held. No pit at Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh, Tobar dhearg or Àth leathan is to raise coal after the day you arrive. You'll post men at the pit-heads. You'll not close the town, nor the road, nor any house that isn't a pit-head. That's the whole of it."

"And if they raise coal anyway."

The councillor answered that one, before Fearghas mac Aonghais could. "Then you'll have shown Comhairle nam Mèinnean's order cannot be held with forty men and a captain, and the council will decide what it wants to do about that. Your business, mac Lachlainn, is to hold the order, not to fight a war nobody has declared."

Somhairle mac Lachlainn took the order as it was given, because it was not his place to say that a company sent to stand at five pit-heads in a country that had buried its dead against Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own kind within living memory was already more than a policing matter, whatever the council chose to call it. He asked for, and was given, forty men of his own choosing rather than whoever the southern stations cared to spare, choosing men who had served under him before over younger men who had not, which the master noted and did not question.

Two days ahead of the company he rode north alone, to see the ground before he brought men onto it, and crossed the boundary stones on a grey morning with rain coming and going in the west, much as another man had crossed them sixteen years before on a different errand. He did not stop at the stones. He had crossed them a dozen times in Comhairle nam Mèinnean's service, weighing coal at a scale or carrying a summons, and had never yet been north of them with a company at his back, and he found, riding alone through the same bare ridges Seumas mac Eachainn had ridden as a young man, that the difference sat on him more heavily than he had thought it would.

He lay that night at a road-house south of the western Seann Skell, a low place with a dubhan lamp over the door and a landlord who asked no questions beyond what he was owed for the bed, and lay awake a while going over the ground he had ridden and the ground still ahead of it, in the way a man does the night before a piece of work he has not done before, whatever its size. He had put men in harm's way often enough, at a smuggler's landing or a quay brawl gone past reason, and had never yet lost one; he set some store by that record, privately, the way another man might set store by a relic, and did not care to think too closely about how far it could be made to stretch. Forty men at five pit-heads in a country that had not seen a company under arms since before his own grandfather's time was not a smuggler's landing, and he knew it, and had said as much to the councillor at Cathair mhòr in words careful enough not to sound like a refusal, because refusing had never once, in eighteen years of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's service, been a road that led anywhere he wanted to go.

He thought, before he slept, of the scar over his eyebrow, taken at Ros dhomhain's quay from a smuggler's boat-hook in a scuffle that had gone wrong in the space of ten seconds and never once, in the years since, made him careful in the way other men's scars seemed to make them careful. It had taught him instead to move first and explain after, which had served him well enough among smugglers and harbour thieves, men who understood a blow and a purse and little else. He did not know yet what it would teach him among five towns that understood a great deal more than that, and meant, plainly, to go on understanding it in their own time and by their own lights, whatever paper Comhairle nam Mèinnean sent north to tell them otherwise.

## VI. Of the Company at the Western Seann Skell

The company came up the western road on the sixteenth of an Dàmhair, forty men and their baggage-carts, in a rain that had set in for the day and showed no sign of lifting, and the western Seann Skell watched them come from doorways and from the harbour wall without a stone thrown and without a word of welcome, which Somhairle mac Lachlainn, riding at their head, judged the worse of the two receptions a town could give.

He quartered them at the old customs shed above the harbour, the only building in the town large enough and empty enough to hold forty men, and paid the maor's clerk for the use of it out of Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own purse rather than requisition it, which the clerk noted in his book without comment and which Somhairle did for reasons he would not have found easy to set out plainly even to himself, beyond a sense that a company that paid its way stood on firmer ground than one that did not, in a country where it had no ground of its own to stand on.

He sent men to Muileann àrsaidh, to Cnoc thais and to the three smaller towns within the week, not to close the pits by force, which forty men scattered across five towns could not have done in any case, but to stand at each pit-head with Comhairle nam Mèinnean's order nailed to the winding-gear and to report, twice weekly, whether coal came up. At Cnoc thais, Tom Varley met the two men sent there at the pit-head himself, read the order nailed to the post without a word, and told them they were welcome to stand there as long as they liked, the coal coming up whether they stood or not, and that he would see they were given tea at midday like anyone else standing about in the wet, since it was no fault of theirs that they had been sent.

They took the tea. The coal came up.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, at Muileann chaol, wrote the whole of it into the day's page when word reached her, some ten days after the company had marched, in fewer words than the matter seemed to her to deserve, and added at the foot of the entry, in the small close hand she kept for the things she judged worth remembering past their own day, that it was the first company the kingdom had sent armed toward the north since An Dealachadh itself, near ninety years gone, and that no one now living who had signed the truce at the boundary stones was left to say whether it was meant as more than a policing matter, or whether anyone, on either side of the stones, still believed that it could stay one.

Seumas mac Eachainn read the report of the company's quartering at his own table at Cathair mhòr, three weeks after he had written the figures that had, by a road he had not chosen and could not now shorten, put it on the road north, and found in himself no surprise at all, which troubled him rather more than surprise would have done. His figures had been true. He had drawn them carefully, checked them twice, and would draw the next quarter's the same way, because that was the whole of his office and he did not know how to hold any other. But he understood now, reading of forty men quartered above a harbour he had once ridden into as a young man with Iain Ruadh's mare setting the pace, that a true figure asked to serve a purpose would serve it faithfully, and that the purpose was never his to choose, only the figure, and that he had known this from the evening he sat with the report before him and the candle burning down, and had sent it anyway.

He wrote the next quarter's returns without adding anything the master had not asked for. It was, he told himself, the whole of what a clerk owed the truth: to draw it exactly, and to leave what was done with it to hands that were not his.
