## I. Of the Night near Midsummer

So the old people of the north tell it, in both their tongues, and they tell it the same way at the harbour wall of Caol mhòr and at the hearths of the hill farms: there was a man who had failed for ten years.

Every season had seen him fail. He had tried the spoil that the pits tip down the hillsides, the grey waste that will not burn for the stone in it, and the black slurry that settles in the sumps, and the dust swept up from the floors of the coal-sheds. These things he had boiled and dried, pressed them and baked them, mixed them with this and with that, with things the tellers do not name because they do not know them. Most of what he made would not light. What would light went out, or smoked a room blind, or cracked in the fire and threw its sparks across the floor. The tellers give no count of his trials. They say only that for years nobody thought to ask how the work went, because the answer was always the same.

Nobody in the south knew his name that summer. Nobody at Cathair dhearg was watching him, and the clerks of Comhairle nam Mèinnean, who counted every sack that came up out of the vein, had no line for what went down the tip. His name was Dubhan. His people were the Tuathaich, whom An Dealachadh had pushed to the island's northern edge, and the coal had never been theirs to inherit. What he had to work with was what the kingdom threw away.

The summer of that year came in dry and dear. In the north, at midsummer, the sky never goes wholly dark: the light goes down behind the hills in the north-west, and it runs along under the rim of the world as a green-grey band, and it comes up again in the north-east before a man has properly slept. On such a night, near the longest day, he banked his fire for the night as he had banked it on a hundred nights before. He set his crucible in the heart of it, packed with pit waste, and covered the whole with ash and turves so that it would burn low and long. Then he lay down beside it and slept.

That is all the telling gives of the night. There is no speech in it. The tellers do not say what he hoped, and they will not guess.

In the morning he raked the ash away. The crucible had not cracked. In it, where the waste had been, lay a cake of something black and close-grained and heavy for its size, with a dull skin on it like the bloom on a sloe. He broke it with a hammer on the hearthstone, and it broke clean. He laid a piece of it on the embers.

It took the fire slowly, the way good coal takes it, and it burned with a low red heat and a thick yellow smoke that stank of tar and bad eggs. There was no cracking and no spitting. At last it fell to a fine grey ash, and the ash was cold before the man had moved from where he knelt.

So, the tellers say, he broke another piece and laid it on, and watched that burn too.

The north keeps the day as the day the fire came. The years of this age are counted from that morning, which was {{date:VI-0001}}. It was a rough thing, what lay in the crucible, and it was no trade and no wealth to anyone. Nobody bought it or sold it. It was one man's answer to a need that the people who ruled the island had not yet looked in the face. In the north they still say of a thing that works at last after long failing, *thàinig an teine*, the fire came, and a child who is slow to walk and then walks is said to have banked his fire.

## II. Of the Dry Summer and the Fourth Floor

Of the kingdom as it stood that summer its own record has a great deal to say, and none of it is cheerful.

Mairead reigned at Cathair dhearg. She had come to the throne heir to a kingdom whose wealth was plainly running out, and the running-out had not stopped for her crowning. The lowest galleries of An Sloc Mòr lay under water, because the pumps cost more to run than the coal beneath them was worth; the minute of Comhairle nam Mèinnean called it a pause, and the pause was now in its tenth year. The yield of the upper galleries fell each quarter by a little. Its clerks entered the little in a firm hand.

Coal-fuel was dear everywhere. At Fèill Muileann òg its price had doubled in the autumn before, and the court of the fair had fixed a ceiling it could not hold. All that summer the dealers sold above it, and the court fined them, and the dealers paid the fines and put them on the price. At Cnoc fhiadhaich, in the pit country, the households still went up onto the spoil-heaps at dusk with baskets to pick out the black among the grey, and the watchmen posted there, being men of the town, still saw nothing.

In the capital the street-lamps that the humans had set up, which had burned coal since they were raised, were lit later in the evening that year and put out earlier in the night, and the lamp-men lit fewer of them in the lanes behind the fish-quays and under the walls. The poorer streets were darker than they had been in the memory of anyone living in them. People who had to go out after dark in those streets went in twos.

At Achadh dhomhain, where Comann nan Gualadairean kept its hall, the day of binding came round in the spring as it always had, and the masters sat in their places in the long room with the oath-stone on the table in front of them, and no family brought a son or a daughter to be bound. It had happened first in the year of Mairead's crowning, and in more years since than not. That first year the masters had waited until noon. This year they waited an hour, and then the clerk wrote the day into the book with nothing under it, and the masters went home.

The census had been published seven years before, openly, for the first time in the kingdom's history. It had changed nothing in the frame of things, except that the north now knew in print what it had known already.

In the north, Tional nan Tuathach sat at Caol mhòr under Daniel Frayne, as it had sat under one speaker or another since the first years after An Dealachadh, with no standing in any court of the kingdom and minute-books that ran unbroken from its first wet evening. It met that summer on its usual days. Its minutes speak of the herring, of the wool, of a roof at the school at Doire ghlas and of the price of coal. They say nothing of any fire in any crucible, because nobody had yet brought word of one to Caol mhòr.

And at Muileann chaol, in the eastern hills, the record of all these things was kept.

Leabharlann Muileann chaol stands above the river on the edge of the narrow mill town that has lived on it since the new kingdom was young. The old building, where the keeper and the chronicler have their rooms, is of the grey hill stone, with small windows and deep sills. The new wing, raised when the shires' chests were called in, is of stone from An Sloc Mòr, and it is darker, and it has four floors.

The shires' chests filled three of them. The fourth was empty. It had been left for what had not been sent, and in the summer of that year it was still waiting.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, who was Seanchaidh na Rìoghachd and had been since Tormod Pinn died at his desk, worked in the old building on the side that looked over the river. She was a woman of some fifty years that summer, small and straight, with ink in the creases of her right hand that no soap had moved in twenty years. The room had been Tormod Pinn's and still had his chair in it, which she did not use, and his inkstand, which she did. From her window she could see the paper-mill's wheel turning in the race, and beyond it, a mile down the valley, the ford at Àth dhomhain, where every reader bound for an Leabharlann crossed.

It was her habit, and she had made it the office's habit, to write at the foot of each year a note of what the year had kept from her. Her first such note had been about the census. The census clerks in the northern shires had been given their figures and had not counted them, and the figures they had been given were the figures of Tional nan Tuathach. The kingdom's first honest census had been honest in the north because the Tuathaich had done the counting for it. She had written that down in plain words, and nobody in the council had written to her about it, which she took for leave to go on.

In the months of that summer she was doing the thing that a chronicler does most of the time, which is reading. The shires' chests had come in over half a dozen years, and the copyists had been at the work of listing them ever since. That summer they finished the last of the lists. Ailig mac Thormoid, the eldest of the copyists, a thin man with a cough he blamed on the new wing's dust, brought her the tally on a single sheet in the second week of an t-Iuchar.

"One thousand and six chests," he said. "Every shire has sent something, except the ones that haven't."

"And the one that burned its chest in the yard."

"We've put Seann Tarr down as sent, keeper's orders. Sent to the fire." He laid the sheet on her desk. "The fourth floor is as it was."

She went up with him that afternoon, because she had not been up there since the spring. The stair in the new wing was steep and the treads were still sharp at the edges. On the first three floors the chests stood in their rows with their shire marks painted on the lids, and the air smelt of dry wood and old paper and a little of mice. On the fourth floor there was nothing. The windows had shutters and the shutters were closed, and the light came through their slats in bars across a floor that had been swept and had nothing on it to sweep. Their feet were loud on the boards.

"Mòrag used to say we would fill it in her lifetime," Ailig said.

"Mòrag used to say a great many things." Beathag walked the length of the room to the far wall and back. Nothing had been sent from the north. The minute-books of Tional nan Tuathach were at Caol mhòr, and the schools' registers were in the schools, and the vigil-books of Baile chrom were wherever the people of Baile chrom kept them, and no one had asked for any of them. That was the fourth floor, and the kingdom had built it on purpose. She had understood that from the first, and it still made her tired to stand in it.

On the way down Ailig said, "The carriers are late this week."

"The carriers are late every week."

"They say it's the price of fodder. Everything's the price of something."

## III. Of the Carrier's Word

Dòmhnall mac Ailein carried on the library road. He brought up paper and ink and lamp-oil from the landing at Ceann dhomhain, and the letters of the capital, and in winter the ration of coal for an Leabharlann, which grew smaller every year and dearer every quarter. He was a big slow man with a big slow horse, and he had been bringing things up the hill to Muileann chaol for longer than Beathag had been chronicler. Talk came out of him while he unloaded, and nobody at an Leabharlann had ever found a way to stop it.

It was he who first brought the word, on a hot afternoon in an Lùnastal, while he stacked reams against the wall of the yard.

"There's a thing in the north," he said, "that the Tuathaich have got hold of."

Beathag was in the yard because the carrier's cart was the nearest thing to news that came up the hill, and she had learnt long before to be there when it did. "What sort of thing?"

"A fire out of spoil." He set a ream down and straightened his back. "Out of the tip. I had it off a drover at the landing, and he had it off a boatman who'd been up the coast. There's a man up there, a Tuathach, has made a fuel out of what the pits throw away, and it burns."

"Everything burns if you put enough under it."

"That's what I said." He looked pleased at the agreement. "But the drover said no, it burns on its own, like coal. Stinks worse than a tannery, he said, but it burns."

"Who is the man?"

Dòmhnall thought about it. He was an honest carrier, which meant that he did not like to give a thing he had not been given. "The drover had a name for him. Dubhan, he said, or near it. It might have been Dubhain. The boatman said it one way and the drover another, and I've said it both ways since, so I couldn't swear."

"And where in the north?"

"The drover didn't know. The boatman had it from Caol mhòr, where some pedlar had a cake of the stuff on the market, but that's only where the boats are." He lifted another ream. "They'll tell you anything, the Tuathaich, in their own tongue, and nothing in ours. Or the other way about. I never know which."

She wrote it down that evening in her day-book, which was her own and not the kingdom's: *Dòmhnall mac Ailein: a fuel of the north made from pit waste, said to burn like coal. A man's name given with it, Dubhan or Dubhain. From a drover, from a boatman, from Caol mhòr.* She looked at it a good while, then underlined *from* three times, and went to bed.

The next week she asked Dòmhnall whether he had heard any more. He had not. The week after, he had heard that the fuel was made of tar, which she did not believe, and the week after that, that it was a trick of the Tuathaich to sell their spoil to fools, which she believed less. By the end of an Lùnastal the story had come up the library road four or five times in different mouths, and in every mouth it was a little larger and no nearer.

She said as much to Raghnall mac Mhuirich, the keeper, when she met him in the yard at the end of the month. He was three years younger than she, a tall stooping man who had been Mòrag nic Coinnich's deputy for most of his working life and had become keeper the summer before. He was still getting used to the room, and he spent his days as he had always spent them, in the stacks with a pencil behind his ear.

"The carriers have a fuel in the north," she said. "Made of spoil."

"So I hear. Ailig told me. Ailig tells me everything, eventually." Raghnall took the pencil from behind his ear and looked at it, as if it might have news of its own. "Have you seen any?"

"No."

"Has anyone you trust seen any?"

"Dòmhnall mac Ailein trusts a drover who trusts a boatman."

"Then there's nothing to keep." He put the pencil back. "Beathag, if there is a fuel, someone will write it down. Comhairle nam Mèinnean will write it down, or the court of the fair will write down that it was sold, or the Tuathaich will write it in their minute at Caol mhòr, and one day, if anyone sends the writing, it will come here and we will keep it. That is what we are for. An Leabharlann keeps. It does not chase."

"I am not an Leabharlann," she said. "I am the chronicler."

"You are the chronicler in an Leabharlann." He said it kindly. "It comes to the same in the end, when the council reads the bills."

## IV. Of the Pedlar at the Ford

The market of Muileann chaol is held in the yard of an Leabharlann, as it has been since the town was given its charter, and on the market day in the middle of an t-Sultain the yard was full of stalls under the chestnut trees: paper and pens and binders' thread, eggs and cheese from the hill farms, knives, pots, a man selling ribbons, a woman selling honey out of a crock with a spoon for tasting. The copyists came down from their tables to buy, because the market was the only day in the month when anyone came up the hill with anything but paper.

Beathag spent the morning at her desk with the shutters half closed against the sun, going through the letters of the northern maoir for the year, and she did not go down until the afternoon, when the stalls were being packed.

Ailig mac Thormoid met her at the foot of the stair. He had a slip of paper in his hand, one of the narrow slips the copyists use for marking a place in a chest, and he was excited, which was rare in him.

"There was a Tuathach at the market," he said. "A pedlar. Needles and pins and buttons, and a bit of lace from Doire ghlas. And he had a cake of the black stuff wrapped in sacking, the stuff Dòmhnall talks about."

"Where is he?"

"Gone. He went at noon. He was for the landing, he said, and the boats south." Ailig held out his slip. "He burned a piece of it for us, on the flagstone by the pump. He wanted a penny a look, and then he didn't, because everyone was looking anyway. I wrote down what he called it."

She took the slip. Ailig wrote a good copying hand and a poor hurried one, and this was the hurried one. It gave the name of the fuel, or of the man, it was not clear which, spelled a third way, different from both of Dòmhnall's.

"Is that how he said it?"

"That's how I heard it. He said it quick, in the northern way. I asked him to say it again and he said it again and it sounded different. Then he laughed and said we could call it what we liked, it'd burn the same."

"Did it burn?"

"It burned," Ailig said. "Slow. Red, with no colour at the edge, not like ours. And it stank." He wrinkled his nose at the memory of it. "The whole yard stank. The honey-woman packed up. The ash is still there by the pump, if you want it."

She went and looked at the ash. It was a small grey heap on the flagstone, fine as flour, and when she crouched and put her finger in it, it was cold and left a grey mark on her skin and nothing else. It smelt of old smoke. Anything in the world might have made it.

Then, because she was Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, she put on her boots and went down the valley to the ford.

It is a mile from an Leabharlann to Àth dhomhain, downhill all the way, on a road that the carts have worn into two stony ruts with grass between them. The afternoon was warm, and the dust of the road came up round her skirts, and the river on her left ran low and brown between its stones after the dry summer. At the ford the ferryman's boy was sitting on the bank with his feet in the water. Yes, he said, a Tuathach had come down with a pack, round about noon. He had waded over, because he said the ferry was dear, and gone on along the far bank towards the landing at Ceann dhomhain. He'd have caught the evening boat if it went, and it went most evenings.

"Did he have anything with him? A black thing, wrapped in sacking?"

The boy shrugged. "He had a pack. I didn't look in it. You don't, with the Tuathaich." He considered her. "He gave me a button."

She stood on the bank for some time, looking at the far side of the ford, where the road went up between alders and out of sight. It was three miles to the landing and the boat would have gone. She was fifty years old and she had come down in her indoor boots. She turned and walked back up the hill to an Leabharlann, and it was dusk when she came into the yard, and someone had swept the ash from the flagstone by the pump.

## V. Of the Letter to the Maor and the Foot of the Year

In the first week of an Dàmhair she wrote to the maor of Caol mhòr.

She wrote it three times before she was satisfied, and the copy she kept is the third. It is a short letter and a correct one. It says that the chronicler of the kingdom has heard report, from carriers and from a pedlar of the north, of a fuel made from the waste of the pits, burning in the manner of coal; that the report names a man of the north, whose name is variously given; that she has not seen the fuel and can find no one who has seen it made; and that she would be grateful if the maor, or any clerk of his, could send her a piece of it by the carrier, with a line to say where it came from and who had made it, so that she might set down in the record what was true. She signed it with her name and her office, and sealed it with the office's seal, which was pressed with coal ink as every seal of the kingdom was, and gave it to Dòmhnall mac Ailein for the landing and the post north.

"Caol mhòr," Dòmhnall said, looking at the direction. "That's a long way for a letter to go and come back."

"It is sixty miles and more. A letter can do it in ten days."

"A letter can," he said. "An answer's another thing."

No answer came in ten days, or in twenty. At the end of an Dàmhair the rains came in from the west, the first of the autumn, and the library road went to mud, and for a week Dòmhnall's cart could not get up the last hill and the reams came up on a sledge. The coal for an Leabharlann came up with them, the first of the winter's ration, and it was less than the year before and had cost the council more. Raghnall called the copyists together in the long room of the old building and told them that there would be no fire in the reading-room before the middle of an t-Samhain, and none after dark at all, and that anyone who wished to copy in the evenings could do it at home. Ailig coughed and said nothing. There had never been a fire in the stacks, and never a lamp, because of the old condition on the first loft of papers that nothing in it should be burned. The town had kept that condition when it had kept little else.

Beathag asked Dòmhnall each week whether there was a letter from the north for her, and each week he said there was not.

"They don't answer letters, up there," he said. "Not ours."

"The maor is the kingdom's man."

"Then he's a kingdom's man living among Tuathaich," Dòmhnall said. "And that's a man who has learnt not to answer letters."

In the middle of an t-Samhain she went to Raghnall. He was in his room, which had been Mòrag's, with a shawl round his shoulders and a book open on the desk in front of him, reading by the last of the daylight at the window. It was a little after three in the afternoon. He had not lit his lamp.

"I have had no answer from Caol mhòr," she said.

"I had not thought you would." He closed the book on his finger. "You want to write the fuel into the year."

"I want to write the year. The fuel is in it. Or the talk of the fuel is in it. I don't know which, and I don't know how to find out from here, and I don't know who else will, because Comhairle nam Mèinnean has sent no one north and its clerks will not write down what they have not counted. If I leave it out, the year is short by a thing that half the carriers of the library road are talking about. If I put it in, I am putting in a drover's tale."

"You can leave it for next year. If it is real it will still be real."

"And if it is real, the record will begin a year late, and someone at this desk after me will want to know why."

Raghnall looked at her over the book and said nothing. The light was going out of the window behind him while they talked, and his face was growing hard to see.

"You will not write what you have not seen," he said.

"No."

"And you will not leave it out."

"No."

"Then you had better find a way to do both," he said. "It is your office, Beathag, not mine. I only keep the rooms." He opened the book again, though he could scarcely have read it in that light. "Write what you have. Write that it is what you have. That is what the note at the foot of the year is for, isn't it? You invented it. Use it."

She wrote the note on {{date:VI-0002}}.

That was the day the office closed the year's record, as it did every year a month before the shortest day, so that the copyists could make the fair copy for the council before the roads closed. It was a still, grey day, and raw, with the smoke of the town lying flat in the valley and the mill-wheel stopped, because the dry year had left the race too low to turn it. By the middle of the afternoon the light in her window was gone. She lit her lamp early, an oil lamp with a glass chimney that had been Tormod Pinn's, and the room smelt of hot oil and of the damp coming through the shutters.

The year's record lay in front of her in its loose leaves, forty-one pages in her own hand. Mairead's reign, and the council's sittings, and the orders of Comhairle nam Mèinnean, and the price of coal-fuel at the fair and the ceiling unheld. The day of binding at Achadh dhomhain with nothing under it. The chests counted, one thousand and six, and the fourth floor empty. The watchmen at Cnoc fhiadhaich. The coal ration. The rains. It was a thin year, as the years had been thin for a long while, and every page of it was about something running out.

She turned to the foot of the last leaf, where the note would go, and sat a long time with the pen dry.

She had three spellings of the name, and she did not know which of them was right. There were Dòmhnall's two, as the drover said it and as the boatman said it, and there was Ailig's slip with the pedlar's word, written in a hurry by a man who had heard it said quick in the northern way. No letter had come from the maor. All else she had was a cold grey smear of ash that someone had swept away. She had not seen the fuel, and she had not seen it burn, and she had not spoken to anyone who had seen it made.

In the end she wrote two lines. The first says that a fuel made without the vein, from the waste of the pits, is reported out of the north this year, and that it is said to burn in the manner of coal, and it gives the name that goes with it as Dòmhnall's drover gave it, and then as his boatman gave it, and then, in the second line, as Ailig took it down from the pedlar at the pump. The second line says that the chronicler has not seen it.

Among the spellings she did not choose. She could not, and would not pretend to. All three she let stand side by side in the kingdom's record, the one word three ways in two lines, so that whoever came to the desk after her would know how the word had come up the hill: late, in pieces, by the mouths of carriers, with no one to vouch for it.

That is the first mention of dubhan in the royal record. There is no earlier one.

When she had written it she sat back and read the two lines over by the lamp, and then she read the whole leaf, and then she sanded it and laid it on the pile. The lamp was smoking a little and she turned the wick down. Outside the window it was full dark, though it was not yet five o'clock, and down in the town the few lamps that were lit made yellow points in the valley, and past them nothing, all the way to the north.

She thought of Raghnall in the stacks saying that an Leabharlann keeps and does not chase, and of the ferryman's boy with his feet in the water, and of the pedlar going along the far bank of the ford between the alders with his pack on his back and the black cake in it wrapped in sacking. She thought that somewhere in the north, far past the reach of her letters, a man whose name she could not spell had made a fire that burned.

*A chur san Leabharlann*, they say on the island: to put a thing in an Leabharlann is to settle it. She had put this in, and it was not settled at all.

She left the lamp to burn down, which she never did, and went to bed.
