# Of the Dark Town

*DE 21*

## I. Of the Third Fleet

An treas cabhlach was raised at Baile ghorm on the south-west coast on {{date:VI-0107}}, out of a fishing town that had never, so far as anyone at Ceann mhòr could say, raised so much as a harbour-watch before that winter, and Dùghall mac Iain saw the first of its hulls a week later, from the quay at Ceann mhòr, where she came in round the point on a grey forenoon riding low and black in the water with the dubhan sloshing audibly in her belly whenever the swell took her. She had no name on her bow. A clerk of the fleet had chalked *Trì* there, for the fleet's number, for want of any properer name yet given her, and the chalk had half run in the spray of the passage.

She had come the whole length of the west coast in four days, up from Baile ghorm past the capes and the closed water off the western Seann Skell, carrying the army's dubhan to the regiments in the hills and on the northern coast, and nothing else. That was the whole of An treas cabhlach's business, her master said, a man named Griogair who had fished the south-west waters forty years before the fleet ever thought to want him: to carry fuel north, since a regiment that could not light its own lamps or run its own lorries was no regiment at all, whatever guns it carried. Dùghall was on the quay with a file to see the barrels ashore, and Griogair, who had little enough to do once his hull was made fast, stood with him at the tank-cock through the better part of the unloading and talked, when he talked at all, in the manner of a man laying down one plain fact at a time and leaving the hearer to make of it what he would.

It was Griogair who told him that the fleet's hulls were fitted, every one, with the same tanks a lorry carried, only built larger and set low in the belly for the ballast the fuel's own weight gave her, so that a hull running full rode lower and steadier than one running light, and a crew learned within a season to judge a fair-weather passage by nothing more than how much dubhan she still had aboard. He told him too, without being asked, that he had buried two brothers and a father to that sea in the ordinary course of a fishing life, and that a war which asked him only to carry fuel rather than to fight was, by his own reckoning, the easiest service any kingdom had yet asked of him, whatever else might be said of it. Dùghall thought, hearing it, of the coal engines laid up in the Muileann chrom siding that he had heard tell of from a driver's son in his own company, and of how strange it was that a war fought in the hills should turn, in the end, on fishing boats fitted with tanks. He found he had no answer ready for Griogair, and gave none, and Griogair did not seem to want one.

The town was little changed since the regiment had landed: the fourth still quartered in the old human magistrate's house on the rise above the water, Tional nan Tuathach's protest against that quartering still, so far as Dùghall could learn, sitting wherever the council kept protests it did not mean to answer, and the widow he was billeted on, a Tuathaich woman named Barbara Keir who kept a loom in the room behind her kitchen, still answering him in Dia-thìris when he spoke to her in it and in nothing at all when he tried, of an evening, the few words of the humans' tongue he had picked up from men in his own company, though he had heard her, more than once, speaking that same tongue easily enough to her own neighbours across the yard wall.

## II. Of Barbara's House

Barbara's house stood two streets back from the harbour, a low stone building with the loom-room built on at the back in a later age than the rest of it, by the look of the joists, and Dùghall had been billeted on her since the regiment first came to Ceann mhòr, an arrangement neither of them had asked for and neither, so far as he could tell, much liked, though both had settled by then into the kind of wary household truce that two people keep who share a roof without ever agreeing to share anything else. She fed him at the same table she fed herself, plain fare and not much talk over it, and took the billeting-due the regiment's quartermaster paid her without comment, the way Dùghall supposed she took a good many things that autumn without comment, having no clear way of refusing any of them.

He had tried, early on, to make some small conversation of an evening in what little of the humans' tongue he had picked up, thinking it might ease the two of them into something more than a silent table, and had found each attempt met with the same flat courtesy: a nod, perhaps, or a word in Dia-thìris that answered the sense of what he had asked without troubling to answer it in the tongue he had asked it in, so that he came, after the third or fourth such evening, to let the matter drop rather than go on pressing a door she had made plain enough, in her own quiet way, she did not mean to open to him. He had heard her, once, through the wall between the kitchen and the loom-room, singing some low tune to herself at her shuttle in that same tongue she would not use to him directly, and had stood a moment in the passage listening before he thought better of it and went on to his own room, feeling, without quite being able to say why, that he had come near to overhearing something he had no right to.

## III. Of the File to Baile chrom

In an Dùbhlachd, with the days grown so short that the regiment's patrols went out and came back by lamplight both, Dùghall was given a file of six men and an order to go north along the coast road as far as Baile chrom, a bent little town on the far headland that the regiment's own maps marked only as the seat of a shire and the site of one of the five lighthouses, and that no patrol of the fourth had yet troubled to visit in the two months since the regiment had come to Ceann mhòr. The order named no particular business at Baile chrom beyond the general one every patrol that autumn carried, which was to search whatever ground it covered for arms, for men of the western hills who might have come so far north, and for anything else a report could usefully call *nothing found*, since nothing found was, by then, the commonest single line in every regimental return Dùghall had ever been made to copy fair.

He chose his six men the evening before from among those of his own company least likely to make trouble of a night's search, and found himself, going down the roll in his own head, settling more than once on men from the river towns rather than the coast ones, on no better ground than a private feeling that a river man would carry himself quieter in a stranger's house than a man used to the rough manners of a fishing harbour, a feeling he did not trouble to examine any further than that, since the order asked him only to bring back a report and not an account of his own reasons for choosing one soldier over another. The regiment's quartermaster gave him three days' rations for the file and a lamp apiece, the days by then being short enough that a patrol which meant to search a town properly could not count on daylight to do it in, and told him, handing over the last of the lamps, that Baile chrom lay far enough round the headland that no patrol before his had troubled to go so far, the town having sent in no complaint, raised no trouble, and given the regiment, in four months of war, no reason at all to spend a file's time on it.

The road north ran along the shoulder of the coast the whole way, with the sea on the left hand and, on the right, a country of low stone walls and thin pasture that put Dùghall in mind, more than once, of the ground round Caol bheag before the strait widened into the river proper, though the light here was flatter and greyer than any light he had known at home, coming off a sea that his father, a river man all his life, would have called no proper sea at all but a doorstep to one. They marched two days by way of the headland farms, which the order bade them visit on the road, to cover what the map called four miles straight, the light failing early both days, and slept the one night between in a drover's bothy that had plainly not sheltered a drove in some years, its thatch gone soft with damp and its single hearth cold enough that two of the men spent the night's watch arguing, in low voices Dùghall pretended not to hear, over whose turn it properly was to have thought to bring dry kindling.

They passed, on the second day's march, two farms and a mill whose wheel stood idle for want of water, the burn that turned it run low with the season's dry spell, and at each the file was watched from a doorway by whoever stood in it but never once hailed, the watching itself carried on in a silence Dùghall found he minded more, by the second farm, than any shout would have troubled him. One of his men, a stolid soldier named Cailean who had said little the whole march, remarked at the mill that a Dia-thìreach farm on the same road would have had a child out asking them their business inside a minute, favourable or not, and that a farm which only watched and said nothing was a farm that had already decided, before the file came in sight, exactly how much of itself it meant to show a soldier of the regiments, which Dùghall thought a fair enough observation and did not add to.

He knew, coming up the last rise before the town, that it was the eve of some observance of Eaglais nan Tuathach, having heard two of his own men, both of them river-born like himself and neither much given to piety of any kind, mention in passing that the north coast kept a vigil that night which the low country's own Christian houses did not, a Grey Night, one of them called it, without being able to say much more of it than that his own grandmother, who had come south from somewhere near this coast before Dùghall was born, used to go quiet and short-tempered for the length of one evening every year without ever properly explaining why, and that the family had learned, long before he was old enough to ask, simply to leave her be until morning. Cailean, who had grown up nearer the capital and knew the coast towns only by their trade, said he had heard the priests of Manannan at Ceann mhòr call it a prayer of their own gone astray in the north, kept there for a bird the church itself no longer named the same way the south did, and that the two houses disputed the matter without either, so far as he knew, troubling to settle it; Dùghall said only that he supposed a file ordered to search a town on the one night of the year it kept its silence deliberately had drawn, among the whole regiment's patrols that season, a particularly poor lot, and none of the six men with him disagreed.

## IV. Of the Unlatched Door

Dùghall had come up the coast road that autumn expecting, if he expected anything in particular, the kind of welcome the regiment's other patrols had reported from other Tuathaich towns: shutters put up at the first sound of boots on the road, a dog or two set loose to bark the length of a street, a woman calling a warning from an upper window to whoever in the town most needed the warning carried to them first. He had heard all three from men of his own company who had searched other coast towns that season, and had gone north with some private notion, never spoken aloud even to himself in so many words, that a night search would at least give a man something to push against, some sound or sight that told him the town knew he was there and minded it.

Baile chrom gave him none of that. The file came down off the last rise into the town's one bent street a little after full dark, the road following, as one of his men remarked, no straighter a course than a burn finds down a hillside, and not a single lamp showed in any window the whole length of it. No dog barked, not from the first house nor the last, and the only sound the whole way in was the sea working at the stones below the headland and, nearer at hand, his own men's boots on the road, which began, without any order given, to fall softer of their own accord the further into the town they came, as men will when a silence asks something of them that no order has yet put into words.

He tried the first door himself, a low house set back a little from the street with a byre built on to its gable end, and found it unlatched, the way a door is left in a house that expects no visitor and fears none either. Inside, by what grey light came through the one small window, he made out a man and a woman and three children, the youngest of them no more than five or six by his own guess, sitting round a hearth gone entirely cold, their hands folded in their laps in a stillness that put him more in mind, oddly, of a church he had once been made to sit through as a boy at his mother's insistence than of anything a soldier searching a house for arms had any right to expect. None of them spoke. None of them, so far as he could tell in that light, so much as turned a head toward the door.

He asked them, first in Dia-thìris and then, haltingly, in the little of the humans' tongue he carried, who else was in the house, and whether there were arms kept anywhere on the ground. The man only shook his head once, slowly, the whole weight of the gesture in its slowness rather than its motion, in the particular way, Dùghall thought afterward, that a man shakes his head at a child in church who has asked a question the room itself has already answered by staying quiet, a headshake that asked nothing further and offered nothing further either, and that closed the matter, so far as the man plainly judged it closed, more completely than any word could have.

It was the same at the next house, and the one after that, through the small hours of the night: unlatched doors, cold hearths, families sitting in a stillness Dùghall came to think of, by the fourth or fifth house, not as fear exactly, though he supposed fear was somewhere in it, but as a thing closer to a held breath that the whole town had agreed, without ever agreeing it in his hearing, to hold together until the file had passed through and gone. He found no arms in any house he searched that night, and no man or woman of an age to fight who was not already sitting, hands folded, in that same stillness.

The house nearest the lighthouse road, the last but one on the file's way through the town, held only a single old woman, sitting alone at a hearth as cold as every other, her hands folded the same way and her eyes open and steady on the doorway before Dùghall had so much as raised his hand to knock, as though she had heard the file's boots on the street some houses back and had settled herself, in her own time and by her own choosing, to receive whatever came through the door before it came. She gave her name when he asked it, the first person in the whole town that night who answered him at all beyond a headshake, and gave it plainly, in Dia-thìris, as Margaret Crane, and said nothing further, neither complaint nor welcome, only that same steady look that Dùghall found harder to stand under than any of the silences that had gone before it, a look that seemed to him less to be asking what he wanted than to have already decided, before he had finished asking it himself, what she meant to give him and what she did not.

He asked her, as he had asked at every other house, whether there were arms kept on the ground, and whether any man of the town was elsewhere that night who ought properly to be accounted for; and she answered only that the town kept its vigil that night as it kept it every year, and that she herself kept it in that house as she had kept it, she said, since before the war had a name to be kept from, and that a soldier was welcome to search the house from floor to rafter if the order he carried required it of him, since she had nothing in it he need trouble himself to hide from a searching. It was, of the whole night's houses, the one answer that gave him more words than any other and told him, when he came afterward to turn it over, no more than the rest had told him with none at all. He searched the house as she had bid him, finding nothing, and one of his own men, the same soldier who had walked heel first past the threshold at the house before, set his own lamp down of his own accord in the passage outside her door rather than carry its light in past her, a small courtesy Dùghall had given no order for and did not afterward ask the man to explain.

He learned only much later, from a clerk at Ceann mhòr who had served two seasons on that coast before An ceathramh rèisimeid ever came to it, that the vigil the old woman kept was held, in that one town before any other on the island, by the greater part of its people rather than the lesser, a night of silence kept, the clerk said, for some bird of their church that the south's own house of the same faith no longer named the same way, and that the whole town's custom, on that one night of the year, was to sit each in their own house with hearth and lamp alike unlit and speak no word aloud until the vigil's morning released them from it, a custom no soldier of the regiments coming up that coast in ignorance of the calendar could have known to expect, and no order Dùghall had been given had troubled to warn him of.

## V. Of the Report That Said No More

In the end he wrote the report every patrol that autumn had learned to write, that the town had been found dark and silent throughout, that it had been searched house by house, and that nothing was found: no arms, no men of fighting age unaccounted for, no sign that Baile chrom held or had ever held anything the regiment's order had sent him north to look for. The file's own clerk copied it fair the next morning at the bothy where they slept the second night, a younger man than Dùghall who had joined the fourth straight from a shipwright's yard at Ceann mhòr and had not yet lost, in Dùghall's private judgment, the habit of asking questions an older soldier learned to leave alone; folding the report into the satchel that would carry it back to the regiment's own returns, he asked whether there was more to be said of the night than the three lines it held.

Dùghall told him there was not, which was true in the only sense the order had asked him to make it true, and did not tell him of the man who had shaken his head once, slowly, nor of the very old woman his own soldier had stepped past on his toes, nor of the particular sound the sea had made on the stones below the headland while the file stood a long moment outside the last house before turning back for the road, a sound he found, walking south again the next day with the report already gone ahead of him, he could not afterward call to mind without the whole of that night coming back with it entire, the unlatched doors and the cold hearths and the headshakes, in a way three written lines had done nothing at all to put down or settle.

The file marched south again the next morning, the vigil's silence lifted by then, or so Dùghall judged from a single lamp he saw lit at last in a window as they left the bent street behind them, and he found the walk back to Ceann mhòr passed in a quiet among his own six men greater than any that had marked the march north, none of them offering much conversation and none pressed to, as though the town they had left behind had asked something of the file's own tongues as well as the town's. Cailean broke the silence only once, on the second day, to say that he had searched a good many houses that autumn on a good many coasts and had never yet come away from a search feeling, as he put it, that the house had searched him back, and that he did not care to go north to Baile chrom again if the regiment could find another man to send.

Word came to the regiment, a week after Dùghall's own report went in, that Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg of the hills, the last two of the five towns still holding out, had opened their own roads to the regiments that same week without so much as a shot fired on either side, their pits flooded on Comhairle nam Mèinnean's order the same day the roads opened, and An Clàr Foscailte, when Dùghall came to see a copy of it some weeks later pinned in the guardroom at Ceann mhòr, named no holdout taken prisoner at either town, the same three words, nothing found, standing in the place where a longer account might have stood had there been one to give. He thought, reading it, of Baile chrom's own cold hearths, and found himself wondering, not for the first time that winter, whether a return that named nothing found was a return that had looked in the right place to begin with, or only a return written by a man who had learned, as he himself plainly had, to stop asking a question once the room had already answered it by staying quiet.

He mentioned Àth naomh's opening to Barbara one evening over the plain fare she still set before him without much talk to go with it, thinking she might have some word of her own on it from whatever channel carried news among the Tuathaich households of the coast faster than any regimental return carried it among his own kind, and she told him only, in Dia-thìris, that a pit flooded was a pit flooded whichever order flooded it, and asked nothing further, and offered nothing further, and took up her mending again in a silence he had by then learned not to press. Watching her needle go in and out by the one lamp she allowed herself of an evening, he found himself thinking that the whole coast, Dia-thìreach and Tuathaich both, seemed that winter to have settled on the same three words the regiment's own returns had settled on, each in its own house and its own tongue, and that a war fought mostly in silence on both sides was, whatever else might be said of it, no easier a thing to carry home at night than one fought with any noise at all.

On an Nollaig that year An naoidheamh rèisimeid was raised at the southern Seann Skell, to stand guard over the dubhan works the shipmasters had built there, and Dùghall heard the news of it read out at Ceann mhòr's own guardroom two days after, one more line in a winter that seemed to him, turning the last months over as he lay awake in Barbara's back room with her loom silent behind the wall and the widow herself long asleep, to have been made up of nothing but such lines: a fleet raised here, a regiment there, a town's roads opened without a fight, a report that said no more than three words allowed it to say. He had not slept well since Baile chrom, waking most nights somewhere in the small hours with the sea-sound of that headland in his ears though Ceann mhòr's own harbour lay near enough that he could not always be sure, half waking, which shore the sound in fact came from; and he did not know, lying there with the winter's short daylight still some hours off, what exactly it was about that one bent street that would not let him sleep the way the other towns his file had searched that autumn had let him sleep easily enough, only that it would not, and that no line he could write in any report, then or afterward, was going to tell him why.

He thought, more than once in those wakeful hours, of trying the words for it in the humans' tongue, the few he carried, as though a thing that would not settle in Dia-thìris might yet settle in the other, and found each time that he did not have the words at all, in either tongue, for a stillness that had asked nothing of him and given him, in the asking, nothing to answer with; and he let the matter lie, the way the town itself had taught him, without much hope by then that letting it lie would do any more for him than it had plainly done, over some longer span of years he could not guess at, for Margaret Crane's own steady, waiting look.
