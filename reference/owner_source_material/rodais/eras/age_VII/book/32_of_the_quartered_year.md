## I. Of the Pass at the Harbour Wall

The regiments did not fight that year. Martha Greaves came to know it the way the whole north came to know it: from the plain evidence of a winter passing into a spring and a spring into a summer, with no name added to any list she had not already entered by hand, and by the middle of am Màrt she had stopped waiting for the next battle the way a woman stops listening for a caller who has twice failed to come, without ever quite deciding to stop, only finding one morning that she no longer did it.

What came instead of battle was the quartering, and the quartering asked more of the harbour at Caol mhòr, in its own quiet way, than any single engagement had asked of the hill towns. A patrol boat lay off the harbour mouth through that whole spring, always of the same low grey build though seldom the same hull twice running, and a soldier stood at the head of the harbour steps from first light to last with a book under his arm and a list of names in it that grew longer by the week; and any man or woman of the town who meant to go beyond the harbour road, to a farm inland or a market at Doire ghlas or a burial two parishes off, learned to come to that soldier first and show him the small square of paper the Board's own office had begun, that spring, to issue against a name and a face and a stated errand, which the townsfolk had taken, within a month of the first of them being handed out, to calling simply the pass, though the paper's own heading gave it a longer name that nobody troubled to read past the first line.

Martha came to the harbour herself on Moot business three times that spring, carrying the minute-books down from her own mill at Doire ghlas in the same worn satchel Walter Hale had carried them in before her, and each time she showed her own pass to the same soldier, a stolid young man from one of the southern shires who plainly found the asking as tiresome as she found the answering, and each time he wrote her name in his book beside the hour, though he had by the second visit ceased to trouble her further than that, the way a toll-keeper ceases, after enough passings, to weigh a coin he already knows to be sound.

It was on the third of these visits, in the last week of an Cèitean, that she came down to the wall in time to watch the same soldier's own captain board Joe Ashby's boats.

She had heard of Joe Ashby before that morning only as a name in Somhairle mac Lachlainn's own reports, the fitter of Caol mhòr who held no guild's paper and answered to none, and she watched now from the head of the steps as two of the captain's men went down into the bilges of a hull she took, by its lines, for one of the half-dozen the town's people called the engine-boats, and came up black to the elbow, and found, so far as she could tell from where she stood, nothing that troubled the captain's own book beyond a line written and the next boat called for. The fitter himself stood at his shed door through the whole of it with a wrench still in his hand, watching in the particular stillness of a man who has done this dance before and knows every step of it, and when the captain came to him at the last and asked, as Martha heard it carried on the wind off the water, whether he held any guild's leave for the engines he fitted, Joe Ashby answered that there was none in that harbour would give him one, in a voice that carried no more heat than a man remarking on the weather.

Martha did not speak to him that day, having business enough of her own with the harbour-master over a Moot petition on the tithe, but she wrote the whole of it into her own private book that evening, the one she kept apart from the Moot's minutes and showed to no council, because it seemed to her a thing worth setting down: that a harbour a year clear of any battle could still be searched, boat by boat, on the strength of an order nobody at the wall had asked to see written out in full, and that the town bore it the way it bore the pass and the soldier at the steps and the patrol hull off the mouth: without gladness, and without much noise, the plain unspectacular labour of a people getting on with a war that had stopped fighting and had not yet stopped asking.

She heard, some weeks later, from the same harbour-master, that the depot's own men had sent for Joe Ashby again before the summer was out, a fleet hull this time whose engine no guild-trained fitter at Seann Skell could put right, and that he had gone and mended it and taken his coin and asked no more of the matter than the coin required, which struck Martha, hearing it, as neither a betrayal of anything nor a submission to it, only the same plain labour again, carried on wherever the labour was wanted and paid for, war or no war.

The pass, small square paper that it was, reached further into the harbour's ordinary year than Martha had looked for when she first watched it handed out. A carter of the town buried his own father in am Màrt at a parish two roads inland, and stood at the harbour steps the morning of the burial with the whole of his family's mourning black already on him, showing his paper to the same stolid soldier as any market-day errand, because the office that wrote the passes had made no separate provision for a burial and the carter had not thought to ask one of it; and Martha, hearing the tale from the harbour-master over the same tithe business that had first brought her down, found she could not say afterward which struck her the harder, that the office had thought so little of a burial's difference from a market errand, or that the town had by then stopped expecting it to think of any such difference at all.

By the height of summer the soldier at the steps had learned most of the harbour's own faces well enough to wave the plainest of its errands through with no more than a glance at the paper held up, and the town, for its part, had settled into carrying the little square papers the way a fisherman carries a knife, without remarking on it, only reaching for it when the moment asked; and it was this settling, more than any single search or any single asking, that Martha found hardest to set down plainly in her own private book, since a thing borne quietly enough for long enough ceases, in the telling, to look like a thing borne at all.

## II. Of the Names Added at Àth leathan

The Moot kept its register in the same low room behind its own house at Caol mhòr where it had kept it since the Severance, a single long shelf of bound books running back further than any living speaker could put a date to without consulting them, and it fell to Martha, in the second week of an Giblean, to open the current volume to the column headed the dead and the maimed and enter, at last, the names of Àth leathan.

The battle was thirteen months gone by then. The open record had counted the kingdom's own dead at the field below the town the spring before, twenty-three by its last tally, and had let the holdouts' dead go uncounted, as the open record let a great many things go uncounted that the Moot's own register did not; and it had taken the Moot's own clerks the better part of a year, working from what letters and lists the hill towns themselves would send south under the disarming, to put together a fuller reckoning than the open record had ever meant to give. Peter Hale, who by then carried the rebound minute-books through half the Moot's own business the way his great-grandfather Walter had carried the first of them, brought the western list to her himself on a morning of steady rain, a single folded sheet in a hand she did not know, and told her only that it had come up from Cnoc thais by three hands and a fortnight's delay, and that he had checked it twice against the letters already filed and found no name in it that any other list contradicted.

She read the names aloud to herself before she wrote them, a habit she had kept since her first year at the desk, on the private reasoning that a name spoken once by a living voice was less entirely lost than a name only ever set down in ink; and she found, reading the Àth leathan list through for the first time, that Ruth Calder's own name stood nowhere on it, neither among the dead nor the maimed nor, so far as the list troubled to say, among the living, which told her, as it had told every clerk who had looked for that name in the year since, precisely nothing at all.

The names went in through that whole wet morning, in the same clear hand the register had always been kept in, a column of them running to the foot of one page and the head of the next, and she thought, dipping her pen for perhaps the fortieth time, that a register was a strange kind of mercy: too slow to comfort anyone in the year a death happened, and yet, a year on, still willing to receive it when nothing else on the island any longer was.

It was an Giblean too, in that same fortnight, that she went up to the old chapel above the mill at Doire ghlas on the morning the Tuathaich Church kept its Easter, less from any faith of her own in the Church's rite than from a long habit of standing at the back of whatever house of worship her own town's people kept, to see for herself how a thing was borne rather than take another's word for it. She found the chapel fuller than she had looked for, the whole small congregation of Doire ghlas's own Tuathaich households gathered in, and a single soldier of the regiments standing at the door through the length of the service, not unkindly, only present, the way the soldier at the harbour steps was present, a book under his arm in which he wrote, so far as Martha could see from where she stood, nothing at all, the whole service passing without a word exchanged between the door and the altar. Before the last hymn was sung she left, having seen what she had come to see, which was that the north kept its own feasts that year under watch and kept them anyway, and she carried the sight back to Doire ghlas with her the way she carried most such sights, unspoken to anyone, set down only in the private book the Moot never saw.

She wrote the register's own list closed that same week, ruling the double line the book's own form asked of a finished column and setting the date under it in the same hand she had used for every name above; and she found, ruling it, that she had grown, over the year of no battles, into a kind of clerk she had not set out to become when the Moot first gave her Daniel Frayne's own chair: still the speaker who carried the north's grievances south to a council that mostly did not answer them, and now, besides that, a keeper of a book that answered, in its own slow and unhurried way, what the council never troubled to. How many further columns the same book would ask of her before the war she had not yet learned to call ended was done asking, she could not say, closing the Àth leathan column; only that the book would take them, page after ruled page, whatever the council chose to do about the asking.

## III. Of the Women from Cnoc thais

Ellen Marsh had turned Agnes Pike's old schoolroom over to the families from the west the autumn before, when Cnoc thais fell and its people came down out of the hills with what they could carry, and by the second spring of their sheltering there she had come to know most of the women of that town by name, sitting three and four to a bench in the evenings after the day's small work was done, while she went from bench to bench with her own ink and paper and took down, in the plain hand she had used for eleven books before this one, whatever each woman judged worth the telling.

Peigi NicAonghais, who had kept a byre of six goats above the second shaft before the road was cut, told her, on an evening in an t-Ògmhios, of a farm two ridges back from the town's own ruin where the wife still baked twice what her own household could eat and left the second baking on the sill overnight, and where the loaves were always gone by morning and the sill never once watched to see by whom; and she told it, Ellen noted, in the flat unhurried voice of a woman reporting a fact of the weather, neither boasting of the farm's courage nor fearing to name it, since every woman on that bench already knew, without needing to be told, that a war which had stopped its battles had not stopped its hiding, and that the hills above Cnoc thais still held men who had gone up into them the year the road was cut and had not yet, whatever the disarming at Muileann àrsaidh and Àth leathan had settled for the towns themselves, come down.

Ellen wrote the farm's telling into her own twelfth book without a name attached to it, on the same private rule she had kept through all eleven books before, that a fact could be kept and a name spared where the naming would cost the teller more than the telling was worth; and she was still turning that rule over in her own mind three weeks later when word came up from the hill country, carried by a carter's boy who had it from a cousin still at Cnoc thais, that a patrol of the regiments had found a farm much like the one Peigi had named, though not that one, close enough to it that the whole schoolroom bench went quiet at the telling, and had put its thatch to the torch on the strength of a barn found half full of grain no ration book of the district could account for.

No one was killed at that burning, the boy's own telling said, the family having been turned out of the house before the thatch was fired and left standing in their own yard to watch it go; but the byre went with the house, and the season's hay with the byre, and Ellen set the whole of it down in her twelfth book that same week in the same flat voice the women themselves used for such tellings, because she had learned, over eleven books and the better part of a life spent taking down what the north would tell her, that the flat voice was very often the only one a fact of that kind could safely be carried in.

Mòrag NicChoinnich, who sat the bench nearest the door most evenings and said little until the room had thinned to two or three, told her own account of the same burning some weeks after the carter's boy had first carried it down, having had it herself from a sister still living within sight of the ruin, and her telling added only one thing the boy's had left out: that the family put out into their own yard that morning had been given time, before the thatch was fired, to carry out what they could of the house's own goods, a chest and a loom and the year's stored meal, by the very patrol that had come to burn it, and that no one on the bench that evening could rightly say whether this was mercy in the men who did the burning or only the ordinary carefulness of soldiers who had learned, over a long year of finding nothing, to make what small amends they could within an order they had not written themselves. Ellen set that down too, unresolved as it stood, since she had come to think, closing in on her twelfth book what her first had taught her at the very start of the gathering, that a full account left its own contradictions standing rather than smooth them away for the sake of a tidier page.

She finished the twelfth book in the first week of an Dàmhair, closing it on an evening when the schoolroom benches held fewer of the west's own women than they had a year before, some gone home already to whatever their own hill towns had left standing, some gone nowhere anyone at Doire ghlas could say; and she sat a long while with the finished book closed on her own table before she wrote, on its last blank leaf, a single line that owed nothing to any woman's telling but her own: that the war her first eleven books had never troubled themselves to speak of had, by the time this twelfth one closed, written its own record into the north as plainly as the Severance had, and that she did not yet know, closing it, which of her twelve books a reader two generations off would find the harder to believe.

## IV. Of the Hymn Through the Wall

The fourth regiment kept its patrols on the Ceann mhòr road through the whole of that year, in a rhythm Dùghall mac Iain had come, by the spring, to know almost without troubling to think of it: a file out at first light, a file in at dusk, the same stretches of coast and hedgerow walked and walked again until the road seemed, some evenings, to walk the file rather than the other way about. No arms were found on any of those patrols after the winter's search of Baile chrom, and no man of fighting age was taken up who could not give some account of himself that the file's own corporal, whoever he was that week, judged worth passing without further trouble; and the regiment's returns, which Dùghall still copied fair as often as any other corporal of his own company, settled by the spring into the same three words that had closed every report of the autumn before, so that a man reading a twelvemonth of them end to end, as Dùghall once did on a wet afternoon with nothing else to fill it, would have found a year's patrolling summed up in little more than a list of dates against which nothing had been found.

The farms along that road told their own year plainer than any return did. A byre Dùghall's file had passed shuttered in the winter stood open by the summer with a boy of ten or eleven driving the same three cows out to the same field each morning, and the boy came, by the second season of the same patrol going by, to lift a hand to the file as it passed, not quite a greeting and not quite its refusal, a small loosening Dùghall noticed and did not remark on to the men behind him, judging that a thing noticed aloud too soon had a way of curdling before it could properly set. Other farms kept the same watching silence they had kept the year before, the door held to and the field worked in a stillness that asked the file nothing and gave it nothing, and Dùghall had stopped, by the spring, expecting either kind of farm to become the other, having learned that a household's own manner toward a patrol was no more likely to change for a year's quiet than a man's manner toward the sea was likely to change for a calm summer, both being founded on a memory no summer could touch.

He was still billeted on Barabal, the arrangement having become, over the better part of a year, less a wariness endured than a plain fact of his own days, the two of them keeping the same table and the same thin courtesy across it without either, so far as he could tell, wanting it otherwise or expecting it to become anything more. She fed him, and took the billeting-due without comment, and answered him in Dia-thìris when he spoke to her in it and in nothing at all when he tried, of an evening, the little of the humans' tongue he still carried, though he had long since stopped minding that particular silence the way he had minded it the first autumn, having come to understand it, or think he understood it, as a boundary she kept for reasons that were hers to keep and none of his to press.

It was on an evening of an t-Sultain, with the regiment's patrols grown so much a matter of habit by then that even the men who walked them had stopped remarking on the empty houses they no longer troubled to search, that Dùghall heard, through the wall between his own room and the loom-room, a hymn he did not know sung low and unhurried in the humans' tongue, a different air from the shuttle-song he had overheard the autumn before and heavier in its cadence, the kind of tune a house keeps for its own church rather than its own work. He stood a while in the passage as he had stood the first time, and went on to his room as he had gone on then, telling himself it was no more his to overhear a second time than it had been the first.

The same air came again a fortnight later, and again after that, always of an evening, until by the second week of an Dàmhair he found he could carry the whole of its shape in his own head without meaning to have learned it, the rise and fall of it if not the words, the way a man comes to know the toll of a bell he has lived long enough beside without ever climbing the tower to see it rung. He did not ask Barabal what the hymn was called, judging, by then, that the asking would cost more of the household's thin peace than the answer was worth; but he found himself, walking the Ceann mhòr road of a morning with his own file strung out behind him, humming a bar or two of it under his breath before he caught himself at it, and stopping, each time, with the particular unease of a man who has found a stranger's coin in his own pocket and cannot say how it came there.

He mentioned it, once, to Cailean, who had marched with him to Baile chrom the winter before and had said little of that night since; and Cailean, hearing two bars of it hummed low over a cold supper, said only that his own grandmother had kept a tune very like it, and that he had never asked her the words of it either, and that a man billeted long enough on a house not his own generally came away, whether he meant to or not, carrying some small piece of it home in him that no order and no regiment could ever quite account for in its returns.

## V. Of the Word That Came to Caol mhòr

Through that whole quartered year, from the harbour at Caol mhòr to the schoolroom at Doire ghlas to the coast road above Ceann mhòr, ran a single rumour that none of them, telling it to one another across the width of the island in letters and carters' talk and a soldier's idle word over a cold supper, could ever quite run to ground: that something was gathering in the western hills that the disarming had not reached, and had not been meant to reach, and that no report from any regiment's own patrol, searching the same empty farms and the same silent doorways it had searched for a year without once finding arms or men to answer for them, had troubled to explain.

Martha heard a version of it from a letter out of Cnoc thais that reached the Moot's own house in the last week of an t-Sultain, a plain note from a Board clerk stationed there that said only that the hill paths above the town carried more foot-traffic by night than any patrol's own daylight counting could make sense of, and that the clerk did not know what to make of it and set it down all the same, since a fact unexplained was, in his own judgment, still worth the Board's having. Ellen heard it in gentler form from the last of the Cnoc thais women still on her own schoolroom benches, a shrug more than a telling, that the hills had always kept more than any regiment's list could count and would go on keeping it whatever the disarming said. Dùghall heard it from Cailean, who had it from a corn-factor at Ceann mhòr who had it, in turn, from nobody he could rightly name, that the western hills were said to be emptying of something, though of what, and toward where, the corn-factor himself could not say and did not, by Cailean's own account, seem greatly troubled not to know.

None of the three of them, in all that quartered year, ever heard the rumour told twice the same way, and none of them, telling it on in turn, thought to connect it with any other thing they themselves had seen: not with the farm above Cnoc thais that fed a night's coming and going the disarming had never quite ended, not with the hymn Dùghall had learned without meaning to through Barabal's own wall, not with the pass a soldier at the Caol mhòr steps still asked of every fisherman and carter who wanted the harbour road. It was simply the one thing that whole year that would not settle into any report's three closing words, the one line no regimental clerk and no Moot's register and no schoolroom's twelfth book could make read as nothing found, because nothing, in this one matter alone, had ever been offered for the finding.

Word came to Caol mhòr in the last week of an Dàmhair, brought up the harbour road by a Board carrier who had ridden two days without pausing to change his own horse twice over, that a company of some hundreds, out of no town the carrier's own dispatch troubled to name, had come down out of the western hills and turned south-east across the middle of the island, keeping to no road the regiments had thought to watch, and bound for no destination the dispatch would guess at. Martha read the carrier's note at her own desk in the Moot's low room, with the register's current volume still open on the shelf beside her at the page she had closed that spring over the names of Àth leathan, and found, reading it, that the rumour the whole quartered year had carried without ever once running to ground had, in the space of one carrier's ride, stopped being a rumour and become, instead, the first plain fact any report that autumn had troubled to set down.

She did not yet know, folding the note away to carry to the council in the morning, how long the road the company had chosen would run, nor where it would end, nor what name, if any, the open record would ever trouble to give the man the western hills' own tellings were already, by every account reaching her desk, calling their own. She knew only, sealing the note into her satchel beside the minute-books that had carried the Moot's whole quartered year, that the year of nothing found was over, and that whatever came after it would not, this time, close on three words and a clean page.
