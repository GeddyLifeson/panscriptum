## I. Of the Speaker's Last Night

The minute-book of Tional nan Tuathach has no entry for the first week of am Faoilleach in the eighth year. The clerk at Caol mhòr would not call the room to order while the speaker lay dying, and nobody asked him to. What the book leaves out, the mill-hands of Doire ghlas and the binders of Muileann leathan have kept between them, and they tell it much alike.

On the eighth of the month a boy came up the mill road at Doire ghlas on a pony as wet as himself, with a note folded small in his fist. Martha Greaves was in the carding-room with her sleeves pushed up, counting fleeces that the tally said were forty and her eyes said were thirty-eight. She took the note without looking at the boy and read it standing among the bales. It was in the eldest binder's hand, which she knew from thirty years of the delegates' accounts, and it said only that Daniel was asking for her, and that he had not asked for anyone before.

She finished the count first. It was thirty-eight. She wrote the two missing fleeces against the carrier's name, put on her coat, and told the overseer he had the mill until she came back, and that if he let the east wheel run dry again she would know.

The road from Doire ghlas to Muileann leathan runs east under the grey oaks and then out along the edge of the coast, some seven miles. The cart took the better part of the afternoon. It rained the whole way, the fine sideways rain that comes in off the sea to the north in the dead of winter and finds its way in at the collar however the collar is buttoned. Martha sat beside the carter with the oilcloth over her knees and kept her own counsel. Fifty-nine that winter, broad in the shoulder, grey, and known in every northern town as a woman who read a ledger faster than most people read a name, she had managed the mills at Doire ghlas since the year the delegates' money built them. For eleven years she had sat in Tional nan Tuathach as delegate for the mill-town and had never once spoken in it for longer than it took to give a figure.

Muileann leathan stands on its stream inside an old wall, with the keep of the Dia-thìrich governors at one end and the church of the northern congregations at the other, and the mill that gives the town its name squatting broad and low across the race between them. The bindery was a narrow house under the wall, down a lane that smelt of wet stone and flour. A lamp was lit in the upper window.

The binders were in the workroom below, all of them, though there was no work on the benches. There were eleven. They had been boys and girls when Daniel Frayne first taught them to fold a sheet and sew a gathering, in the years when no guild in the south would take a Tuathach apprentice for that craft, and now the youngest of them was going grey. They sat among the standing presses and the sewing frames with their hands in their laps, and the room smelt of paste, hide and cold glue. The eldest binder, a lame woman who had sewn her first gathering for him at twelve, got up when Martha came in.

"He's awake," she said. "He's been awake since noon, waiting on you. He'd not let us send before."

"Why not?"

"He said you'd a mill to run."

Martha went up. The stair was steep and bare, and the room at the top was hardly wider than the bed. Daniel Frayne lay propped against a bolster with his hands laid flat on the blanket, the way he had laid them flat on the speaker's table at Caol mhòr for twenty-three years when he meant to keep them still. He was seventy-three, and the skin of his face had gone thin and yellow over the bone. There was a dubhan stove in the corner, one of the little iron ones the pedlars sold, and it gave off a steady heat and a smell like a tarred rope smouldering.

"Martha," he said.

"Daniel."

"Sit. The chair's sound, whatever it looks."

She sat. Through the small window, past the roofs and the wall, she could see the headland where Taigh-solais Muileann bheag stood, and its lamp had just been lit. The light had been turned to dubhan the winter before, fed by carts from the north, and it burned whiter and steadier than the coal flame had. She had not got used to it.

"You'll want to know what I want," he said.

"I'd guessed you wanted something."

He nearly laughed, and it cost him, and he waited till his breath came back. "The books," he said. "The minute-books. The first twenty years are coming out of their boards. Walter's paste was flour and fish-glue, and there's nothing left of it but dust and a smell. I've been meaning to rebind them since before you were a delegate. Every year there was something the room wanted more."

"The clerk's been keeping the loose leaves in order. I've seen him at it."

"The clerk's been keeping them in a drawer with a stone on top." He moved one hand on the blanket, an inch, and put it back. "I want them done. All of them. From the wet afternoon onward. By my own, down there. They know the work. They'll do it for the cost of the thread if the room lets them, and more carefully than anyone in Cathair dhearg would do it for gold."

"That's for the speaker to ask."

"Yes," said Daniel Frayne. He looked at her with his pale eyes and said nothing more about it, and she understood that he had said all he meant to on that head. It was his way in the chair as well. He put a thing on the table and let it lie there until someone picked it up.

They talked a little after that, of small things. He asked after the mill and the price of fleece. She told him about the two fleeces short at the carrier's, and he said the carrier's father had been a thief before him and his grandfather a thief under the humans, and that it showed a proper respect for a trade to keep it in the family. Then he slept, and woke, and asked for water, and slept again.

The eldest binder came up with a candle at about ten and sat on the top step. Martha did not go down. Toward the small hours his breathing changed and slowed, and grew loud, and then far apart. The lame woman came and stood at the foot of the bed with her hand over her mouth. The rain had stopped. Out on the headland the white lamp of Muileann bheag turned and turned, and its light came round in the window at each turn, and went.

He died a little before light on {{date:VI-0020}}. Martha heard the breath go out and not come back, and she waited a long while to be sure. Then she folded his hands one over the other, since there was no longer any need to keep them flat, and went down the stair to tell the others, who already knew.

## II. Of the Burial at Muileann leathan

They buried Daniel Frayne four days later in the churchyard of Muileann leathan, at the east end of the town, under the wall where the wind comes over from the sea.

The church there is the largest in the north after Doire ghlas, a long grey building with a squat tower, raised on the stones of a Dia-thìreach hall that stood on the spot before An Dealachadh. Eaglais nan Tuathach has kept it since those years. It was full that morning, and the lane outside was full, and the people who could not get in stood along the churchyard wall in their dark coats with the rain dripping off their hat-brims. They had come from all along the coast: fishing families from Cathair gheal, millers and weavers from Doire ghlas, carters, harbour-men from Caol mhòr, a knot of hewers from the western hill towns who had walked two days and stood a little apart, with the black of their trade still in the lines of their knuckles.

The binders carried him. There were eleven of them and the coffin wanted six, so they took it in turns down the length of the lane, stopping at every twenty paces to change, and the crowd waited each time without a word. The eldest binder walked in front with her stick. Martha walked behind with the clerk of Tional nan Tuathach, a thin nervous man called Thomas Rook who had brought the speaker's chair-cushion wrapped in a cloth under his arm and did not seem to know what to do with it.

The priest read in the humans' tongue, which was the tongue of the northern churches and of most northern kitchens, though every soul in the church could have followed him as well in Dia-thìris. He read the psalm the north keeps for its dead, of the waters and the hills, and then he spoke briefly of Daniel Frayne's life, and said that he had taught the children of this town a trade when no one else would, and had kept the northern delegates' books in good order for twenty-three years, and had never been heard to raise his voice in either work. Then the schoolchildren of Doire ghlas, whom their teacher had brought seven miles in two carts that morning, stood up in the gallery and sang the hymn of the sea road, and some of the fishing families sang with them. Martha found she did not trust herself to sing and did not try.

At the grave the ground was soft and heavy, and the diggers had laid planks along the edge. The binders let the coffin down on their own straps. When the priest had done, each of them in turn stooped and dropped something into the grave before the earth went in. Martha, at the foot, saw that it was thread: a short length of linen binding-thread from each of them, waxed and cut, the kind he had taught them to draw through the fold.

When it was over the crowd stood about in the lane and would not go home. The women of the town brought bread and hot broth out of their kitchens, and people drank it standing under the eaves. Martha found herself backed against the churchyard wall with a cup in her hands and a ring of delegates round her, pretending to talk of the weather.

Peter Hale was one of them, the delegate of Caol mhòr, thirty-eight years old, long-armed and fair, and he kept the tally on the Caol mhòr fish-quay as his great-grandfather Walter Hale had kept the humans' harbour books before the northern towns had any gathering of their own. He had a way of standing straight as a mast when he was nervous. He was standing so now.

"Mistress Greaves," he said.

"Peter."

"The room will sit at Caol mhòr at the month's end. For the chair."

"I know when the room sits. I've sat in it eleven years."

"They'll put your name," he said. "Cathair gheal's said so, and the hill towns, and I'd put it myself if nobody else did."

"Then somebody had better put another one beside it," said Martha, "for I've two hundred looms and a carrier who can't count to forty, and I'm no talker."

"Daniel wasn't a talker."

"Daniel was a bookbinder. It's a trade for a patient man." She drank off the broth and gave him the cup to hold. "Ask Mistress Marsh. She stands up in front of forty children every morning and makes them listen. That's a gift."

Ellen Marsh had come up behind him while they spoke, with her two carts of children marshalled along the wall by the older girls. She was a narrow dark woman of fifty-two, the teacher at Agnes Pike's old school in Doire ghlas, with ink on her middle finger that never quite washed off, and a dry voice that carried to the back of any room without being raised.

"Mistress Marsh is not a delegate," she said, "and would be no use to you in the chair, for she'd make the council parse its own letters before she answered them." She looked at Martha. "They've asked you already, Martha. They're only waiting now for you to hear it."

Martha said nothing. Down the lane the eldest binder was standing at the door of the bindery with her stick, looking out at the crowd, and behind her the workroom was dark. It came to Martha then, with the broth still burning in her throat, that whoever sat in the chair would be the one to ask for the books, and that she had sat by his bed and told him so herself.

## III. Of the Chair at Caol mhòr

Tional nan Tuathach sat at Caol mhòr on the last day but one of am Faoilleach, in the long low room over the net-lofts by the harbour where it had sat since Walter Hale's wet afternoon. The humans' harbour office had stood below it once. The room still smelt of damp wool and fish-scale, and would until the sea dried up. A fire of driftwood and turf burned in the grate at one end and did nothing for the other. There were benches along both walls and a trestle down the middle, and at its head the speaker's chair, a plain high-backed thing of ship's oak. Thomas Rook had laid the cushion back on it, and nobody had sat down there.

Forty-four delegates came, which was more than had come to any sitting in years. The fishing towns came, and the mill towns, and the farms of the northern shires, and the pit towns of the western hills, whose hewers still worked their own seams under their own captains and paid An Crùn its fifth by the old ruling, to a Comhairle nam Mèinnean that entered it as arrears. Thomas Rook read the notice of Daniel Frayne's death into the minute in his careful clerk's voice, and the day and the place of his burial, and the room stood for the space of a breath and sat down again.

Then an old fisherman from Cathair gheal got to his feet without being asked and said, "I put the name of Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas," and sat down again, as if that settled it.

Martha stood up at once. She had meant to speak calmly, and found that she was angry, and could not have said at whom.

"I have a mill," she said. "Two mills, and a dye-house, and three hundred people who eat by them. I've no learning past the figures. I've never stood up in this room longer than it takes to read a row of figures. There are men and women on these benches who can talk a council clerk into a stupor and out again. Put one of them."

"Daniel had a bindery," said the old fisherman from where he sat.

"Daniel had eleven binders who could run it without him."

"And you've an overseer," said Peter Hale, "who let the east wheel run dry in an t-Samhain, and you've not sacked him."

There was laughter at that, not unkind, and Martha had nothing to say to it. The room had heard her, and went on as if it had not.

A delegate of the farm towns put a second name, the name of a Ceann mhòr carter, a big genial man who had spoken in the room more often than anyone. The carter got up and said with great good humour that he withdrew it, since he could not read, and that he would sooner see a speaker who could add. Nobody else put a name. Thomas Rook looked down the trestle at the rows of faces, and then at Martha, and asked the room in the old form whether it would have Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas in the chair. The hands went up along both walls, the fishermen's and the millers' and the hewers', and he did not trouble to count them.

She sat down in Daniel Frayne's chair on the cushion he had sat on, and found that it was too high for her, and that her feet did not lie flat on the floor. She kept them still.

The room waited for her to speak. She had no speech. She looked down the trestle, over the ink-pots and the delegates' hats laid in a row, to the fire at the far end, and then she said what was in her mind.

"Daniel asked me for a thing on his last night," she said. "He asked me to ask the room for it. So I'll do that first, and then we can get on."

She told them. She spoke in the humans' tongue, as the room mostly did, and when an old woman from the western coast, who kept Dia-thìris by preference and made a point of it, called out that she had not caught the last of it, Martha said it again in Dia-thìris without a pause and without apology, and went on. The first books were coming apart. The clerk kept the loose leaves in a drawer with a stone on them. Daniel's binders at Muileann leathan would rebind the whole run from the first meeting for the cost of their thread and board, and he had wished it done.

Thomas Rook went to the press at the side of the room, a tall cupboard with a lock, and brought out the first of the books and laid it on the trestle before her. It was a thick ledger in a split calf cover. When she opened it the front board came away in her hand, and a gathering of pages slid out after it and fanned across the table. The delegates at the near end leaned to look. The ink on the top leaf was brown and small and cramped, and it gave the day, and under it the names of the towns that had come.

"There's sixty-odd of these," said Thomas Rook, "and the first twenty are like that one, or worse."

A delegate of the fishing towns asked who paid, and how much, and for how long the room would be without its books. They were fair questions, and Martha found that they steadied her, since they had answers and she could get them. She said that she would have the binders' reckoning for the next sitting, and that if the room's purse could not meet it, the mills at Doire ghlas would, and the room could argue with her afterwards about whether it ought to have let them.

It was moved and carried that the matter lie over to the next sitting for the binders' figures. Thomas Rook wrote it in. Then the room got on, as she had said it would: a quarrel between two fishing towns over a mooring, the price of seed-oats, a letter from Comhairle nam Mèinnean about the western pits that nobody could make head or tail of. Martha heard them all out with her feet off the floor, and kept the minutes short.

When the sitting rose at dusk, the delegates went down the outside stair into the wind, and she stayed. She sat on in the chair with the loose pages of Walter Hale's book in front of her, gathering them square, until Thomas Rook coughed from the door and said he would be locking up. The minute-book for that day records her first sitting as speaker. It is the shortest minute of the year.

## IV. Of the Minute of the Second Set

The room sat again on {{date:VI-0021}}, in a hard gale from the south-west that shook the net-lofts beneath the floor and set the lamps swinging on their hooks.

The binders' reckoning had come to Martha at Doire ghlas a week before, in the eldest binder's upright hand, on a single sheet. She had gone through it at her desk in the counting-house one evening with the mill quiet below and a pot of tea going cold at her elbow. It was a good reckoning. It set down the number of books, which was sixty-three, and the number of leaves in each so far as the clerk's drawer allowed, and the linen and the board and the hide and the paste by weight, and the days of work, and at the foot a sum so small that she went through it twice to find the mistake. There was none. The binders had charged nothing for their hands.

She read it to the room. It passed without a division, and Martha said that the mills at Doire ghlas would give the hide.

The eldest binder had come herself to hear it, seven miles by cart and then the long road round the head of the strait to Caol mhòr, and she sat on the bench by the door with her stick across her knees. When the vote was taken she got up and came down the room, and laid a second paper on the trestle before the chair, folded twice.

"There's one thing more," she said, "if the speaker will have it read."

Martha unfolded it. It was the wording of a minute, written out fair so that the clerk could copy it straight. It said that the books of Tional nan Tuathach were to be rebound by the binders of Muileann leathan and kept thereafter in the delegates' press at Caol mhòr; and that a second set of the same was made, and was hidden elsewhere; and that this minute should stand as all the gathering knew of it.

She read it through twice. The gale drummed on the roof. Then she looked up at the eldest binder, who looked back at her with no expression at all.

"Made," Martha said. "Not to be made."

"Made," said the eldest binder.

"Since when?"

"Longer than you've been in that chair, Mistress."

Martha read it out to the room. There was no sound at the end of it but the wind and the swing and creak of the lamps. Forty faces looked at her along the trestle, and she looked at them. She saw Peter Hale sitting stiff as a mast, and the old fisherman of Cathair gheal with his eyes shut, and the hewers of the western hills side by side on their bench with their black knuckles folded, and she could not tell from any face whether it knew more than she did.

"Is there anybody in this room," she said, "who knows where?"

Nobody answered.

She sat for a while with the paper under her hand. She thought of Daniel Frayne with his palms laid flat on the blanket, putting a thing on the table and letting it lie. The census figures had once been read out in this room, and he had written two words under them. She thought, because she was a mill manager and could not help it, of the kingdom's clerks at Cathair dhearg, and what a speaker of Tional nan Tuathach might one day be asked across a table by a man with a pen, and of what it would be worth then to be able to say that she did not know.

"Then I shan't ask," she said. "Thomas, write it as it's written."

Thomas Rook copied it into the book, word for word, while the room watched his pen. When he had done he turned the book round on the trestle and held out the pen to her, and Martha Greaves signed the minute under the line that said a second set was made and hidden elsewhere. Her hand was steady. She blotted it herself.

The eldest binder took back her paper and folded it into her sleeve. "Thank you, Mistress," she said, and went back to her bench by the door and sat down.

The last business of the sitting was the carrying. The books could not go to Muileann leathan, for the bindery had been Daniel's house as well as his shop and his kin were clearing it, and there was no room in it now to lay out sixty-three books and their loose leaves on a table at once. Ellen Marsh had written from Doire ghlas to say that her schoolroom was long and dry, with a good north light and a stove that drew, and that the children could be taught in the church meanwhile, since the priest had said he would be glad of the noise. The binders could come to it from Muileann leathan in a morning, and Caol mhòr could send the books to it in a day.

The room thanked Mistress Marsh by a vote, and it was moved that the delegate of Caol mhòr carry the books to Doire ghlas and back, since his great-grandfather had written the first of them. Peter Hale went red to the ears and said that he would.

## V. Of the Schoolroom and the Press

Peter Hale brought them in the first week of am Màrt, in a hired cart with the tilt laced down and the books packed upright in three tea-chests lined with oilcloth, and he walked beside the horse the whole nine miles from Caol mhòr with one hand on the tailboard. At Doire ghlas the children had been let out early to see the books come in, and they stood in two lines under the grey oaks outside Agnes Pike's school in their pinafores and boots, and watched him lift the chests down as if they held a king's regalia. Ellen Marsh did not shoo them off. She said that they might as well see the town's history carried in the door once, since it was so seldom that any of it was.

The schoolroom was what she had said: long, whitewashed, with tall windows to the north and a black iron stove at the end. The benches were pushed back and the children's tables set end to end down the floor. The binders came over from Muileann leathan the next morning in two carts with their standing presses and sewing frames lashed down under sacking, and by noon the schoolroom smelt of paste and warm glue and the stove, and nobody who went in could tell any longer that it had ever been a school, except for the alphabet painted round the top of the walls in the humans' letters, and under it the same in Dia-thìris.

Martha came over from the mill most evenings when the looms were stopped. It was a short walk under the oaks. She told herself she came to see that the hide was the hide she had paid for, and after the first week she stopped telling herself anything. She would sit on the end of a bench with her coat still on and watch them work by the lamps until Ellen came in from her rooms at the back with a pot of tea and sat beside her.

The binders took the books apart first, all of them, before they put any together. They cut the old stitching, lifted the gatherings out of the boards and laid them flat on the tables in order, sixty-three rows of loose pages under weights, so that the whole long record of the room lay open down the length of the floor at once. Martha walked the length of it one evening from the stove to the door, and it took her twenty paces, and she thought that it was seventy-seven years.

When they came to take Walter Hale's first book out of its boards, the eldest binder called Martha and Ellen over to the table under the lamp.

"Look here," she said.

She had slit the old spine, and turned back the first gathering, and at the inside of the fold, between the stitching and the first page of minutes, there was a row of narrow stubs where leaves had been cut out close with a knife. On the stubs, at the edge, were the ends of ruled columns, and a few figures in a neat brown hand, and on one of them the tail of a word in the humans' tongue.

"It's a harbour ledger," said the eldest binder. "Tonnage and dues. He cut out what he'd written for the humans and used the rest."

Martha bent close. The figures on the stubs were the same brown and the same hand as the minute that followed. Walter Hale had been a harbour clerk under the humans. When the humans' harbour office closed and the Tuathaich came together in the room above it, he had taken the ledger he had in his desk, cut out his old masters' accounts, and written the first day of Tional nan Tuathach on the next clean page. On that page the ink was a little blotted, as if the pen had been wetted too full.

"He hadn't a new book," said Ellen Marsh.

"He hadn't anything," said Martha. "None of them had." She straightened up. "Keep the stubs in."

"I'd meant to," said the eldest binder.

They went on through am Màrt and into an Giblean. Gathering by gathering the old pages were mended where they were torn, backed with fine paper where they were weak, pressed flat, and sewn onto new cords with the waxed linen that Daniel Frayne had taught them to draw. The boards were covered in the brown hide from the Doire ghlas tannery, and the eldest binder cut the lettering on each spine herself with a hot tool, the years and nothing else. The finished books stood in a row on the long shelf under the window, and every day the row was a little longer.

The children came in and out. The eldest binder let them, provided they kept their hands behind their backs, and a few of the older girls were set to fold and trim the backing paper and did it well. One wet afternoon Martha found a boy of nine standing at the table where the minutes of the census year lay spread under their weights, reading them upside down with his lips moving.

"What's that say, missus?" he asked her, pointing.

She looked. It was a page in Daniel Frayne's small upright hand, with rows of figures read out from the kingdom's paper, and underneath, alone on the line, the two words.

"Now written," she said.

"What's now written?"

"That's the question," said Martha, and after a while, since he was still waiting, she told him about the census, and the northern towns set down at half the measure, and the room in Caol mhòr where it was read out, and the speaker who sat with his hands flat on the table and would not raise his voice. The boy listened with his hands behind his back, as he had been told. When she had done he said that he thought the speaker ought to have shouted, and went off to fold paper.

Ellen had been listening from the stove. When the boy was gone she came and stood by Martha at the table and looked down the long rows of pages.

"It's all what was decided," she said. "Motions and votes and letters sent and letters received and filed. The price of seed-oats. Every page of it."

"That's what minutes are."

"I know it." Ellen touched the edge of the census page with one ink-stained finger, lightly, not to disturb the weights. "There's not a line in seventy-seven years of what happened to anybody."

Martha did not answer that. She had no answer ready, and she was not a woman who said things she had not thought out. After a time Ellen went back to the stove and poured the tea, and the binders worked on.

The last book was finished on the twenty-sixth of an Giblean, a clear cold morning with a high wind. It was Daniel Frayne's own last book, which ended in the middle of a page with the room's business for the last week of an Dùbhlachd, and after that the first minute in Thomas Rook's hand of the speaker's death. The eldest binder cut the spine with the two years it held and set it on the shelf at the end of the row. Then she stood back, leaning on her stick, and looked at the sixty-three books together for a long time, and said that it would do.

Peter Hale came for them the next day with the same cart and the same tea-chests. The children lined up under the oaks again to see them go, and Ellen Marsh stood in the door of her school and watched until the cart had gone out of sight among the trees. Then she went in and began to push the benches back into their rows.

Martha rode in the cart. It was a bright blowing day, with the rain gone east, and the road ran down through the oak country toward the head of the strait, and they came into Caol mhòr in the middle of the afternoon with the tide out and the fishing boats lying over on the mud. Peter Hale carried the chests up the outside stair one at a time. Thomas Rook had the press open and had scrubbed its shelves.

Martha laid the books in herself. She took them out of the chests one by one, in order, reading the years on each spine before she set it in its place, beginning at the top left with Walter Hale's harbour ledger in its new brown hide and ending low on the right with Daniel's. It took a long time, and neither of the men offered to help. When the last was in she ran her hand along the spines once, top shelf to bottom, the way she ran it along a row of bolts in the warehouse to be sure the count was right. It was right. She shut the doors of the press and locked them, and took the key off the lock, and put it on the ring at her belt with the keys of the mills.

"There's a minute wants writing," said Thomas Rook.

"Write that the books are laid in the press," said Martha. "Sixty-three of them, rebound by the binders of Muileann leathan, at the cost of their thread. Write the day."

He wrote it. He did not ask whether he should write anything more, and she did not tell him to, for there was nothing more that she knew.

She went down the stair afterward and stood a while at the harbour wall in the wind with her coat buttoned to the chin. The tide was on the turn and the boats were beginning to lift. Across the strait the evening came up grey off the water, and along the harbour front the lamps were being lit in the windows, one and then another. The key knocked against the others at her belt when she moved. She stood there until the first boats were afloat, and then she went to find the carter for the road home.
