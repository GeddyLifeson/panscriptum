## I. Of the Capping

The council did not stand the regiments down, and Somhairle mac Lachlainn, reading the minute that said so in the first week of am Faoilleach, understood before he had finished it why the thing had been done, and understood also that no one in the room where it was decided would ever say the reason aloud. A regiment sent home was a regiment that had to be raised again from nothing if the hills gave any more trouble than a farmer's grumbling, and raising nine regiments once had cost the kingdom the better part of two years and more men than the open record cared to total in one place; keeping them, at their own stations, on the wartime tithe that had been laid on every barrel of dubhan sold since the second month of the fighting, cost a line in the Treasury's book and nothing else that anyone in that room was minded to count. The tithe had been called, when it was laid, a thing for the war's sake and for a time only. Somhairle had heard the phrase often enough that winter, from men who plainly believed it and from men who as plainly did not, to know by now which kind of man was speaking by the speed with which he said it.

He kept, that first spring of the peace, to the rooms near the coal line's own offices that the Board had given him through the war and had not yet asked back, and it was there, at a table too large for the papers he had to put on it, that the reports of the western seams first came to him, forwarded by Fearghas mac Aonghais, master of the Mine Board at Cathair mhòr, with a covering note two lines long: the seams were the Crown's by the Leòid ruling of six years before, the holders had stopped paying when the Board stopped carrying, and the Board saw no profit and considerable risk in leaving open ground that had lately held an army. The order that followed asked for nothing subtler than concrete and men enough to pour it, and the seventh regiment, quartered at the western Seann Skell since before the fighting began and idle now in a way that Somhairle suspected its own colonel found harder to bear than the siege had been, was given the men.

He went himself to the first of the cappings, at Àth leathan, in am Màrt, for no better reason he could have given anyone than that he had commanded the taking of the ground and felt it owed him, or he it, the seeing of what came after. The pit-head stood much as the battle had left it, the winding-gear still up though the wheel no longer turned, the spoil-heaps gone green at their lower slopes with the first of the year's grass and black and bare higher up where nothing had yet had time to take. A company of the seventh worked the shaft mouth with barrows and a mixer brought up from the western Seann Skell on a lorry that ran, as every lorry on the island now ran, on the fuel a Tuathach had made twenty years before at a fire he had banked in the dark for reasons the record did not give and Somhairle, standing at the shaft's edge with the smell of the wet mix in his throat, found he did not much want to guess at either. The concrete went down grey and thick and, poured, lay still in a way that struck him, watching it, as unlike the seam it covered, which even flooded had never once in the whole history of the pit lain entirely still.

A sergeant of the seventh, a broad man named Iain Mòr who had held the Àth leathan post's ground since the last month of the war, stood by him at the shaft's mouth and told him, without being asked, that this was the fourth such capping his company had stood by that month, the others at Muileann àrsaidh, at Cnoc thais, and at a smaller working near Àth naomh that had never had a name outside its own valley. "Tobar dhearg of the hills we've still to do," he said, "and after that there'll be nothing left in the west for a man to be sent to guard, unless the council finds him something new to guard." He did not say this bitterly. He said it the way a man reports weather, a fact to be noted and then let go, and Somhairle, who had by then spent the better part of two years learning to hear what a soldier did not say along with what he did, understood that Iain Mòr had already begun, in his own mind, to wonder what the seventh regiment was for, now that the war it had been raised to fight had ended and the ground it had fought over was being filled in under his own boots.

Word came to him that same week of a small matter from the eastern side of the island that had nothing to do with the west at all and everything to do with what the peace was already turning into: Caol ruadh, whose harbour had fed the garrisons and the second fleet through both years of the fighting with more salt fish and biscuit than any town its size had ever been asked to victual before, had been granted, by a council minute so short Somhairle read it twice to be sure he had not missed a clause, a market right of its own for the strait below it. It was the sixteenth town so chartered on the island and, the minute noted in its last line, as if the fact carried some weight worth setting down, the last that would be. Somhairle, who had never in his life had cause to think about market charters one way or the other, found himself thinking of it now chiefly as a kind of book-keeping: the kingdom settling its accounts with the towns that had fed the war, town by town, in whatever coin came cheapest to it, a charter here, a contract there, nothing that could not be filed and forgotten once given.

By the month's end the last of the western workings that were not the Board's own was capped, and Fearghas mac Aonghais's office at Cathair mhòr sent out, without much ceremony, a further note that the trade in coal-fuel, outside what the Board itself still cut for its own account at the Sloc Mòr, was at an end on the island; no law had ever been troubled to forbid it; it ended all the same, the way a road empties when, at last, nowhere along it is left that anyone still wants to go. The Sloc Mòr's own galleries were named nowhere in the note, and Somhairle understood the omission for what it was: a mine close enough to the capital for the Board to keep an eye on it directly was one matter, and the western hills, a two days' ride from anyone minded to ask an awkward question, had plainly been another.

Somhairle read that note at his own table with the lamp lit, for the days were still short, and found his thoughts turning, for reasons he did not examine closely, away from the western pits and toward the poorer streets of the capital as he had last walked them as a young man newly with the Board, dark from the lamp-houses' rationing, and of how thoroughly, in the years since, that darkness had been undone by the very fuel that had just finished undoing the last of the trade it replaced. Every lamp in the capital now burned on dubhan, every lorry and every engine of the Rail Board besides, and a man could go the length of the island, he supposed, and not find so much as a coal fire outside a smith's forge or the Board's own hearths, and yet no council minute, so far as he had read, had ever once said in plain words that lighting, moving and warming the whole kingdom on dubhan was now simply how things stood; it had become true the way the western seams had become quiet, by a hundred small orders that never once named the whole of what they added up to. He wrote nothing of this thought down. It was not the kind a report had room for.

## II. Of the Boundary Stones

Martha Greaves had sat as speaker of the Moot long enough by then to have learned that most of what came to her desk in a given month could be answered by a clerk, and that the little which could not was, in the end, simply lived through and not answered at all; so that when the letter came in an t-Iuchar from the capital's gaol, by way of the council's own office and not, as everything else from that quarter usually came, by way of the Board, she read it twice before she understood that this was neither kind, and that it would want, of her, more than a signature.

The letter said that the holdouts taken at Muileann ghlas the December before, some four score of them who had come out of the byres unarmed on the promise the first regiment's colonel had given them that night, were to be released and sent north under guard, as far as the boundary stones, where they would be handed over to whatever authority the shires beyond chose to send to meet them. It named a day. It did not say what the men and the handful of women among them were to do once handed over, nor who, if anyone, was to feed them on the road, nor whether their own towns, most of them long since walled with spoil and flooded, would know what to make of men coming home a year and more after the fighting they had left them in had ended. Martha read all this standing at her own window with the letter in her hand, and then sat down and wrote to Peter Hale, who had carried the rebound minute-books through half the war on his own back and had earned, she thought, more say in how the Moot's part of this ended than any other man in Caol mhòr, asking him to come north with her to the stones.

They went up on the last carriage the coal line ran that far, on a morning with the first real heat of the year in it, the kind of morning that in another year would have had the hill farms turning hay and did that year too, Martha noted from the carriage window, as if the land had not been told a war had passed over it. The boundary stones stood where they had always stood, set some ninety years before at the Severance and never once, in all that time, moved by so much as a foot, low grey markers a man could sit on and often did, worn smooth on their upper faces by generations of shepherds waiting out a shower. A company of the first regiment was drawn up on the southern side of them when Martha and Peter arrived, and beyond, on the northern side, a scatter of Tuathaich had already gathered, word of the day having travelled ahead of the letter by roads the council's own office never troubled to use.

The prisoners came up the road on foot, in a column no longer than the fourth part of a mile, and Martha, watching them come, found she had braced herself for men marked by a year's close keeping in a way that none of them, when they were near enough to see plainly, quite were; thinner, most of them, and slower on their feet than men of their years should have been, but walking under their own strength and looking, more than anything else, like men who had stopped some time before expecting to be looked at as anything worse than tired. The colonel in charge read out a paper at the stones that said, in the dry way such papers said everything that year, that these were released on the council's order and were the Moot's charge from this point north; and it was at that word, charge, that Martha understood what her own part in the day was to be, and stepped forward with the register she had brought up from Caol mhòr in her own bag rather than trust it to a clerk.

The Moot had kept, since the Severance, one long list of its dead and its maimed, added to every year the war or the pits or the fever took a name from the north, and it was to the foot of this list, in a fresh column ruled that morning by Peter's own hand because no other paper in the party would serve, that Martha now added, one by one, as each man or woman came forward and gave a name and a town, the names of those who had gone into the byres at Muileann ghlas and come out again. She wrote plainly, town beside name as the corporal's list of the regiments' own dead had been written the December before, though she did not know that list existed and would not, this book must say, ever come to read it; and more than one of those who gave her their name that morning gave, along with it, some further word she had not asked for and set down anyway, because it seemed to her wrong to take a name and refuse whatever the man attached to it, that morning of all mornings, wanted said with it. A woman of Cnoc thais gave her son's name and then, unbidden, the words he is not among these, meaning that her son had not come out of the byres at all, and Martha wrote both the name given and, beside it in a smaller hand, the four words, because she judged, rightly or not, that the register would be a poorer thing without them.

Peter Hale stood at her shoulder through the whole of it and said little, though Martha noticed, more than once, that his own pen had gone still over the page at a name he seemed to recognise, and it was only afterward, on the carriage south, that he told her the fourth man to come up had been apprenticed, before the trouble began, to the same binder at Muileann leathan whose old pupils had rebound the Moot's minute-books at Daniel Frayne's dying wish, fifteen years before, and that the man had not known him, or had known him and chosen, that morning, not to say so. "I could not have said which," Peter told her, "so I let it go unasked. It did not seem a morning for asking a man to remember more of himself than he had already been asked to give up that day." Martha thought this the truest thing said to her the whole of that summer, and told him so, and neither of them spoke of it again.

Not every man who came up the road that morning gave his name readily. One, older than most, stood some while before the register without speaking at all, and Martha, who had by then learned in her years as speaker not to press a silence that might yet fill on its own, waited with her pen lifted until he said, at last, that his name would mean nothing written down beside a town that no longer stood in any shape a name could be sent back to, Muileann àrsaidh having been, by the last report she had herself read, more spoil-heap than street by the war's end; and Martha told him, as truly as she knew how, that the town would stand again in whatever shape its own people chose to build it in, register or no register, but that the register would hold his name either way, against the day it did. He gave it then, and his town with it, and walked on north without waiting to hear whether she believed her own answer, which she was not, in fact, entirely sure she did.

By the time the last name was taken the sun stood well past noon, and the released men and women had already begun, in ones and twos, to go north past the stones under no guard at all, the first regiment's company having no order to do more than see them handed over and no great wish, that Martha could see, to linger on a road that led nowhere its own men wanted to walk. She closed the register at last with some four score names added to its foot, a column of its own beside the long column the war before this one had begun, and rode back to the coal line's carriage with Peter beside her and the book on her knee, and found, somewhere on that road south, that she could not have said with any confidence whether the day had been a mercy or only the last administrative act of a war that had run, by then, so long past any single act's power to end it that mercy and book-keeping had come, in practice, to look much the same thing.

## III. Of the Seat and the Post

The council's summons to Somhairle came in an Lùnastal, three lines on a single sheet, inviting him to take a seat among the twelve at the next sitting after harvest, and he read it, when it came, with less surprise than he thought a man ought to feel at being asked to sit among the twelve who had, for two years, decided from a room he had never once been admitted to what his own regiments were to do and where. He had commanded a war, by then, longer than most of the twelve had held their own seats, and it occurred to him, folding the letter and setting it under the ink-stand for want of anywhere better to keep it, that a soldier given a seat on the council was less an honour done to the soldier than an insurance the council was taking out against its own next reckoning, so that the next war, should the island ever raise another, would be made by men who had at least once stood close enough to a field to know what the word cost in more than coin. He did not refuse it, having not, by then, refused much that the council had asked of him in three years, and saw no cause to begin with the one thing it offered rather than demanded.

The autumn sitting seated him, the first soldier to hold a chair there since the captains of the Severance war, whose own names, he had occasion to remember, he had himself read aloud from the council's chests not two years before to raise the very regiments he now, as a councillor, would help to keep or stand down. He said little at that first sitting, by his own later account, chiefly because the twelve about him, Anna nic Phàil among them, spoke that day mostly of the harvest tithe and the coal line's autumn schedule and other matters a soldier had no particular fitness to weigh, and he judged it wiser, in a room he did not yet know the temper of, to listen a full season before he ventured a view the others might have cause to remember against him.

Anna nic Phàil was the one member of the twelve who spoke to him directly that day, catching him at the chamber door as the sitting broke, to say, without much preamble, that she had stood on the bench in the passage the Christmas two years before when Martha Greaves carried the Moot's last petition into that same room, and had voted, since, for every small mercy the council's own business allowed her to move, and meant to go on doing so, and thought a man who had commanded the regiments ought to know it before he had sat among them long enough to suppose the room held only the twelve kinds of silence he had likely met there that first day. Somhairle thanked her and said little more, less from want of an answer than because he had not yet decided, in his own mind, which of the twelve kinds of silence he had in fact met that morning was hers, and did not think it a courtesy to guess wrong to her face on the first day of their both sitting there.

The seventh regiment, meanwhile, having capped the last of the western seams by midsummer, was set that autumn to a work of its own choosing rather than the council's, though the council's leave was asked and given readily enough: a post of stone and timber on the road below Àth leathan, at the crossing where the holdouts had once formed on the morning of the battle, built by the regiment's own hands over six weeks with materials carted up from the western Seann Skell. It went into the regimental returns under the plain name the lists had used from the first order that raised it, the Àth leathan post, and Somhairle, reading that return at his own table in the same room where he had once read the order to march on the town, found that the name sat oddly with him, a name so exactly what the thing was, a post kept at Àth leathan, that it told a reader nothing at all of what had been fought for on that ground or what had been lost taking it.

He learned, from a report of Iain Mòr's some weeks later, that the town itself, the four-and-thirty souls who had come back to what was left of it since the pits were flooded, called the post something else, though the report did not say what, Iain Mòr having judged, in his own words, that it was not a soldier's business to carry a name like that back to his own colonel to be entered anywhere the council might one day read it. Somhairle did not press him for it. He had learned, in three years, to recognise the shape of a thing a man had decided not to tell him, and had learned, too, that pressing rarely got him more than a different silence dressed as an answer; and there was, besides, some part of him, which he did not examine too closely that autumn any more than he had examined the thought at the capping in am Màrt, that judged the town's own name for the post, whatever it was, none of the council's proper concern, and least of all his own, now that he sat among the twelve who might, given the chance, ask him to have it struck from every mouth that used it.

## IV. Of the Stone and the Sown Field

The plough had gone into the field at Muileann ghlas that same spring, while Somhairle was still watching concrete settle at Àth leathan and Martha had not yet had the letter from the gaol, for the farmer who held the ground, a broad quiet man named Aonghas mac Phàdraig who had worked that field and his father's before him, saw no reason a battle fought on it once should keep it from feeding his own house a second winter running, and turned the first furrow in am Màrt as he had turned it every spring of his life. He found the first of the iron before the furrow was half done, a bayonet gone to rust, turned up whole and lying across the share as if it had been laid there rather than buried, and he stood some while over it, by his own account given afterward to more than one neighbour, before he judged there was nothing to be done but finish the furrow and carry the thing home at the day's end, which he did, and gave it, some weeks later, to a corporal of the first regiment who came through the district collecting rents on the Board's account and had, it turned out, no better notion than Aonghas himself of what ought to be done with a rusted bayonet turned up by a plough, but took it anyway and entered it, for want of any other heading that suited, on a list he kept for exactly such things. He would keep that list for years, the corporal, adding to it each spring as Aonghas and, in time, his sons after him turned up more of the same, a bayonet, a broken rifle stock, once a whole cartridge box gone green with the years in the ground, and no two men who ever saw that list agreed, when asked, on what it was truly a record of: the dead, who could not be counted any other way, or only the ground, giving back, in its own slow time, what two armies had once been in too great a hurry to stop and gather.

The stone at Cathair mhòr was raised in an Dùbhlachd, a year to the week since the battle it named, beside the market cross that the house of Mac Ùisdein had kept at the town's centre for longer than anyone now living could say, and Somhairle, seated by then among the twelve, was asked, as the regiments' own councillor, to stand at its unveiling and read the names cut into it aloud before the town. He had not sought the office. He had not, if the truth were told, much wanted it, having spent two years reading rolls of names in halls and camps and once, at Muileann ghlas itself by report only, at a trestle table turned to a desk by a corporal with no other paper to hand, and finding each reading harder than the last rather than easier, as he had once supposed such things grew with use. But the council had asked it of him as it had asked the seat, and he had learned by then which of the council's askings a man in his place could safely decline, and this, he judged, standing before the stone in the cold with the town's whole population, or near it, gathered in the square before him, was not among them.

The stone bore one-and-sixty names, eight-and-thirty of Àth leathan and three-and-twenty of Muileann ghlas, cut in two columns under a single heading that gave neither battle nor date but only, in the plainest words the mason's letter to the council had proposed and the council had accepted without change, the regiments' dead. Somhairle read them from a paper copy rather than from the stone itself, the light being poor and his own eyes, he supposed, no longer what they had been at thirty, and read them slowly, town by town as the corporal's list and the sergeant's return had first set them down, a boy of Cathair mhòr's own western quarter, two brothers of Seann Bhral who had enlisted together and died, the return noted, within the same hour of the same morning, a man of Inis mhòr old enough to have a grown son of his own already serving in the same company. The square kept a silence through the whole of the reading that Somhairle, listening to his own voice fall into it name after name, found harder to bear than any noise the crowd might have made instead, for a silence like that asked nothing of him and gave him, in return, nothing to answer, only the sound of his own voice going on alone until the last name was said and there was, for one long moment before anyone in the square moved or spoke, nothing left to read.

He stood a while longer at the stone after the crowd had begun to break up and go home to its own suppers, the paper still in his hand, and thought, as he had half expected he would from the moment the council first asked him to do the reading, less of the one-and-sixty names he had just given back their voices for the length of a winter afternoon than of the others the stone did not carry and never would: the column's own dead, buried in more lime than three-and-twenty graves could need and never once counted by any hand the open record trusted; the sixty who had come out of the byres and gone, some of them, into the register Martha Greaves had carried up to the boundary stones that summer with four words written beside one name that was not, in the end, among those released; a woman's son of Cnoc thais who was not among these, and whose own stone, if his mother ever raised one, would stand nowhere the council's own mason had been asked to cut, in a field or a chapel yard the open record had no occasion ever to visit. Sixty-one names were a great many to read aloud in one winter afternoon, and Somhairle, folding the paper at last and turning for the coal line's carriage that would carry him home to the capital and the twelve who waited there to hear how the unveiling had gone, found that the number staying with him longest, walking away from the stone in the last of that short day's light, was the one no mason on the island had yet been asked, or would ever be asked, to cut anywhere at all, and never the one already cut into it.
