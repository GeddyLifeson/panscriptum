# Of the Old Reckoning and the Last Hewers

*DE 26*

> **Part Eight · Of the Present Year**

## I. Of the Word Nobody Had Coined

No one afterward could say who had said it first, or where, though a dozen towns of the north claimed the saying for their own before the year was out, each with a different mouth to hang it on: an old woman of Doire ghlas selling eggs at her own door, a boatwright of Caol mhòr with his hands still black from a hull's engine, a schoolboy at his slate who had heard the two words joined at his own supper table and brought them back to his class the next morning as though he had made them himself. What was certain, by the time Sìleas nic Rath came to set the matter down at Muileann chaol, was only that the word had not existed at the turn of the year and could not, by its end, be kept out of any Tuathaich mouth that spoke for more than a minute together of the eleven books Ellen Marsh had asked the Library to keep, or of the motion for amends that Anna nic Phàil had carried into the Moot's minute the year before and that no vote had ever come to answer.

Seann-chunntas, they had begun to call the two things together, the tabled request and the dead motion, as though a single word could hold what two separate papers, filed a year apart in two separate rooms, had somehow failed between them to close. The old reckoning. No one in the north claimed to know what settling it would look like, or who would be the one to say the account was even; it kept, rather, the shape of a wrong no court had ever been asked to try, carried forward from one year's ledger to the next, the line never struck because no one on either side had found a way to strike it without first owning that the line had been drawn at all.

Sìleas heard the word first from a carrier who came up the coal line road with salt fish for the Library's own kitchen and stood talking a while in the yard with the porter before he went on, and she heard it again within the month from a letter of Ellen Marsh's that reached her desk unbidden, a short civil note asking after nothing more than the eleventh book's binding, in which the schoolteacher had used the word twice in passing, as a woman uses a word she has stopped noticing is not yet in general currency. She copied both instances into her own working notes, as she copied any word she had not met before, meaning at first only to ask Raghnall, when the season allowed, whether the Library's own glossary of the north's speech ought to carry it. It was some months before she understood, turning the carrier's yard-talk and the schoolteacher's letter over together on a wet afternoon with little else on her desk to occupy her, what the word truly was: a name the north had given to something the kingdom's own record had never troubled to name, and the giving of it, whatever the council's twelve made of it, an entry that belonged somewhere in the open chronicle, though she was hard put, that same afternoon, to say under what heading a chronicler ought properly to enter a word.

She entered it, in the end, under no heading at all, in a single line at the foot of a page otherwise given over to a Board return on the coal line's autumn schedule, noting only that the word had been heard that year in more than one northern town, joining the Library's tabled request of two years before with the Moot's dead motion of the year past, and that no Dia-thìreach in office, so far as her own hearing of the council's business went, had yet used it or shown any sign of having heard it. She wrote the line, she told her clerk some years after, less because she supposed it would matter to any reader fifty years on than because a chronicle that let a whole people coin a name for its own grievance and troubled to record everything else that year, the fair's takings, the regiments' postings, the price of salt at Àth bheag, but not that, would have failed, in her own judgment, at the one thing a chronicle was for.

## II. Of the Last Twelve

The order to shut the upper galleries of the Sloc Mòr came down from the Mine Board to the maor of Achadh dhomhain in an Gearran, four lines on a single sheet not unlike the order that had sealed the Library's own war record two years before, and it gave, as that order had, no reason beyond the plain fact of the thing required: the upper workings, which had given the kingdom the greater part of its coal before the vein began to fail and gave it now, by the Board's own reckoning, less than the cost of keeping the pumps running against the water, were to be closed, the timbering left standing and the shafts capped against a day the Board did not trouble to name. One face only was to be kept, the deepest and driest of the old workings, and that face was given over entire to the Rite, to cut altar-coal for the houses of Brìde's order, a few baskets a year, no more, and no less either, than the Rite's own accounting required.

Twelve hewers were kept to work it, chosen by the maor from among the few dozen still living who had learned the trade before the Guild of Hewers dissolved itself at Seann Toll fourteen years before, and the Rite, having no oath of its own ready-made for coal-cutters, took the guild's dissolved rolls, which Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh had carried to the Library with his own hands in the year of the dissolution, and drew from them a form of words the high custodian's own house judged fit for men who cut fuel for an altar rather than a market. The twelve were sworn at Achadh dhomhain on a morning of hard frost, in the yard above the one shaft still open, by a priest sent down from the southern Seann Skell for the purpose, who read the oath from a single sheet and set his own hand to a second copy that went back south with him, while the twelve stood bareheaded in a line that had not, within living memory of the oldest among them, been drawn up for any purpose but a shift's muster.

They were, by every account this book has found, an ill-assorted dozen for so solemn a swearing: two brothers who had cut coal together since boyhood and would go on doing so, the elder said afterward, until the vein or their own backs gave out, whichever came first; a widow's son barely twenty who had never worked any face but the one now shut and had asked to be kept on the altar-face less from love of the trade than from want of any other the maor's district could offer him; and, at the end of the line nearest the priest, an old man well past sixty who had hewed at the Sloc Mòr since before Cathal was crowned and who told the priest, when the oath was done and the man asked, out of ordinary courtesy, whether he had any words of his own to add to it, that he had sworn the guild's oath once already in his life and had thought, swearing it, that it would be the last oath a coal-cutter's trade would ever ask of him, and that he supposed a man in his years ought not to be surprised any longer at being wrong.

The eleven upper galleries were capped within the fortnight, the timbering left to rot in the dark as the order required, and the maor's own report to the Board that season noted, in the same flat hand it gave to every other return, that the coal caste of Achadh dhomhain, the families whose blood the old measured handful had touched and who had worked no trade but the vein since the Ancient Age, said nothing against the closing and asked nothing of the Board in its place, as they had said nothing and asked nothing at every turn the vein's failing had brought them these thirty years past.

## III. Of the Còmhrag and the Watch at the Library

Of two things that same summer the open record kept a plainer account, and Sìleas entered both in the ordinary way, without the private line she had given the north's new word. At Ceann leathan, where the yearly melee had been fought every summer since before any Dia-thìreach now living could remember its founding, the regiments entered fighters for the first time, a company from each of the nine sending its own man to the field below the town where the fair-wardens had raised the gate-price twice already since the war's own gate-money first taught them how much a crowd would bear. The ninth regiment's man, a broad quiet Dia-thìreach of the southern Seann Skell who had refitted engines under Peigi nic Ascaill's own charter before the regiments took him, won it in the third bout of the afternoon, and the crowd, Sìleas's own return notes, cheered him no louder and no softer than it had cheered any winner of the Còmhrag in the years before the regiments had any part in it, which she took, setting the line down, for as plain a mark as any that some things in the kingdom had not yet troubled themselves to notice a war had been fought at all.

At Muileann chaol that same season the second regiment, quartered at Àth dhìreach to guard the coal line and the library road as it had since the war's own first musters, was given a further charge the council's order did not trouble to explain: a guard of six men, stood at the Library's own gate through every hour of the day and night, over a room whose existence beyond the Library's own walls no more than a dozen people in the kingdom were supposed to know of at all. Raghnall mac Mhuirich watched the first of them take up the post from his own window on the morning they arrived, a sergeant and five men with a tent pitched in the yard where the copyists' own handcarts usually stood, and wrote to the council, within the week, in a letter Sìleas copied into the day's returns before she quite knew what to make of it herself, that the Library had not asked for a guard, had never in the whole of its history needed one to keep a room the Library's own lock already held shut, and would be glad of the council's reasons, whenever the council found leisure to give any.

No reasons came, that year or the next. The guard stood its watch through every season after, relieved by the second regiment's own rota without a word from the Library's own gate to the soldiers' tent or from the tent to the gate beyond the ordinary courtesies a sentry and a porter exchange across a yard they must both cross a dozen times a day, and Raghnall, telling Sìleas of it some years after, said only that a man learns, keeping a library thirty years, to recognise the shape of an answer the council has decided not to give, and that he had stopped, by the second summer, expecting this one.

The sergeant who stood the first watch, a young Dia-thìreach of Cathair dhearg new enough to the regiment to find the whole business as strange as Raghnall did, asked him once, crossing the yard on a wet morning with nothing better to fill the silence, whether the keeper knew what it was his own men were set to guard against, and Raghnall told him, with the same flat courtesy he gave every question he had no answer for, that he supposed the council knew, and that a keeper's own business was to keep the room shut whether the guard outside it stood there for a reason or for none. The sergeant thought this over a while, walking on toward his own tent, and said, before he was quite out of earshot, that it was a queer sort of soldiering, standing watch over a door that had never once, in a whole season, opened from either side while he was there to see it; and Raghnall, watching him go, judged it the truest thing either of them said to the other that whole year.

## IV. Of the Last Coal Train

Eachann mac Ruairidh drove the coal line on the tenth of an t-Sultain as he had driven it, on and off, for six-and-thirty years, since a boy of nine had first been let up onto his own father's footplate at Muileann chrom to hold the whistle-cord and be told, each time he pulled it, exactly how long a blast the crossing ahead required. He was given the run three days before by the yard's own masters, who told him plainly, handing him the manifest, what no manifest of the arms years had ever troubled to tell him: that this would be the last train the coal line carried under that name for coal's own sake, the Board having decided, after fourteen years of carrying dubhan under a name the line had never troubled to change, that the last few wagons still owed to the southern lamp-houses under an old contract might as well go down in one final working before the contract lapsed and the name lapsed with it in every mouth but the timetable's own.

He asked for no ceremony, and none was offered him. The engine that took the wagons out of Achadh dhomhain that morning was the same dubhan engine he had driven for sixteen years, its firebox burning the same fuel it had burned every working day since the coal engines were laid up in the Muileann chrom siding and never ran again, and the six wagons behind it held coal only because the contract said coal, a scant load by the standards of the trade's old years, the kind of load a driver of his father's generation would have called barely worth the coupling. He signed the manifest at the yard office in the grey hour before first light, in the same hand he had signed every manifest of the arms years in, and found, walking out to the engine across a yard he had crossed ten thousand times before, that his own legs carried him at something less than their ordinary pace, though he could not, questioned about it afterward by his own fireman, have said why a man ought to walk any slower to an engine he meant to bring home again by noon.

The run went as any ordinary run went, the branch past Muileann mhin no worse and no better under him than it had been the week before, the crossings sounded at their proper distances, the gradient above Cathair dhearg's own goods-yard taken at the same careful speed he had always taken it since a boy on his father's footplate first watched the needle climb there and asked why the driver eased the regulator rather than pressing on. He thought, taking that same gradient for what he supposed, without quite letting himself finish the thought, might be the last time under that name, of his own father, who had driven coal on this same branch for forty years and had told him, the year the dubhan engines first came to Muileann chrom, that he would not ride one, and had kept to it, so far as Eachann knew, to the end of his own working days; and he thought, too, though less clearly, of the yard family's son killed under a wagon in the changeover fourteen years gone, a boy he had known only by sight and by the single line the yard's own register had given him, and of how strange it was that a line as long as this one, carrying coal and then dubhan and then arms and now, for one last morning, coal again, should have so few names left along its own length that a driver crossing it could still call to mind.

At Cathair dhearg's own goods-yard, where the wagons were to be unshunted and the coal handed over at last to the lamp-houses' own carters, a small knot of men had gathered along the platform edge who had no ordinary business there, yard hands off their own shift and two or three of the older drivers who had heard, in the way such things were heard along a working line before any bell announced them, that this was the coal line's last coal working under that name. They said little to him as he brought the engine in, no cheer and no speech, only a few raised hands and, from one old driver near retirement himself, a nod Eachann took, bringing the engine to its stand, for as full an accounting as the morning was likely to give of thirty years' worth of coal carried down that same branch by men most of whom the register held no line for at all.

He unshunted the wagons himself rather than leave it to the yard hands, working the couplings in the cold with his own bare fingers gone stiff before he was done, and stood a while afterward at the head of the empty engine while the carters loaded the coal onto their own wagons for the last stretch to the lamp-houses, watching six wagons' worth of a trade that had built the kingdom's own coin and crowned its own kings go out of the yard behind horses rather than an engine, the lamp-houses having judged, that same week, that six wagons did not warrant the trouble of a second engine's steam merely to finish what one engine had already finished carrying. His own fireman, the same broad quiet man who had ridden the arms trains with him five years before and asked him nothing then either, stood beside him through the whole of the unloading and said only, when the last sack had gone over onto the carters' own wagon, that it was a poor sort of coal by the old reckoning, small stuff and dusty, the kind a hewer of thirty years before would have thrown back down the shaft rather than send up to the weigh-house at all; and Eachann, who had thought the same thing himself without troubling to say it, answered only that poor coal carried the same as good coal, so long as the wagon held together, which was as much of an epitaph as either man saw fit to give it.

He rode the empty train back to Muileann chrom that afternoon at the ordinary speed, no faster and no slower than any light working he had ever brought home, and signed the day's return at the yard office in the same hand, and told his own fireman, walking out into the last of the light, that he supposed the line would keep the name a good while yet in the timetables, whatever it carried, because a name, once printed often enough on enough paper, outlasts by some years the thing it was first printed to describe. He walked home by the yard rows that evening rather than by the shorter way along the embankment, past the siding where the old coal engines had stood laid up and rusting these fourteen years, their fireboxes cold and their own paint gone the colour of the ground under them, and stopped a while at the fence to look at them, as he had not troubled to do in some years, before he went on to his own supper and said nothing of the day's run to his own wife beyond the plain fact that the coal line had carried its last coal, and that he had been the one to drive it.

## V. Of the Pass, the Wool, and the Foot of the Year

The returns that reached Sìleas's own desk through the rest of that autumn came, as they always came, in no order she would herself have chosen, so that the sixth regiment's northern posts and the fair's own wool-count arrived within the same week's post and lay together on her table some days before she found leisure to enter either. The sixth, quartered at Cnoc bheag since the war's own last musters, had begun that same month to keep posts of its own at the boundary stones on the northern road, the first stones Seumas mac Eachainn and old Iain Ruadh had ridden past six-and-twenty years before to learn whether a new fuel burned as men said, and for the first time since the Severance itself a Tuathach going south past those stones was asked to show a pass, a single printed sheet the maor's own clerks had begun issuing that autumn from a table set up in the shadow of the stones themselves. The council's minute on it, when it reached the Library, called the passes a measure for a time only, in the same three words the wartime tithe had been given six years before and had not, in all that time, been taken away again, and Sìleas, copying the minute into the day's page, set down the three words exactly as the council had given them and added nothing of her own beside them, though she found, writing them, that her own pen paused a moment longer over that particular line than the rest of the page had asked of it.

The fair at Muileann òg that same season kept the shape it had kept since the first fair any record of the island remembered, traders of every shire the state now held bringing their own goods to the ford where a Stone King and a holder of the Red Hill had once agreed salt for wool, and Tuathaich wool sold there still, as it had sold every year since the truce, on its own northern day, through Dia-thìrich factors who took their own cut of every fleece before a northern seller saw the coin. Ùna nic Ailein's own court entered the day's takings in the ordinary column, no different in its figures from the year before or the year before that, and Sìleas, setting the fair's return beside the sixth regiment's own pass-list on the same page of her working notes, found herself thinking, though she wrote none of it into the chronicle proper, that a trade six generations old and a pass six weeks old had come, in that one autumn, to stand for the same plain fact from two different directions: that the north sold to the south as it always had, and crossed into the south, now, only by the south's own leave.

It was in the last week of the same month, with the year's ledgers still open on her desk and the fair's own figures not yet fully copied in, that the letter came up from Àth dhìreach in the hand of Beathag's own sister, saying that Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh had died in the night, quietly, in the room she had kept these six years past since laying down the chronicler's office, and that the household there had thought Sìleas would wish to know of it before the news reached her by any slower road.

Sìleas set the letter down and did not, for some while, take up her pen again. Beathag had been five-and-seventy years old, by her own sister's own reckoning given in the same letter, a span her own mother's people had thought a fair age and no more, in a kingdom where even the king's own kin no longer lived past ninety and the last of the old long years had run out three generations before either woman was born; and Sìleas, who had come to the Library at eleven as a girl who could barely form her own letters and had been taught every stroke of the chronicler's hand by the same woman the letter now told her was dead, found that she could not, for the better part of that whole grey afternoon, set down in the open chronicle the one entry the day plainly required of her, though she had entered a hundred deaths of greater and lesser note in the thirteen years she had kept the office herself.

She wrote it, in the end, at the day's ordinary hour, in the same plain hand she gave every other entry: that Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, royal chronicler of the kingdom from SE 65 to DE 20, had died at her sister's house at Àth dhìreach, and that the Library owed to her hand the first note of the fuel that named this whole age, the re-heading of fourteen years of the kingdom's own record, and a great many questions that no chronicler after her, Sìleas included, had yet found the means to answer. She added nothing more, no word of grief and no word of praise beyond the plain accounting of what the dead woman's own hand had left behind her, holding, as she set the pen down at last, to the same rule Beathag herself had given her the day she laid down the office six years before: write what you are let, and write down what you are not.

Folding the letter away into the same drawer where she kept every private paper the open chronicle had no proper room for, she thought of the word the north had coined that same year for its own unsettled account, and found, turning the two things over together in the last light of that short autumn day, that she did not know whether a kingdom's own record could be said to keep an old reckoning of its own, in the way a family keeps one, or whether that manner of keeping belonged, as so much else that year seemed to belong, to the north alone. It was, she judged, closing the drawer, a question fitter for a chronicler with more years left to her than the one now sitting at this desk, and she returned, the next morning, to the day's first ordinary business, exactly as she had returned to it every morning since the door at the end of the gallery was shut.
