# Of the Siege of Muileann àrsaidh

*DE 21*

## I. Of the Wall Angus Rennie Did Not Finish

They tell it in the western hills, those few of that town's own people left to tell anything at all, that the regiments came up the southern road on the twenty-first day of an Cèitean in the grey hour before a full morning, and that the first anyone in Muileann àrsaidh knew of it was the sound of the ditch-bridge going down under wheels that were not a carrier's cart. Angus Rennie had dug that ditch himself the autumn before, on his knees in the cold with his own breath smoking, and had said to Ruth Calder at the time that a road dug across at the right place could be made near as good as a wall for a fraction of the labour; and it had held a season and more against nothing heavier than a lorry turned back with stones. It did not hold against men with timber and their own backs to lay it. By the time the sun was properly up, the bridge was down and a company of the fifth regiment's advance, though nobody in the town yet knew it by that number, stood on the wrong side of Angus Rennie's ditch looking in.

Sarah Dunmore heard it from her own kitchen, where she had been feeding porridge into a boy of nine who did not want it, and knew before she reached her own door what the sound under the other sounds was, because she had spent the better part of two years by then learning the particular weight of a great many boots moving together, a sound no cart or drove of cattle ever quite made, however you tried to tell yourself otherwise in the first half-minute of hearing it. She set the boy's bowl down, told him to stay where he was in a voice that made him mind her for once without an argument, and went out into the street in her apron with her hair still not properly pinned, because there was no time left that morning to be a woman who minded such things.

The town had known the regiments were coming for the better part of a fortnight, in the way a town in the western hills always knew such things before anyone official troubled to tell it: a carter's report, a shepherd who had seen dust on the southern road that no drove of sheep raised, a cousin's letter from the western Seann Skell saying that men and wagons had been moving north out of the port in numbers nobody there had counted before. What the town had not known, and could not have known short of standing on the road to see it, was which of the wall's several weak places the regiments' engineers had marked for their own use, and it was, as it happened, the same stretch at the northern end that Angus Rennie had left unfinished the autumn before, out of what he had called simple exhaustion at the time and what the whole town understood privately to be his own unwillingness to finish a wall built clean round a town, that being, in his judgement, too near a boast for any hill town to make. He was an old man now, older than he had been that autumn by a year of siege already half begun in everyone's mind if not yet in fact, and he stood that first morning at the gap in his own unfinished work with a mattock in his two hands and eleven other men and women about him, none of whom had slept, and none of whom had any clear notion of what a mattock would do against a company of riflemen beyond the small comfort of having something in the hands.

It did nothing, in the end, because the fifth regiment's officers, whoever they were and whatever their orders said, chose that first morning to do no more than close the gap from the outside and set a picket on it, rather than force it with men still forming up from a night's march; and Sarah, coming up the street in time to see the last of that decision made, understood, watching soldiers she had never before seen stand still and businesslike on ground that had been Muileann àrsaidh's own pit-yard the evening before, that the town was not being taken that morning so much as it was being shut, the way a hand closes slowly round a thing it means to keep rather than crush.

By noon the southern road was cut as well, a second company come up behind the first and dug in across it a half-mile below the town where the ground rose enough to give a clear sight of anyone coming or going, and by the end of that first day Muileann àrsaidh, which had built its wall against a lorry and never seriously against an army, found the wall mattered little either way, because the roads it stood beside were closed at both ends and the hills behind it, steep and trackless enough that no regiment had thought them worth a picket, offered no way out that a cart or a wagon of stores could use, only paths a person on foot might take by night and at some risk. Tom Varley came to Sarah's kitchen that evening, his knee long since mended and his face the grey of a man doing sums he did not like the answer to, and said, standing in her doorway with his cap still on because he had forgotten to take it off, that he had walked the whole ring of it that afternoon, as near as the pickets would let him go, and that there was no gap in it he could find that a man might drive a cart through, only the hill paths, and those good for nothing heavier than what a back could carry.

"How long have we coal for," Sarah asked him. She kept the answer herself, to the sack, and asked it all the same, the way a person sometimes wants a bad sum said aloud by another voice before they will properly believe it.

"Weeks," Tom said. "Coal and potatoes both, if it's managed right, which it will be, because Meikle's already at the counting of it and Meikle counts things carefully." He looked at her a moment longer than the answer needed. "Weeks isn't long enough, Sarah, not truly, and we both know it. It's more than I'd have said we had a year ago, before any of us thought to lay any of it by."

She did not ask him the other question in her mind that evening, which was how many weeks a town of six hundred souls, swollen now by however many of Àth leathan's own people had come up the ridge path with him in the spring and stayed on rather than go further, could hold water and grain and courage all together before one or another of the three ran short first; she had learned, in the two months since the battle, that Tom Varley answered a plain question honestly and a question dressed as despair not at all, and she had need of him answering plainly rather more than she had need of comfort. She put the kettle on instead, because it was the one thing in the house that still worked exactly as it always had, siege or no siege, and set it to boil over a fire built, that evening and every evening after for weeks to come, out of the last of Cnoc thais's poor black coal.

## II. Of the Pit-heads Taken One by One

The dressing-station had never properly closed since the spring, Sarah's own kitchen having done service for the wounded of Àth leathan before it ever needed to do service for a siege, and it filled again within the first week, though more slowly than it had after the battle, a man brought in with a shoulder opened by a stray shot from the northern picket, a girl who had put her foot wrong on the hill path carrying water up from the one spring inside the ring the regiments had left them, an old woman whose heart, already failing before any of it began, gave out entirely on the third night of the siege in a way that had nothing to do with soldiers at all and everything, Sarah thought privately, to do with the sound of the bell.

The bell of Muileann àrsaidh's own small chapel had rung for weddings and for burials the whole of Sarah's life and her mother's before her, a single note struck by hand from a rope in the porch, since the town had never run to more than the one bell and had never, until that spring, needed to ring it for anything but the ordinary business of being born and married and dying. It was Meikle, of all people, the same careful man who kept the town's writing and now kept its counting of coal and potatoes both, who proposed that the bell be rung once for every pit-head the regiments took, on the reasoning, offered to the town gathered in the pit-shed on the fourth evening of the siege, that a town under siege wanted some way of knowing plainly, all at once, what had been lost that day, rather than hearing it piecemeal from whoever happened to have been nearest the fighting and was willing to talk of it.

The pit-heads went one at a time, through the last week of an Cèitean and on into an t-Ògmhios, not all at once and not, so far as anyone in the town could afterward make out a pattern to it, in any order that served the regiments' convenience over the town's, but simply as each fell in its turn to whatever pressure was brought against it that day: the northern head first, nearest the unfinished stretch of wall, taken on the sixth day after a fight that lasted the better part of an afternoon and cost the town two men Sarah had known since they were boys; then, a week later, the eastern head, given up rather than fought for, on Tom Varley's own counsel, because the ground about it could not be held without stripping men from the wall that mattered more; then a long stretch of near a fortnight in which nothing at all was taken and the town began, cautiously, to let itself believe the regiments had decided the remaining ground was not worth the cost of it, a hope Sarah did not share aloud though she felt the pull of it same as anyone, because she had watched, from her own doorway, the number of tents on the ridge above the southern road grow rather than shrink through that fortnight, and knew what a growing camp generally meant.

The bell rang twice on a single evening in the third week of an t-Ògmhios, for the southern head and the smaller working above it both, lost within an hour of each other in a fight Sarah heard more than saw, being at that hour elbow-deep in a wound at her own table and unable to leave it; and it was that evening, with the second stroke of the bell still going out over the roofs and the light failing fast the way it does in that month even at its longest, that she understood, setting a dressing in place with hands that did not shake though she had expected by then that they might, that the town's own reckoning of the siege, which had begun the first week with plain sums of coal and potatoes, had somewhere in the weeks between become instead a reckoning of pit-heads, the way a body counts down from a full hand of fingers, and that there were, by her own count kept without meaning to keep it, three left standing of the six the wall had once been built to hold.

Tom Varley came to her kitchen most evenings a fight had happened, some to be tended himself, though only twice was the tending more than a scratch, and the rest of the evenings simply because there was nowhere else in the town a man wanted to sit out the day's end but somewhere with a fire and another living person in the room. He rarely spoke of Ruth Calder that summer, having by his own telling asked every question there was to ask of every farm within reach months before, and had every answer there was to have, none of them an answer worth the name; he spoke of her once, late in an t-Ògmhios with the bell silent for three days running and the whole town holding a stillness that felt, Sarah thought, heavier in its way than the ringing had been, saying only that he found himself glad, in a way he could not properly account for even to his own self, that whatever had become of her, she was not inside this particular ring counting down pit-heads along with the rest of them, since a person could only be asked to carry so much ground in her own chest before something in her gave the way a shaft-timber gives, all at once and with little warning beforehand. Sarah said nothing to that beyond setting another cup of what passed, by then, for tea in front of him, judging rightly that he had not said it wanting an answer.

The stores held better than the fear had, through those first weeks, Meikle's counting proving out as careful as his reputation, and the town settled, as towns under siege will, into something that was not quite ordinary life and was not quite anything else either: a rota for the water, a rota for the walls where walls still stood, the chapel's one service a week kept as it had always been kept though the congregation that came to it grew thinner by attrition to the walls and to the dressing-station both, and Sarah's own kitchen, feeding fewer mouths now on smaller portions than it had fed at Ruth Calder's meeting five years before in what felt, to everyone who remembered it, like another age of the world entirely.

Children still played in the one street those first weeks, in the way children will play under almost any roof so long as somebody keeps the worst of the fear from their own faces, a game of counting pit-heads themselves that Sarah overheard one afternoon from her own doorway and had to go inside for a moment rather than let the children see what it did to her, four small voices chanting the names of the six pit-heads in a rhyme none of them could afterward say who had made up, with a hand slapped down at each name the way a hand slaps down at a lost point in any other game, and no understanding at all, mercifully, of what the game's own ending would mean once the last name in it had been slapped down for the last time. Sarah never told Meikle of it, nor Weir either, judging that a thing children had made of their own fear to carry it was not a thing grown people ought to take from them before the fear itself was done.

## III. Of the Sunday Kept and the Grave Behind the Chapel

The one thing the siege did not ration, Sarah came to think through those weeks, was Sunday, and she was glad of it more than she ever said aloud, because a town that had given up counting the days of the week by anything but the bell's tolling for a pit-head needed, she judged, some other reckoning left over that had nothing to do with what had been lost. The priest of Muileann àrsaidh's own small congregation, an elderly man named Weir who had buried Sarah's husband under the fall of stone and married a good half the town besides, kept his one weekly service through the whole of the siege without missing a Sunday, though the benches held fewer each week as men were called instead to the walls and the dressing-station both, and though the bread on the little table by the door, kept there since before Sarah could remember for whoever in the parish had none of their own, stood empty for the first time in her recollection by the fourth week, there being nobody left in Muileann àrsaidh with bread enough spare to leave any on a table.

Weir preached short that summer, shorter each week if anything, on the plain reasoning, which he gave to Sarah once over the graveside of the old woman whose heart had failed the third night, that a congregation standing a siege wanted its comfort given briefly and its own strength left for the rest of the day's work, and that a long sermon on a short summer's morning was, in his judgement, a kind of vanity no man of his trade could properly afford that year. He read the same psalm three Sundays running in the second month of it, the one about a strong tower and a very present help, without any comment on the reading at all beyond the reading itself, and Sarah, sitting in her usual place near the back with her hands raw from the dressing-station's work, found that she did not need the comment, the psalm having said, plainly enough for anyone in that room to hear it, exactly the thing the room had come needing to hear.

The graves went in behind the chapel through those weeks at a rate the town had not seen since the fever year Sarah's own grandmother used to speak of, though never so fast that the ground itself grew short, the western hills having buried their dead in that same plot since before the Severance and having, by Weir's own dry reckoning, ground enough behind the chapel wall to serve three towns the size of Muileann àrsaidh twice over before it wanted extending. The old woman went in first, in the second week, and after her the two men lost at the northern head, and after them a scattering through the rest of the summer that Sarah, standing at the graveside for most of them because there was seldom anyone left in a household to stand there otherwise, found herself keeping a private count of without ever meaning to, the way she kept the pit-heads' count without meaning to either, until the two counts together sat behind her breastbone most evenings like a second and a third heartbeat she could not still by any effort of her own.

Meikle's rationing held through it all with a steadiness Sarah came, by midsummer, to depend on the way a person depends on weather holding fair through a hay-making: a set weight of meal to a household by the week, a set weight of coal to a hearth by the day, a smaller portion again once the second month of the siege began and the town's own store of the year's potatoes, laid by against an ordinary winter and not against a siege at all, began visibly to shrink in the pit where it was kept. He came to Sarah's own kitchen twice a week with his figures written small on a scrap of paper, not because her table needed watching over more than any other in the town but because he had, she came to understand, nowhere else he wanted to sit with the figures once they were done for the day, and she fed him from her own reduced portion without remark either way, since a man who kept a whole town's counting straight through eleven weeks of siege had, in her private reckoning, earned rather more than his own strict share of whatever there was left to give.

## IV. Of the Third and the Fifth

Word of what moved beyond the ring came into the town the way most news came into a besieged place, in pieces, carried by whoever had last spoken to whoever had last spoken to a man who had actually seen the thing himself, so that Sarah learned of the third regiment and the fifth both from no report she could have set any store by, only from a picket's own boast, shouted across the gap where the northern head had been to two of the town's own men on watch there, that the town might as well open its gates now as later, since Cathair mhòr had sent a whole regiment north under the Mac Ùisdein's own cross to see the business finished, and Seann Warr another besides, and that between the two of them and what already sat on the southern road, there would shortly be more soldiers ringed about Muileann àrsaidh than the town had ever had people in it at any one time in its history.

It was Angus Rennie, of all people, standing his watch at the gap he had once dug and never finished walling, who brought that boast back down into the town and set it before the pit-shed that evening as plainly as he had once set his mattock down at the same spot, saying only that a boast shouted across a gap by a bored picket on a slow evening was no thing to build despair on; still, he had counted the smoke of new fires on the southern ridge for three mornings running and there were more of them than there had been the week before, which was a thing a man's own eyes could tell him and no picket's boasting was needed to confirm. Sarah, sitting near the back of the shed with a mending she had brought to keep her hands from idleness, thought that this was Angus through and through, to distrust the shouted word and trust the smoke, and thought also, though she said nothing of it aloud, that a regiment raised at Cathair mhòr under a house's own cross and another raised on a lake shore two days' ride away, both sent north in the same fortnight for no other business she could imagine but this one, told her something the boast alone would not have: that whatever Muileann àrsaidh's few hundred souls had thought they were worth to the kingdom that besieged them, the kingdom, spending two regiments' raising and marching on the answer, plainly thought them worth rather more.

What came of the third and the fifth, once they came, Sarah never afterward learned in any order she trusted, the pit-shed's evening talk through the last weeks of the siege being less a council by then than a place where rumour was aired and mostly, though not always, found wanting: that the new men had been set to strengthen the southern road rather than force any new ground, which Tom Varley judged likely, from what little he could see of their dispositions on his own watches; that a company of them had been sent instead down toward the coast to see to some trouble with the fishing boats that had nothing to do with Muileann àrsaidh at all, which Meikle judged less likely, though he could not say why beyond a general distrust of any rumour that made the town's own trouble smaller than it felt; and that Somhairle mac Lachlainn himself, whose name the town knew by then the way a town under siege learns the name of whoever commands the ring around it, without ever having laid eyes on him, had come up from the western Seann Skell to see the reinforced lines himself, which nobody could confirm and everybody, in the way of these things, half believed regardless of the confirming.

It was in that same stretch of weeks, from a man taken briefly at the wall and let go again the next morning under some understanding between the two sides that Sarah never fully grasped the shape of, that word reached the town of the capital's own streets going dark again, the poorer ones at least, the dubhan that had once lit them being taken now, as coal once had been taken before it, for the army's own use first and the ordinary lamp-houses after, if anything was left over to give them; and Sarah, hearing this third or fourth hand in her own kitchen from a man still shaking with the two nights he had spent not knowing whether he would be let go or kept, found in it a small, cold satisfaction she was not proud of and did not try hard to be rid of, that the fuel the kingdom had taken up in place of coal, and had sent an army north to make sure the western hills took it up too, could not after all light every street it claimed to serve once that same army wanted feeding first.

## V. Of the Door Nobody Was Left to Hold

By the second week of an t-Iuchar the bell had rung for five of the six pit-heads, and the sixth, the one nearest the town's own street and nearest, therefore, to Sarah's own kitchen door, stood behind a line of men and women so thinned by the weeks before it that Tom Varley, walking it on what would prove to be his last full round of the walls before the end, told her plainly that evening, sitting at her table with a cup he did not drink from, that he did not think the line would hold a second real push, and that he had told the same to Meikle and to Angus Rennie both, and that neither of them had disagreed with him, which from either man was as near to a confession of the same fear as either was ever likely to give.

The push, when it came, came in the grey hour before a full morning, the same hour the whole siege had begun in eleven weeks before, and it came not with any great weight of shot or shouting, so far as Sarah afterward could piece it together from the little she saw with her own eyes and the rather more she was told by others in the days just after, but with a quietness that frightened her more, in the moment, than noise would have done, the line at the last pit-head simply giving way in the dark the way a rope gives that has been fraying for weeks and finally parts on no particular pull heavier than any that came before it. She woke to Tom Varley's own voice at her door, not loud, saying only her name and then that it was over, that the last of the pit-heads was taken and there was no line left anywhere in the town for anyone to hold, and that she should open her door before someone with less patience than the men who had let a prisoner go free a fortnight before came and opened it for her. He did not stay to say more than that. He had kin still at Cnoc thais, he told her once, months back, over the same table, and a shaft of his own there he meant to see fired by his own hand and no other's before any regiment came near it, and Sarah understood, watching him go back out into the dark toward the hill path rather than toward the street where the soldiers would shortly be, that he meant to keep that word if the hills would let him, and did not try to keep him from it.

She dressed and went down and found the street already full of a grey, tired quiet that was not quite surrender and not quite anything a person could properly name, townsfolk standing in their own doorways or sitting on their own steps because there was, in the end, nowhere left to go and nothing left to hold, and soldiers coming up the one street in no great hurry, as men come who have already won a thing and know it and see no cause to make more noise about the winning than the winning required. Sarah stood in her own doorway with her hair, as on the first morning, not properly pinned, and watched them come, and found, when the first of them reached her, a young man with a face no older than her own son's would have been had her son lived past his second winter, that she had nothing in her to meet him with beyond the plain fact of the open door behind her, opened by her own hand before he could think to ask, there being no one left in the town, herself included, with the strength or the reason left to keep it shut.

He asked her, in Dia-thìris first and then, when she did not at once answer, in the humans' own tongue, whether there were arms in the house, and she told him there were none beyond a kitchen knife she had used that morning to cut bread, and stood aside to let him look, which he did, briefly and without unkindness, before going on to the next door, and the next, the whole street being worked through in that same brief, businesslike way by men who had, she understood watching them, done this same work in other towns before this one and would likely do it again in others after, and found in that thought, standing in her own doorway with the morning coming up grey and ordinary over a town that had held for eleven weeks and now, in the space of a single dark hour, had nothing left to hold with, neither comfort nor its opposite, only a kind of tiredness too complete to hold either.

What the open record gives of that day, Sìleas nic Rath's own hand setting it down some months later at Muileann chaol from whatever report crossed her desk, is the day, and nothing more: that Muileann àrsaidh fell on that date, after a siege of some weeks, and was found, on the regiments' own taking of it, to hold nothing further worth a report. Of the bell, and of Angus Rennie's unfinished wall, and of Meikle's careful counting of coal and potatoes both, and of a kitchen table that had been a dressing-station through a whole spring and half a summer, the open record says nothing at all, having no way of knowing it, and Sarah Dunmore, telling it afterward to whoever in the hills still cared to ask her, never troubled herself greatly over the open record's silence, having long since learned, in eleven weeks of counting pit-heads by the striking of a single chapel bell, that a thing could be entirely true and entirely unwritten both at once, and that the second did not, in the end, make the first any less so.
