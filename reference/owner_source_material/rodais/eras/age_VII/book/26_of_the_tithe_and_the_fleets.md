# Of the Tithe and the Fleets

*DE 21*

## I. Of the Clerk beside the Pump

Ùna nic Ailein had kept Cùirt na Fèille at Muileann òg for four-and-twenty years, and in that time she had written into its books every kind of sale a market town on Rathad na Fèille might see: cattle, cloth, coal by the cartload and then by the sack, dubhan by the barrel and then by the tank, a woman's dower, a dead man's debts. She had not, until the second week after Àth leathan, written a tax.

The order came down from the capital with the same rider who brought word of the battle, so that the town had the two together before it had properly finished with either: the field lost and won in the same breath as the clause that would pay for the losing and the winning both. It was short, as such orders went, and plain in the way orders are plain when the thing behind them is not: for the war's sake, and for so long only as the war should last, every sale of dubhan within the kingdom was to bear a tithe of one part in twelve, taken at the point of sale and not after, and a clerk of An t-Ionmhas was to sit at every depot and every fair-court pump where dubhan changed hands, to see it taken. The word the order used, twice, was *for a time*.

Muileann òg's own pump stood in the yard behind the toll-house, where the fair's dubhan tank had been sunk the year the town gave up its last coal cart, and it was there, on a wet morning three days after the order reached her, that Ùna met the clerk An t-Ionmhas had sent to sit beside it.

He was a young man named Cailean Ros, thin in the wrists, with a ledger of his own bound in An t-Ionmhas's grey boards and a stool he had brought with him because nobody had thought to leave one for him. He set the stool down in the lee of the tank, where the drip from the eaves did not quite reach, and opened his ledger on his knee, and looked up at Ùna with the particular unhappiness of a man sent a long way from home to do a thing he had not chosen and could not undo.

"I'm told to sit here," he said, "and mark what's drawn."

"You'll want the tank's own book as well as your own, then," Ùna said, "or your figures and mine will never agree, and one of us will be made to answer for it before the year's out."

He had not thought of that. She saw him not think of it, in the way his face did nothing at all for a moment before it did something. He wrote her name down, and the tank's, and Cùirt na Fèille's, in his grey book, under a heading that said only *Muileann òg*, and asked her, civilly enough, whether she would show him how the tank's own draw was measured, since nobody in the capital had thought to tell him that either.

She showed him. It took the better part of the morning, in the rain, the two of them bent over the tank's brass cock and the marked rod that measured what came out of it, and by the end of it Cailean Ros had learned to read the rod as well as she could, and had written, in a hand still stiff with being taught something new, the first entry of the tithe ever taken at Muileann òg: eleven gallons drawn to Seumas Buidhe the smith, at the market price, and one part in twelve of the price set aside before the coin changed hands, and Seumas Buidhe standing by with his hands in his apron looking at the young clerk as though a second smith had set up shop in the yard without asking anyone's leave.

"Where does it go," Seumas asked, when the counting was done, "the twelfth part?"

"To An t-Ionmhas," said Cailean Ros, "for the war."

"And when the war's done?"

The clerk looked at his own ledger as if the answer might be written in it, and it was not. "I couldn't say," he said, which Ùna judged, watching him, to be the truest thing he had said all morning, and also the thing that would serve him worst in the weeks to come, because a smith who has just paid a twelfth of his coin to a stranger from the capital wants an answer, even a false one, more than he wants an honest silence.

Seumas Buidhe took his eleven gallons and went home without another word, and Ùna wrote the sale into Cùirt na Fèille's own book below the line where the tithe was noted, in the plain hand she used for every sale, because a smith's dubhan was a smith's dubhan whatever An t-Ionmhas took off the top of it, and the court's book did not change its manners for a war.

She wrote, too, in the margin, the thing she had not been asked to write and wrote anyway, as she had once written that a coal cart was the last of its kind: *Cailean Ros of An t-Ionmhas sat first at this pump on the thirtieth of an Giblean, and the first tithe taken here was one gallon in twelve of Seumas Buidhe's eleven.* It was no more than Cùirt na Fèille's habit of a hundred years, to set down what was new the day it was new, before the newness of it wore smooth into simply the way of things. She did not think, writing it, that anyone but the court's own clerks would ever read it. Eighteen years before she had thought the same of a margin note about a coal cart, and had been wrong about that too.

Cailean Ros came back the next market day, and the one after, with his stool and his grey ledger, and by the third week he had stopped setting the stool in the lee of the eaves and had asked the toll-house keeper, through Ùna, whether a corner of the upper room might be spared him against the worst of the weather, since a wet ledger served An t-Ionmhas no better than a dry one served the king's enemies. The toll-house keeper, who was Ùna's own second cousin and disliked strangers on principle, gave him the corner nearest the door, which was the coldest in the room, and Cailean Ros took it without complaint, and Ùna, watching him take it, thought that a man who does not complain of the corner he is given generally means to stay in it a good while.

## II. Of the First Complaint

The complaint came in the fourth week, on a market day with the first real heat of the year in it, and it came, as most complaints at Muileann òg came, from a woman who had waited a fortnight to be sure she was not merely in a temper before she brought it to the court.

Her name was Barabal nic Rath, and she kept three looms in a shed behind her house and sold cloth at the fair every second week, and she had bought dubhan for her looms' small engine from the fair pump since the engine was fitted, four years before, because a loom that ran on dubhan wove faster than a loom worked by foot and hand, and a woman with three looms and no sons old enough to work them had needed the faster weaving or given up two of the three. She came up the toll-house stair with her ledger under her arm, a thin cloth-bound book she kept herself in a hand less fair than Ùna's own but every bit as careful, and she set it down on the court's table and opened it to a page marked with a scrap of blue thread.

"I've kept my own count," she said, "these four weeks, against the pump's, because I didn't trust my own arithmetic to hold a grudge fair, and now I've kept it long enough to be sure the grudge is honest."

Ùna looked at the page. Barabal nic Rath's count agreed with the tank's own book, gallon for gallon and tithe for tithe, to the last measure; there was no cheating in it, no clerk's thumb on the scale. What the page showed, set down in the woman's careful hand week by week, was the twelfth part alone, added up: what her looms had paid An t-Ionmhas since an Giblean, against what she had earned by them in the same four weeks, and the second figure was not large enough, set beside the first, to leave her easy about the six weeks still to come, or the six after that, if the order's *for a time* meant what she was beginning to suspect it meant.

"I'm not asking the court to strike the tithe," she said. "I know the court's no more power over a Treasury order than I have myself. I'm asking that it be written down, here, in this book, that a woman of Muileann òg brought a complaint against it in the fourth week of its taking, and what the complaint was, so that when the *time* it was laid for is over, and somebody asks whether the time was long or short, there'll be a paper that says when the asking began."

Ùna took up her pen. Cailean Ros, in his corner, had stopped writing in his own ledger and was watching the table with the stillness of a man who knows a thing is being made that will outlast him in that room whichever way the war goes.

"I'll write it," Ùna said, "as you've said it, no more and no less, unless you'd have me soften it."

"I'd have you write it as I said it."

She wrote it as Barabal nic Rath had said it, in Cùirt na Fèille's book, under the day's date, the first complaint the court had recorded against Deachamh a' Chogaidh: the weaver's name, the looms, the four weeks' figures set down from the woman's own ledger and checked against the tank's, and the plain sentence the woman had asked for, that the complaint stood against the tithe's silence on its own ending, and left its justice, which she did not judge, alone. Ùna turned the book round, as she had once turned it for a carter who could not read, and Barabal nic Rath read her own words back in the court's fairer hand and nodded once and went down the stair to her cloth.

Cailean Ros came over from his corner once the woman was gone and looked at the entry a long while without asking to.

"Will you send a copy to An t-Ionmhas?" he said at last.

"I'll send a copy where the court's book always sends copies, which is nowhere, unless it's asked for. It's the court's book. It answers to the fair, not to the capital." She closed it and set it on its shelf. "If your masters want to know whether the tithe is loved, they can ride down and read it for themselves, or they can ask you, since you sit beside the pump five days in the six and would know better than any letter could tell them."

"They haven't asked me," Cailean Ros said, "what I think of it."

"No," Ùna said. "They wouldn't."

He went back to his corner, and Ùna, setting the day's other business in order, thought that a young man sent a long way from home to sit beside a pump and mark what was drawn from it was learning, faster than she had expected, the one lesson Cùirt na Fèille's own books had taught every clerk who ever kept them: that a thing written down plainly outlives the mood that made someone want it written, and outlives, sometimes, the very order that called the writer into being. She did not know, setting the ink-horn back under its cloth against the evening damp, how many other pumps in the kingdom that week had a Cailean Ros beside them, or how many other women had kept a ledger of their own four weeks running against a grudge they meant to be sure of before they brought it up any stair. There were a good many, she supposed. What was before her she wrote, as the court had always done, and the rest she left to whoever, someday, might read every such book at once.

## III. Of the Boats Come Home Late

Joe Ashby heard of the tithe before he felt it, because a fitter who buys no dubhan to sell, only to burn in his own forge and pour into other men's tanks at a price he set himself, was a step removed from any pump. What struck Caol mhòr's harbour first that spring, and struck it hard, were the ships.

A' chiad rèisimeid, raised at Inis mhòr from the capital's own companies to stand about the council and the mint and the bridge over the Abhainn dhomhain, meant nothing to a harbour four days' sail to the north and was three weeks old before word of it reached Will Tanner's shed at all, brought by a carter who had it third-hand from a corn-factor at Cathair mhòr. The fleets meant a great deal more, and meant it sooner. Within the month a fleet was raised from the south-west harbours and stationed at Àth gharbh, its hulls lying at Doire shean a short walk along the shore, and sent round the western cape; and hard on it a fourth fleet out of Cuan shean joined the first off the Tuathaich coast, and between the two of them the sea-reach of the western Seann Skell and the waters off Cnoc bhàn in the north-west were closed to every hull that was not a fishing boat, and the fishing boats themselves were searched.

Caol mhòr's own boats were fishing boats, in the main, whatever engine Joe had fitted under their thwarts, and it was the searching, not the closing, that came first to the harbour wall.

The first of the king's boats to put in was a low grey hull out of An ceathramh cabhlach, with a lieutenant aboard her young enough that his coat still looked new on him, and she came alongside the wall on a morning with the herring boats half unloaded and their crews still at the baskets. The lieutenant came ashore with two men behind him and a paper in his hand, and read from it, in Dia-thìris, that every hull of the harbour was to be boarded and its hold and its bilges searched, by the order of the fleet, for coal or dubhan carried contrary to the closing of the western waters, and that any master who refused the search would have his boat's papers taken and his name sent to the maor.

Nobody refused. It was not, Joe thought, watching from the shed door with a wrench still in his hand, that anyone at the wall had anything to hide; it was that a harbour which had spent seven years learning to answer a fleet clerk's letters with silence had not yet learned how a young lieutenant with two men and a paper was to be answered at all, and silence, this time, would not do, since the man was standing on the wall asking, plainly, to be let aboard.

They boarded Jean Fisher's boat first, because it lay nearest the steps, and the two men went down into her bilges with a lantern and came up black to the elbow and found nothing but bilge-water and a winter's rust, and the lieutenant wrote something in his own book and moved on to the next. It went that way down the whole of the wall through the morning: down into the bilges, up black to the elbow, a line in the book, the next boat. Will Tanner's own boat took the longest, because Will had, in an idle winter three years before, built a false locker under the stern thwart to keep a jar of the smith's own spirit out of his wife's reach, and the men found the locker and opened it and found the jar and nothing else, and the lieutenant, to his credit, laughed once, briefly, and wrote nothing about it at all.

They came to the engine-boats last, the half-dozen hulls in the harbour that Joe himself had fitted with a dubhan engine under the stern rather than oars alone, and here the lieutenant's manner grew careful, gentle still but slower, the way a man's manner slows when he has been told, before he ever left the fleet's own harbour, that this was the true business of the day and the bilges only the excuse for it. He asked, of each engine-boat's master in turn, who had fitted the engine, and when, and whether the fitter held any guild's leave to do it, and each master gave him the same answer in the same flat voice, that the engine was fitted by Joe Ashby of this town, guild or no guild, and the engine ran true, which was more than any Seann Skell guild's own work could always promise, and the lieutenant wrote that down too, without comment, in the same book as the bilges.

He came to Joe himself at the shed door before he left the wall.

"You're the fitter," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I am."

"You hold no guild's paper."

"There's none in this harbour would give me one."

The lieutenant looked at him a moment, and at the shed behind him, the bench with its rows of tools and the half-stripped engine laid out on sacking that Joe had been working over when the boat came alongside. "The fleet's own hulls run on engines fitted by men who do hold the guild's paper," he said, "and half of them, by what I've seen this year, run worse than the ones I've just come off. I'll write what I've seen, and no more, and what's done with it after that is the fleet's business and not mine." He touched his cap, in a way that was neither quite courtesy nor quite its opposite, and went back down the steps to his boat, and by noon the grey hull was gone round the point, north, toward whatever other harbour's wall was next on his own book's list.

## IV. Of the Hull Nobody Would Name

The letter came to Will Tanner's shed a fortnight after, carried up from Caol mhòr's own harbour depot by a boy who would not say who had sent him, only that he was to wait for an answer and bring it back the same day.

It was short, and it named no fleet and no rank, only that a hull now lying at the depot's own jetty had an engine that would not answer past half its speed, that the depot's own men had looked at it twice and could not say why, and that whoever could put it right, guild or no guild, would be paid in coin at the harbour's own rate and asked no question the work did not answer of its own accord. A mark stood where the signature should have been, no name under it anywhere.

Joe read it twice, standing in the shed door with the boy waiting on one foot and then the other, and then he asked the boy plainly whose hull it was, since a letter that would not say so plainly meant to be read carefully by whoever it was sent to.

"The depot's," the boy said. "One of the fleet's."

Joe folded the letter and put it in his apron pocket and stood a while without answering, and the boy, who had been told only to wait, waited. It took him no time at all to know his own mind; he knew it inside the first reading, the way a man knows before he has finished a sentence whether he means to say yes or no to the end of it. The while he stood there went instead into weighing what the deciding would cost against what the refusing would cost, setting the one sum beside the other until he was satisfied neither would grow smaller for further looking.

A fleet hull was a fleet hull, whatever coin it paid in, and the fleet that hull served was the same fleet that had closed the western Seann Skell's waters to keep dubhan from going where the western hills wanted it, and had raised a tithe on every gallon a Muileann òg weaver drew for her looms, and would, before the year was out, though Joe did not yet know the shape of what was coming, carry arms and men to towns whose names he had heard all his life without ever once thinking of them as places a Caol mhòr man's own hands might help to reach. He thought of Baile chrom's report, pinned outside the custom-house fourteen years before, that had named a town and not a man; he thought that a fleet which could not be bothered to name the fitters of a lighthouse was not likely to trouble over-much with the conscience of the fitter it now wanted for an engine none of its own men could mend.

He thought, too, and did not let himself think it away, that Ned Fowler's boy was down with a chest complaint that winter's damp had settled into him and had not yet loosed, and that the harbour's own rate for an engine's mending, paid straight and without argument, was the kind of coin a man in Joe's own trade did not often see offered without three months' haggling first; and that a boat's engine, once he had it in pieces on his own bench, would tell him nothing at all about where that boat had been or where it was bound next, being made, as every engine he had ever fitted was made, of iron and copper and a burner's throat, and answering to nobody's flag once it was stripped down to its own parts on a sheet of sacking.

"Tell them," he said at last, "that I'll come and look at her. I'll say what I can do once I've seen her, and not before."

The boy went, and came back within the hour with word that Joe was to come to the depot's jetty in the morning, and that the depot's own men would clear the shed for him and ask no questions of anyone he brought with him, if he wanted a second pair of hands.

He took Ned Fowler, whose own hands were as good as any at the pumps and better than most at a stripped engine, and the two of them walked down to the depot in the grey of a morning with the tide half out and the gulls loud over the mud. The hull lay at the jetty's outer end, low and grey like the lieutenant's own boat had been, with the fleet's mark painted small on her bow where a merchant hull would have carried her name, and no name of her own anywhere on her that either man could find, fore or aft, though every fishing boat in the harbour behind them wore its own painted plain for any child to read.

"No name," Ned said, looking her over from the jetty.

"No," said Joe. "Nor any it'll answer to, once she's running again, that either of us will ever hear."

They went aboard, and down into the engine well, and by noon Joe had the fault in his hand, a valve seated wrong from the yard that built her, the kind of small blind error that a rushed builder leaves behind in a hundred engines and a careful fitter finds in one, and he sat over it the rest of the short afternoon with Ned beside him passing tools, the two of them saying little, because there was little that wanted saying about a valve's seat that the valve itself did not say plainer, and because both men knew, without either putting it into words on the jetty that day, exactly whose engine they were mending and to what further use, unnamed and unasked-after, the mending would be put.

By evening the valve was seated true and the engine answered past half speed and on to its full, and the depot's own men, who had stood back the whole day watching two Tuathaich fitters do in an afternoon what their own hands had failed twice to do in a week, paid Joe at the harbour's own rate, in coin, and asked him nothing at all, as the letter had promised, and Joe took the coin and put half of it, that same evening, into Ned Fowler's own hand for the boy's chest, and said nothing about where it had come from beyond that it had been fairly earned, which was true, and which he told himself, walking home along the harbour wall in the last of the light with the grey hull already standing out past the point on the evening tide, was the whole of the answer he owed anyone.

## V. Of Two Books That Would Not Meet

Will Tanner asked him, over the bench a few evenings after, whether he meant to do it again, if the depot's boy came back with another letter and another mark instead of a name, and Joe said he supposed he would, if a chest wanted clearing or a boy wanted mending or the coin was fair, and that he did not see how a man in his trade was to run a harbour's worth of accounts on a war he had had no hand in starting and no vote in ending. Will said nothing to that, because there was nothing in it he could rightly argue with, and the two of them went on stripping a winch that had nothing to do with any fleet at all.

No paper anywhere on the island set the two of that spring's small transactions side by side: Cùirt na Fèille's book at Muileann òg, where a weaver's complaint against a twelfth part in twelve stood written under the day's date for anyone who cared to climb the stair and ask for it, and the depot's own tally at Caol mhòr, unsigned, which recorded only that an engine had been mended and a sum paid out, and named, in its own hand, no fitter at all. Ùna's book meant to be read; that was the whole of Cùirt na Fèille's hundred-year habit, to set a thing down so that someone, someday, asking after it, would find an answer waiting rather than a silence. The depot's tally meant the opposite, and meant it just as carefully, a mark standing in for a name the way the sea-reach off the western Seann Skell now stood closed to every hull that could not prove itself a fishing boat, which was its own kind of writing, done in absences instead of ink.

Between the two books, that spring, ran the whole distance of the tithe and the fleets both: an order that named itself only for a time, taken at every pump the kingdom had thought to guard, and answered, in the fourth week, by a woman with a ledger of her own; and a fleet that closed a coast to keep dubhan from the hills that had first made it, and came, before the season was out, to need the very hands it would not put a name to. Neither book troubled over the other, and no one that spring stood in any office of the kingdom with both open on the same table, to ask what the war had cost, taken all together, in coin drawn at one end of the island and coin paid out, unnamed, at the other.
