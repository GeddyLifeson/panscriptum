# Of Ruth Calder's Letter

*DE 19*

## I. Of the Wall and the Trench

The frost came early to Muileann àrsaidh that year, hard enough by the third week of an t-Samhain to ring under a boot on the spoil-heaps before the sun was properly up, and Ruth Calder, who lodged at Sarah Dunmore's that winter to see the walling done, was out among the pit-heads in it most mornings before the men were, walking the line as her father had walked the line at Àth leathan, though he had walked it to see that the timbering held and she walked it now to see that the wall did.

It was not much of a wall to look at, and she knew it. A cartload of spoil tipped and levelled, a length of pit-timber laid along the top of it and pegged, a gap left for the cart-track and nothing more, built a load at a time through the back end of the autumn by men who came to it after their shift underground and worked it by lamplight when the days ran short, so that it had grown the way a coral grows, she thought, without anyone in particular deciding on its shape, and stood now waist-high the length of the pit-yard and higher than a man's head where it crossed the old cart-road into the town. It would not have kept out a company of the Board's enforcers with proper tools and half a morning to spend on it. It kept out nothing but a lorry, and a lorry was, in the end, the only thing that had come up that road in eighteen months that anyone in Muileann àrsaidh had cause to keep out.

Old Fraser had the digging of the trench across the southern road, being the best hand in the town with a spade and the only one old enough to remember, from some tale of his own grandfather's, that a road dug across at the right place could be made near as good as a wall for a fraction of the labour. He stood in it to his knees that morning, breath smoking, and called up to her as she came along the bank.

"Another two days and she's done, captain. Deep enough a cart won't chance it and narrow enough a man on foot needn't get his boots wet crossing, which I make out to be about right, since it's carts we're stopping and not men."

"You'll want it filled with water before the frost sets it solid, or it's a bridge by am Faoilleach and not a ditch at all."

"I'll want a great many things I'm not like to get." He leaned on the spade a moment, looking up at her with the particular squint he kept for anything he thought worth saying plainly. "You know what this is, the pair of us standing here talking about drainage. It's not a ditch for keeping the wet out of a cellar."

"I know what it is."

"I only say it because there's men down there" — he tipped his head toward the pit-shaft behind them, where the morning cage was just then coming up with its first load of the poor Cnoc thais coal that Muileann àrsaidh had taken to burning itself since the Board stopped carrying the good — "who'd tell you it's only a fence, the way a man tells himself a dog's only barking at the wind. It isn't only a fence, Ruth. You want to have your mind made up what it is before somebody else makes it up for you."

She had thought, more than once through that autumn, that old Fraser said more true things to her in a morning's digging than the whole of the letters that came up from the south said in a twelvemonth, and this was one of them. She let it sit a moment before answering, since any answer she could find that morning ran either smaller than the trench deserved or larger than she was ready to speak aloud.

"It's a wall and a ditch," she said at last, "same as it looks. What anybody south of it chooses to call it is their own business."

old Fraser went back to his digging without pressing her further, being a man who knew the difference between a question worth a second asking and one that was not, and Ruth went on along the bank to the pit-head, where the day's shift was already gathering at the cage with their lamps, and took out her father's tally-book, which she had carried over the hill with her and had kept, in one hand or another, every morning for going on four years now, and began the day's count of coal raised, coal burned, coal owed, the same three columns she had kept since the week they buried him, in a hand that had grown, she thought sometimes without much pleasure in the noticing, very nearly as neat as his.

The word from the south that autumn came thin and came late, by whatever carrier still troubled to climb the hill road once the weather turned, and most of what it carried was not news so much as the absence of it: no answer from the Board to the collective letter the five towns had sent in the summer, unsigned by any one name and carried hand to hand, that said only that the pits would go on until the seams themselves were spent; no word of the enforcers at the western Seann Skell beyond the plain fact of their being there still, quartered in the old customs shed above the river harbour, building something by the river that the carrier's talk called a depot and could not say more of than that; no sign, from Caol mhòr or from Cathair dhearg either, that the winter now closing in on the hills was expected, by anyone south of them, to end in anything but more of the same silence it had been given all that year.

It was into this silence, and not out of any clearer counsel, that Ruth began, in the last week of an t-Samhain, with the trench half dug and the wall not yet finished, to write.

## II. Of the First Drafts

She did not tell anyone at first what she meant to do, partly because she had not yet decided to do it and partly because she had learned, in four years of keeping a pit captain's books, that a thing said aloud too soon in Muileann àrsaidh came back to you by evening in a shape you no longer recognised, with three opinions attached that you had not asked for and could not now easily set down.

The first attempt was made at her father's table, in the stone house above the pit-head at Àth leathan, on a Sunday home, on a night when the wind was coming hard enough off the hill to want the shutters latched twice, with the tally-book put away and a single sheet of the good paper in front of her that she kept for the collective letters and had, until that night, always used for someone else's words and never her own. She wrote the salutation first, *To the Council of Rìoghachd Dia-thìr*, and looked at it a long while, because it seemed to her a great thing to have written at the top of a page in a pit captain's kitchen, and then she wrote nothing else that night at all, and went to bed with the sheet still lying there, the ink dry and the rest of it blank, and told herself in the morning that a letter begun and not finished was no different from a letter never begun, which was not, she found, much comfort.

The second attempt, a week later, she tore across before it was a third done, because she had written three long paragraphs on the history of the agreement at Muileann àrsaidh and the wrong done the towns by Breith Leòid, and reading it back she heard, under every sentence, her own voice raised the way it rose at the pit-shed meetings when Tom Varley or one of the Cnoc thais men said a thing she thought unjust, and she knew, without anyone having to tell her, that a letter written in that voice would be read by whoever read it at an Leabharlann as one more northern grievance among the hundred they must have on file already, and set beside the rest, and answered, if it was answered at all, the same way.

"You're writing it like a complaint," Sarah Dunmore said, the one evening Ruth brought the torn half-sheets to her kitchen and laid them out on the table where the towns' agreement had first been drafted three years before, in much the same seats, with much the same tea gone cold between them. "And they've a great many complaints. What they haven't got, I'd wager, is an offer. Nobody in four years has offered them anything. Everybody's told them what's owed, or what's been done wrong, or what they mean to keep doing whatever's said against it. Nobody's put a thing on the table and said, here, take this, and we'll both be the better for it."

"There's not much on our table to offer. We've coal and nothing else."

"Coal's what they want. It's the only thing anybody in this quarrel has ever wanted from us, whatever else they've said about seams and rulings and An Crùn's own right. Offer them the coal, on your own terms, and let them see whether they'd rather have it that way than not at all."

Ruth turned the idea over through the whole of that week, at the tally-book in the mornings and at the table above the pit-head in the evenings, and came, slowly, to see that Sarah Dunmore had put her finger on the one thing the four years of letters, hers and the Board's and the council's between them, had none of them yet tried. Every letter she had read or heard read aloud at the pit-shed meetings had been an argument about a right: whose the seams were, whose the fifth was, who had broken faith with whom first, and each argument had answered the last with a sharper one, until the whole exchange had the shape less of a negotiation than of two men shouting the same three sentences louder each time, in the hope that volume alone might eventually pass for proof. She did not think she could win that argument. The towns' case was sound, she still believed that; the trouble was that nobody who had the power to answer it was troubling any longer to listen to it as an argument at all.

An offer was a different thing. An offer did not ask to be believed. It only asked to be weighed.

## III. Of the Terms

She wrote the third draft in a single sitting, on the last evening of an t-Samhain, with the trench filled and frozen behind the pit-yard and the wall finished but for a length at its northern end that old Fraser had left off out of what he called simple exhaustion and what everyone privately understood to be the same caution he had shown her that morning in the ditch, a wall built all the way round a town being, in his private judgement, a thing too near a boast to finish.

This one did not begin with the history. It began with the thing itself.

*We will pay An Crùn's fifth in coal, the same fifth the Board's own ruling names, on every load raised from the seams at Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh, Tobar dhearg and Àth leathan, from this day until the seams themselves are spent, if the Board will carry that coal to market as it carried it before, and make no further claim upon the pits while the fifth is paid.*

She read that sentence back four times before she let herself write the next, because it was, she understood even as she set it down, a very great deal to offer and a very little to ask in return, and she wanted to be certain, before she went further, that this was truly what she meant and not merely what desperation had put into her pen. The fifth had gone unpaid for four years, by the Board's own reckoning, and Breith Leòid made a great deal turn on that unpaid fifth; to offer it now, in coal and not in coin, backdated to nothing and forward-looking only, was to give the Board the one thing its own ruling said it wanted and to ask, in exchange, only that the pits be let alone to work until they were worked out, which was, on the towns' own reckoning, a matter of years and not of generations, the seams at Muileann àrsaidh being already thin in places and Cnoc thais's reopened shafts thinner still.

It was, she thought, setting the pen down a moment to warm her hand over the fire, a bargain the Board ought by any sane reckoning to take, since it lost nothing it had not already lost and gained the fifth back besides. She knew, even as she thought it, that sane reckoning was not the only reckoning a council weighed a matter by, and that a body which had already sent a company of armed men north over a fifth part of a coal seam was not, perhaps, a body that could be counted on to want the quarrel settled quietly and without a victory to show for it. But she wrote the offer anyway, because it was the true offer, the one Sarah Dunmore had asked her to find, and because a letter that asked for what she privately expected to be refused was still, she judged, worth more than no letter at all.

She went on from there more plainly than she had written anything before: that the five towns had never sought to take from the Board what the Board itself had ceased to want, the Board having stopped carrying northern coal to southern markets long years before the towns stopped paying for the privilege of a carriage that no longer came; that the coal raised in the western hills, being poor coal by any southern measure, fetched no price outside the north that would tempt any buyer the Board itself still served; and that the wall now standing round Muileann àrsaidh's pit-yard, which she did not doubt some report had already carried south in a shape larger than the thing itself, was built to keep out lorries and not men, and would come down the day a lorry had no further reason to come up that road.

She did not write the word war, in that draft or in any of the three that followed it over the fortnight after, though she thought it, reading each draft back, more times than she would have owned to any of the pit-shed meetings. The same thin carrier-talk that brought every other scrap of southern news that autumn had brought word that regiments not called out since An Dealachadh were being spoken of again at Cathair dhearg, spoken of and not yet raised, which was, she told herself, still a distance short of raised; and into the space that distance left she wrote her letter, believing, or choosing to believe, that a plain offer honestly meant might yet close it before it closed on its own account.

## IV. Of Christmas Day

The chapel at Àth leathan kept its service on the morning of the twenty-fifth as it had kept it every year Ruth could remember, the small stone building above the ford road filled to its narrow doors with the town's own people and a good number come down from Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais besides, since the western hills had never troubled themselves greatly with the difference between the two faiths the island kept, most households along that coast holding to both without much sense of contradiction, a custodian of Òrd Mhanannain and the humans' own priest sometimes standing at the same graveside within a season of each other and neither thinking it strange.

Ruth walked down from Muileann àrsaidh the evening before with Tom Varley and a dozen others of the pit towns, the road frozen hard and easy underfoot for once, the whole company more cheerful on that walk than she had seen any of them in months, singing a little in the last stretch coming down into Àth leathan, where the chapel lamp showed early through the bare trees. She had not meant, setting out, to finish the letter that day. She had meant only to keep the feast, as her father had kept it and his father before him, and to let the letter lie another week in the drawer where it had lain, in one draft or another, since an t-Samhain began.

The chapel was cold, the small dubhan stove at the back doing little against a building built for a warmer age, and Ruth stood through the service between Sarah Dunmore and Tom Varley with her hands deep in her coat, her mind for once on her father, who had stood in that same pew every an Nollaig of her life until the last one, and on the particular quiet that came over a small crowded room when the whole of it, for the length of a carol, wanted nothing from any of the others in it but to be there together, warm or cold.

It was afterward, walking back up from the chapel in the thin winter light with the congregation breaking up into its knots of neighbours and kin, that the thing came clear to her, not as a new thought so much as an old one finally allowed to finish: the crowded chapel, the carrier's road frozen and passable for once, the whole of the five towns keeping one feast together whatever else divided them from the country south of the wall, was the plain thing her letter was asking the Board to leave alone, standing for an hour in front of her on the one day of the whole turning year best fitted to send it.

She did not go up to her own cold house that afternoon. She went instead to the kitchen of Tom Varley's sister, who had married at Àth leathan and kept a room for her brother when the roads were bad, and took out the last draft, and read it through once more by the window in the fading light, and found, this time, nothing in it she wished to soften or to harden either.

The fair copy took her under an hour, set down in the same hand she kept for the towns' ledgers, plain and even, with no flourish at the start or the close; a letter asking to be weighed and not believed, she had decided somewhere in the reading, ought to look like a ledger and not like a plea. She dated it at the top, the twenty-fifth of an Dùbhlachd, and signed it with her own name and the names of the pit captains of the other four towns beneath it, Tom Varley's among them. None of the four had seen the final draft, Sarah Dunmore alone excepted; their names stood there because she had asked each of them, weeks before, whether she might sign for them if the letter, when it came, said nothing they would not themselves have said, and none of them had refused her.

She sealed it with the same wax she used for the tally-book returns, there being no other in the house, and sat a while at the window with it finished in her lap before she carried it down to find Tom Varley.

## V. Of the Post at the Western Seann Skell

He was in his sister's kitchen when she found him, half asleep by the fire with a cup of something warm going cold at his elbow, and he came fully awake the moment she set the letter on the table in front of him, because he had known, better than most of the others, what she had been carrying in that drawer all through an t-Samhain, and had asked her twice already, without pressing, whether it was done.

"That's it, then."

"That's it."

He turned it over once in his hands, reading the address on the outside without opening it, since it was not his to open, and set it down again with a care she noted and was grateful for. "You want it to the post at the western Seann Skell, I expect, and not the Muileann àrsaidh carrier, since he's not been up the hill in a fortnight for the drifts."

"The Seann Skell post. It's the only office this side of Cathair dhearg that still keeps regular hours, whatever else sits beside it in that town now."

He did not ask her what she meant by that, both of them knowing well enough what else the western Seann Skell now held, the depot by the river and the company quartered in the old customs shed, and he did not, she noticed, offer to have someone else carry it, though the five miles down from Àth leathan to the town were not, on an evening of an Nollaig with the light already going, a walk either of them would have chosen for its own sake.

"I'll go tonight," he said. "It'll be there before the office shuts if I go now, and I'd sooner it were posted before I've had time to think better of the terms myself, which I haven't, mind, but I'm an old man and old men change their minds in the night for no reason they can afterward name."

"You've not seen the terms."

"I've seen enough of you these two months to trust the terms, Ruth Calder, whatever they say, which is more than I'd have told your father I'd say of anyone twenty years back, him included." He said it without much ceremony, pulling on his coat as he spoke, and she did not answer it, because there was, again, no answer that would not have made more of the moment than either of them wanted made of it on a feast day.

He walked the five miles down to the western Seann Skell in under two hours, by his own account afterward, the road clear and the frost holding, and found the post office lit and open as she had said it would be, kept that evening, as it happened, by a clerk who had stayed past his hour rather than leave a stack of an Nollaig letters to wait another day, and who took Ruth Calder's letter across the counter without any particular notice of it among the dozen others in his hand that evening, weighed it, entered it in his book, and told Tom Varley it would go north with the regular carrier in three days' time, the roads and the holiday allowing.

It went, as promised, three days after, by the ordinary carrier and no other, folded in among the season's other correspondence with nothing about its plain sealed outside to mark it apart from a hundred like it, and it travelled the length of the island by the same slow stages any letter travelled that winter, town to town, hand to hand, until it came, in the second week of am Faoilleach following, to Leabharlann Muileann chaol, where it was read, entered in the day-book in Raghnall mac Mhuirich's own clerk's hand, and laid, as Ellen Marsh's An t-Aon Leabhar Deug had been laid two years before it, upon the table of the council's business, to wait there beside whatever else waited.

A copy of it went, by a slower road still, into Tional nan Tuathach's own minute at Caol mhòr, carried by one of the western towns' own people who thought Tional nan Tuathach ought at least to have the sight of it, whatever Tional nan Tuathach chose to do or not do with the sight once it had it; and there, in Tional nan Tuathach's own hand, it is recorded to this day, four short paragraphs and five names, under a heading that says only what it was and when it came.

No answer to it came from the council, that winter or the next. Ruth did not, in the weeks after, ask whether one might yet. She had learned enough, in four years of keeping her father's book, to know what a silence of that length generally meant, and she went back to the tally-book and the pit-yard and the wall's unfinished northern end with no great expectation stirring in her that the offer she had spent a season making plain would be met with anything plainer in return.

She did not wish it unsent. Setting it down that an Nollaig evening with her father's tally-book closed on the shelf behind her, she knew it for a fair offer honestly meant, the fairest either side of that quarrel had yet put forward; and she knew, just as plainly, folding it into its seal by the last of the daylight, that a generous offer was not, of itself, a thing that had ever yet been enough to answer for.
