# Of the Depot Nobody Would Bless

*DE 19*

## I. Of the Ground by the River

Somhairle mac Lachlainn had been a soldier of paper before he was a soldier of anything else, and it showed in the way he laid out the depot: string and pegs first, then the foundations, then the men. He walked the ground above the river at Seann Skell in the west on a grey morning in am Màrt with Comhairle nam Mèinnean's surveyor's plan under his arm, and where the plan said the wall should run he set a peg, and where it did not he let the whin stand.

The company had been three months in the town by then, quartered in the old customs shed above the river harbour, which Comhairle nam Mèinnean paid for and nobody else wanted, and in three months Somhairle had learned the shape of the place the way he had once learned the shape of a muster roll: the citadel on its rock, empty since before his grandfather's time, its gate rehung with new iron; the river road running north to Muileann àrsaidh and the pit towns, ten miles and more, climbing all the way; the quay where the coal barges had once tied up three deep and now lay two, and those two waiting on cargo that did not come. He had learned, too, which doors opened to him and which did not, though nobody in the town had struck him or thrown a stone at him yet, in am Màrt. That came later, and by a different road than he expected.

The depot was Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own notion, and a plain one: the enforcers' lorries wanted dubhan, and dubhan wanted a tank, a pump, and a roofed place to keep the barrels dry between the boats that brought it up from the south and the wagons that would carry it on to wherever Comhairle nam Mèinnean's order sent it next. Somhairle had built worse things with less. He set the masons on the tank's foundations first, six courses of dressed stone with a puddled clay lining that Comhairle nam Mèinnean's engineer swore by, and while they worked he rode the ten miles to Muileann àrsaidh and back twice a week to see what the pit towns were doing, which for the most part was nothing he could report, since a wall of spoil and timber going up slowly, a cart at a time, over a winter, does not look like defiance until it is finished.

He wrote his first report on the fourteenth of am Màrt, three plain paragraphs: the depot's ground broken, the quay's second berth cleared for the dubhan boats, no incident at Muileann àrsaidh worth Comhairle nam Mèinnean's attention. Every word of it was true, and he sealed it believing so. Some months would pass before he learned that a report could be true in every sentence and still be a kind of lie, a thing a man came to by doing it, not by being told.

The masons finished the tank's stonework by the middle of an Cèitean, and the carpenters raised the roof over it the same month, a long shed of tarred boards not unlike the shipmasters' sheds Somhairle had seen once at the southern Seann Skell, save that this one had no chimney and no smell but pitch and new-cut wood. The lorries themselves had not yet come; two were promised from the capital for midsummer, the first the western Seann Skell had ever seen, and the town's children had already worked out where they would stand to watch them arrive, a fact Somhairle knew because his corporal reported it to him with more relish than he reported most things.

He had come to the west with forty men and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's authority to shut what Breith Leòid had declared shut, and had expected, from what he had read at Cathair mhòr, a fight or the threat of one within the month. What he found was a town that answered him in two tongues and told him nothing in either, pit towns above him on the river road going on hewing coal as though no ruling had been made at all, and a season of building instead of a season of soldiering, which suited him less than he would have said aloud to any of his officers. The depot itself he had not asked for. It came down to him in an order three lines long, much like the note the house of Òrd Mhanannain would send him in return before the season was out, and he built it because it was built, the way a man drills a company because the drilling is ordered, without troubling himself overmuch about what it would be used for once it stood.

The town watched him build it. Seann Skell in the west was not a large place, three thousand and more souls between the quay and the citadel, but it had eyes enough, and Somhairle came in the first weeks to know which of the faces at the market stalls and the bridge-end were merely curious and which were counting: the courses of stone as they rose, the size of the tank, the number of men set to the work each week. He did not think, that spring, that the counting meant anything he needed to answer. It was a plain building. He never understood, afterwards, why it was the building nobody would bless.

## II. Of the House That Would Not Bless

The custom of the town, and of every town on that coast so far as Somhairle had troubled to learn it, was that a new roof got a blessing before it was worked under, the way a new boat got one before it was launched: a house of the old faith sent a man to walk the ground and say the words, and the town's masons would not lay a floor inside walls that had not had it. Piety was too grand a word for it, most of them would have said; it was the same caution that kept a farmer from ploughing a field on the wrong day of the moon. Somhairle had watched it done for the customs shed's new door the winter before, a custodian of Òrd Mhanannain come down from the house on the hill with a bowl of sea-water and a few words nobody troubled to explain to him, and thought no more of it than he thought of any other local weather.

He sent his corporal to the house of Òrd Mhanannain in the last week of an Cèitean, with a civil note asking that a custodian come and bless the depot's roof before the tank was filled, and naming a day that suited the masons. The note came back within the hour, in a hand Somhairle did not know, three lines long: the house of Òrd Mhanannain at Seann Skell did not bless what was built for Comhairle nam Mèinnean's enforcers, and hoped the captain would trouble it no further on the matter.

Somhairle went himself the next morning. It was not pride so much as disbelief; he had asked leave to be refused things before, by councils and by weather, but never in three lines from a house he had never troubled.

The house stood on the rise behind the citadel, a low grey building with a well in its yard and a single window facing the river, unglazed, the shutter folded back. The man who came out to him at the gate was younger than Somhairle had pictured from the hand of the letter, thin, sunburned across the nose the way a man gets who works outdoors and forgets his hat, in the plain grey the order wore.

"I had your note," Somhairle said, "and I came to ask it again myself, in case the corporal put it badly. It's a roof over a tank, nothing more. I'm told the custom is to bless a new roof before the town's men work under it, and my masons are the town's men."

"Your masons are the town's men," the priest said. "What's under the roof is Comhairle nam Mèinnean's, and what will stand beside it before the year's out is yours." He did not raise his voice. "We bless a boat because the sea doesn't ask whose it is before it drowns a man. We bless a roof because rain doesn't ask either. I'll not pretend the same of a depot built to keep your lorries fuelled on the road to Muileann àrsaidh, captain, because you and I both know the rain isn't the thing I'd be blessing it against."

Somhairle had heard evasions dressed as plain speaking often enough to know the difference, and this was not that. He stood a moment with the morning coming up warm off the river behind him and found he had no answer that was not either an argument about theology he had no standing to make, or a threat he had no wish to make.

"Then I'll trouble you no further," he said, and did.

He wrote of it to Àrd-choimheadaiche at the southern Seann Skell that same afternoon. The house of Òrd Mhanannain answered to no one outside itself, being its own master under a different order entirely; he wrote to Alasdair mac Ìomhair anyway, for want of anyone else in the whole of the old faith who might see the thing plainly and not as a quarrel between the enforcers and the north. He set down the refusal in the priest's own words, as near as he could remember them, and asked, since the house on the hill would not come, whether some other house might, so that the masons would work under a blessed roof as any other masons on that coast expected to.

The answer came back inside the fortnight, over Alasdair mac Ìomhair's own hand, and said less than Somhairle had hoped and more than he had expected. The Àrd-choimheadaiche would not command a house of Òrd Mhanannain, he wrote, no more than the priest's own master could command a custodian of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail; but Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail blessed what it was asked to bless, north or south, Board or town, and if the depot's roof wanted the words said over it, a custodian of the southern house would come and say them.

He came in the first week of an t-Iuchar, a young custodian named Pàdraig, sent the two days' road from the south with a letter of introduction and nothing else, no escort, riding a hired horse he did not know how to manage and arriving stiff and saddle-sore an hour after the masons had given up expecting him. He said the words over the depot's roof at evening, with Somhairle and half a dozen of the enforcers standing by and none of the town's own people, though a good many watched from the bridge and further off, at a distance that made plain it was watching and not attending. The words themselves were nearer to what Somhairle had watched said over the customs shed's door than he had expected, the same bowl carried out and the same slow walk round the four corners of the building, and he found himself, standing bare-headed in the last of the evening light with his men behind him, oddly moved by a thing he had asked for chiefly as a matter of custom and had not thought to feel anything about at all.

Pàdraig ate with the officers that night, a quiet young man who asked Somhairle a great many questions about the road north, how far it ran, how the towns along it stood, whether Comhairle nam Mèinnean's writ carried past Muileann àrsaidh or stopped short of it, and answered none of Somhairle's questions in turn about the house of Òrd Mhanannain or why the southern house had sent a man two days' ride when a house on the hill above the depot could have walked down in a quarter of an hour. He rode south again the next morning, stiff still, and thanked Somhairle for the horse's keep in a way that made plain he did not expect to be sent north again on such an errand, and he was not.

The masons worked under the blessed roof without complaint, being, as the priest had said, the town's men and glad of the wage whoever paid it. But it was noticed, and remembered, that the depot had been blessed by a stranger's hand from the south, and not by the house that stood in sight of its own roof, and the town had a name for the building inside the month that was not Comhairle nam Mèinnean's name for it, though Somhairle never learned what it was, his corporal having more sense than to tell him.

## III. Of the Letter Filed

What Somhairle did not see, and could not have, was the letter Martha Greaves wrote from Caol mhòr at the summer's end, though it touched his depot as nearly as anything in that year.

She wrote it at Tional nan Tuathach's table, with Peter Hale reading over her shoulder and putting in a word here and there that she mostly did not take, because the letter was hers to write as speaker and she had turned it over in her mind on the road down from Doire ghlas until she had it by heart before she set pen to paper. It said, in substance, what Tional nan Tuathach's delegates had been saying to one another in the corridor outside the hall for a month: that the five towns of the western hills would not shut their pits for a letter carried by a Board's clerk, whatever Breith Leòid said the seams now were; that Tional nan Tuathach was no party to the quarrel and wished to be none; and that a year's delay, while Tional nan Tuathach itself sent men to treat with the towns, would cost Comhairle nam Mèinnean nothing it had not already lost, and might cost the kingdom a great deal less than the alternative.

She did not write the word the alternative meant, having no wish to be the one who first put it on paper, and Peter Hale, reading the sentence over her shoulder, said nothing about the omission either, though both of them understood it.

The letter went up to the capital by the ordinary post, and was read, Tional nan Tuathach's minute later recorded, at a sitting of the council in the first week of an t-Sultain, at which Somhairle mac Lachlainn was not present and of which he knew nothing beyond what his own masters chose to tell him, which was nothing at all. The council's clerk entered it as received. No answer was sent. It went, with the rest of that quarter's correspondence, into the press where such letters were kept, in a folder with a paper label in a clerk's hand that said only *Tional nan Tuathach, western towns*, and there it lay through the autumn and the winter that followed, read again, so far as the record shows, by no one until long after the matter it asked about had been settled by other means.

Martha Greaves learned, some weeks later, that the letter had been received and filed, and no more than that; the phrase itself, filed, has stood ever since in the north for an answer that is not one.

Peter Hale, who had wanted a harder letter and had said so at Tional nan Tuathach's table before Martha wrote the one she meant to write, told the delegates at the next sitting that a filed letter was better than a refused one, since a refusal closed a door and a filing merely left it shut, which was not the same thing and might yet be opened. Ruth Calder, who had walked out of Tional nan Tuathach five years before and sat now among the western towns' own people rather than among Tional nan Tuathach's, heard him say it, and said nothing back. Tional nan Tuathach's letter went into the record as its own act, the last it would take in that quarrel that asked for anything so plain as a year's grace, and it is remembered in the western hills, where such things are remembered exactly, for what it asked and for the single word that answered it.

## IV. Of the Stones on the Road

The first skirmish that An Clàr Foscailte does not name happened on the Muileann àrsaidh road in the second week of an t-Sultain, and the north tells it thus.

A lorry of the enforcers, one of the two promised in midsummer and only lately come, was sent up from the depot with a load of timber for the post Comhairle nam Mèinnean meant to build on the road below Muileann àrsaidh, the same post whose foundations had not yet been dug. It went up the river road in the early morning with two men in it, a driver and a corporal, and it did not come back by dusk, when it was looked for. It came back after dark instead, one wheel stove and the timber gone, and the corporal with a cut over his eye he would not say how he came by, though the driver, a Seann Skell man with kin in the town, was not so close-mouthed among his own people, and what he told them, in the way of such things, went from the depot's yard to the quay to the whole town inside two days, and from there, by the carriers who worked the river road, to Muileann àrsaidh itself before the month was out, told there a little differently than it was told at the depot, as such things are.

The telling that held in the pit towns says that the lorry came up past the second milestone below the town in the grey of the morning, and that word of it had gone ahead by whatever road word travels faster than a lorry on a bad surface, so that by the time it reached the narrow place where the road runs between the old spoil-heap and the river, there were men on the heap above it, not many, the telling says, and none of them known to any teller who did not already know them before that morning. Most of them had nothing to fire with that year, and none fired. They threw stones, and rolled two loosened boulders down from the heap into the road ahead of the lorry, and stood where they could be seen and counted, though the telling is careful never to give a number, until the driver turned the lorry about in the narrow place, no easy thing, and took it back down the way it had come, the corporal's cut earned, the telling says, by a stone and not by a blow, though it does not press the point.

The enforcers' side of it, as a man of the depot told it afterwards, to another man who by then had his own reasons to ask, runs shorter and says less. He said they had gone up with the timber as ordered, had found the road blocked above the spoil-heap by stones that might have fallen or might not, had turned back rather than clear it themselves with no escort and no orders covering the case, and that somewhere in the turning the corporal had taken a knock he made light of. He did not say he had seen anyone on the heap. Asked directly, years afterwards, by someone who had reason to press him, he said he had not looked up at the heap at all, being occupied with the lorry. The man who asked him did not press further, though he did not look wholly satisfied either.

Neither telling gives a name. An Clàr Foscailte gives none either: it was not written down at the time, being, in Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own later word for such things, not great enough to name, and it was one of a dozen or a score that autumn on that road and others like it, none counted, none put in any report that reached beyond the depot's own book, until they had between them made a thing too large to call by any of their separate names.

There was a second such meeting on the same road before an t-Sultain was out, and a third on the shorter track above Àth leathan that the escorted wagons had begun to use instead, and Somhairle came, over that month, to recognise the shape of the thing before he had words for it: a load sent up, a delay reported, a man come back with a graze or a fright or nothing at all beyond a story he told differently each time he told it. None of it was war. He would have said so himself, that autumn, and believed it. The wall at Muileann àrsaidh grew a little higher through the same weeks, a cartload of spoil and a length of timber at a time, and no one on either side of the shire line called that war either, though it was built for nothing else.

Somhairle heard of it from the corporal himself that same night, the cut still open, the man more shaken by the stove wheel and the lost timber than by the blow, or saying so. He asked him three times whether he had seen who threw the stones, or how many there had been, and got, each time, a slightly different answer. Somhairle judged it no lie: a frightened man in a turning lorry on a bad road in poor light does not see clearly, and does not know afterwards how much of what he remembers he has built since. That judgement went into the report he began that night and did not finish until the following morning.

## V. Of the Report That Said Less

He wrote three drafts.

The first said what the corporal had told him, in the corporal's own uncertain words, three men seen or perhaps five, stones and two boulders, the lorry turned back, the timber lost, no shots fired on either side, no man badly hurt. He read it over and struck out *seen or perhaps five*, because he did not believe the corporal had seen anything of the kind, and wrote instead *a number not established*.

The second, written the next morning with a clearer head, said less again: an obstruction on the Muileann àrsaidh road, the lorry returned undamaged beyond a wheel, the timber not delivered, to be sent up again under escort. He looked at *undamaged* for a long while, the wheel in fact having cost three days at the wheelwright's, and let it stand, because a stove wheel was a fact Comhairle nam Mèinnean's engineer would ask after in a way a captain's judgement of a crowd on a spoil-heap would not be asked after at all, and Somhairle had begun, without quite deciding to, to weigh his sentences by what question each one would draw down on him, and to prefer the ones that would draw down none.

The third was the one he sent. It said that a lorry sent to the Muileann àrsaidh road with timber for the new post had met an obstruction and returned; that the timber would go up again under proper escort; that there had been no engagement. The word stones did not appear in it, nor the corporal's cut, which had by then closed and would leave, the surgeon said, no scar worth the noting. Somhairle read the third draft over twice, found nothing in it that was false, and sealed it. Riding out the next morning to see the escort chosen for the timber's second try, he told himself that a report existed to answer the questions a superior officer needed answered, and that a captain who filled every page with every uncertain thing a frightened corporal half remembered was not doing his superiors a service, whatever he was doing for the truth of the matter.

He believed this for the length of the ride, more or less, and found, dismounting at the depot's gate, that he no longer entirely did, though he could not have said where in the three miles the belief had worn through.

It was some weeks after, at the foot of the year, that Comhairle nam Mèinnean's clerk at Cathair mhòr forwarded to Somhairle, without comment, a copy of a letter that had come up from Seanchaidh na Rìoghachd at Muileann chaol, asking Comhairle nam Mèinnean for its returns of the incidents on the northern roads that autumn, so that the record might be kept as fully as the record was kept of everything else the kingdom did. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh had written it herself, in the same plain hand she had used for every such asking in the four-and-twenty years she had kept the kingdom's chronicle, believing, as she wrote it, that the office she wrote to would answer as every office had always answered a chronicler's plain question, with what it had, no more and no less; that had always been the custom of the kingdom's record, and she had not yet had cause to doubt it held. She set the letter down among the day's other copying and thought no more of it than the fifty other such letters she sent out in an ordinary year.

It was Sìleas nic Rath, reading the returns as they failed to come through the following weeks, who first said aloud in an Leabharlann's upper room that Comhairle nam Mèinnean's silence on this one letter did not feel like the ordinary silence of an office too busy to answer. The old woman at the next desk told her she was young yet, and would learn in time the difference between a thing not answered and a thing refused; it generally took longer to learn than a body would like.

Comhairle nam Mèinnean did not answer it, that year or the next. Somhairle never learned the letter's fate after it left Cathair mhòr, whether it went unread into a press like Martha Greaves's, or was read and answered with a silence someone had chosen rather than a silence that simply happened; he was not, that autumn, high enough in Comhairle nam Mèinnean's counsel to be told which. He knew only that no order came down to him changing anything in the reports he sent, and that he went on writing them as he had begun to, three drafts to one, the first honest and the third sent, and that by the time the first snow of that winter closed the higher road above Muileann àrsaidh, he had stopped noticing that he did it at all, which was, though he did not think of it in these words and would not have thanked anyone who put it to him so plainly, the whole of what the habit was for.

He rode out on the last clear day before the snow to look at the depot's roof, blessed by a stranger's hand and standing sound, and the tank full under it, and the two lorries in their shed, and thought, as near as the matter can be told, of nothing at all worth the writing down. That, too, went into no report.
