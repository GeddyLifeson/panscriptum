# Of the Charter of Export

*DE 27*

## I. Of the Còmhrag and the Two Docks

Joe Ashby had fitted boats at Caol mhòr for eleven years by his own count, and in all that time he had never once gone up to watch the Còmhrag, though the men he fitted for went every year, win or lose a shift's pay on it, and came back the next morning with their knuckles wrapped in whatever cloth their wives could spare. That year the fight went to Ceann leathan as it always went, in the middle weeks of an t-Iuchar with the weather set fair over the whole island, and two regiments and the fighting men of nine shires sent their best into the ring the town keeps for it, with the old rule still standing that no edge is carried past the rope, only fists and the stave. The ring at Ceann leathan is nothing more than a square of trodden ground behind the market cross, roped off once a year and left open to the weather every other week of it, and the men who fought there that year fought under the same sky and before the same crowd of shire-folk and off-duty soldiers that had watched every Còmhrag since edged tools were first banned from it, a crowd that came for the fight and stayed, more years than not, to see nothing worse than a broken hand and a fair winner carried home on his own friends' shoulders.

That year was not one of the fair years. A man of the third regiment took a stave-blow above the ear in the second round and did not get up again, and was carried off the ground still living and was dead before the tide turned. Nobody at Caol mhòr could have named him. The word came down the coast three days later with the fish-carrier, along with the name of the shire that had lost him, and by the time it reached Joe's own dock it had already worn smooth in a dozen mouths, the way such news does, until it was no longer a man's dying but a fact about the year, one line among the others: the Còmhrag was fought, and one man died of it, as had happened before and would happen again.

He thought of it only because the fish-carrier brought, in the same breath, another piece of news that mattered to him rather more, which was that the eleventh of Ellen Marsh's books was finished at last, copied out fair in a schoolroom hand at Doire ghlas and sent north with the same carrier who had carried the first ten. Joe had never read a page of any of the eleven, could not have told a stranger what was in them beyond the little Ellen herself had once said to him on the quay, years back, before he knew her for more than a woman who asked careful questions and wrote down the answers without once correcting a man's grammar to make him sound a fool. He knew only that the books held every claim of loss the northern towns had ever put to paper, going back past his own memory into his father's, and that Ellen had spent eleven years gathering them from kitchen tables and chapel vestries and the backs of account-books nobody else had thought worth keeping, and that when she had finally carried the whole of it down to the Library at Muileann chaol and offered it for the record, the Library had taken her letter and filed it, and had not, so far as anyone at Caol mhòr had heard, taken the books. He had asked Peter Hale once, straight out, whether the Library meant to refuse them outright or only to leave them waiting, and Peter had said that a catalogue that lists a request and not the thing requested has not yet said either word, and that a man could grow old waiting to find out which. The schools of the north kept copying anyway, one hand teaching the next, since a thing worth the writing was worth the writing twice.

The Moot met that year at Caol mhòr as it met every year, in the low stone hall by the fish-quay that had been built, so the story went, in a single wet week by men who agreed on little else and agreed the roof leaked worse than any argument they were like to have under it. Joe went to that, as he went most years, less for what was said than for the plain fact of the minute-books being opened again, the same long shelf of them running back to Walter Hale's first meeting, a run broken by no war and no charter and no regiment's say-so, though every one of those had tried its hand at the Moot in its turn. Peter Hale opened the year's book himself, being new enough to the chair that he still did it with his own hand rather than passing the pen to a clerk, and read out the tally of what stood recorded and what did not: no ruling from any Dia-thìreach court that named the Moot a lawful body, and no request ever made that it should be. Joe had heard the same two lines read every year of his life and had stopped, some years back, expecting them to change. What he watched instead, that year, was Peter's own hand on the page, steady enough, and thought that a man could keep a thing going a long while on steadiness alone, whatever else he lacked.

He noticed, walking home along the quay afterward past the engine-sheds where he no longer had leave to work, that every lamp lit along that stretch of water burned dubhan now, his own trade's old wick-oil gone from every window these six years, and that the two boats drawn up for the winter had dubhan engines fitted where their masters could afford the fitting and oars where they could not. He fitted the oared ones himself, outside any guild's book, for whatever a man could pay him, and had done since the charter rule put his name off the Fuel-Fitters' rolls along with every other Tuathach fitter on the island. It was not a trade he complained of within his own hearing. A man who has been let go once learns to notice, without much bitterness left in the noticing, how completely the thing that let him go has since come to run everything he still has to walk past on his own quay.

An old man who had fitted lamps at Caol mhòr since before Joe was born, and had been let go from the same rolls the same season, caught him at the shed door and asked, as he asked most weeks, whether Joe had heard any more of the eleventh book. Joe told him what the fish-carrier had told him, and the old man stood a while working it over, the way he worked over most news these years, slowly and without much hope of it coming to anything, before he said that a book finished was still a book, whatever the Library chose to do with it, and that his own grandchildren would at least have the whole of it to read when they were grown, catalogued or not. Joe agreed with him, because there was nothing in it to disagree with, and because the old man had earned the right to say such things without being answered too closely.

'They'll list the asking of it,' he said, 'same as they list the asking for amends. A kingdom can keep a request on a shelf a long while and call that keeping.'

'Longer than either of us has left to watch it,' Joe said, and the old man laughed at that, a short dry sound with no bitterness in it that Joe could hear, and went back to the lamp he was mending, which at least, being his own and not the Company's nor the council's, he was still let to mend.

## II. Of the Common Account

By that year there was little left on Dia-thìr that did not run on dubhan, and less that anyone thought worth remarking on in the running. Every lamp lit after dark, every engine that turned a wheel, every ship of the fleets and every lorry on the roads the regiments had built, drew on the same fuel one man had first made from what the pits threw away as worthless. The coin of the kingdom was pegged to it now as it had once been pegged to the vein's own coal, and the Treasury took its tithe on every sale of it as it had once taken a tithe on every hundredweight dug. A clerk at Cathair dhearg closed the year's roll that autumn at nine thousand five hundred and sixty-eight purses, six parts in ten of it from the head-due taken on every five thousand heads across the shires, the rest from the market-due at seventeen in the hundred on every sale the market towns made, with the wartime tithe carried forward on its own line beneath the regiments' pay, as it had been carried forward every year since the war that made it was said to have ended. The windy coast paid neither due, being reckoned still too poor and too far to be worth the collecting, and the clerk's own note said as much in the same plain hand he gave every other figure, without complaint and without pride, a fact set down beside other facts.

It was a common account, and common accounts rarely stop a kingdom in its walking to be looked at twice. The clerk who closed that year's roll had closed a dozen before it, and set down the nine thousand five hundred and sixty-eight purses in the same column, the same hand, the same order of the due before the tithe, as every year he had kept the office; a stranger reading only his ledger could not have told, from the figures alone, that the fuel behind two purses in every three of them had not existed at all within the memory of men still working the scales beside him. At Ros dhomhain, where the barrels of the ordinary trade came down to the quay all that year as they had come down every year since the dubhan works first opened at the southern Seann Skell, the same Treasury clerk who weighed and marked them for the home market had begun, some months before the council ever sat on the larger question, to keep a second tally of his own, of barrels that left no due behind them worth a coin, since the fuel that ran the fleets' own hulls and the regiments' own lorries was reckoned the kingdom's, and paid the kingdom nothing beyond what it had already cost the kingdom to raise the regiments that burned it. He kept the second tally without being asked to, and showed it to nobody, and folded it each quarter into the drawer where a careful man keeps the figures he is not sure anyone above him wants to see.

What stopped the council of the kingdom that year, and held its twelve members three sittings running when most business was disposed of in one, was a single man's petition to carry his own fuel past the island's own shore, a thing no council at Cathair dhearg had granted to any goods of Dia-thìr since the ships of the Severance had first been turned back from the eastern mist.

## III. Of the Three Sittings

Sìleas nic Rath had sat through a great many council sittings in her thirteen years as chronicler, most of them dull enough that she kept her own attention only by the discipline of the pen moving under her hand, but she remembered afterward, better than she remembered most weeks of that whole grey year, the particular quality of the quiet that fell over the red hall the first time Dubhan stood before the twelve to ask leave to sell his own fuel beyond the water.

The red hall had not changed since Sìleas first came into it as a girl trailing Beathag's own ink-stained sleeve, thirty feet of stone the colour its name gave it, twelve chairs set in a half-round below the window that let the morning in over the councillors' own shoulders and left the petitioner facing the light, so that whoever stood to ask the twelve for anything stood also half blind, and every chronicler who had ever kept the room's minute had noticed it and none had ever written it down as anything but the room's own custom. Somhairle mac Lachlainn sat that year among the twelve, the one soldier in the half-round, a seat given him at the peace and kept since, and he said less in any of the three sittings than any other councillor there, though Sìleas marked him watching Dubhan the whole first hour with the particular stillness of a man measuring another man he has not yet decided whether to trust.

Dubhan himself was an old man by then, in his sixty-ninth year, though not so old that the room mistook stillness in him for weakness; he had made, by Sìleas's own count, four fortunes and lost two of them since the pits first began to fail, and every councillor in that hall had burned his fuel in his own house for a dozen years or more, whatever any of them privately thought of the man who had made it. He came that first sitting, in the depth of the winter before, with two men of his own age at his shoulder whom Sìleas had not before had cause to enter in the open chronicle by name, Seachran and Brean, men who had known him, by the look that passed between the three of them, since long before any of the twelve now judging him had been born to their seats. Neither man spoke that day nor at any sitting after. They stood, and they were there, the way a wall is there, and Sìleas set their names down for that alone, since the record is meant to hold who stood in a room as faithfully as who spoke in it.

She copied the petition twice into her own hand that first winter, once for the chronicle and once, at the clerk of council's own asking, for the council's private minute, and found it a plainer document than she had looked for from a man of Dubhan's reputation: no plea of hardship, no claim upon the kingdom's gratitude for the fuel that ran its lamps, only a bare request that he be let carry a measured share of dubhan past the island's shore, on terms the council might set and change at its own pleasure, since he asked a leave and not a right. He read the four sentences of it standing, in a voice pitched low enough that the councillors nearest the window had to lean to catch the last of them, and sat down again before any of the twelve could think of a question to put to him, so that the whole of the first sitting passed with the petition read once and answered not at all. The twelve heard it in silence and did not answer that day. A petition of that weight, the clerk told her afterward, is not a thing twelve men decide before their dinner.

Sìleas walked back to the Library that first evening by the long way, along the wall above the river, and found herself turning the petition over much as she had turned over, two years before, the one line the same council had given to Anna nic Phàil's own asking on the Moot's behalf: allowed to die. Both were requests brought to the same twelve men in the same hall under the same window; one had waited two years for a single line and was waiting still, and the other, she already half suspected before the second sitting ever sat, would not wait so long. She set neither thought into the chronicle, a chronicler's private suspicion being no fact the office was paid to keep, and carried it with her all the same, the way a woman carries a stone in her shoe some while before she troubles to stop and shake it out.

The second sitting came on in the spring, and it was there that Anna nic Phàil rose to put the question Sìleas had privately expected from the first day, though she had not expected it from Anna, who had brought the Moot's own petition for amends before this same council not two years past and had watched it read into the minute in a single line and left to die there without a vote. Anna asked Dubhan, plainly and without heat, whether the fuel he meant to carry abroad was made wholly of what the pits had thrown away, or whether some of it drew, however thinly, on the vein's own coal that the kingdom had sworn never to sell. Dubhan answered that not one basket of the vein's coal had gone into any barrel he had ever sold at home or meant to sell abroad, and that the custodians of Òrd Bhrìde might seal every hold he loaded and open none they had not first searched, and he would thank them for the trouble of it. Sìleas wrote the exchange down whole. It seemed to her, setting down her pen that evening, the nearest the three sittings came to the question the whole kingdom might have asked and mostly had not, and the part of it that stayed with her past that evening was less the fuel's own cleanness than the years the whole kingdom had burned it before any councillor thought, until Anna rose that day, to ask.

The third sitting fell as the summer was going out, and it was over inside the hour. The twelve granted the charter on the terms Sìleas had already copied fair from the clerk's draft two days before it was read: the Treasury to set the measure sold in any year, the loading to be done at Ros dhomhain and nowhere else on the island, a clerk of the Treasury to take his tithe on every barrel there, the kingdom's own depots and the Moot's to be served before any barrel left the shore, and the custodians of Òrd Bhrìde to set their seal on every hold before it sailed. Dubhan bowed to the twelve, once, and thanked them in fewer words than his own petition had used, and Seachran and Brean stood at his shoulder as they had stood at the first sitting and said nothing at the last. Sìleas, gathering her own papers, saw the three of them pause a moment at the hall's own door, and heard, not meaning to hear it, Brean say something too low for her to catch, and saw Dubhan laugh at it, a short surprised sound she would not have looked for from a man three sittings deep in his own petition, before the door took the three of them out into the street together. It was the one unguarded thing she saw him do in the whole year of watching him, and she did not write it into any minute, since a laugh shared between three old friends at a door was not, whatever else it might have been, a fact the council's business required her to keep.

The street outside ran down toward the bridge as it always ran, and half the lamps burning along it that evening already carried the very fuel the council had just that hour agreed the man might sell beyond the water, a thing Sìleas noted only because she happened to be looking for something else to note, folding the minute into her satchel with her mind still half in the hall behind her. She wrote the whole of the sitting into the open chronicle in her plainest hand that same night, adding neither praise of the council's care nor doubt of its wisdom, since thirteen years at the desk had taught her that the day for either judgment, if it ever came, was not a chronicler's day to make it. She added only the terms, in full, as the clerk had given them to her: the Treasury's measure, the loading at Ros dhomhain alone, the tithe on every barrel, the kingdom's own depots served first, and the custodians of Òrd Bhrìde to seal every hold before it left the shore, since nothing of the vein might go out under any charter the council had ever granted or was ever like to grant.

## IV. Of the Ship Going North-About

The tank-hull went out from Ros dhomhain in an Dàmhair, loaded over three days at the quay under the eyes of a Treasury clerk who marked the first barrel and the last alike and every fifth barrel between, and under the eyes, too, of two custodians of Òrd Bhrìde sent down from Seann Skell for the purpose, who opened every hold before it was filled and searched it by lamp for any trace of the vein's own coal before they would let a single barrel of Dubhan's own fuel go down into it. Finding none, they set their seal in wax at the coaming of each hold in turn, pressing the order's own mark into it while it was still soft, and stood by until it had hardened enough that no hand could open the hold again without breaking what they had set. She carried the name An Eala Dhubh painted fresh on her bow, a name Dubhan himself had chosen and given no reason for. She ran north-about, as every ship bound for the water beyond the mist had run since the Crossing first found the one passage there was to find, out past the north cape and down along the coast toward the north-western capes, where the sea opens and the island's own charts give out.

Joe Ashby was on the water that morning too, out beyond Caol mhòr's own headland in the boat he had fitted with his own hands from a hull three other men had given up on, tending a line that had caught nothing worth the tending, when An Eala Dhubh came up past him under full steam, low in the water with her load, a hull no bigger than a dozen he had fitted in his time but riding, that morning, like something the whole island had built together and no part of it owned. He watched her the better part of an hour, until she was no more than a mark of smoke against the northern sky, and thought, watching her go, of the six years since his own name had come off the Fuel-Fitters' rolls under a charter written for a different fuel than the one that ship now carried past the edge of everything he had ever fitted a boat to reach. A man's mind will run to such things on a slow morning with an empty line, and his ran on to a wick he had once turned in a lamp not so different from the ones now burning the length of that ship's own wake, the fuel changed and the charter over it changed twice, and his own hands, which had done the turning either way, let go from both. Last of all he thought of the eleventh book finished at Doire ghlas and the one line in the Library's catalogue that named the asking of it and not the thing, and found, watching the smoke thin against the sky, that he could not have said with any confidence which of the two, the book uncounted or the fuel now going out under seal and charter, the kingdom would remember longer.

At the southern Seann Skell that same morning, where the works Dubhan had first raised now stood twice the size they had been in Joe's own boyhood, the priests of Manannan came down to the quay as the tide turned and said, over the water, the same prayer that had been said at every sailing out of that harbour since ships first went north-about from it: that the god of the sea keep it shut behind whatever went out on it, and send back to the shore whatever the sea was willing to send, and forgive the island whatever debt a sailing left unpaid on either side of the mist. They had said it over grain-ships and over the fleets' own hulls and, in years further back than any priest now living could name from his own memory, over the ships of the Severance that had never sailed home at all; they said it that morning over a hull of dubhan bound past the mist under one man's narrow charter, in the same words, in the same order, asking the same god the same question he had never yet, in all the island's telling, troubled himself to answer. An old woman of the harbour stood at the edge of the crowd with a child on her hip and said the last line of the prayer along with the priests, under her own breath, the way a person says a thing learned so young that it no longer feels like an asking of anyone in particular; no one near her remarked on it, the day being one on which the whole quay had its own reasons to want the sea kept shut.

Joe brought his own boat in empty that evening, the line having given him nothing all day worth the carrying home, and told his wife only that he had seen the ship go out, and that she had gone north-about as they all went, and said nothing more of it, since there was, when he came to set the day in his own mind that night before sleep, nothing more in it that words would carry any better than the sight had already carried it, out past the headland, into the smoke, and gone.

So the year closed on that account: a man dead of the Còmhrag whose name went unrecorded at Caol mhòr, an eleventh book finished and a catalogue still silent on it, a Moot that met and was not asked to answer for meeting, a Treasury roll of nine thousand five hundred and sixty-eight purses drawn from a fuel no council had thought to tax before one man made it, and, over three sittings that had held the twelve of Cathair dhearg longer than any petition in Sìleas nic Rath's own thirteen years at the desk, a charter granted to the same man to carry that fuel past the water his own kingdom had kept shut since the Severance. The north's old rhyme asked whose the fire was, and the island that year answered the question in every particular but the one the rhyme was asking, setting down who might load it and where and under what seal and at what tithe, and never once who among its makers had gone first without a wick to turn or a rolls-book to keep his own name on.
