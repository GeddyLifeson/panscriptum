# Of Blàr Àth leathan

*11 an Giblean, DE 21*

## I. Of the Eve at Àth leathan

The snow had gone off the hills in the last week of am Màrt, all at once, the way it always went in that country, and for ten days after, every burn between Àth leathan and the western Seann Skell ran the colour of strong tea and loud enough at night to be heard from the pit-yard gate. Ruth Calder had walked down to the ford below the town on the evening of the tenth to look at the water for herself, because a woman from Muileann àrsaidh had sworn it was falling and a man from Tobar dhearg had sworn just as hard that it was not, and she had found it running high still, brown and quick over the stones, the old crossing-plank half under. She had stood a while with her boots on the last dry stone and watched it, and thought that whichever army came at the town from the south would have to come by the road and not across the fields, and had gone back up to tell the pit captains so, since it was the one piece of news that whole month she was glad to carry.

Five years she had kept her father's place by then, near enough, though she did not count the time that way; she counted it from the letter that had gone unanswered, four months now since Tom Varley had carried it the five miles to the post at the western Seann Skell on Christmas evening and given it over the counter before it closed, and no word had come back from Muileann chaol in all that time, not a refusal, not a line to say it had been read. The watching for a rider from the Library had stopped some time in am Faoilleach, about the week the first companies from the southern shires were seen on the western road, and she had understood then, without anyone troubling to say it to her plainly, that the letter had had its answer after all, only not in writing.

Àth leathan itself had never been much of a town to fight over, and she knew it better than any soul now gathering in it, having been born within sight of the pit-head and carried to its own small chapel within the week for her christening, in the plain way of a town that had never in her lifetime held more than a few hundred souls between the pit rows and the farms on the slope above them. Her father had kept the pit thirty years before her, and his father the coal seam under it before that was ever properly opened, and she had grown up knowing the shape of every gallery below the town by ear before she knew it by sight, the particular note the props gave when the roof above them was sound and the different, flatter note they gave when it was not. It was strange to her, walking the yard that afternoon with the companies coming in from three directions at once, to think that the same ground she had learned as a child for what lay under it might that week be measured instead for what could be held across the top of it, and stranger still that she should be the one asked to do the measuring.

The five towns' companies came into Àth leathan through that afternoon and the evening after, by the hill paths and not the road, since the road had regiments camped along the better part of five miles of it by then and nobody cared to be counted crossing it. Muileann àrsaidh's people came first, near ninety of them under old Robb Sinclair, who had taken the pit captain's place there when the last one broke his hip on the January ice and never so much as offered to be spared it; then Cnoc thais's, better than two hundred, with Tom Varley walking at their head the whole ten miles with a stick he did not lean on once that anyone saw; then, well after dark, the last of Àth naomh's hewers, tired and short of food, having come the longer way round by the hill above Muileann leathan to keep clear of a patrol. Tobar dhearg sent what it had, some thirty of its forty hewers, the rest kept back at the one pit that town still worked, and their captain, a broad quiet man named Fearchar MacRath, came in near midnight leading a pony with two barrels of salt fish roped to it, which he said was the whole of what the town could spare and was sorry it was not more.

Before ever the counting began there was the feeding of them, since none of the five towns' companies had come far without hunger travelling with them, and it was Ruth's own kitchen, her father's kitchen before her, that fed the first of them that evening, a great pot of oatmeal and salt beef kept going on the fire from midday until well past dark, ladled out by two of the neighbour women into whatever each company had brought to carry it in, tin cups and bowls and, for more than one of Àth naomh's tired hewers, cupped hands held out and filled without complaint from either side. Robb Sinclair ate standing at the yard gate with his own bowl, watching his people settle, and said to Ruth, when she came out to see how the feeding went, that he had buried a wife and two sons in his sixty years at Muileann àrsaidh and had thought, until that week, that there was no ground left in him a single winter could still find to frighten him, and had learned this one that he had been wrong in that, the same as in most things a man thinks he knows for certain at sixty.

Ruth kept the count on a slate in the pit office, rubbing out and writing again as each company came in, and by the time Fearchar MacRath set his slate figure against hers near one in the morning the two of them made it out between them at a little under nine hundred souls, counting the carriers, the farm carts sent down from the hill steadings with what oats and salt beef could be spared, and the boys too young to be hewers who had come anyway to lead the ponies and carry water. She wrote the number twice, once for herself and once, on a torn half-sheet, for Tom Varley, who folded it without reading it and put it inside his coat, saying only that a number written down was a number that could be checked against afterward, which was as near as he came, that night, to saying what everyone in the pit office understood without his saying it.

The order of the companies she set herself, at the long table with the others standing round her in the lamplight, Àth leathan's own hewers nearest the pit-heads since it was their own ground and their own homes at their backs, Muileann àrsaidh's beside them under Robb Sinclair, Cnoc thais's along the road itself where Tom Varley wanted them, and Àth naomh's and Tobar dhearg's higher on the slope above the burn, where the ground was steeper and the going, if it came to going, would be slower for anyone following them up it. She had never commanded so much as a fair-day crowd before that winter, and she set it out now the way she had once watched her father set the shifts at the pit-head, by what needed doing first and who was fittest to do it, and found, setting it down, that the shape of it came to her more easily than she had feared it would, which frightened her rather more than not knowing would have.

A messenger had come down from the western Seann Skell that same evening, a carter who ran goods along the road for whoever paid him and troubled himself with neither side's quarrel beyond what it cost him in delay, and he told the pit office, over a cup poured for his trouble, that the regiments camped along the road had struck their tents that afternoon and stood to arms an hour before dark, though they had not moved before he left. It was the first plain word any of the five towns had had in ten weeks of watching that the waiting was over, and the office went quiet a moment at it in the way a room goes quiet at news it has been braced for so long that the bracing itself has begun to feel, wrongly, like a kind of safety. Robb Sinclair asked the carter, since he traded up and down that road as freely as any man on the island that spring, what he made himself of the numbers he had seen camped there, and the carter said only that he did not count soldiers for a living and did not care to begin now, and took his cup and his fee and went back out into the dark the way he had come, owing neither side an opinion and offering none.

It was gone two in the morning before the last of the talk in the pit office broke up and the captains went out to see their own people settled, in the pit-sheds and the byres and, for Àth leathan's own, in their own beds for what might be the last time in some while. Tom Varley stayed after the others, standing at the window with his hands behind him, looking out at nothing Ruth could see, since the night outside was black as the inside of a boot and the lamp behind them threw the glass back at him instead.

"You should sleep an hour," he said, without turning. "There's nothing more to be set right tonight that a slate and a clear head won't set right better in the morning."

"I will," she said, and did not move to.

He turned then and looked at her properly, the way he had not troubled to all the long evening of counting and setting companies, and she saw in his face something she had not let herself look for in her own these last months, which was plain fear, worn openly and without shame by a man old enough, she supposed, to have earned the right to it.

"My father used to say," she said, "that a pit captain's whole trade was knowing which face of the seam would hold and which would come down on you, and that a man who could not tell the difference had no business calling himself a captain at all." She looked past him at the black window. "I have been asking myself the whole of this week which face this is."

"And what answer do you give yourself?"

"None that I'd trust a slate to," she said. "I wrote the offer of the fifth myself, Tom. Every word of it. I thought it fair when I wrote it and I think it fair yet, and no one has ever told me it was not, only that it went unanswered, which is a different thing, and I have spent four months now not knowing which of the two I ought to be angrier at." She looked at him. "If it comes to tomorrow, and I am not there to say it again, will you say it was fair? Only that. Say the offer was a fair one, whatever anyone chooses to do about it after, and let the rest of it be."

"I'll say it," Tom Varley said, "and I'll say it was yours, since it was, word for word, and no committee's."

She let that stand a moment, the two of them alone with the lamp between them, and then she said the thing she had turned over in her mind the whole evening and had not, until that moment, been certain she would say aloud at all.

"If it goes hard tomorrow, I'd sooner you took the towns' voice after me than any other man living. Not for kinship, since there's none between us, but because you've never once told me a thing was settled when it was not, the whole four years I've known you, and that's worth more to five towns than any blood I could name."

Tom Varley did not answer that at once. He picked up his stick from where it leaned against the table, turned it once in his hand as if to be certain of its weight, and set it down again.

"You'll say it yourself," he said, "since you've a stubbornness in you that has carried five towns' business through worse mornings than this one promises to be, and I've seen nothing in you these four years to make me think it's used up." He went to the door, and stopped with his hand on the latch. "But I heard you, Ruth Calder, and I'll not forget it, whatever the morning brings."

He went out into the dark, and she heard his stick on the yard stones for a while, and then heard it stop, and understood he had gone to sit with Cnoc thais's people by their fire rather than to any bed. She sat on a while longer at the table, the slate figures still chalked on it, nine hundred and some odd souls set down in a hand she barely recognised as steady as it looked, and then she put out the lamp and went out herself into the last of the night, to walk the line of the companies once before whatever the light would bring.

## II. Of the Grey Hour

The sky over the hills east of Àth leathan was the colour of wet slate when she came back to the pit-head gate, at an hour too dim yet to call morning, light enough all the same that the shapes of men lying wrapped in the pit-sheds had stopped being shapes and become, one by one, men she knew by their faces: a boy of fourteen from her own row asleep against a coal tub with his father's coat over him though his father sat awake beside him with his back to the wall; two sisters from Àth naomh sharing a single blanket between them, their hewers' picks laid across their knees the way another woman might have laid a shawl; old Angus Rennie, who had dug the trench across the southern road two winters before and never once, in all that time, been asked to say what he had dug it for, sitting apart from the rest with a hand-axe across his knees, turning it over and over without, so far as she could tell, looking at it at all.

She did not wake any of them. There would be time enough for waking, and she had learned enough of her father's trade to know that the last hour of quiet before a shift, however hard the shift to come, was not an hour to be spent early. She walked instead along the whole line the companies made, from Àth leathan's own at the pit-heads down past Muileann àrsaidh's, along the road where Cnoc thais lay, and up the steeper ground above the burn where Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg had made their fires, and at every fire she found awake she stopped long enough to be seen and to say a word, though she could afterward have told no one what the words had been, only that she had said them and had been answered in kind, in the ordinary way people answer each other before a shift, whatever the shift.

At Cnoc thais's fire along the road she found Tom Varley's people already up and rolling their blankets, and one of them, a girl no older than sixteen who had come down from the reopened shafts with her own father's pick since her father himself had died of the lung the autumn before, asked Ruth outright whether it was true the kingdom's men would give quarter to any who threw down their tools and walked out onto the road with their hands shown empty. Ruth had heard the question asked in one shape or another at every fire she had stopped at that hour, and she gave the girl the same answer she had given the others, which was that she did not know, and that no one in the five towns' companies could rightly know, since none of them had asked the kingdom's men the question directly and the kingdom's men had not troubled to answer it unasked; and she added, because the girl's face asked for more than the plain truth alone would give her, that whatever quarter was or was not given, a tool laid down in fear was still a tool that could be taken up again another day, which was as near to comfort as she had in her that grey hour to offer.

Higher on the slope, among Àth naomh's people, she found two sisters sharing the one blanket between them their picks laid across their knees, and stopped only long enough to be known and to be answered in the same few words everyone answered her in that morning, before going on to where Fearchar MacRath's Tobar dhearg hewers had made their own small fire apart from the rest, forty of them short by ten who had stayed to keep the one pit working, singing under their breath, when she came up to them, a working song she had heard hewers sing at shift's end her whole life and had never once, until that morning, heard sung instead at a shift's beginning.

The burn was still running high when she came down to it last, brown in the growing light, loud over the stones, and she stood a moment at the crossing-plank, half sunk still, and thought again, as she had thought ten days before, that whoever came at the town would have to come by the road. She could see, from where she stood, the first grey of the road itself where it came up out of the south between the hills, empty yet, no column on it that the light would show her, though she knew, as every soul in the five towns' companies knew without needing to be told, that it would not stay empty past the hour the sun properly cleared the eastern ridge.

Fearchar MacRath found her there and stood beside her a while without speaking, the two barrels of salt fish long since unroped and given out, his pony tethered somewhere behind them cropping the wet grass as if the morning held nothing in it worth its notice. He was not a man given to much talk, by what she had seen of him the evening before, and he did not trouble her with any now, only stood, and looked where she looked, at the empty road and the brown water between.

"Forty hewers is not much of a town," he said at last, "to be here for."

"It's what Tobar dhearg had to give," she said, "and it's here."

"Aye," he said. "It's here." He looked at the water a moment longer. "My grandmother used to say the burn ran high in the spring because it remembered the snow and grieved it going. I never believed her, mind, only I think of it every year the water rises, whether I believe it or no."

Ruth said nothing to that, only watched the water a while longer with him, brown and loud and indifferent to whatever either of them believed of it, and then Robb Sinclair's voice came up from the road below, not loud, only carrying in the particular way a voice carries in the wet grey hour before a thing begins, saying that there was dust now on the southern road where no dust had any business rising on ground still soft from the melt, and that it was time, if it was to be time at all, for every captain to be at their own company and not standing at a burn's edge watching water.

She went. She did not run, though she felt the want of running the whole short distance back along the line, and she came to Àth leathan's own company at the pit-head gate with the light properly up now, grey and flat and without any warmth in it, and found her father's old place at the head of them already kept for her, a space left in the line as if the ground itself had known where she belonged in it. She took it, and looked once along the whole shape of the companies as she had set them the night before, Àth leathan at the pit-heads, Muileann àrsaidh beside them, Cnoc thais on the road, Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg on the steeper ground above the burn, nine hundred souls and their carriers strung along a mile of wet hillside in the grey light of a morning that had not, until that hour, seemed to her to be any particular morning at all.

Somewhere down the southern road, too far off yet to be more than a darker line along the pale ground, the dust that Robb Sinclair had seen was thickening into a column, and the column, as she watched it, did not stop coming.

## III. Of the Field as the North Tells It

So the old people of the western hills tell it yet, those few who were children then and heard it first from their own fathers and mothers at the fire, and it is told the same in Cnoc thais as in Àth naomh as on the windy coast where some of that day's company ended by settling, which is as near to one telling as the north keeps of anything from that war.

They tell that the kingdom's host came up the southern road in the full light of morning, fourteen hundred men or near it by every count the north ever made of them, with their carts and lorries running on the fuel one of their own maker's countrymen had made, and that the western companies watched them come the whole slow mile from the place where the dust first showed to the place where the road narrowed between the last two fields before the pit-heads, and that no one on the hillside moved before they had to. They tell that the burn was still running high and loud enough, at the height of what came after, that men on one bank could not always be sure what they heard from the other, and that more than one company took a shout for an order and an order for a shout, in the confusion the water made of every sound that morning.

They tell that Ruth Calder was seen at the pit-head gate with Àth leathan's own hewers about her the whole first hour, her voice carrying along the line in the ordinary way a captain's voice carries, steady enough that men further off took heart from the mere sound of it without troubling always to make out the words; and that when the kingdom's companies came at last to the narrow ground between the fields, where the road ran close under the pit-heads and gave no room for a line to spread, it was Àth leathan's own people who held them there the longest, since they knew the ground the way no company sent from the south could ever have learned it in a single winter's camp.

Of the kingdom's own coming up the road, the tellings agree on this much, that it came in three long files with the dubhan lorries between them, and that the lorries' engines could be heard, those first minutes, over even the burn's noise, a low steady sound the western companies had none of them heard so near before, since no engine of any kind had ever yet been brought up that road in peace; and that it was that sound, more than the sight of the column itself, that first told the whole mile of hillside just how many men were coming, since a column can be misjudged by eye at that distance but an engine's note, once heard, does not shrink with the distance the way a marching man's shape can seem to.

They tell it went on the better part of the morning and into the early afternoon, the western companies giving ground slowly along the steeper hillside above the burn, first Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg, whose ground it was to give first by Ruth's own setting of the line the night before, and then, as the morning wore, Muileann àrsaidh's people falling back from beside the pit-heads toward the hill paths they had come down by two days before, until by early afternoon it was Cnoc thais's company, and Tom Varley at the head of it, that held the road itself the longest of any, giving the others what time the ground and the water allowed them to draw off toward the hills.

They tell, too, of Fearchar MacRath's Tobar dhearg hewers, who held the highest ground above the burn the whole of the morning and were the last of the five companies to be given the order to draw off, since the steepness that had made their ground the slowest for a follower to climb made it also the slowest for a defender to leave in good order; and they tell that when the order came at last it was carried up the hillside by a boy too young to be counted a hewer at all, who had begged to run it when no grown messenger could be spared, and that Fearchar MacRath heard it out, sent the boy back down at once, and held his own thirty a further quarter-hour after that to see every wounded man of his own company carried off before he would give the ground himself. Of the ten Tobar dhearg hewers who had stayed behind at the one working pit, none is named among the dead of that day in any telling the north keeps, and the pit itself, it is said, was still raising coal the following week, though with fewer hands at the face than it had had the week before.

They tell that the dead that day, on the western side, were past counting properly by anyone who tried, since the fighting spread along the better part of a mile of broken hill ground and no single teller saw the whole of it, and that the number has never since been agreed exactly among the towns, save that it ran into the hundreds by every reckoning any of them ever made, more than Àth leathan alone had souls to lose and fewer, it is always added, than the kingdom's own count would afterward allow.

And they tell, last of all, and every teller of the north tells this part the same, that Ruth Calder was seen at the pit-head gate in the first hour of the morning and was not seen certainly by anyone after the fighting reached the narrow ground between the fields, where the noise of the water and the press of the retreat together made it impossible for any single witness to say afterward what had become of any one soul among so many. Some say she was seen going up with Àth leathan's last company toward the hill paths in the early afternoon; others say she was not, and stayed at the gate with the youngest of her own people longer than any captain had cause to; and no teller in the whole of the western hills, then or since, has claimed to know for certain which telling is the truer one. They only agree that her voice was heard along the line in the first hour, steady as her father's had ever been at a shift change, and that by nightfall no one could any longer say for certain where she was, or whether the hillside she had set out that morning still held her.

## IV. Of the Open Record

The regiments' own returns, filed separately from the Board's, gave the day a different shape again: three lines of advance up the southern road, the ground taken and held before nightfall, the pits secured, and a note, unsigned but in the same clerk's hand throughout, that the western companies had "withdrawn in good order toward the hills," a form of words the Board's clerks would go on using of every western retreat for the whole twenty months of the war after, whatever the ground had actually cost either side to hold or to give. Of the command on the field that day the returns say only that it was exercised under the commander appointed that autumn to the regiments; no return that Sìleas nic Rath was shown gave any fuller account of how the day had gone from any post nearer the fighting than the western Seann Skell, five miles south, where the first word of it reached the enforcers' own office some hours after the pits had already been secured.

The Board's return for that day, entered in the ordinary course at the western Seann Skell three days after and copied thence to Leabharlann Muileann chaol by the regular post, gives the battle a single heading and a short body, in the plain hand of a clerk who had not himself stood on the field: that the regiments engaged the holdouts of the five western towns below Àth leathan on the eleventh of an Giblean, that the pits were secured by the close of the day, and that the kingdom's dead numbered thirty-eight, each entered by name and company in a schedule appended to the return.

Sìleas nic Rath, royal chronicler from the past autumn and new enough yet to the office that she copied the whole schedule into the record by her own hand rather than trust a clerk to do it, wrote nothing beside the thirty-eight names but the date and the place, as the office required of her and as Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh had told her, on the last morning she held the desk herself, that a chronicler ought always to do: to write what she was let, and to write down, plainly, what she was not. What she was not let, that week and for a long while after, was any count from the Board of what the holdouts themselves had lost, the western towns keeping no returns of their own that any office of the kingdom troubled to ask for, and the open record, set down as it was from what came to Muileann chaol by the regular post and no other road, has never carried a number for the other side of that field at all.

Against Ruth Calder's name, in the Board's own roll of those it had listed the winter before among the towns' spokesmen and captains, Sìleas nic Rath entered, some days after the battle, a single word where a schedule elsewhere might have carried a date of death or a note of capture: *unaccounted*. It was the word the Board's own clerk had used in his covering letter, and she copied it as she found it, adding nothing of her own, since she had not been at Àth leathan and had no ground of her own to add anything to a word another office had chosen. The name has stood so in the open record since, with no other entry following it in any month after, and no clerk of the kingdom has yet been asked, or has troubled on his own account, to strike it through or fill it in.

The seventh regiment, raised that same week from the levies of the west coast and sent up to hold the road behind the army, took its first post on the field itself within the fortnight, and its earliest patrols, sent out along the road and up onto the steeper ground above the burn once the water had begun at last to fall, reported the field empty: no dead left above ground that had not already been carried off by one side or the other in the days after the battle, no wounded, no arms save what had been gathered up already by the Board's own men, only the broken ground itself, the burn running lower now but still brown, and the pit-heads standing as they had stood the morning before, though the pits beneath them would be flooded within the month on the Board's own order and never worked again.

Of the thirty-eight names in the Board's schedule Sìleas nic Rath found, going through it by her own hand, that better than a dozen gave a company raised from the west coast itself, so that more than one household within a day's walk of Àth leathan had lost a son to one side of that field's fighting or the other; and she added a note beside the schedule, and no other, that the open record carried nowhere in its own body any word of where a dead man's people lived, only his name and his company, though she had gone looking herself and found the two facts sat closer together, in that one battle, than the schedule's plain columns let on. She wrote the note because Beathag had also told her, that last morning at the desk, that a chronicler kept two records always, the one the office required of her and the one her own conscience would not let her leave wholly unwritten, and that the second was never to crowd the first off the page, only to sit beside it, plainly marked as what it was.

By the time the light went that first evening, the eleventh of an Giblean, the road below Àth leathan lay as empty as the patrols would find it weeks after: no column moving on it from the south, no company holding it from the north, only the last grey light going off the wet hillside and the water still loud in the dark, and whatever had been decided on that ground that day, decided finally and for good, in a way that no letter written afterward, from either side of it, would ever again be in time to answer.
