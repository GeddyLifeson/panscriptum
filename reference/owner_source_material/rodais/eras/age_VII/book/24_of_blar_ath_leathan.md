# Of Blàr Àth leathan

*11 an Giblean DE 21*

## I. Of the Burn in Spate

The three months since the regiments went west had been the slowest Ruth Calder had ever lived through, and also, she came to think afterward, the fastest, in the way that a long wait can be both at once: slow in the living of each single day at the pit-shed office with the fires banked and the ink freezing, and fast in the looking back, so that am Faoilleach and an Gearran together seemed, from the far side of them, to have gone by in the time it took to draw one long breath. Word had come up the western road twice a week or better, carried by carters who had their own reasons to travel Slighe a' Ghuail's western branch and their own reasons, most of them, to be glad of the coin Ruth paid for a true account rather than a rumour dressed up as one: that the enforcers held the western Seann Skell in strength; that the regiments camped beside them through the worst of the winter and drilled, when the ground allowed it, on the flats above the river; that the manor house had been made a headquarters and the maor's own hall a store for the lorries' fuel. None of it told her a day or an hour, only the slow gathering of a thing readying to move, and she had learned, through those weeks, to read it the way her father had taught her to read a seam before it gave, from the sum of many small signs taken together.

She had not looked for the thaw to come so fast. Through am Faoilleach and into an Gearran the snow had lain deep enough on the hill paths that the companies coming in from Muileann àrsaidh and Tobar dhearg of the hills went a full day slower than the walk should have taken, and she had grown used to a world of white fields and black timber and a cold that got into the ink and stayed there. Then in the first week of an Giblean the wind came round to the south-west and did not go back, and within four days the snow was gone off every slope but the north faces of the higher hills, and the burn below Àth leathan, which she had crossed dry-shod as a girl in most winters, came up brown and quick and loud enough that she heard it from her window in the pit-shed office before she was properly awake.

She went down to look at it on the tenth, the evening before, with Tom Varley beside her because he had taken to walking the ground with her most evenings that month, the two of them saying little and looking hard at whatever there was to look at. The ford below the pit-heads, which any cart had crossed dry in a normal spring, was gone under two feet of brown water moving fast enough to turn a stick end over end, and the flat meadow beyond it, the one everyone at Àth leathan still called the fair field though no fair had been held there in three years, lay soft and dark with the last of the melt still standing in its furrows.

"They'll not bring a lorry over that," Tom said, meaning the ford, and meaning by it more than the ford.

"They'll not need to," Ruth said. "The road from Seann Skell doesn't cross it. It comes down the near side and stops at the pit-heads. Whatever they bring, they bring it dry."

She had walked that road herself twice that winter, once as far as the river and once, in secret and against Tom's counsel, most of the way to Seann Skell, to see with her own eyes what the carter's word had already told her: the regiments quartered beside the enforcers, their lorries drawn up in rows under canvas, more of them than she had troubled to count past a hundred because the counting past a hundred did not change what she meant to do about it. She had not told the five towns the whole of what she saw, only Tom, and Tom had told the pit captains, who told their own companies only so much as steadied a man rather than loosened his knees, which was the same discipline she had grown up watching her father keep about a bad seam or a flooding gallery, and which, standing at the swollen ford with three months of waiting behind her and a night before whatever the morning brought, she still knew how to keep.

The five towns had been at Àth leathan since the last week of am Faoilleach, near enough four hundred names on her first count and closer to five hundred and fifty by the time the last of Tobar dhearg's boys came down with their fathers in an Gearran, once the whole matter had stopped being a gathering and become, in everything but the word for it, a muster that would not disperse. Through the waiting months she had done the thing that a captain's daughter does without being taught it in so many words, which was to turn a crowd of men and boys glad of one another's company into a body that could be told where to stand: five companies, one to a town, each under its own captain and answerable, in the matter of where the whole body moved and when, to Tom Varley, whom the five towns had agreed on without much debate once it was plain nobody much fancied the office and Tom least of anyone.

Cnoc thais's company was the largest, near a hundred and sixty under Tom himself, most of them hewers of his own two reopened shafts and the families that had followed the coal down from the ridge. Àth naomh's sixty-odd kept their two-abreast order even at the pit-head, a habit Ruth had stopped remarking on by the second week. Muileann àrsaidh sent the most of any single town, near two hundred, Sarah Dunmore's oatmeal carts having by then made three further journeys down from the mill, each one shorter than the last as the mill's own reserves thinned. Tobar dhearg of the hills sent, in the end, near forty, every man and boy the hill town's one pit could spare and a dozen more besides who had walked down all the same, leaving the pit worked by old men and women for the season. Àth leathan's own company was Ruth's, by the same accident of a dead father that had made her the towns' spokeswoman, and it stood at ninety hewers of the town's three pits, with a scatter of wives and elder daughters who had, by then, made themselves as much a part of the muster as any man with a pick, carrying water and dressings and, where a household had a fowling piece from before An Dealachadh and no man left to carry it, carrying that too.

Nine hundred souls, near enough, stood at Àth leathan by the tenth of an Giblean, counting the carriers among them, and Ruth's list, grown thick with names crossed through and names added, held every one. Against them, by every account the carters brought up from the river, the kingdom would send near fourteen hundred, drawn from the companies quartered at Seann Skell and the enforcers already settled there, with lorries enough to carry them the five miles from the river port to the pit-heads in a single morning's work. Ruth had done that sum more than once through the winter, turning it over the way she turned over any figure that would not come out even, and had found, each time, that it changed nothing about what the five towns meant to do, only the manner of doing it.

The waiting months had worn the gathering thinner in ways no muster roll troubled to record. Two of Sarah Dunmore's cart-horses had gone lame on the Muileann àrsaidh road and were not replaced; a fever had gone through the byres behind the chapel in an Gearran and taken an old woman of Cnoc thais who had come down only to be near her grandsons; Ellen Marsh, teaching still at Doire ghlas, had sent up through the winter two letters that Ruth kept folded inside the same list-book as the names, neither of them saying anything Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own censors would have troubled to strike out, both of them saying, in the plain way Ellen wrote everything, that the school's benches were being kept ready should any family need them. Ruth had not answered either letter. She was grateful for both, and she had found, by the second month of the muster, that she no longer trusted her own hand to write anything short enough to be worth the carter's trouble.

They had picks and shovels and the hewers' own short axes, sharpened that winter past any use a coal face had ever asked of them. They had, among the whole nine hundred, perhaps sixty firearms fit to be called such, most of them fowling pieces older than any man carrying them, a dozen better rifles bought or begged from who knew where and never asked after too closely, and four kegs of blasting powder from the pits themselves, which Tom Varley meant, if it came to that, for the narrow places where the road from Seann Skell ran between the spoil-heaps and the flooded lower galleries, where a charge set right might do more than sixty fowling pieces together.

"You'll not fire the powder unless I say," Tom told her that evening at the ford, not for the first time. "A charge set too soon warns them off the ground we want them on."

"I know it," Ruth said. "I've known it since you first told me, and the second time, and the third."

"I'll go on telling you," Tom said, "because it's the one thing between us and a rout, and I'd sooner bore you with it than have you forget it at the wrong minute." He said it without much heat, the way he said most things that winter, and Ruth, looking at the brown water going by faster than either of them could easily have swum it, found she was glad of the boring, glad of anything said plainly and repeated, because the alternative, she had learned these three months, was a silence in which a person's own thoughts ran on faster than the burn.

She did not ask him, that evening, what he thought the morning would bring, because she had asked him some version of that question most evenings for a fortnight and had stopped expecting an answer different from the last. He only looked a while longer at the water, and then at the dark shapes of the pit-heads against a sky gone the colour of a struck match, and said that they should get what sleep the night would give them, because the towns' companies would want their captains steady in the morning whatever the morning brought.

On the way back she passed Tobar dhearg's captain, the stooped man who had brought his own lamp down off the hill path in am Faoilleach and set it on her trestle table before he gave his name, sitting outside his men's fire with a cup he was not drinking from. He asked her, in the flat way he asked most things, whether the chapel at Àth leathan would be open before light, and she told him it would, since the priest had said as much himself, and the old man nodded and said that a man ought to have been inside a chapel at least once before a morning like the one coming, whatever else the morning held, and that he meant to see to it his own men had the chance of it too, those who wanted it. Ruth said she would pass the word along the line, and did.

Walking back up to the pit-shed office with the ford's noise falling away behind her, she thought that Tom's answer, plain and repeated and not much of an answer on its own terms, was as near to one as she was likely to get, and found, turning it over, that it was answer enough.

## II. Of the Word Ruth Gave at Dawn

She woke before light, as she had every morning that month without needing anyone to rouse her, and went out into a cold that had lost, overnight, the last hard edge of winter and taken on instead the raw, wet cold of a spring morning after rain, the kind that gets into a coat faster than any frost. The sky to the east, over the road from Seann Skell, was going from black to a grey the colour of wet slate, and the burn, louder now than it had been the evening before, ran on unseen below the pit-heads with a sound like water that had somewhere particular to be.

The companies were mustering already, town by town, along the low ground between the pit-sheds and the fair field, exactly as she and Tom had marked out the ground for them three evenings before: Cnoc thais on the left flank nearest the flooded lower gallery, where the ground did most of a wall's work; Àth naomh in the centre, keeping their two-abreast order even now, forming and re-forming it as their captain called them into it as though the order were a kind of armour worn; Muileann àrsaidh on the right, where the road from Seann Skell came down between the spoil-heaps into the narrowest ground of the whole field, the ground Tom's blasting powder was laid to answer if it came to that; Tobar dhearg's forty held back a little, by their own captain's word and not by anyone else's ordering, at the mouth of Àth leathan's own pit, where a man too old for the front line could still see what came and send word of it; and Àth leathan's ninety in the last gap between Àth naomh and the pit-heads proper, Ruth's own place among them though she carried, that morning, no pick and no fowling piece, only her list, folded small inside her coat where it had ridden for three months, and a lantern not yet lit.

She thought, standing a moment at the pit-shed door before she started down the line, of her father, five years dead now and buried at the chapel she had just sent word about, and of the last thing he had ever told her of the seam under Àth leathan, which was that a working face tells you what it means to do a long while before it does it, if you trouble to listen, and that the trouble of listening is most of what a captain is for. These three months she had listened to as much as the north's own carters and her own eyes could tell her, and this morning found she had run out of anything further the listening could give her; what was left was only the doing.

She went down the line as the light came up, as she had gone down it every morning of the muster, though this morning she found herself looking at faces rather than counting them, which she had not let herself do before: a boy of fourteen from Tobar dhearg whose father had come down with him and whose name, she remembered without needing the list, was the last one she had taken on the sixth evening of the gathering; one of Sarah Dunmore's carters, too old by rights for the front line and standing in it anyway because his two sons stood either side of him and he would not be parted from them; a woman of Àth naomh, no older than Ruth herself, with a fowling piece across her back and a baby not eight months born left, that morning, in the care of an aunt at the pit-shed, whose name Ruth had entered on her list under the mother's own insistence, since the child was to be counted as being of the muster too, in some fashion the woman had not troubled to explain and Ruth had not troubled to question.

Along the line she passed the Muileann àrsaidh company forming up on the right, where Sarah Dunmore's carter stood between his two sons as he had every morning that month, and past him the company's own captain, a mill-hand turned pit-hand named for the season only as Muileann àrsaidh's captain, since he had never troubled anyone with more of a name than that and nobody had troubled him for it, checking the priming of the dozen better rifles one by one down the front rank with the same unhurried care Ruth had watched a smith give to a set of new-forged tools. He told her, as she passed, that the ground below the spoil-heaps was as good as it would ever be, the road narrowed there to less than a cart's width by the heaps on either hand, and that if Tom's charge went off true it would do the work of fifty men. Ruth said she hoped he was right, and went on.

She found Tom at the centre of the line, where he had gone to speak with Àth naomh's captain about the ground below the spoil-heaps, and she came to him not with any speech prepared, because she had tried, the night before, to write one in her head and found every version of it either too grand for what the morning was or too small for what the last three months had been, and had given it up.

"They'll be in sight within the hour," Tom said. He had a spy-glass, an old thing of his grandfather's that had done service, he'd told her once, watching for An Dealachadh's ships that never came a second time, and he had it trained now on the road below the spoil-heaps, where nothing yet moved but the light. "Dust first, if the road's dried enough. Then the column."

"And after that," Ruth said, "it's yours to give the word, not mine."

"It was always going to be," Tom said, not unkindly. "You kept the list. I keep the line. That's the shape it's taken, and I'll not pretend either of us chose it different from how it fell." He lowered the glass and looked at her a moment in the grey light, and whatever he had meant to say next, he did not say it, and instead put out his hand for the plain clasp two people give each other before a thing neither can promise the shape of, and Ruth took it.

"Tell them," she said, meaning the five towns' companies both, "that whatever comes of today, the list stands. Every name on it, town by town. Whoever comes looking after, for whatever reason they come looking, the list will say who stood at Àth leathan and who they stood beside."

"I'll tell them," Tom said. "Go back to your own company now. They'll want to see you standing with them when it starts, not off talking to me."

She went back down the line to Àth leathan's ninety, and took her place among them, not at their front, since she carried no weapon and had never claimed to be any kind of soldier, but near enough the front that any man there could see her if he turned his head, which several of them did, briefly, as the light came fully up and the grey overhead thinned toward a colour that might, on another morning, have been called a fair sky.

For a while, standing there, there was nothing to do but wait, and the waiting had a shape of its own that Ruth had not met in three months of waiting for other things: the burn loud below them, a lark going up somewhere over the higher ground as though the morning were an ordinary one, one of the Àth leathan hewers beside her murmuring the numbers of a psalm under his breath without seeming to know he did it, and, from along the Tobar dhearg line, the sound, thin on the wind, of the old captain's men singing something low in An Eaglais's own words, which she followed only in part and understood well enough for what it was. She found she was glad of it, the singing, in the same plain way she had been glad of Tom's repeating himself the night before: a thing said or sung that steadied more by its saying than by its sense.

The dust came first, as Tom had said it would, a pale smear along the road below the spoil-heaps where nothing else moved, and then, before the dust had well settled in her sight, the column itself came, dark and long and closer than Ruth had let herself think it would be so soon, moving at a steady pace that had nothing hurried and nothing hesitant in it, the pace of men who had been told exactly where they were going and saw no reason to arrive there other than on time.

Ruth Calder stood at the front rank of Àth leathan's company, in the raw cold of a spring morning with the burn loud behind her and the dust of the regiments' coming rising pale along the road ahead, and watched them come on, and this is where her own account of that day ends, because after this hour no page in her own hand, nor any word set down as hers by anyone who stood beside her, tells what she said or thought or did when the column reached the ground below the spoil-heaps and the first order, whoever gave it and on whichever side, was given.

## III. Of What the North Tells

So the people of the five towns tell it, those who stood in one company or another that morning and lived to walk home from it, and those who had it from them at their own hearths.

They tell that the regiments came on past the dust and did not slow at the ford, because the ford was not their road, and that the fighting began at the spoil-heaps rather than the ford, where Muileann àrsaidh's company held the narrow ground and Tom Varley's charge, when at last he gave the word for it, brought down enough of the loose spoil to check the column's head for the better part of an hour, though it did not turn them back, because a column of fourteen hundred does not turn back for the loss of its head, only slows, and re-forms, and comes on again by a wider way round the fallen ground.

They tell that the fighting spread from there along the whole line through the morning, Cnoc thais holding longest on the left where the flooded gallery did a wall's work as Tom had judged it would, Àth naomh's two-abreast order breaking at last into no order at all when the regiments' own line reached and passed it, and that the fair field, the soft dark meadow beyond the ford that Ruth and Tom had looked at the evening before, was by noon a ground no one who stood on it that day has ever agreed to call by its old name again.

Cnoc thais tells its own part of that morning apart from the rest, as a town will keep the telling of its own dead closer than it keeps any other part of a shared grief, and its telling holds that Tom Varley was seen three times in the worst of the fighting by the flooded gallery, each time carrying a wounded man or boy back from the line with his own hands before going forward again, and that it was Tom who at last called the retreat toward Cnoc thais and Muileann àrsaidh when the gallery's ground could no longer be held. Muileann àrsaidh's own telling, kept separately again, holds to the charge below the spoil-heaps and to little else, as though the town had decided, without ever quite deciding it aloud, that the one thing it had done that day was worth the telling and the rest belonged to other towns to keep.

They tell of the pit-heads changing hands more than once before the day was out, of men fighting with picks and axes against rifles because that was what the five towns had brought to fight with, of the boy of fourteen from Tobar dhearg whose father would not let him past the pit-shed door and stood in the door himself the whole of the morning, and of the woman of Àth naomh with the fowling piece, whose name is remembered in that town still, though not written here, because the naming of her is the north's to keep and not this book's to spend.

Doire naomh, whose grove lies within sight of the field though its own people took no part in the fighting, tells only what could be seen from that distance: smoke over the pit-heads by mid-morning, though nothing there had been fired that either side has ever owned to setting; the sound of it carrying on a south wind as far as the grove, so that the grove's own people, going about their ordinary morning, heard the whole of it without seeing more than the smoke; and a silence, toward evening, that some of them said afterward was worse to hear than the noise had been.

They tell that by early afternoon the five towns' companies, what remained whole of them, gave up the ground below the pit-heads and the fair field both, and fell back in a scatter of smaller bodies rather than one, along the hill paths that had brought them down in am Faoilleach and up again now in the opposite direction, toward Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais and the higher ground the regiments' lorries could not easily follow. Of the nine hundred who had stood at dawn, the towns' own reckoning, made afterward and never once written down together in a single tally, holds that something under two-thirds came home to one hill town or another by the second evening; what happened to the rest is the part of the telling that varies most from mouth to mouth, since a scattered retreat over broken ground in failing light leaves as many questions behind it as a battle does, and the north's telling does not pretend to answer them all.

They tell the dead in the hundreds, and they do not agree past that on any closer number, because no one town's count was ever set beside another's, and because more than one family of the five towns lost, that single day, every name it had sent to Àth leathan, so that there was no one left of that household to be asked how many, or which, or when.

Of Ruth Calder herself the north tells nothing certain. Some say she was seen at the pit-head door in the worst of the afternoon, calling names from memory to men who could no longer answer to them. Others say she went down with Àth leathan's company when it broke, and was carried off the field by two of her own hewers who did not themselves come home to say so. A few, the fewest, say she was seen on the Muileann àrsaidh path that evening, walking, her list gone from her coat, though every teller who says this admits in the same breath that they had it third-hand or fourth, from someone who was not there to see it either. No teller in the north, asked plainly, will say for certain which of these is true, or whether none of them is, and the north has learned, in the years since, to let the question stand unanswered rather than choose among three stories none of which any single mouth can swear to.

## IV. Of What the Open Record Gives

An Clàr Foscailte, kept that year by Sìleas nic Rath at an Leabharlann in Muileann chaol from the regiments' own returns, says a good deal less, and says it more plainly.

It gives the day, 11 an Giblean DE 21, and the place, the field below Àth leathan, and it gives the kingdom's dead by name and by company, thirty and eight in number, the returns of that day counted and signed by officers whose own names stand beside the count. Somhairle mac Lachlainn's own return, written from Seann Skell within the week, names the companies that had marched from Cathair dhearg the winter before and does no more than name them; it says which held the ground below the spoil-heaps and which came up after, and says nothing of how any man among the thirty and eight came to be counted, only that he was. The west-coast levies who had guarded the road behind the column are named among the dead too, a dozen of them, men the return does not yet call by any regiment's number, since none of the companies that fought at Àth leathan had, on that day, been given one. The rest of the thirty and eight are scattered among the capital's own companies, drawn from Cathair dhearg and Inis mhòr, ordinary men whose names, set down in a clerk's even hand on a return meant only to balance a muster roll, are the fullest account An Clàr Foscailte gives of anyone who died at Àth leathan that day.

The return notes, in the same even hand, that the kingdom's host had been victualled through the campaign from Inis àrsaidh, the river town nearest the field by water, and that its stores had held good through the fighting and after; it is the only line in the whole return that speaks of anything going right for anyone, and it speaks of bread and salt fish rather than of men.

Of the holdouts' dead An Clàr Foscailte gives no number at all. It notes only that the field was held by evening, that the pit-heads of Àth leathan passed into Comhairle nam Mèinnean's keeping, and that the companies of the five towns, what remained of them, had withdrawn toward Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais by nightfall. It does not say how many withdrew, nor how many did not.

Ruth Calder's name does not stand among the thirty and eight, which is no matter, since she was never of the kingdom's regiments for An Clàr Foscailte to count. It does not stand anywhere else in An Clàr Foscailte either, that day or after. Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own list, the one Tom Varley had word of on the drove path months before, does not appear in what an Leabharlann was given to keep; if it survives, it lies where the rest of the war's sealed record lies, and no one now living has read it. Every page an Leabharlann holds open of that spring, and every page after, is searched in vain for her name. Sìleas nic Rath, taking down the regiments' returns that season in a hand still new to the office, wrote once, in a private note kept with her own papers and not with Eachdraidh na Rìoghachd proper, that she had looked for the name a second time and a third, thinking she had missed it among so many others, and had not found it any of the times she looked.

She wrote, in that same private note, that she had thought once of asking Somhairle mac Lachlainn directly whether his own return, or any paper that had passed under his hand, named a woman among the dead or the taken, and that she had not sent the question, because a chronicler new to the office learns early which questions the office will let her ask and which it will not, and this, she judged, was one of the second kind.

Near Àth leathan, in the grove the Dia-thìrich of Doire naomh keep closed to strangers, many of the field's unclaimed dead were laid that week and after, the grove's own people going out onto the ground once the regiments had passed on toward Muileann àrsaidh, and carrying what they found. The town of Àth chrom, a short way off at the bent ford, sent its own people to carry others, its own drovers going out along the road with carts they had until then used only for coal and salt. Neither town's record says whose dead they carried, of which side, or how many, only that they carried them, and that the grove has kept its graves since, closed to strangers, as it kept them before. Baile fhiadhaich, further off on the west-coast harbour road, sent no one to the field, lying further off than the others, but its own people took in what wounded reached that far, and the town has kept the remembering of that day in its own way since, apart from any teller's account written down elsewhere.

The field, the fair field below the pit-heads where nine hundred souls of five towns had stood at dawn, lay empty by the time the last light went out of that day, the burn still running loud and brown below it, the spoil brought down by Tom Varley's charge still lying where it had fallen across the narrow road, and no plough has turned that ground since.
