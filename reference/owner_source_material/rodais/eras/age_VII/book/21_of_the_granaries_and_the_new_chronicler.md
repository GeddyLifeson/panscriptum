# Of the Granaries and the New Chronicler

*DE 20*

## I. Of the Nine Votes

The room over the net-lofts at Caol mhòr was colder than Martha Greaves had known it in twelve years in the chair, and she had known it in every kind of weather the north could send. The driftwood fire at the near end had been built up twice before the sitting and still could not warm the far wall, where the ice on the inside of the panes had not melted by noon. Forty-one delegates had come through snow that had closed the fair road three days before and was not yet done falling, and five benches along the harbour wall stood empty, as they had stood empty for five years now, since the day the western delegates had risen without a word and gone down the outside stair and not come back. Nobody moved to fill those places. Thomas Rook kept them chalked in the roll all the same, under the towns' names, with nothing after them.

She had not meant to bring the matter before the room herself. It was Peter Hale, delegate of Caol mhòr these dozen years and long-armed and fair still though there was grey now at his temples, who rose first and said that the Moot had sat three sittings running without a word said of the western hills, and that a room which called itself the gathering of the northern towns and would not speak the plainest fact in the north was not doing the thing it was built to do. There was a murmur along both walls at that, not agreement exactly, more the sound of men and women glad to hear said aloud what they had each been keeping to themselves.

"Say it, then," Martha told him, from the chair.

He said it. The pits at Muileann àrsaidh were walled now, he said, against the Board's own order, the road into the town dug across so that nothing came in or out but what a man could carry on his back over the spoil. The letter Ruth Calder had sent south at Christmas, offering the Crown's own fifth in coal if only the Board would carry it, had gone to the Library and had had no answer, not a refusal even, only silence, and a silence from that quarter, he said, looking round the room as if daring anyone to contradict him, generally meant one thing when it went on long enough. He did not say the word war. Nobody in the room that morning said it, though it sat in the middle of the trestle as plainly as the ice on the far window.

Martha herself had had her own silence to weigh against his, though she did not put it before the room, having no wish to make the matter more hers than it already stood: the letter she had sent the autumn before, asking the Board for a year's delay while the Moot talked with the western towns, had gone the same road as Ruth Calder's and come to the same end, received and filed, no more said. She had not looked for anything better of it. She looked now, hearing Peter Hale lay the two letters side by side without knowing he had done it, at the plain shape the pattern made when two requests from two different hands met the same wall in the same season, and found she believed him a good deal more readily for the coincidence than she would have believed either letter alone.

"And what would you have the room do," asked the delegate of the farm towns, an old woman named Christina Munro who had sat on that bench longer than Martha had sat in the chair, "that it has not tried already? We sent a letter. It was received and filed, same as the last."

"I'd have the room say plainly what it is," Peter Hale said. "Not a fighting man's ally and not the Board's either. Say it, and put it in the book, so that whatever comes after, no one can claim the Moot was ever asked and never answered."

It went round the trestle after that in the old way, each delegate free to speak once before any speak twice, and Martha let it run longer than she generally let anything run, because she judged, rightly as it turned out, that the room needed to hear itself say the thing from every corner before it would agree to say it once, together, in the book. A fisherman of Cathair gheal, whose town sold its catch up the coast for the last poor coal of the hills, said he for one would not have his name set down as neutral between men who dug and men who came with lorries to stop them digging, whatever the word neutral was made to mean in a minute. A miller of the eastern towns, whose trade touched the western hills not at all, said the room had no army to send and no coal to spare and had best say what it could truly do and no more, or it would find its own word worth as little as the Board's. Martha wrote none of it down herself. That was Thomas Rook's work, and he kept at it with his head bent and his pen scratching, filling three pages before the room came, of its own accord and without her steering it there in words, to the shape of the thing Peter Hale had wanted from the start: that the Moot was no party to the quarrel between the Board and the western towns, and would take no side in it by word or by deed; and that it would feed, from its own stores, any soul who came to any of its towns hungry, of whatever town, without asking first what side that soul had stood on.

She called for the show of hands herself, as speaker, standing so the room could see her count and know it was honestly done. Nine hands went up along the near wall for the motion as Thomas Rook read it back, Peter Hale's among them, and not one hand rose against it, though a good many arms stayed folded and a good many faces looked at the table rather than at her, Christina Munro's among those. She waited the space of three breaths, in case any of the folded arms meant to unfold, and none did.

"Nine for," she said. "None against. Carried." And Thomas Rook wrote it in, the day, the count, and the two clauses entire, no party to the quarrel and feed the hungry, in his small careful hand, and the room broke into its usual noise of benches scraping and voices low and urgent, already arguing over what feeding the hungry would want in barley and where it would come from, before the ink on the page was dry.

Christina Munro caught Martha's sleeve on the way down the outside stair, the last of the room ahead of them already scattering into the snow. "Nine," she said, not unkindly, "out of one-and-forty. You'll not tell me that's the whole room's mind."

"I'll tell you it's the whole room's vote," Martha said, "which is the only thing the book can hold. A man's mind is his own to keep."

"And is it yours as well? Nine, and you the tenth, and never raised your own hand at all."

"The chair doesn't vote," Martha said, "except to break a tie, and there was none to break." She said it as she had said it a hundred times before, in a hundred sittings where the rule had never once troubled her to explain further, and saw, in the way the old woman's mouth set, that this time it had not answered the question that was actually being asked. She did not answer that one either, there on the stair with the snow coming down between them, and Christina Munro, who had sat in that room longer than Martha had sat in the chair, let her sleeve go and said no more about it, then or after.

Martha sat on a while after the others had gone down the stair into the snow, as she had sat on the evening of her own first sitting, twelve years before, gathering Walter Hale's loose pages square on the table though there were no loose pages now to gather, only the habit of sitting still a little while in an empty room before she had to be Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas again and not merely the Moot's speaker. Nine votes would not carry so much as a bag of meal to Muileann àrsaidh, walled as it was, nor buy Ruth Calder's letter an answer it had not had. That much she had known before she ever called for the hands. What she had wanted was the thing in the book, in Thomas Rook's own careful ink, because a thing once written did not, whatever else befell it, become less true for having been carried by only nine.

## II. Of the Wool Mills' Takings

She did not speak again of what the nine votes would mean in barley until the carding-sheds at Doire ghlas were running full summer hours and the fleece was in from the spring clip, weighed and sorted and already turning, under her own hand at the tally-desk, into the figures that told her each year what the mills had made and what they had left over. That summer left over a good deal, the fleece having come in heavy after two mild winters and the price holding at the fair better than it had in some years, and Martha sat with the figures a whole evening in the counting-house with the window open on the grove and the grey oaks going gold-green in the long light, before she wrote, at the foot of the mill's own book and not the Moot's, an order to the miller at Caol mhòr and a second to her own granary-keeper at Doire ghlas, that the stores of both towns be filled that autumn beyond the ordinary wont, and paid for out of the mills' own takings, and no other fund drawn on.

She could have laid it before the room first. She was speaker, and the room had voted the feeding of the hungry into its own minute not six months before, and by every custom of the office she might have brought the figures to the next sitting and let the delegates argue, as they argued over everything, how much grain and which towns' stores and whose purse. That she chose not to. Instead she told herself, turning the reasons over on the ride home that evening with the oilcloth over her knees against a shower that never quite came, that a granary filled in am Faoilleach when the need showed itself would be six weeks too late for whoever needed it first, and that the mills were hers to spend as she judged, being no part of the Moot's own purse; and both of these were true reasons, and neither was the whole of it. The whole of it she did not put into words even to herself that evening, only felt it, the way a person feels the weight of a decision already made and only waiting to be carried out: that if the wall at Muileann àrsaidh meant what Peter Hale had said it meant, then a granary at Caol mhòr and another at Doire ghlas, standing full and unremarked through an ordinary summer, might in a year's time be the only thing in the whole north that stood between some family walking down out of the hills with nothing and a winter they did not live through.

The carts began coming up from the coast in the last week of an t-Iuchar, grain bought quietly through three different factors so that no one merchant should wonder aloud why the Moot's towns wanted so much of it in a year when the harvest promised well enough on its own account, and Martha stood in the yard at Doire ghlas most of one long afternoon watching the sacks go up into the loft above the old mill store, counting them herself against the factor's tally the way she had once counted fleeces that the mill's own tally said were forty and her own eyes said were thirty-eight. The granary-keeper, a broad quiet man named John Skinner who had kept that loft for eleven years and had never in all that time seen it filled past two-thirds before the frost, asked her once, straightforwardly, whether he ought to enter a reason in his own book against the extra, since his book was looked at by the room's auditors each spring and a figure without a reason drew a question sooner or later.

"Enter the weight and the date," Martha said, "and no reason at all. If anybody asks you why, tell them to ask me."

John Skinner wrote it so, and asked nothing further, being a man long used to taking a plain instruction plainly and leaving it there. He did, over the weeks the carts kept coming, take to walking the loft himself of an evening after the last sack was up, counting the rows against no tally at all but his own memory of how the place had stood in other autumns, and once, near the end of it, he remarked to Martha, in the mild way he remarked on weather, that he could not recall the loft standing so full at harvest-time in all his eleven years, not even the year of the good barley when the Moot had sold the surplus south at the fair. Martha said only that it was a good year for fleece as well as barley, and let it go at that, and John Skinner, who had his own family in the mill cottages and his own notion of what a full loft might be wanted for before winter was out, let it go with her.

Nobody asked her, that year or for some while after. The Moot's own minute, when Thomas Rook came in the autumn to make the autumn entry of the granaries' state, as he made it every year without any particular interest in the figures, recorded that the stores at Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas stood fuller than the five years' average by a good half again, and gave, because none had been given him to give, no reason for it at all. Martha read the entry over his shoulder when the book came round the trestle for the room's inspection, as it did each autumn, and said nothing, and signed under it in her turn, and passed the book on down the bench to Peter Hale, who glanced at the figure, and then at her, and did not ask either, though something in the set of his mouth said plainly enough that he had done the same sum in his own head and come to the same answer she had, and had chosen, as she had, not to write it down.

Martha was one-and-seventy that year, broad still in the shoulder though the years had begun to stoop it a little, and had managed the mills at Doire ghlas since before most of the delegates now sitting on the benches around her had been born. In the quiet after a sitting her mind went sometimes to Daniel Frayne, dead these twelve years, who had kept the room's minutes for twenty-three years and never once, that she had heard, put a private reason into a public book where a plain figure would do as well alone. She understood him, she found, a good deal better now than she had while he lived.

## III. Of the Desk, the Ink and the Key

It is told at Muileann chaol, in the Library's own upper room, that the handing-over took the better part of an afternoon and would have taken less had either woman been willing to let it go quickly, and that the keeper Raghnall mac Mhuirich, who had found the money for Sìleas's own hiring nine years before out of his own small fund and told the council afterward rather than before, kept well away from the room the whole of that day, saying to anyone who asked that a chronicle changed hands better without a keeper standing over it.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh had written to the council in the spring of that year, in her own plain hand and no other's, that she asked leave to lay down the office of royal chronicler at the close of the summer, being nine-and-sixty that year and finding the winters harder on her eyes than the summers, and that she named, as the office's custom allowed a retiring chronicler to name, her own clerk of near nine years' standing, Sìleas nic Rath, to take up the desk after her, subject always to the council's own leave, which the council gave without discussion in a single line at the foot of a sitting given over mostly to other matters. Sìleas learned of it, in the end, from Beathag herself and no letter of the council's, on an ordinary wet morning in an Gearran, over the same pot of tea they had shared at the same hour for nine years, when Beathag set down her cup and said, in the same voice she used for the weather, that she had written to the council, and what she had written, and that Sìleas had until an Lùnastal to decide whether she still wanted the desk once she had heard the whole of what it cost.

Sìleas thought afterwards, more than once, that she ought to have asked then what Beathag meant by cost, and had not, partly from the surprise of the thing and partly because some instinct told her that whatever the answer was, she would learn it better by waiting for it than by asking for it before she was ready to hear it.

The last afternoon came in an Lùnastal, a still hot day with the river low and slow past the old building's windows and the paper-mill's wheel barely turning for want of water to drive it. Beathag had cleared the desk herself over the week before, a little at a time, so that there should be no single day when the room looked stripped, and by the last afternoon there was little left on it but the inkstand that had been Tormod Pinn's before her, the day's page half-written in her own hand, and a small iron key on a loop of waxed thread.

"The ink first," Beathag said, and pushed the stand a hand's breadth across the desk, though it had stood in that same spot, Sìleas knew, since before either of them had been born. "It was Tormod's. It will outlast the both of us if nobody drops it. Don't clean the well too often. A little of the old ink left in the bottom keeps the new from going thin."

Sìleas said she would remember it.

"The page next." Beathag turned the half-written sheet so it faced the chair Sìleas had not yet sat down in. "I've left it short. You'll finish today's entry in your own hand, not mine, so that the book shows the change where the change was made, and not hidden in the middle of a sentence as if nothing had happened. That's the office's own rule, and a good one. Write in your own hand today, and every day after, and don't trouble yourself trying to write like me. Nobody thanks a chronicler for pretending to be the last one."

Last of all she took up the key, and held it a moment in her closed hand before she gave it over, and Sìleas understood, without being told, that this was the part of the afternoon Beathag had least wanted to hurry.

"This opens the press where the sealed things go," Beathag said. "Not the day-book. The day-book is open to any soul who comes and asks, and so it should stay, whatever the council or anyone above the council would rather. This is for the other kind. You'll know the difference when it comes to you, and it will come to you, sooner than you'd wish, if the roads north stay as they are this autumn." She closed Sìleas's fingers over the key with her own thin ones. "Write what you are let, Sìleas, and write it as fully and as plainly as if no one would ever grudge you a word of it. But write down, too, what you are not let, and where, and by whom, and on what day, even if all you can write is that a letter went out and a silence came back. A chronicle that only shows what it was free to show is no chronicle at all in the end. It is a fair copy of somebody else's leave."

"And if the leave never comes?"

"Then the silence is the record," Beathag said, "and you set it down as carefully as you would set down a battle, because it very likely is one, only fought with paper instead of anything sharper, and paper battles are the ones that get lost soonest if nobody minds to keep the score."

She said little else. She looked once round the room, at Tormod Pinn's chair that neither of them had ever used, and at the window on the paper-mill's wheel, and picked up the small bag she had packed the evening before for the carrier who would take her north-east to Àth dhìreach and her sister's house the following morning, and went to the door, and there stopped, and turned back only far enough to say that Sìleas should not get up, since a chronicler did not rise for a chronicler, only for the office, and the office had already changed hands and was sitting, at that moment, in the chair behind the desk.

The carrier's cart stood waiting in the yard below when Beathag came down the outside stair the next morning, the bag already stowed under the seat and a rug laid ready across the boards, and there was nobody there to see her off but the carrier himself and, at the last, Raghnall mac Mhuirich, who came out from the door of the reading-room in his shirtsleeves with ink still on his fingers and said only that he hoped the road stayed dry as far as Àth dhomhain, since the low ford there was no friend to a laden cart in wet weather. Beathag said she had crossed that ford in every kind of weather it owned and did not expect this crossing to be the one that taught her anything new about it. He handed her up himself, though the carrier stood ready to do it, and stepped back, and the cart went out under the arch and down toward the river road, and that, so far as the Library's own telling goes, was the whole of the leave-taking: a dry ford wished for, and a hand given, and no more said than the road wanted.

Sìleas sat where she was told to sit until the door had closed and the footsteps had gone down the stair and out into the yard, and then a good while longer, with the key still warm from Beathag's hand and the pen dry in the stand before her. When at last she drew the half-finished page toward her and dipped the pen, her own hand, she noticed, ran a shade smaller and a shade more upright than Beathag's, and she let it run so rather than try to match the older woman's slant, remembering what she had been told about pretending. She finished the day's entry, the ordinary business of an ordinary summer's day at the Library, in her own hand entire, and at the foot of it, where the office's custom asked for nothing more, she wrote one further line, because it seemed to her, sitting alone in the room for the first time as its chronicler, that the custom had left a silence exactly where Beathag had told her a chronicler ought never to leave one.

*This day the office passed from Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh to Sìleas nic Rath, by the council's leave and the outgoing chronicler's own naming. What follows in this book is written by the second hand, and the reader is told so here, once, so that it need not be said again.*

She read the line back twice, found nothing in it she wished to soften, and set the pen down beside the inkstand that had been Tormod Pinn's and was now, in the way such things become true only by being used, her own.
