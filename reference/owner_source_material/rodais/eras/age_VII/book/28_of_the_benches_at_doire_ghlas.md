# Of the Benches at Doire ghlas

*DE 21*

## I. Of the First of Them

They came down out of the hills the way water comes down a hill after rain, Ellen Marsh thought, watching the first of them from her own gate on a wet morning in an Giblean: a trickle at first, no order to it that a person could have foretold, and then, before you had rightly marked the trickle had begun, more than the ground about you seemed able to hold. The first family she saw with her own eyes was five people and a handcart, a woman and three children and an old man too stiff in the knees to have walked the whole way on his own legs, though he had, the woman said, because there had been no cart to spare for him and no time to wait for one.

"Àth leathan," the woman said, when Ellen asked her, the one word and no more, standing in the lane with the rain running off her shawl and her eyes gone past Ellen to the grey roofs of Doire ghlas beyond her, as if she could not yet quite believe a town still stood whole anywhere on the island. She did not say the battle's name. Nobody who came down that spring said the battle's name, not for weeks yet; they said the town's name, Àth leathan, and let the listener supply the rest, because the rest was a thing that did not want saying twice by the person who had walked out from under it.

Ellen took them in that first night, the five of them and the handcart both, her own house being the nearest with a fire lit, and gave them what she had, which was porridge and a corner of the byre for the old man's knees to be spared the floor, and did not ask them any of the questions her notebook wanted asked, because a woman who has walked three days with three children and a father-in-law who cannot bend his own legs is not a woman a schoolteacher puts a pen in front of on the first night. She asked instead where they meant to go on, and was told nowhere, that Caol mhòr was said to have work on the boats and Doire ghlas was said to have the mill, and that one or the other would have to do, since there was no third place left in the world that anybody in that family could name with any certainty of finding it standing.

More came the next week, and more the week after, along the same road down out of the western hills that had never in Ellen's memory carried more than a carrier's cart and a drover's cattle at any one time, walking now in twos and fives and, once, a whole clutch of eleven from three farms near Muileann àrsaidh who had left together in the night before the ditch-bridge went down, having judged, rightly as it turned out, that a siege was coming and having no wish to be inside the ring when it closed. They did not all come to Ellen's own gate. Most went on into the town proper, to whatever door had a fire showing through the shutter-crack and a face behind it willing to open, and by the month's end there was hardly a house in Doire ghlas that did not have some part of a stranger's family sleeping on its floor, nor a house in Caol mhòr either, by what letters told her, the two towns between them having taken in, by a count nobody had troubled yet to make formal, some hundreds of souls who three months before had never set foot north of the western hills in their lives.

Ellen kept her school running through all of it, mornings at least, judging that the children who were left to her, her own town's children and now a growing scatter of the newcomers' besides, wanted the one ordinary thing in their days more than they wanted a holiday from it; and she found, teaching sums and letters to a room grown suddenly half again its size, that the new children learned quick and said little, and that the ones from Àth leathan in particular would not, for weeks, be drawn into games that ran too near the door, as though a door left standing open behind them at speed was a thing their bodies had not yet finished expecting.

It was from one of the mothers, a woman named Peigi Dòmhnallach who had taken the empty half of the net-mender's cottage two doors from Ellen's own, that she first heard the word used plainly, in the humans' tongue and then again in Dia-thìris as though the woman wanted to be sure of being understood in both: that the town they had left had been taken, and its pits, which had fed three generations of her own house, filled with water on the regiments' own order within a day of the fighting's end, so that there was, in the plainest sense a person could mean it, nothing left in Àth leathan for any of them to go back to even had they wished it. She said this without much weight in her voice, the way a person reports a fact they have said over often enough to themselves already that the saying of it aloud costs less than it once did, and Ellen, writing nothing down that evening, only listening, thought that this was itself a kind of telling, the flat voice as much a record as the words it carried.

The old man who had ridden the handcart the whole way, whose name Ellen learned only in the third week, when he judged himself sufficiently recovered in his own dignity to give it, was called Coinneach Frisealach, and had been forty years a hewer before his knees gave out and ten more a keeper of the pit-shed's own small library of tallies and wage-books, so that of all the household he alone had thought, in the handful of minutes the leaving allowed him, to bring away a single ledger under his coat rather than any clothing or food, on the reasoning, given to Ellen without apology, that clothing could be begged from any charitable house on the road but a hewer's forty years of wages, owed and part-paid and disputed, existed nowhere but in that one book, and a man who let a debt owed him go under the water along with the pit that owed it was a man choosing, in his own small way, to let the water win more than it had already won. He showed her the ledger once, dried by the fire and swollen at the spine from a wetting it had taken somewhere on the road, and asked her, in all seriousness, whether the schoolteacher thought a book of wages owed by a flooded pit to a hewer three months gone from the town that pit stood in was a book worth keeping any longer, or only a thing an old man carried because he had carried it too far already to set it down. Ellen told him she did not know, which was true, and that she would keep it for him regardless, which was also true, and put it on the shelf in her own front room beside the wrapped and waiting eleventh book, where it stayed the whole of that summer, two ledgers of two different kinds of debt standing side by side and neither of them yet called in.

## II. Of What the Room Did with Its Own Vote

Martha Greaves had sat in the chair at Caol mhòr the day the room voted nine to none that the Moot was no party to the quarrel and would feed any who came hungry, and she had known then, better than most of the room had troubled to know it, that a vote is a promise made to a future the room cannot yet see the shape of, and that the shape, when it comes, generally asks more of the promise than the promise-makers quite bargained for at the trestle. She was proved right of that within the year, and found, proved right, that she took no satisfaction whatever in the proving.

The granaries at Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas had been kept full since the summer before on the wool mills' own takings, against a want Martha had not named aloud even to Thomas Rook, who kept the books and asked no questions she did not care to answer. She opened them in earnest in the third week of an Giblean, the same week the first families reached her own town's gates, and found, doing the plain arithmetic of it at her own kitchen table by candlelight because she trusted no clerk's sum on this particular matter but her own, that what she had laid by would carry the towns' own people and a few hundred more through to the next harvest, provided the few hundred did not become a few thousand, and provided the harvest came in as it generally did.

She said as much to the room at the next sitting, standing in the cold over the net-lofts with the same ice on the same far window that had not properly gone since the winter she called the nine votes, and found the room readier than she had looked for to hear it. Peter Hale moved that the granaries be opened without further vote needed, the nine votes of the year before being, in his own words, a promise already made and now only being kept rather than a new thing the room had to decide over again, and the room agreed to that reading of it with less argument than Martha had braced herself for, though Cairstiona Munro, from the farm towns' bench, asked plainly what would be done if the harvest failed to come in as it generally did, and got no better answer than that the room would cross that ground when it came to it, which satisfied nobody, Martha least of all, but was the truest answer there was to give.

What she did not put before the room, because it was not the room's business to weigh and she had already weighed it herself in the small hours more than once, was the shape of the choice underneath the vote: that the council in the capital, whose regiments had emptied the western hills of the people now filling her own town's spare rooms, had sent nothing north to feed them and had forbidden nothing that the Moot chose to do about it either, the silence on both counts being, as she had come to understand silences of that particular kind, its own sort of answer. She wrote to Sìleas nic Rath at the Library that same week, less to ask anything of the Library, which she knew by then had no more power over the council's silence than the Moot did, than to have the fact set down somewhere outside her own kitchen table that the Moot had acted and the council had not, in case a day came when that difference wanted proving to someone who had not been there to see it for themselves.

Thomas Rook kept the register from the first week, a plain ledger bought for the purpose and ruled by his own hand into columns, name, town of origin, family, and a last column headed simply *taken in*, which he filled with whichever house or hall had found room for the family named on that line. He came to Martha near the end of an Cèitean with the ledger open at the last written page and his face doing the sum before his voice did.

"Three hundred and forty," he said. "As near as I can make it, and I'll not swear to the last ten, because a family that moves house twice in a fortnight, as more than a few have, is a family a ledger has trouble keeping honest count of."

"Three hundred and forty," Martha said, "out of two towns that between them held four thousand souls at the last count the head-due took."

"Aye."

"And the harvest not in yet."

"Aye," Thomas Rook said again, and closed the ledger over his thumb rather than let it fall shut on its own, the way a man closes a door he means to open again shortly rather than one he is done with, and Martha understood, watching him do it, that he had come to her not to be told what to do about the number, which neither of them could yet do anything about, but only to have another living person hear it said aloud before the writing of it went back into the dark of the ledger's covers where numbers lived until somebody needed them again.

She went down to the harbour that same evening, as she had taken to doing when the day's business would not let her sleep on it plainly, and stood a while watching the boats come in against a wind that had turned since noon, the fishing families of Caol mhòr going about the ordinary business of their own trade as though the town behind them had not doubled its trouble in the space of a season. A boy she knew, one of the harbourmaster's own, ran past her with a coil of rope over his shoulder and called out, in the way of a child who has heard a grown thing said at his own table and repeats it without yet weighing it, that his father said the Moot would beggar itself feeding half the western hills before the year was out and serve the council right if it did, since it was the council's own regiments had made the beggars in the first place. Martha did not correct him, a boy of ten owing no speaker of the Moot a careful answer, and she carried the sentence home with her all the same, turning it over the way she turned over most things said plainly by people who had not troubled to soften them for her hearing, and found, weighing it against her own kitchen-table arithmetic, that she could not honestly call it wrong, only early, the beggaring being a thing that had not yet happened and might yet be held off, granary by granary, harvest by harvest, so long as the towns that had voted the promise kept finding, each season, some new way to make good on it.

There was a second sitting of the room in an t-Iuchar, called at Peter Hale's own asking rather than by the calendar, at which the delegate of the eastern towns, a quiet man named Uilleam Friseal who had said little at the first sitting and less since, rose to ask whether the room had considered that feeding the west's own people indefinitely, without any word from the west's own towns as to how long the feeding was likely to be needed, was a promise the room had made without a floor under it, in the sense that a floor is a thing you can at least say how far down it lies. Martha, from the chair, answered him herself rather than let the room answer for her, saying only that she had asked herself the same question at her own kitchen table more nights than she cared to number, and had found no better answer to give the room than the one she gave herself, which was that a promise made without knowing its own floor was still a promise, and the not knowing was no excuse the room had ever offered itself before for breaking one that mattered less. Uilleam Friseal sat down at that without pressing further, and Cairstiona Munro, two benches along, said nothing at all, which Martha took, rightly or not, for the nearest thing to agreement the farm towns' bench was likely to give her that year.

## III. Of the School's New Trade

The school that Agnes Pike had built at Doire ghlas thirty years before, out of her own money and her own stubbornness against a Moot that had not, in that generation, thought a mill town's children wanted more schooling than their trade required, was given over in the last week of an Cèitean to whatever families still had no roof of their own, its four rooms and its long hall together holding, by Thomas Rook's count, near sixty souls by midsummer, the benches Ellen had taught sums on for a dozen years serving now, pushed together in twos and threes with a blanket over the gap, for beds.

She kept teaching in the one room the families had left her, mornings still, the room crowded now with children who slept twenty feet from where they learned their letters and seemed, on the whole, none the worse for the closeness of it; and in the evenings, when the lessons were done and the hall had settled into whatever a room full of displaced families settles into once the day's small business of eating and washing and quarrelling mildly over blankets has run its course, she took her notebook, the twelfth after the eleven she had already filled and offered the Library, which had answered, by Raghnall mac Mhuirich's own letter, with no promise of taking them up, and began, without quite deciding to begin it, to write down what the families told her.

It was not the same work as the eleven books had been. Those had been given slowly, over years, by old people with a whole life's telling saved up and offered a little at a time to a listener they had come to trust; this was given fast, in the first raw weeks of a loss too near to have found its proper shape yet, by people who had not asked to be listened to and in some cases did not want to be, and who told her, when they told her anything, not the long considered account an old woman gives at the end of her life but the sharp particular things a person cannot help saying because the mind will not put them down of its own accord: the exact word a neighbour had used, standing at his own door, when he chose to stay rather than come; the weight of a handcart's one wheel going soft in a rut two miles from anywhere; a hen someone had carried the whole way in a basket and would not now eat, keeping it instead as though the keeping of it were the last unbroken thing in the house.

Ellen wrote all of it down, in Dia-thìris as she had always written, and found that this book, unlike the eleven before it, grew a name of its own within the first month without her choosing one for it: the families themselves, hearing that the schoolteacher kept a book, began to call it, among themselves, the war-book, and to bring things to her for it the way a person brings a thing to be weighed, half in the hope of having it made lighter by the telling and half only because there was, in a hall crowded with strangers and grief both, nowhere else the thing could properly be set down.

Peigi Dòmhnallach's own contribution to it, given one evening in an t-Iuchar with her three children long asleep on the bench behind her, was the shortest and, Ellen thought afterward, the truest thing anyone gave her that whole summer: that she had left her own kitchen dresser standing, plates and all, because there had been no room on the cart for it and no time to choose what to save from what to leave, and that she thought of the dresser now, of all the things she might have thought of, more often than she thought of the house itself, because a house, once you have walked far enough away from it, becomes a thing your mind can hold whole and finished, but a dresser with the plates still on it stays, somehow, unfinished, a thing still happening somewhere without you.

Coinneach Frisealach gave her something for the war-book too, near the end of an t-Iuchar, once he had settled enough into the hall's own rhythm to think the giving worth his while: not an account of the leaving, which he would not be drawn on beyond what he had already told her of the ledger under his coat, but a description, exact to the number of steps, of the pit-shed at Muileann àrsaidh where Ruth Calder had once stood to read out the agreement the five towns signed, a building he was certain by then stood no longer, or stood flooded to the rafters if it stood at all, and which he wished, he said, set down somewhere in a book with his own name attached to the setting-down, on the reasoning that a pit-shed a man has stood inside forty years deserves one more accounting before it goes under, whatever becomes of the coal beneath it. Ellen wrote the shed down as he gave it, the bench along the north wall, the tally-board by the door, the smell of the lamp oil that never quite left the timbers, and thought, copying his exact steps into her own exact hand, that this was the nearest thing to an epitaph the man was ever likely to compose for a building, and that it would have to do, there being, as with so much that summer, no better one on offer from anywhere else.

Not every family that came to the hall gave the war-book anything at all, and Ellen made a point, from the first week, of asking nobody twice who had once turned the asking aside; there were households who ate at the long table and slept behind their own blanket and spoke to her only of the weather or the children's sums for the whole of their time under Agnes Pike's roof, and she let them, judging, as she had judged of the family at her own gate the first wet morning, that a book kept by a schoolteacher was a use for the telling and not a debt the telling owed.

## IV. Of the Name a Child Gave Her

It was a girl of no more than eight, one of the Muileann àrsaidh eleven who had left in the night before the ditch-bridge fell, who came to Ellen at the school in the last week of an t-Iuchar, after the evening's lessons and before the hall's evening business had properly begun, and asked her, without any of the preamble the older families generally gave their askings, whether the war-book had names in it.

"Some," Ellen said. "The names of the people who tell me things, when they want their own name kept with what they've said. Not always. Some would rather the telling stood alone."

"Would you put a name in it that I gave you," the girl said, "even if I can't tell you anything true to go with it? I don't know anything true. I only know the name."

Ellen set down her pen, because she had learned, in eleven years of taking down old women's tellings, that a question asked in that particular flat, careful way was not a question to be answered with the pen already moving. "Whose name is it?"

"Ruth Calder's," the girl said. "My mother knew her. Everyone in Muileann àrsaidh knew her, or knew of her, which my mother says is near the same thing in a town that size. Nobody's put her name anywhere since the spring. My mother says the open record has all the dead of the battle written down proper, every one, but Ruth Calder isn't dead, or isn't known to be, so she isn't in the dead ones' list, and she isn't in anything else either, because nothing else got written down about her at all, not since, and my mother says that's not right, a person going about not dead and not written down anywhere as if she'd never been a person in the first place."

Ellen looked at her a moment, and then picked the pen back up, because there was, in the end, no true reason not to, and turned to a clean page near the back of the book, the pages she kept for things given without a full telling behind them, single lines standing alone the way a stone stands alone in a field that has otherwise been cleared. "Tell me what your mother says, then," she said. "Not what you think I want. What she says."

"She says Ruth Calder drafted the agreement at the pit-shed five years back, the one that let the five towns work their own seams and sell to whoever'd buy honest coal from them. She was chosen to speak for the towns because nobody else wanted the work of writing the letters, and she wrote them anyway, every one, and the last one on Christmas Day after church, and nobody answered it. My mother says she was at Àth leathan the morning of the battle with a list in her hand, counting who'd come to fight, and that's the last anyone from Muileann àrsaidh saw of her or heard of her, and that's all. That's the whole of it. I don't know anything else, because there isn't anything else to know."

Ellen wrote it down exactly as the girl gave it, in the girl's own order, changing nothing, adding nothing, and did not write beneath it any of the things her own mind wanted to add, that the open record's silence on the matter was likely to be permanent, that a name once dropped from every list a kingdom keeps rarely finds its way back onto one, that she herself, in eleven years of this same work, had never yet taken down a telling that ended in anything so plainly unfinished as this one did. She closed the book instead on the girl's last line, *that's all, that's the whole of it, I don't know anything else, because there isn't anything else to know*, and thought, walking home along the lane past the school's lit windows with the hall's evening noise going up behind her, of Thomas Rook's ledger and its last column, *taken in*, and of how many things a town could be asked to take in that a single ruled column had no proper heading for at all.

She wrote nothing more in the war-book that night. The girl's line stood alone on its page, as she had meant it to stand, and Ellen, closing the cover over it, found she had no wish, that evening or any evening after, to be the one who decided what ought to be written beneath it.
