# Of Blàr Muileann ghlas

*DE 22*

> First and third regiments on the field. The enemy's dead not counted.
>
> — the open record, 10 an Dùbhlachd, DE 22

## I. Of the Field

The tenth of an Dùbhlachd came up grey and stayed grey, the kind of short northern day that seems to give its light back almost as soon as it has lent it, and by the middle of the morning below Muileann ghlas the frost had not lifted from the furrows so much as thinned, so that the ground under the first and third regiments' boots was neither hard nor soft but something between the two that took a print and held it. The mill town, three hundred souls and a green-slated roof or two among the thatch, stood a little apart from its own fields that day, its doors shut, its one bell not rung, as if the place had drawn itself in the way a snail draws in its horn at a footstep. Its river, the small one that turned the mill and gave the town half its name, ran high and brown with the autumn's rain, and made, all that morning and into the early afternoon, the only steady sound on the field besides the guns.

The open record, which is what this book must mostly follow here, since the rest is kept from it by the Library's own hand, gives the day in the manner it gives every day of that war: plainly, and without the years the north would spend telling it over. The first regiment held the ground nearest the river, the third the rising ground to its right, where the old byre stood; the column, which the record calls, when it calls it anything, those of no shire, held the line of a low bank and a stand of thorn along the field's northern edge, dug in as well as a fortnight's marching and two nights' frost had let them dig. They had come from the western hills the best part of two months before, some four hundred strong at their setting out, fewer by the day they reached the fields at Muileann ghlas, for the road south-east through the middle of the island had cost them men to sickness and to loss of heart in about equal measure, and had cost them, too, whatever hope any of them had carried that the towns they passed would rise and join them. Muileann mhin had sold them bread. Àth bheag had reported them, honestly and at length, to the first clerk of the Board its maor could find. No town on the whole of that road had done more than watch them go by and write down what it saw.

The regiments opened the day at first light with the guns, four pieces the third regiment had brought down from the capital on the coal line and hauled the last two miles by hand through ground too soft for the horses, and the open record gives their number and not their effect, which is the record's way with most things that would otherwise need a harder word than it cares to spend. What is told in the north, from those who stood near enough to that field to have some right to speak of it, is that the guns found the thorn stand within the first half-hour and did not need to find it twice; that the column's line broke there first, for a stand of thorn on a low bank is no cover at all against a gun that has found its range, whatever the will of the men behind it, and that what held after that held along the bank, in the frozen mud, hand to hand and at close range, through most of the short forenoon. The open record says only that the field was carried by early afternoon, which is a sentence long enough to hold a great deal of a morning and says nothing of what it holds.

Three-and-twenty of the kingdom's dead were named that week in the register the regiments keep for their own, a list drawn up by a corporal of the third regiment at a trestle table in the mill itself, with the miller's own ledger turned backward and his account of grain crossed through to make room for it, since no other paper could be found in the town that morning and the corporal, a careful man by the look of his list, would not wait for one to be sent for. Twenty-three names, each with a town beside it and, where it was known, an age, written in a hand that grew smaller as the page filled and the light went, so that the last few names on it are barely legible at all, as if the man writing them had begun, by the end, to write mostly by feel. The column's dead the open record does not count. Some tried to count them: the regiments' own burial parties, working through what light the short day still had and then by lantern into the evening, are known from a single surviving requisition to have asked the quartermaster for more lime than twenty-three graves would need, a good deal more, and that requisition, filed and answered and forgotten in the ordinary way of requisitions, is the nearest thing the open record holds to a number for the column's own loss. No number stands in its place. Only the shape one leaves, once it has been taken out of a record and something quieter put where it stood.

The mill changed hands twice before noon, by the surgeon's later account, once when the column drove out a picket the first regiment had set in it before dawn and once when the first regiment took it back with the guns already turned on the byre beyond, and each time the miller's wife, who would not leave her own kitchen for either army, is remembered by the surgeon to have gone on quietly moving her pots and her flour sacks out of whatever corner the fighting had lately occupied and into whatever corner it had lately left, as if the day were no more than an unusually thorough cleaning forced on her by two sets of careless lodgers. She fed the surgeon a bowl of broth some time in the afternoon, without being asked and without, so far as he could tell, distinguishing in her own mind between the man who ate it and the uniform he wore, and this too he set down, years after, among the handful of things about that day he found himself still able to speak of plainly.

The colonel of the first regiment, who is not named twice in the open record and whose own report of the day survives only in the quartermaster's copy of it, wrote that the column had held the bank longer than any force he had faced in two years of the war, and set this down for one reason only, that a commander who does not tell his own council the truth of what he found on a field does that council no service at all; praise the record was never made to give, and he gave none. It is one of perhaps three sentences in the whole open record of the war that reads as though a man wrote it for himself and not for the file it was put into, and this book has chosen, of the little that record allows it, to keep this one.

This was not the first battle fought below Muileann ghlas. An old man of the town, brought before the regiments' officers that evening to answer for a store of grain they wanted and he was reluctant to give up, told them, when he had given it up and was let go, that his grandmother's grandmother had shown him, as a boy, the low place in the same field where men of the Brothers' War had fought two centuries and more before, and that the town still called it, in the old way, the taken ground, for a captain of that older war had been taken alive there rather than let escape across the river, which ran the same course then as now. The officer who heard this wrote it down, more from a wish to have something in his own notebook besides the day's dead than from any use he could see in it, and it is by that accident, and no better reason, that the two battles of Muileann ghlas are set beside each other in this book at all. The island's fields do not choose to be fought over twice by design. They are simply not so many, in the end, as its history is long.

By the day's short dusk the guns had stopped, and what remained of the fighting had gone to ground in the mill and the two barns nearest it, where perhaps sixty of the column held out past dark rather than come out into a field the regiments now commanded on every side. The first regiment's colonel, whose name the open record gives once and not again, sent in word before full dark that any man who came out unarmed would be marched, not shot, to the capital's gaol, and had the word carried by a Tuathach prisoner already taken, under a white cloth tied to a byre-fork, since no one else on the field that evening could be found who both peoples would trust to carry it and bring back an honest answer. The sixty came out a little after full dark, in ones and twos at first and then in a body, into a cold that by then had begun to bite in earnest, and were counted, and searched, and marched under guard to the mill floor for the night, where the regiments' own surgeon, a Dia-thìreach of Cathair dhearg two years out of his training and new enough to the work that his hands, by his own account written years after, would not stop shaking until well past midnight, did what could be done for wounded of both sides by the light of two lanterns and a banked fire, and made no distinction between them that the open record, at least, thought worth setting down.

By the day's end, when the ground had gone from grey to black and the two lanterns in the mill were the only light for a mile in any direction, the surgeon's tally, kept on the back of the same requisition that had asked for the lime, stood at three-and-twenty of the regiments buried or dying and near ninety of the column's wounded brought in, some by their own side before the field was carried and some, after, by the regiments' own burial parties, who are recorded, in a note the surgeon thought worth adding though no one had asked him to, as having carried in the column's hurt with no more roughness than they used on their own, which he set down, he wrote, because he had gone into that day expecting otherwise and had been glad, once, to be wrong.

## II. Of the Tellings

This much the north keeps of that day and gives it, as it gives most of its history, from mouth to hearth to grandchild, a telling handed on at the turn of the year when the fire is banked and the youngest in the house has been let stay up past its hour for once, the teller beginning, as often as not, with some such plain word as *my sister stood on that ground*, which is a kind of proof the open record cannot offer and does not try to.

What is told in the north of that day begins, in nearly every version this book has been able to gather, with the frost before it begins with the guns, for it was the cold, the tellers say, that the column felt first and longest, two nights of it in the open with fires kept low or not kept at all for fear of what a fire on that flat ground would show the regiments below. A woman of Cnoc thais, who was a girl of sixteen on that column and carried a message twice between the bank and the byre before the guns opened, has told it more than once since to her own people, and it has been taken down, in the north's own way, by more than one hand since: that the last night before the battle the column sang, low and by turns, company after company. It may have served them for courage, she allows, though that was never her own reason for it: a body that is singing cannot easily be heard to be afraid, she says, and there were children on that field, brought by mothers and fathers who had nowhere else on the whole island to leave them, and the singing was as much for the children's sake as for the men's.

The tellings give the column's leader a name the open record never once sets down: Owen Barrow, a man of the west hills who is said, in the tellings, to have walked the whole line himself in the last hour before first light, speaking to every company by name and by name to a good number of the men and women in each of them, and to have said, to more than one teller's memory, much the same short thing each time, that the hills were behind them now and could not be walked back to before the guns found them on open ground either way, and that a man who has come this far owes it to himself to be found, if he is to be found at all, facing the way he came and not the other. Whether he said any such thing, or said it in those words, this book cannot know and does not pretend to; what it can say is that the saying is told the same way in three towns that have, so far as the record shows, had no occasion to agree it between them, which is as much as the north's own memory usually offers by way of proof, and more than it often troubles to give.

Of the fighting the tellings are, in their way, both more and less than the open record. They are less because no teller who was in the thorn stand when the guns found it has left more than a handful of words about it at all; the girl of Cnoc thais, asked directly, by her own mother among others, what it had been like, answered only that it had been loud, and then quiet, and that she did not care to say more; and that this is the whole of her answer every time she has been asked it since is a kind of telling in its own right, though not one that fits easily into a book. They are more because they keep, where the open record does not, the names of those who did not come back from the bank and the thorn: not all of them, for the fighting scattered families as well as companies and not every name found its way home to be kept. A good many did, kept in the way the north keeps its dead, spoken at firesides and at the turn of the year, without a stone to hold them and without, until this book, any page in the kingdom's own record to be spoken in.

It is told, too, of the byres, where the sixty held out past dark, that among them was a boy of no more than fourteen who had carried water to the thorn stand all that morning rather than carry arms, being thought too young for arms even by a column that had by then grown short of grown men to bear them, and that when the white cloth went up on the byre-fork it was the boy who asked, of the man beside him, whether the marching to the capital's gaol that the colonel's word had promised them was truly better than staying where they were, and was told, by the same man, that a boy who lives to ask a foolish question in a gaol has done better for himself than one who dies asking a wise one in a field, which the tellers offer, more often than not, without further comment, as if the answer needed none. The boy is not named in any telling this book has found. Whether he lived out that winter in the capital's gaol, or after it, this book cannot say, and no teller in the north has claimed to know.

## III. Of the Roads Home

Word of the field reached the western hills before the wounded did, carried by men who had broken from the fighting early enough to run and had run without stopping, by the tellers' own account, for the better part of two days on tracks the regiments did not know to watch. It found, in the hills, not an army so much as what an army leaves behind it when the greater part of it has already marched away to be broken elsewhere: old men and women who had kept the last of the flocks through two summers of war, a scatter of the sick and the maimed too far gone to walk south with the column in the autumn, and here and there a handful still under arms, holding a pass or a spring that no one on either side had thought worth fighting over directly, because it fed a dozen families and not a regiment.

To these the news came, in every telling this book has gathered, without argument attached to it. No one is remembered to have proposed holding the hills a season longer, or waiting for a spring that might bring the column's survivors back to them, or sending word south to ask what terms, if any, the regiments would give. What is remembered is simpler and, in its way, harder to set down plainly: that within a matter of days, in ones and twos and small parties, without much speech between them and none at all, so far as any telling says, with the regiments, the last of the hills' people came down out of the high ground and along the Àth leathan road to where the seventh regiment had kept its posts through two summers of a war it had spent mostly waiting, watching a road that had, until that week, carried very little for it to watch.

The seventh regiment's own return for those days, which survives in full where a great deal else of the war's paperwork does not, is a document of a particular kind: a list, kept post by post along the road, of those who came down, their town where it could be got from them, and the arms, if any, they carried and surrendered, drawn up by the post's own sergeant in a hand a good deal steadier than the corporal's list at Muileann ghlas, for the seventh regiment's war had been, whatever else it had been, a long watch on a quiet road rather than a morning of guns and dead by a river, and the men who kept it had time, that week, to do the counting properly. The weapons came in a strange mixture even for a war that had, by its last year, armed itself from whatever could be found: fowling pieces older than any man then living, two or three rifles of a pattern the regiment's own quartermaster did not recognise and noted as such, pikes made in the first months of the trouble from scythe blades and ash poles, and, from more than one post's list, nothing at all, for a good number of those who came down that week had carried no arms in the first place and had followed the column, or stayed in the hills that kept it fed, without ever once being asked to fight.

Among those who came down to the northernmost post on the second of those days was a man of Cnoc thais, past sixty by his own reckoning and lame in one leg from an old fall down a shaft years before the war, who had carried no weapon at all and told the sergeant so before he was asked, and who had with him, on a rope halter, the one cow his family had kept through two summers of soldiers moving over their ground, on the plain reasoning, as he gave it to the sergeant and the sergeant set down more or less word for word, that a man might be let go home with an empty rope and starve there just as surely as he might be shot for coming down armed, and that he had thought it worth the asking either way. The sergeant let him through with the cow, noted the exchange in full, being, as his list shows throughout, a man who set down more than his orders strictly required of him, and added nothing further, though this book, reading his hand now, finds in that one unnecessary sentence more of the war's true cost, and more of its end, than in a page of the guns' account from Muileann ghlas.

They were disarmed at the posts, searched, and sent home rather than to the capital's gaol, as the sixty from the byres had been, to whatever the hills' towns still had standing for them, which in more than one case, Muileann àrsaidh chief among them, was not a great deal. The seventh regiment's sergeant at the northernmost post, whose own list this book has followed more than any other for the plainness of its hand, noted at the foot of his last page, after the final name was entered and the last weapon counted, that no more had come down the road since noon of that day, and that he took this to mean the counting was done. It is the whole of his comment on the matter, and it is, so far as the open record allows this book to say, very nearly the last entry made anywhere on the island in the name of An Cogadh Fada, the Long War, which had begun on a bitter morning at Àth leathan nearly two years before, and ended, as such things end, with a sergeant deciding, on the evidence of an empty road, that there was nothing left for him to count.

## IV. Of Somhairle After

Somhairle mac Lachlainn was not himself at Muileann ghlas that day. He had commanded the regiments from the capital through the better part of that autumn, by then, at a table in a room the Board had given over to him near the coal line's own offices, and the open record's silence on his own movements through those two years, a silence this book has met before and will meet again, holds here as everywhere else: what he did in the rooms where the war was made this book cannot tell, because the record of it went into the sealed archive with the rest, and the man himself, in the years since, has kept to it as closely as the Library does.

He would say, in the one account of that day this book has found written down, given since the peace, that he had heard the guns were done at Muileann ghlas from a clerk who came into his room without knocking, an hour or so past the short day's dusk, and that his first thought on hearing it, before any other, had been for the coal line's timetable, whether the wounded could be got up to the capital that same night or would have to wait for morning, and that it was some while later, sitting alone in that room with the lamp burned low, before he let himself think of the day in any other terms at all. This is the whole of what he is remembered to have said of it, told to a councillor since, who set it down more or less as he heard it, and it is offered here for one reason: it is the nearest this book can come to Somhairle's own voice on the one day of the war that ended it, and because a man who spent two years commanding a war from a room he never once, by his own account, thought to leave, is entitled to be heard, at least, on what it was he thought about when word came that the room's work was done.

He was given a seat on the council the following year, the first soldier so honoured since the Severance, and he sat in it, by every account this book has, without once speaking of Muileann ghlas from that chair, nor of Àth leathan before it, nor of any of the two years between. What he did instead, in the first weeks of that peace, by his own clerk's later account, was to have the coal line's schedule for the wounded checked twice against the surgeons' own returns, to be certain that no man carried north that winter had been carried on a train meant for something else. It is a small thing to be remembered for, out of two years of war; it is the thing this book has found, and a chronicle that has so little else of the man cannot afford to leave it out.

So ended An Cogadh Fada, the Long War, twenty months from the bitter morning at Àth leathan to the sergeant's empty road, and it left behind it, standing to this day, nine regiments and four fleets, each carrying still the name of the town where it was first raised, whether or not that town had cause, before the war, to think of itself as a soldiering place at all. It left behind, too, a mill at Muileann ghlas with a floor that had held both armies' wounded in one night and a miller's wife who is not remembered to have spoken of it again to any stranger who asked; a corporal's list, twenty-three names long, kept afterward at the capital and copied onto a stone the following year; and a requisition for lime that outlived, by the accident of a filing clerk's carelessness, every grander paper the war produced, and says, to anyone who reads it rightly, more than most of them do.

At Caol mhòr, in the days after, where word of the field reached the harbour before it reached most of the north by any road, an old Tuathach standing at the fish quay with the news new in his ears is remembered, by more than one who was there, to have said, to no one in particular and to everyone near enough to hear him, that they had got long-warred, chaidh am fadachadh, meaning it, the tellers agree, as a joke against his own people and no one else's, for a fadachadh is what the north calls the waiting a fisherman does for a boat that is not coming back, drawn out past all sense until the waiting becomes the thing remembered, longer than whatever it was waiting for. He said it, by every account, as a man says a thing he has said before about smaller griefs and finds, to his own surprise, still fits a larger one. Those near him on the quay that day are not remembered to have laughed, then or after, though the saying has been carried the length of the north since, and is said still, of any hope held too long past the day it was worth holding, always as a jest, and never, in this book's hearing, by anyone who laughed while they said it.
