# Of the Five Towers

*DE 7*

## I. Of the Carts to Muileann bheag

Two of the five lights had been turned by the end of the year before. Taigh-solais Baile ìseal on the eastern cape had burned a dubhan lamp in its yard for a hundred nights before it burned one in its tower, and Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg on the south coast had let its coal flame go to the altar in the high summer, in a lantern carried by its keeper. That left three: one on the northern point, one on the north-eastern cape, and one on the headland at Baile chrom in the far north-west, which was farther from the capital than any work the kingdom kept.

Peigi nic Ascaill, engineer to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, went north in am Faoilleach with two of the board's fitters, a box of drawings and a letter under the board's seal. The letter said that Taigh-solais Muileann bheag was to be turned to dubhan before the spring gales. It did not say where the dubhan was to come from.

The other lights were fed from the south. The tanks for Baile ìseal and Tobar dhearg had gone out of Seann Skell on the south coast by boat, from the shipmasters' quay below the works, and the barrels that followed them went the same way. Muileann bheag stood on the north coast of the Tuathaich country, and a boat out of the southern Seann Skell must go round half the island to reach it. In am Faoilleach no master on that coast would take a laden hull round the western capes for any board's letter.

So the fuel for Muileann bheag came by cart. Peigi bought it at Caol mhòr, in the market by the harbour, where the black cake had been sold off the pedlars' barrows since the first year of the fuel and was now sold from a shed with a slate roof and a Tuathach behind a counter. She asked him where it was made. He said, in the Dia-thìrich tongue and politely, that it was made in the north. She asked where in the north. He said in the other tongue something to the man beside him, and the man laughed, and then he said to her again, in her own, that it was made in the north, and would she be wanting it in barrels or in the sack.

She took it in barrels. Twelve carts went out along the coast road on a grey morning with the wind off the sea, drawn by the small hill horses of the north, and a Tuathach walked at the head of each horse. The road ran some fifteen miles, low along the shore through the mill country, past the mill-dams frozen at their edges and the white fields, and it took the carts the whole of a short day. Peigi rode on the last cart with her feet on a barrel. The board's two fitters, who had never been north of the capital, sat together on the one in front and looked at the country as if it might bite.

The carters did not look at it at all. They talked among themselves in the humans' tongue, of a boat come in at Caol mhòr with her mast sprung and of the price of meal, and one of them sang. When the cart ahead stuck in a rut at a burn-crossing they all came back to it without a word from anyone, and put their shoulders to the wheels, and it came out. Peigi wrote in her notebook that night: *Carters of Caol mhòr, twelve, paid at the rate of the town. Good men.* Then she struck out the last two words, because the board did not want its engineer's opinions of carters, and wrote *Sound* over them.

Taigh-solais Muileann bheag stood on its point above a little mill town of grey houses, with the sea on three sides of it. It was the fourth of the five towers to be raised, and the first to burn coal, and under its foot the ash of an older shore-fire went down three yards into the red earth. The fire on that point was older than the humans and older than the kingdom. Its keeper that winter was a Dia-thìreach of the south named Calum Bàn, who had kept the light for nineteen years under the royal warrant and in all that time had been to church in Muileann bheag twice, at two funerals, and had stood at the back.

He met Peigi at the tower door with his hands folded under his arms against the cold. He looked at the carts and the carters, and at the board's two fitters, and last at her.

"They told me it would come by sea," he said.

"No boat would bring it."

"No," he agreed. "They wouldn't." He stood aside to let her in. "The coal came by sea. Every sack for nineteen years."

The work took nine days. The old coal-grate came out of the lamp-room in pieces, the board's men swearing at the bolts, which had rusted to their seats in the salt air; the tank went up the stair in the carters' arms, for the stair at Muileann bheag was wide and the tank was the small pattern, and two of the carters stayed on for the lifting and were paid for it. The burner was Peigi's own, the one she had drawn after the hundred nights at Baile ìseal, with a wider throat to it and a screw to clear the jet without putting out the flame. She fitted it herself. The board's men watched her, and Calum Bàn watched the board's men.

On the ninth evening, which was {{date:VI-0016}}, she lit it. The flame came up white and steady and threw its light out over the northern sea, where there was nothing to see but the dark water and, far out, the wall of grey that lay on the sea to the north in every weather. Calum Bàn stood at the glass and looked at it a long time.

"It's brighter," he said at last.

"Yes."

"It stinks."

"Yes. Less, in the tower. You'll not notice it in a month."

He did not answer that. In the morning he showed her the coal-store at the tower's foot, three-quarters empty, the last of it gleaming dully in the cold, and asked her what he was to do with it. She said she would write to the board. He said he would keep it, then, until the board wrote back, and locked the door on it, and put the key on the nail inside his own door where the other keys were.

The carts went back to Caol mhòr empty. Of the five lights, Taigh-solais Muileann bheag alone was fed thereafter by the road and not by the sea from Seann Skell, and its barrels came along the coast in Tuathaich carts from Tuathaich stores, and were paid for in the kingdom's coin at the market price, which that winter was a little under the price in the south.

## II. Of the Crane from Seann Tarr

The north-eastern cape was a harder matter.

Taigh-solais Àth dhearg stood on the high ground over the sea-passage in toward Seann Tarr, a stone tower raised by the townsmen of Àth dhearg in An Aois Choigreach and paid for, as the keepers still said, stone by stone. Its keeper, Iseabail Ruadh, had the light from her father, and kept on the shelf of the lamp-room the old daybook of Tormod mac Pheadair, who had written down every ship he saw from that gallery in the years when ships still came, and she would show it to visitors who asked. Peigi asked. The book was bound in sealskin gone black with handling. The last ships in it were a long time ago.

The tank for Àth dhearg had come round by sea from the southern Seann Skell and lay on the quay at Seann Tarr under a tarpaulin, the large pattern, for the light on the north-eastern cape was the strongest of the five. It came up the coast road to the tower on a dray behind four horses, and at the tower door it stopped. The stair of Taigh-solais Àth dhearg had been built by masons of the town who had built nothing but houses before it, and they had built it tight and turning, like the stair of a house. A man with a sack of coal on his shoulder went up it sideways. The tank would not go up it at all.

Peigi measured the stair. The board's men measured the tank. Then they measured the stair again, because they did not like the first answer.

"We cut the tank," said the elder of the board's men. "Two halves. Seam it up at the top."

"And it leaks at the seam into a lamp-room with a naked flame in it," said Peigi. "No."

"Then we cut the stair."

"Then the tower falls down," said Iseabail Ruadh from the doorway, "and I'll not be in it."

They stood about the dray in a wind that came straight off the sea with sleet in it. It was Iseabail who said, in the end, that the rope-walk at Seann Tarr had a crane for lifting the hemp-bales off the boats to the loft, and that the rope-makers owed the light a good turn, since their boats steered home by it every night of the winter.

So it was done that way, and it took a month more than the others.

The rope-makers of Seann Tarr were a guild of their own, the last that the old custom had chartered, and their master was a heavy slow man who came up to the tower to look at the thing before he promised anything. He walked twice round the tower, and looked up at the gallery, and looked at the tank on its dray, and said the crane could do it if the ground held. Then he went home and said nothing more for a week. At the end of the week the crane came.

It came up the three and a half miles of the coast road from Seann Tarr in pieces, the jib on one wain, the mast on another, the drum and the iron on a third, with half the rope-walk walking beside it. The weather had turned, as it turns there in am Màrt, from sleet to a hard bright wind, and Àth dhearg came out on the road to watch it pass, the children running ahead and the town's priest of Na Seann Spioradan standing at the gate of his house with his hands behind him. It took them two days to raise the mast on the rock below the tower and stay it with cables to rings the masons let into the stone. The master of the rope-walk would not let the board's men touch the cables. He said they were the rope-walk's cables and the rope-walk's knots and if they parted it would be the rope-walk's shame.

On the day of the lift the tank went up the outside of the tower in the slings, slowly, turning a little on its rope, with the sea behind it very blue and the gulls going round it as if it were a fish. Peigi stood on the gallery with Iseabail Ruadh. The board's men had taken out the great window of the lamp-room, frame and all, and it leaned against the parapet in its sacking. When the tank came level with the gallery rail it swung in the wind, out and back, and every man on the rock below leaned with it. Then the rope-makers on the drum let it down a hand's breadth at a time, and Peigi and the board's men caught it with their hands and walked it in over the sill.

Nobody cheered. The master of the rope-walk looked up at the gallery, and Peigi lifted her hand to him, and he nodded and turned to see to his cables.

The window went back in its frame; the glaziers of Àth dhearg sealed it; the burner was set; and on {{date:VI-0017}}, a month after the day she had meant to, Peigi lit the light on the north-eastern cape. Its beam went out over the passage, and at Seann Tarr that night, it is said, the boats came in by it as they always had, and nobody on the quay remarked that it was a different fire.

Iseabail Ruadh wrote that night in her own daybook, below the last line of her father's, that the light was turned to dubhan on this day, the tank being raised by the crane of the rope-walk at Seann Tarr. She named the rope-walk, and the board's engineer. Peigi read it over her shoulder and did not say anything.

The rope-makers would take no hire for the crane. The master sent word after it that the light owed them nothing, only let it burn.

That night at the inn at Àth dhearg Peigi wrote her letter to the board. Four of the five lights now burned dubhan. There remained Baile chrom. It was the farthest of the five, some twenty-five miles west of Caol mhòr on the headland at the edge of the Tuathaich country, and the board's fitters were wanted in the south, where the lamp-houses of the capital were putting in dubhan burners faster than there were men to fit them. The board had two fitters who could be spared, and they were the two she had with her, and they had been away from home since the new year and said so every evening.

She wrote to the board that she could not do Baile chrom before the autumn gales with the men she had. Then she took a second sheet and wrote, in her own name, to Tional nan Tuathach at Caol mhòr.

She knew no one at Caol mhòr, and one Tuathach fitter by name. Three years before, at the shipmasters' works at Seann Skell, there had been a man among the Tuathaich hands at the retorts, a lean man with his sleeves rolled and a burn on his wrist, who had shown her how to wrap a tap so that it did not drip and had chalked the lights' mark on her twenty barrels. He was of Caol mhòr, and he had gone home the winter after, as most of the northern hands went, when the rents of the south ate their pay.

She wrote: *To the speaker of Tional nan Tuathach at Caol mhòr, or to whoever keeps his letters.* Then, being tired, she crossed out the assembly's name, which she was not sure she had spelled as they spelled it, and left *the speaker at Caol mhòr*. She asked for four fitters of the town to turn the light at Baile chrom, the drawings and the burner to be sent them, the tank and fuel to come by sea. If a man named Ashby could be found, she would be glad of him. Who would pay them she did not say, because she did not know.

## III. Of a Week of Rain at Baile chrom

The letter came to Caol mhòr in an Giblean and lay on the speaker's table for a fortnight, because Daniel Frayne was ill that spring and read his letters slowly. When he had read it he sent for Joe Ashby. Frayne had been speaker of Tional nan Tuathach for more than twenty years, and he was past seventy.

Joe was fitting a winch on a herring boat at the harbour wall when the boy came for him, and he finished the winch first. Then he went up to the assembly's house in his working clothes and stood in the speaker's room with his cap in his hand while the old man read the letter out to him in the humans' tongue, turning it into that tongue as he went, word by word, with his finger on the line.

"She asks for you by name," said Daniel Frayne. "Do you know her?"

"At the works in the south," said Joe. "She bought barrels off us. I marked them for her."

"Did you speak to her?"

"About taps."

The old man looked at him over the letter for some time. He had a long face, grey and folded, and he breathed as if the room were short of air. "The board has no one to send," he said. "That's what this says. And it says nothing about pay." He put the letter down. "We'll pay you, then. The assembly will. Four men. Choose them yourself."

So in the middle of an t-Ògmhios four fitters of Caol mhòr went west along the coast road with their tools on a hand-cart and their food in a sack: Joe Ashby, and Will Tanner, who had worked in the Caol mhòr yards since he was a boy, and Ned Fowler and his cousin Abel, who had both been at the retorts in the south and come home. They walked the twenty-five miles in two days, sleeping the one night in a byre at a farm whose people gave them milk and would take nothing for it, and it began to rain on the morning they set out and did not stop until the work was done.

It was the rain of the far north-west in summer, which does not fall so much as stand in the air and soak through everything that stands in it. It came in off the open sea to the west, where there is nothing between the headland and the edge of sight, and it hid the sea, and the fields, and the road ahead. They came into Baile chrom at evening on the second day wet to the skin, and walked down its crooked street, which bends along the shore with the land, and the people in the doors looked at them and nodded, and a woman called to Will Tanner by name.

The boat from Seann Skell had come in two days before them and lay in the little bay below the headland, where there was a landing of stones but no quay. The tank lay on the landing under a sail, and the barrels were stacked beside it under another. The shipmasters' men who had brought her round had gone into the town to drink and had not come back. Joe went down and lifted the corner of the sail and looked at the tank, and put his hand on it, and came back up the path.

"Small pattern," he said. "It'll go up."

The keeper of Taigh-solais Baile chrom was a Dia-thìreach named Uilleam mac Dhòmhnaill, a spare man with a clipped grey beard, the last of a line of Dia-thìrich keepers who had kept that tower under royal warrant since the first years after An Dealachadh, a warrant that bade them report on the land as well as on the sea. In eleven years at Baile chrom he had come to know every family in the town by sight and some by name; he went to the town's houses when he was sent for and not otherwise, and the town left him to his tower.

He met the four of them at the tower door with a lamp in his hand, though it was not yet dark, because the rain made the evening dark. He looked at their wet coats and their hand-cart and the tools on it.

"You're the fitters," he said, in the Dia-thìrich tongue.

"We are," said Joe, in the same.

"From the board?"

"From Caol mhòr."

The keeper considered that, and then he stood aside. "The coal-grate's to come out," he said. "I've done what I could with the bolts. You'll want to eat first."

They ate in his kitchen at the tower's foot, bread and a piece of mutton, with the rain on the window. He did not eat with them. He sat at the end of the table with his daybook and wrote, and when Abel Fowler asked him what he was writing he said the weather.

They began in the morning. The grate came out in four days, piece by piece, down the stair on their shoulders, iron that had stood in the fire for more than a century and was burnt and scaled and heavier than it looked. The tank came up from the landing on the hand-cart with four men of the town pushing behind, and up the stair in the arms of all eight, turning at every turn, and Joe going first and backwards with his hands on the lip of it and his eyes on the next step. It went up. They set it on its bed and bolted it down and ran the pipe.

All that week the rain came in off the sea. It came in under the door of the lamp-room and down the inside of the glass where the old putty had shrunk, and Will Tanner spent a day raking the glazing out and setting new, swearing at the putty, which would not take on the wet stone. The coal-store at the tower's foot flooded to the ankle. The keeper came up the stair twice a day to see how they went on and went down again without a word. In the afternoons, when the light was worst, they worked by the lamp he had carried up the first night, and the smoke of it hung in the lamp-room with the smell of wet wool and oil.

On the fifth day the burner jammed. It was Peigi nic Ascaill's burner, sent round in the boat in a box of its own, with the drawings in a tin case beside it and a note on the drawings in a quick small hand: *The throat is wider than the old pattern. Do not trim it. Clear the jet with the screw, not a wire.* Something had been bent in the box on the voyage round the western capes, and the screw would not turn. Joe took the burner apart on the lamp-room floor on a piece of sacking and laid the parts out in rows. He sat over them most of the afternoon while the others worked about him. Then he took the bent part down to the town, where there was a smith who shod the horses and mended the boats' ironwork, and the two of them straightened it on the anvil with the door shut against the rain. The smith did not ask what it was for. Joe paid him out of his own pocket.

On the evening of {{date:VI-0018}}, which was the eve of the longest day, the burner was set and the tank was full and the glazing held. The rain still fell, thinner now, and in the west under the cloud there was a strip of yellow light over the sea.

The keeper came up the stair with his daybook under his arm. The four fitters stood back from the lamp. There was no one else in the lamp-room, and no one on the headland below but a few children of the town who had come up to watch the tower and stood in the wet grass.

"Who lights it?" said Uilleam mac Dhòmhnaill.

The others looked at Joe. Joe looked at the keeper. "It's your light," he said.

The keeper hesitated. Then he put down his daybook on the sill and took the taper Joe gave him and lit the flame. It came up blue and then white, and spread, and steadied, and the great lens took it and threw it out west over the sea in a long beam that the rain caught and showed, so that for a mile out the drops were lit like sparks. On the headland below the children shouted.

The keeper watched it a while. Then he picked up his daybook and went down the stair, and they heard his door close at the bottom.

In the morning, before they left, he gave them breakfast again, and shook each man's hand at the door, and said the work was good. He said it in the humans' tongue, which none of them had heard him speak all week, and he said it badly, and they thanked him in his own.

They walked home to Caol mhòr in two more days of rain. At the assembly's house the clerk paid them from the chest of Tional nan Tuathach for nine days' work and four days' walking, at the rate of the town, and entered it in the assembly's book: *To four fitters of this town, for the turning of the light at Baile chrom*, and their four names under it, and the sum.

## IV. Of the Paper at the Custom-house

The keeper's report reached the board at the end of the summer. Every keeper of the five lights sent one at each quarter-day, telling in a fair round hand what he had seen on the sea and on the land. The board copied the part that concerned the turning of the light and sent it round the kingdom to be pinned up where such things were pinned, that the shires might know their lights were turned. One copy came to Caol mhòr, because Baile chrom lay in the Tuathaich country, and the maor pinned it on the board outside the custom-house by the harbour, beside the notice of the fish-prices and the harbour dues.

Joe Ashby read it on his way home from the harbour, on an evening of an Lùnastal with the sun still high.

Will Tanner had seen it that morning and told him, and Joe had walked past the custom-house three times that day without looking. The fourth time he stopped.

It was a short paper. It said that Taigh-solais Baile chrom was turned to dubhan on the twentieth of an t-Ògmhios. It said the tank had come by sea from Seann Skell and was of the small pattern, and the burner of the new pattern drawn by the board's engineer. It said the coal-grate was taken out and the glazing of the lamp-room renewed. It said the light burned well and was seen by the boats. It said the town gave no trouble. It said the work was done by fitters from Caol mhòr.

Joe read it twice. Then he read the fish-prices beside it, and the harbour dues, and walked home.

His name was not in it. None of their names were. He had known it would not be; he had seen keepers' reports before, pinned on that same board, and they did not name fitters, or carters, or the men who carried things up stairs. Iseabail Ruadh's daybook at Àth dhearg named the rope-walk; he had not seen it, and would not have known it if he had. What he knew was that a Dia-thìreach keeper had stood beside him on the eve of midsummer and had lit the light because Joe gave him the taper, and had gone down his own stair and written *fitters from Caol mhòr*, and that the words were true.

He did not speak of it that night. His sister, with whom he lodged, said afterwards that he sat a long time at the table with a cup he did not drink, and then went out, and she heard him in the shed behind the house, at the bench, sharpening every tool he owned.

The Tuathaich of Caol mhòr have a way of telling it, when they tell it at all, which is short. They say that the Baile chrom light was turned by four men of the town, and they name them, and they say that the keeper's report named the town. Some say it as a grievance and some as a joke. Will Tanner says still that it was the only time Caol mhòr had been in a report of the kingdom's for anything but fish.

A week after, there was a letter for Joe at the assembly's house. It came from the southern Seann Skell and it was written in the same quick small hand as the note on the drawings.

Peigi nic Ascaill had left the board's service at the end of the summer, the letter said, and taken service with the shipmasters' works at Seann Skell as their master-fitter. The works were building a second range of retorts and wanted fitters through the winter, and the shipmasters paid better than the board and a great deal better than the assembly at Caol mhòr. She had had the Baile chrom report and knew who had done the work, for the assembly's clerk had written to the board for the fitters' pay to be made good and had named all four. She would take any of the four who would come, and she would take Joe Ashby first. There was a room over the forge at the works that a fitter might have.

The board did not make good the assembly's money that year, nor the next. The clerk entered the sum in the book as owed, and it stood in the book as owed.

Joe went south in an t-Sultain. Will Tanner would not go; he had a wife and a boat. The Fowler cousins went with him. They took the northern road to the capital and the south road from there to the sea, five days' walking, and came in to Seann Skell on the south coast on an evening of wind with the works' chimney standing up out of the town like a mast, taller than the house of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, taller than anything on the coast, its smoke going out flat over the sea.

Peigi met them at the gate. She had been told they were coming and had come down from the office in her apron. She shook each man's hand, and she looked at Joe a moment longer than the others.

"You mended the burner," she said.

"The screw was bent."

"I know. I've changed the box." She turned to go in, and turned back. "The report named no one."

"No," said Joe.

"The assembly's book did."

He did not answer that. She took them in to see the new range, its brick half-raised in the long shed and its iron lying in the mud, and then the room over the forge.

## V. Of the Flame over the Hammer

At the foot of that year the lamp-makers of Muileann mhin began to make dubhan lamps.

They had made lamps for the pits since before anyone at Muileann mhin could remember. Muileann mhin was a river town east of the capital, on the Abhainn fhionn, a town of flour-mills and of the capital's order, and among the mill-wrights and the millers there had grown up a street of lamp-makers who made the coal lamps for every shaft on the island. They had been chartered as Comann nan Lòchran in An Aois Rìoghachd, and their mark was a flame over a hammer, stamped into the brass of every lamp they made. Of all the guild marks of the island it was the only one the humans' traders had ever copied.

There were fewer shafts now. When coal grew scarce in the old reign the lamp-makers had halved their making for want of coal to burn in the lamps they made, and the guild's minute-book of those years was a list of old lamps lent from pit to pit. In the autumn of this year the master of the guild had written to Seann Skell, to the shipmasters' works, for the drawing of a burner, and Peigi nic Ascaill had sent it.

In the last days of the year Peigi went up to the capital on the works' business with the lamp-houses, and at the shipmasters' asking took Joe with her to look at a pump the lamp-houses said did not draw. They came back by Muileann mhin because the master of the lamp-makers had written that he had something to show her.

It was {{date:VI-0019}}, a day of hard frost with the sun low over the river and the mill-wheels stopped with ice. The lamp-makers' street ran down to the water, a row of narrow shops with their doors open for the light and their forges going, and the sound of small hammers came out of every door. The master of the guild met them at the door of the guild's hall at the street's foot, an old man in a leather apron with burns on his forearms like a hewer's scars, and took them in.

On the long table in the hall stood a lamp. It was a pit-lamp of the old shape, the brass bowl and the gauze and the hook to hang it by, but the bowl was deeper, and in its throat was a small burner of Peigi's pattern, made smaller. Lit, it burned with a clean white flame behind the gauze and gave off a thin reek of dubhan.

"Twelve of them," said the master. "We made twelve this month. We've orders for a hundred, from the pits that are left and from the yards of Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn, and from the inns on the old roads."

Peigi bent to the lamp and turned it in her hands and looked at the burner. Joe looked over her shoulder. The work was fine.

"It's good," she said.

"It's good," the master agreed. He turned the lamp so that the bowl was toward her, and put his thumb on the brass where the mark should be. There was nothing there. "We can't stamp it."

The guild's charter, he explained, gave the mark of the flame and the hammer to the coal lamps of Muileann mhin. It said coal. It had been written when there was nothing else to burn in a pit, and the men who wrote it had seen no reason to say more. Some of the guild, the older masters, held that a lamp that burned dubhan was no coal lamp and could not bear the mark, and that to stamp it would be to break the charter. Others said the mark was the mark of the hands that made the lamp and not of the thing burned in it. They had argued it at the guild's table since the harvest.

"So we've written to the guild court," said the master. He took a paper from the table and gave it to her. "Asking leave to set the mark on them. I'd be glad of your name on it, as the one who drew the burner."

Peigi read the paper. Then she took the pen he held out to her and signed it at the foot, under his name and the names of the lamp-makers who were for it, *Peigi nic Ascaill, master-fitter of Obair-dhubhain Seann Skell, who drew the burner*. She held the pen out to Joe.

Joe looked at her.

"You mended one," she said.

He took the pen and wrote his name under hers in the humans' letters, *Joseph Ashby, fitter, of Caol mhòr*, and gave the pen back to the master, who looked at the name a long time, and then blotted it.

The petition went to the guild court at Muileann mhin that afternoon, carried by the master himself in his good coat. Peigi and Joe went on by the road to the south-east in the last of the light. Behind them, in the window of the guild's hall, the lamp without a mark burned on the long table, where the master had left it, and the street of the lamp-makers went on with its hammering into the frost of the evening, and the year ended.
