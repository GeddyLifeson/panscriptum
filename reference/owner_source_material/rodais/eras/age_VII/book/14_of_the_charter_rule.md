# Of the Charter Rule

*DE 13 – 14*

## I. Of the House of Mac Ùisdein

The stone house at Cathair mhòr had been built, four generations back, of the spoil of the Sloc Mòr: grey rubble from the tips that no mason would otherwise have troubled to dress, hauled up from Achadh dhomhain on the Crown's own carts because the first Mac Ùisdein to hold the concession had wanted it said that his hall was built of the very stuff that paid for it. It was said still, by anyone who wanted to flatter the house, and by a good many who did not.

Coinneach mac Ùisdein sat in that hall in am Màrt of the thirteenth year with the pit's returns open on the table before him and did not need his clerk to tell him what they meant. He had read the Sloc Mòr's returns every quarter of his life since he was old enough to be shown the ledgers, first at his grandfather's knee and then at his father's, and he knew the shape a failing seam took on a page before he knew what most of the words on it were for: fewer tons at greater cost, more days lost to water, a gallery abandoned here, a face condemned there, and under it all the same steady, patient arithmetic that had been eating the house's fortune for thirty years and showed no sign of tiring of the meal.

"How much is left in it," he said, not as a question, to the clerk who had brought the returns.

"A fifth of what it gave in your grandfather's day, sir. Less, this last twelvemonth."

"And the Crown's share still comes off the top before ours."

"It does, sir. It always has."

Coinneach closed the ledger. He was a broad, unhurried man of five-and-forty who had never in his life cut coal or gone down a shaft, and whose whole knowledge of the Sloc Mòr was the knowledge of a man who owns a horse he has never ridden: he knew its habits, its ailments, its cost, and nothing else. His grandfather Alasdair had borrowed against the house's share to meet its debts in a bad year and never fully paid the loan off; his father had sold half the share outright to the Crown rather than see the whole house go under with it. Coinneach had grown up hearing both transactions spoken of at table as wounds the house had suffered and survived, and he had grown up, too, hearing his father say, more than once and always at the end of a long evening, that a house too proud to sell the rest when the time came would end by having nothing left to sell at all.

He thought the time had come. He did not think it with any great grief, which surprised him a little when he noticed it in his own chest; he had expected to feel the weight of four generations when he finally sat down to write the letter, and instead he felt mostly the relief of a man setting down a sack he has carried a long way past the point of needing it.

Even so, he gave the matter a week more than it needed, out of respect for his father's memory rather than any doubt of his own mind, and spent part of that week walking the tip-heaps above Achadh dhomhain with the clerk who had brought him the returns, looking at ground he had inherited a right to and never once in his life had reason to stand on before. The heaps were higher than he remembered from his grandfather's telling of them, grey and terraced, with a thin scurf of gorse taking on the older slopes where the spoil had lain undisturbed longest; a boy with a barrow was picking over the newest tip for lumps still black enough to burn in a poor man's grate, and looked up at the two strangers in good coats without much curiosity and went back to his picking.

Coinneach wrote to the Mine Board offering what remained of the house's share in the Sloc Mòr's spoil and quarry, the stone-cutting and the tip-rights that had come down with the coal, at a price he judged fair and expected to be haggled down from. The Board's answer came inside a month and haggled him down by less than he had expected; the Board, he understood without being told, was glad to have the whole of a great pit in its own hand at last, and had no wish to argue long over the last piece of it. The Board's clerks took the summer over the papers, and the sale was signed at Cathair mhòr on {{date:VI-0042}}, with two witnesses and a fee to the fair court, and Coinneach mac Ùisdein put what it brought him, and a good part of what the house had besides, into an account with the shipmasters at Ros dhomhain, against the building of three dubhan hulls.

He rode down to see the first of them on her slip in an Dàmhair: a coastal trader of the middling size, her ribs up and her planking half on, smelling of new pitch and sawdust in a yard that smelled of nothing else for a quarter mile around. The master shipwright walked him round her twice and answered his questions plainly, in the way of a man who has been asked the same questions by a great many new owners that year and does not resent it yet.

"She'll carry dubhan or anything else you like," the shipwright said. "The tank sits amidships, out of the way of cargo, and she'll do without it besides, burning wood in the old grate if it ever came to that, though I don't know why it would." He looked at Coinneach a moment. "Your house held coal a long time, I hear."

"It did," said Coinneach.

"And now hulls."

"Now hulls." Coinneach put his hand on the new wood of her side, which was still rough where the adze had left it. "The coal held us as long as it had anything to hold us with. I'd sooner move the house's money before the seam moves it for me."

The shipwright nodded, as if this were the most ordinary thing in the world, which by that year it very nearly was.

## II. Of the Bridge at Cathair dhearg

Coinneach had come up to the capital that summer, while the sale was still in the clerks' hands, to see them about its paperwork, and found the road changed since his last visit two years before. A dubhan bus now ran between Cathair dhearg and Inis mhòr twice a day, a long closed carriage on iron-shod wheels with a driver's box at the front and benches down each side inside, and it went the eleven miles in under an hour where the old coach had taken the better part of the morning. Another line ran out to Cathair mhòr itself, his own town, though he had come up by his own horse this time and had not thought to look for it at the bridge.

The word for the thing, he noticed, was not a Dia-thìris word at all. Men called it *bus*, flat and short, the same sound the humans of an Tìr Thall were said to use for it in their own tongue, and nobody troubled to find another. There had been words made, in the early years of the fuel, for the engine and for the trains that ran on the coal line and now the dubhan tanks beside it, careful compounds out of old roots that the Board's clerks were proud enough of. The bus and the hired car that waited for a fare at the foot of the bridge, its driver leaning on the door with his cap pushed back, had come into the tongue with their humans' names still on them, the way a thing arrives faster than the word-makers can catch up to it. *Tacsaidh*, the hired car was called, and the men who drove them took the name for themselves as well, and neither word troubled anyone enough to be replaced.

Coinneach stood at the foot of the bridge a while, watching the bus load. Six passengers went up the step, a basket of hens tied under one of the benches, a woman with a child on her knee paying her fare in coin new enough that Coinneach could see, when she opened her purse, that it still bore the coal-inked seal. He thought that the change he had made in his own house's fortune was a small thing set against the change the whole kingdom was making around him: a bus line and a hired car and a word borrowed rather than coined, none of it decreed by anyone, all of it happening because it was easier than not happening.

The coal caste of the capital, the old families who had held the mines and the mine-rights since the first grants, said nothing that anyone troubled to write down. Coinneach knew a dozen of them by name, had sat at table with half of them, and heard from none of them any word of complaint at the bus or the tacsaidh or the fuel that ran them; the ones who still held a working seam held their tongues because the pits still paid, however little, and the ones whose seams were spent, like his own family's, had for the most part already begun doing what he had done, quietly, each house in its own time, none of them wishing to be first and none, now, minded to be last.

## III. Of the Court of the Sea

He was at Ros dhomhain in that same autumn, when the first of his hulls had her engine in, and watched her go off on a trial, a short coasting run to Cuan shean and back with a cargo of nothing more than barrels of dubhan for the harbour lamps, and stayed to watch the kingdom's own patrol boats put out on the same tide.

They had been refitted that summer, the whole small fleet of them, and it showed in the way they took the harbour mouth: quicker off the mark than Coinneach remembered patrol boats ever being, with none of the labouring plume of smoke a coal-fired hull threw up, and gone round the headland almost before the gulls that always followed a boat out had settled which one to follow. A harbour-master's clerk, standing near Coinneach on the quay with nothing better to do than talk to a stranger, said they had run down two raider hulks that same month that would have got clean away from them the year before.

"Never used to catch them," the clerk said. "Coal boat's slower off the mark and slower to answer the helm both, and by the time she's got up her head of steam the raider's half over the horizon. These new ones are away before he's well started."

Both hulks, taken under tow, lay at the harbour wall when Coinneach walked down that way in the evening. Their crews lay the winter in the town's gaol, for Cùirt na Mara sat only at midsummer; and in an t-Iuchar of the fourteenth year, when Coinneach came down again to see his second hull launched, the court sat on them in the long room above the customs house, a room Coinneach had been in once or twice before on his own house's business and never for anything so grave. He went in out of curiosity more than anything else, and found the room fuller than he had expected: the raiders' trial was, that year, still enough of a novelty — the fleet's new speed being novel, and the catching of two crews in a month being novel with it — that half the town's idle men had come to watch.

There were eleven of the two crews together, ragged, salt-marked, answering the magistrate in a mixture of tongues that his clerk translated as it came. The oldest of them, a scarred man who gave his name as Callum of no fixed harbour, said through the clerk that he had followed the raiding trade twenty years and had never before this season had a Crown boat come up on him faster than his own could run, and said it, Coinneach thought, less as a confession than as a kind of professional complaint, the grievance of a man whose trade has changed under him without his leave. Three of the eleven, it came out in the course of the morning, were Tuathaich, taken on for the voyage at some northern harbour for want of better work and knowing, by their own account, nothing of where the boat's takings were meant to go once she had them. One of the three, a boy who could not have been past sixteen, wept through most of his own questioning and had to be told twice to speak up, until the magistrate, not unkindly, said there was no need of him to speak at all if the other two would answer for him, which they did.

The magistrate heard the whole of it out at length, conferred with his own clerk over a paper Coinneach could not see, and in the end hanged none of the eleven: he sent the eight Dia-thìrich among them to the quarries at Achadh dhomhain, the same quarries whose stone had built the house Coinneach had grown up in, and told the three Tuathaich, the boy among them, that they were free to find their own way home, north, by whatever road would have them, and that the court had no charge to hold against a man for want of choosing better company to sail in.

Coinneach watched the three go out of the room together, saying nothing to each other or to anyone, and thought it an odd smallness of the world that the sentence on the guilty should send eight men to break the stone his own family had lived four generations off, in the same season his own family's money had gone the other way, into hulls that had helped catch the men now sentenced to break it. He turned the thought over once, the way a man turns a coin he has found in the road, and put it away with the rest of the day's business, and rode home to Cathair mhòr well pleased with his hulls.

## IV. Of the Chartering at Seann Skell

Peigi nic Ascaill had been master-fitter at the shipmasters' works for seven years by the summer the Fuel-Fitters were chartered, and she had known the charter was coming, in the way anyone in the trade had known it, for the better part of a year before it came. The shipmasters' company had wanted it: a guild of its own, rolls that could bind an apprentice and set a wage and keep a bad fitter's name off any yard's books, the ordinary machinery of a trade grown too large to run any longer on a handful of masters' say-so. Every craft in the kingdom that had ever grown large enough to need binding had ended, sooner or later, chartered; nobody at the works had thought the fitters would be different, and nobody was much surprised, in the spring, when word came that the Crown had granted it.

What surprised Peigi, though she told herself afterwards that it should not have, was the single clause near the foot of the grant that fixed the rolls to Dia-thìrich alone.

She read it three times at her desk in the works' office before she understood that she had not misread it the first time. It was not a new kind of clause; every guild chartered in her lifetime carried some version of it, the Hewers before their own dissolving, the Rail Board's drivers, the masons, the smiths; she had simply never before had cause to read one with her own hand about to be asked to enforce it. She sat with the letter a long while after she had finished reading it, and then she did what she had always done with a hard thing since she was a girl learning her trade under men who thought a woman had no business at a lathe: she got up and went to see it done, because putting it off would not make it easier and might make it worse.

The chartering itself was held at the southern Seann Skell on {{date:VI-0045}}, in the works' own long shed with its doors thrown open to the harbour for air, before a Treasury clerk sent down from the capital to read the grant and a custodian of the Rite to bless the guild's new banner, a black swan on grey. Twelve masters were sworn that day, Peigi among the first six called, and she said the words of the oath — to bind no hand outside the roll, to teach the trade truly to those admitted to it, to answer to the guild's own court for any fault in her own work or her apprentices' — in a clear voice that did not shake, because she had decided beforehand that it would not, whatever else happened that day.

What happened that day, for the men she had brought south from Caol mhòr seven years before, was that their names could not go on the roll being read at the same hour in the same shed, and every man in the yard who had worked beside them for those seven years knew it without needing to be told twice.

She found Joe Ashby that evening at the forge where he had a room, sitting on the step with his tools laid out on a cloth beside him, already half packed though the chartering was not four hours old.

"You'll have heard," she said.

"I heard it read." He did not look up from the wrench he was wrapping. "Sworn among Dia-thìrich only. It's plain enough. I've heard plainer things put worse."

"I argued it, Joe. Before the grant went up, and again this spring when I knew it was coming. I went to the company's masters and I told them what losing you and Ned and Abel would cost the works, in plain trade terms, because I judged that was the argument likelier to move them than any other, and I was wrong that it would move them at all." She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame, not coming further in, as though she had not decided whether she had the right to. "I'm a master of the guild that's put you out of the yard. I don't know how to say that to you in any way that makes it smaller than it is."

"Don't, then," said Joe. "Say the other thing you came to say."

She had a purse in her coat pocket, which she had counted out herself that afternoon rather than leave it to the works' clerk, and she set it down on the step beside him. "A month's wage over what's owed you. For all three of you, the same. It's the company's money by rights, not the guild's, and I got it out of them by telling them it was cheaper than the trouble of a bad name up and down this coast, which is also true, though it isn't why I asked for it."

Joe looked at the purse and did not touch it for a moment. "Why did you ask for it, then."

"Because I fitted a burner beside you for two winters before either of us had a guild to answer to, and I don't know how else to say I haven't forgotten that, since the guild I've just sworn into won't let me say it in the only way that would mean anything, which is keeping you on." She said it fast, in one breath, the way a person says a thing they have rehearsed and are afraid of losing their nerve over before the end of it. "I know a month's wage isn't that."

"No," Joe said. "It isn't." He picked up the purse, weighed it once in his hand, and put it in his coat. "I'll take it, because it's owed and because Ned's got a debt at the smith's that wants clearing before we go, and pride's a poor thing to leave a debt unpaid for. But I'll tell you plainly, since you've been plain with me: I'd sooner you hadn't come tonight at all than come and stand in that door and not come in."

Peigi did not come in. She stood a moment longer, and then she said, "I know," and went back up the yard to the office, where there was a night's paperwork waiting that she was, for once in her working life, glad of the excuse to lose herself in.

## V. Of the Boat without a Guild

Joe Ashby went home to Caol mhòr in the first days of an t-Sultain with the Fowler cousins beside him and Will Tanner's letter in his pocket, which had reached him at the works a fortnight before and said only that there was work at the harbour for any man who could still fit an engine, guild or no guild, and that Will had kept the room over his own boat-shed empty on the chance.

Caol mhòr's harbour had grown since Joe had left it seven years before: more dubhan hulls at the moorings than he remembered coal-burning boats ever standing there at once, and a new shed at the harbour's north end with a sign that named it, in the Dia-thìrich tongue only, a depot of the fleet's provisioning, though the men who worked it, Joe saw within a day, were Tuathaich to the last one, paid by the fleet and answering to no guild the fleet had ever heard of asking after.

He fitted his first engine at Caol mhòr inside the week, a small fishing boat belonging to a widow named Jean Fisher whose husband had drowned two winters before and who had been putting off the cost of a proper engine ever since on what a widow's share of a fishing boat brought in. Joe fitted it for less than the southern works would have charged and took his payment half in coin and half in fish, which he judged, correctly, would see him through the winter better than coin alone in a town where every boat had fish to spare and coin was the scarcer thing.

There was no guild roll to enter his name on, north of the boundary stones, and no guild court to answer to if a burner he fitted failed; there was, instead, the harbour, which knew within a season whose engines ran true and whose did not, and told it plainly enough in whose boat a man trusted his own family to. By the turn of the year Joe had fitted engines in eleven boats at Caol mhòr and two at Baile chrom, working from a bench in the corner of Will Tanner's shed with tools he had bought from the smith of Baile chrom who had once straightened Peigi's burner with him on the anvil, at a price the smith called fair and Joe suspected was less than fair, out of a kindness neither of them named.

Ned Fowler took work on the harbour depot's own boats, minding the pumps that filled the fleet's tanks, and his cousin Abel went further, out past the mill country to Muileann bheag, where Calum Bàn the keeper still remembered the four fitters of the rain-soaked week and put in a word that got Abel taken on to help a farmer mend a threshing engine none of the district's own smiths would touch. Will Tanner, who had never gone south at all, said to Joe more than once that winter that the works at Seann Skell had done the north an unintended kindness in casting its fitters back onto it, since the harbour now had, for the price of nothing, four men who could fit an engine to a standard the south's own guild would have been glad to swear by, and answered to no one for it but the boats they served.

He did not write to Peigi, and she did not write to him, though a letter did come that winter, forwarded from the assembly's house, from the master of the guild's court at Seann Skell, informing any who fitted a dubhan engine north of the boundary stones without the guild's leave that they did so at their own risk and without the guild's protection should the work fail. Joe read it once, by the light of the shed's single lamp, and pinned it to the wall above his bench, not out of defiance exactly, though it did the office of defiance well enough, but because Will Tanner said it would make good kindling for the winter and Joe found he did not want it burnt.

## VI. Of the Walk-Out

The Moot met at Caol mhòr on {{date:VI-0047}}, under Martha Greaves in her seventh year as speaker, to settle a question that had been put off, by common consent, through two summers of the fuel's spreading: whether the north should take the new fire up for its own use, as the south had, or hold to its own seams as long as those seams had anything left to give.

It was not, in the letter of the thing, much of a question by then. Half the roofs of Caol mhòr already burned dubhan lamps bought off the harbour depot, and the mill at Doire ghlas had run its winter looms on it two years running for want of coal cheap enough to buy from the south. What the Moot's vote settled was not whether the north would use the fuel, which it plainly already did, but whether it would say so, in its own minute-book, as a thing decided rather than a thing that had simply happened around everyone the way the bus and the tacsaidh had happened around Coinneach mac Ùisdein at the foot of the capital's bridge.

The delegates spoke through the better part of a short winter afternoon, the hall lit early against the failing light by dubhan lamps hung from the same beams that had once held tallow, which more than one speaker remarked on without needing to labour the point. When the vote was called near dusk it went eleven towns for taking the fuel up and five against, the five being the delegates of the western pit towns, whose own seams still worked, however poorly, and who had not come to Caol mhòr that day expecting to be outvoted by towns that had never cut coal in their lives.

It was among the five that a young woman stood to speak before the vote was carried, whom few in the hall had heard address the Moot before: Ruth Calder, sent that year in her father's stead from Àth leathan, where he still held the pit captain's place though his chest had kept him above ground since the winter before, and where she kept his books and did his business for him by no rule anyone could point to but the hewers' own custom.

She was not long on her feet. The Moot's own minute gave her, as it gave every delegate that day, no more than a line: that the western towns had cut coal for their own hearths and their neighbours' since before there was a Moot to vote on anything, and meant to go on doing it as long as the seams allowed, and did not ask the Moot's leave to do so, only its patience while they did. When the vote was carried against her she rose again, with the four other delegates of the five towns beside her, and walked out of the hall before the clerk had finished writing the count into the book, not in anger that anyone present could afterward describe as anger exactly, but in the manner of people who have said what they came to say and see no further use in staying to hear it argued over.

Martha Greaves let them go without calling after them, and said only, to the hall that remained, that the minute would show the five towns' dissent recorded and their delegates withdrawn, and that the Moot's granaries, whatever else the vote had settled, remained open to any household of the north that came to them hungry, coal-burning or dubhan-burning alike, and always would. A delegate of Doire ghlas asked her, once the hall had quieted, whether the granaries' word applied equally to a household of Àth leathan as to one of Caol mhòr, since the five towns had that day, in effect, declared themselves apart from the rest of the Moot's business. Martha answered that a hungry household was a hungry household in whatever town the Moot's charter covered, and that she did not propose to begin sorting granary sacks by how a man's delegate had voted, and the question was not put to her again that year.

The clerk wrote it down as Martha said it, word for word, and under it, in the day's last light before the lamps were trimmed for the evening, wrote the names of the five towns that had walked out, Àth leathan first among them, in a hand no different from the hand he had used for every other name in the book that year. Outside, in the yard, the five delegates stood a while by the lit windows before starting for the western road, none of them in any great hurry to begin the walk home in the dark, and it was there, by Ruth Calder's own later account, that Tom Varley of Cnoc thais said to her that she had spoken better than her father ever had, which she took, she said, for the kindest thing anyone said to her that whole hard year.
