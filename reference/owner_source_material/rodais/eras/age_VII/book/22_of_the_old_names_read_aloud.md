# Of the Old Names Read Aloud

*DE 20*

## I. Of the Rolls Brought from the Chests

The order came up from Cathair dhearg in an Dàmhair, and it said nothing of war. It said that certain companies, disbanded since An Dealachadh, were to be reformed for the guarding of the coal roads and the dubhan depots against disorder, and it named no enemy, because to name one would have been to say aloud what everyone from the clerk who copied it to the boy who carried it already knew and none of them, that autumn, would yet say.

Somhairle mac Lachlainn read it twice at the depot below the western Seann Skell, standing in the yard among the barrels his own company had built the shed to hold, and put it in his coat and went on with the day's business as though it changed nothing, the kind of thing he had learned to do in eighteen years among the Board's enforcers. A man who showed what an order meant to him before his own men had time to decide for themselves what it meant lost a measure of trust he did not get back. He had learned that on the road to Muileann àrsaidh with stones coming out of the dark, and he had learned it again writing the report of that night, choosing each word the way a man picks his footing on wet rock.

He was summoned to the capital three weeks after, in the first cold week of an t-Samhain, and rode down by Slighe a' Ghuail with a corporal and a leather satchel of the depot's returns he did not expect anyone to ask for. Nobody did. He was taken instead, the morning after he arrived, to an old drill hall behind the council's own rooms, a building he had never had reason to enter, long and low, of dressed stone, its high windows gone grey with age and cobweb; used in his own lifetime, so far as he knew, for nothing grander than the storing of the city watch's spare pikes.

Three men were waiting for him there: the master of the Board, a clerk he did not know with an ink-stained sleeve, and an old soldier called Cormac Liath whom Somhairle had met twice before at musters and taken, both times, for a man kept on past his use out of courtesy. It was Cormac Liath who did the talking that morning, and Somhairle understood before the man had said three sentences why he had been wrong to write him off.

"You'll have command," Liath said, without any of the preamble Somhairle had braced for. "Of what there is to command, which by spring will be more than there is now. The council has not said war, and will go on not saying it as long as the word can be kept out of a minute. You'll say muster, and mobilisation, and the guarding of the roads, and you'll be right to, because those are the true words for what you're doing this month. Whether they're the true words for what you'll be doing by harvest is not for you or me to say today."

"And the men?"

"That's why you're here." Liath nodded to the clerk, who set his satchel on the long table that ran the hall's length and began, with the particular carefulness of a man handling a thing he has been told is old and is not sure how old, to lift out ledgers bound in board gone soft and dark at the corners.

They were the rolls of Buidhnean an Dealachaidh, and Somhairle had heard of them the way a man hears of a relic kept behind glass in a chapel he does not often visit: known to exist, never expected to be taken down and read. Ninety years they had lain in the council's chests, through reigns and rulings and the slow forgetting that overtakes any roll nobody calls, and the ink in some of them had gone the colour of weak tea, and in one, near the bottom of the stack, whole pages had stuck together and had to be worked apart with a blunt knife, the clerk's hands not quite steady at it, so that Somhairle found himself wanting to take the knife from him and could not, from his place at the far end of the table, have said whether it was patience or unfitness that kept him from offering.

"These name the companies," Liath said. "Not by number. By what they were called when they were raised, which was mostly for the town that raised them, or for whoever led the raising, or once or twice for a reason no living man can now explain, so that you'll find a company of An Dealachadh called simply the Grey House, and nobody in this room can tell you why, and neither could the last three clerks who had charge of this hall before this one." He laid his hand flat on the topmost ledger, not turning it. "The council wants the new companies to carry the old names, when a name can be matched to a town still standing and willing to give men. It will do the recruiting more good than any bill posted at a market cross. Men come readier to a name their grandfathers marched under than to a number stamped fresh on a drumhead."

Somhairle thought, though he did not say it, that the council wanted one thing more besides, to make the thing feel less new than it was, to dress a war nobody had yet agreed to call a war in the coat of something the kingdom had done before and survived; but he had learned, in the same eighteen years, when a thought of that kind was worth the saying and when it was better kept behind the teeth, and this, with Liath's hand still on the ledger and the master of the Board watching him with the patient, unhurried attention of a man who has already decided what he thinks of you and is only waiting to see whether you will confirm it, was plainly not a morning for saying it.

"Read them, then," he said instead.

It took the rest of that day and most of the next. Liath read, in a voice gone dry and even with age but not weak, town by town, company by company, and the clerk set down beside each name whether the town it had been raised from still stood, still had men of an age to carry arms, and still lay within a day's reach of a road Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's dubhan carts could use, for a company that could not be supplied might as well not be raised. Some towns answered to their names still and would send men gladly, the master of the Board said, reading from a paper of his own; the fisher towns of the east coast, quick to feel any threat to their harbours, and the market towns of the centre that had grown fat on the coal trade and now stood to grow fatter still on the trade that had replaced it. Other names on the roll answered to nothing at all, because the town was gone, swallowed into a larger one or fallen to a scatter of farms with no muster-ground left standing, and those were struck through in the clerk's hand and set aside, and nobody in the room remarked on it, though Somhairle noticed that Liath's reading grew a shade quieter over each one, as a man's voice does at a grave he did not expect to be asked to visit.

By the second afternoon the light in the high windows had gone from grey to the deeper grey that in that month passed for evening, and lamps were brought in, four of them, hung from iron brackets along the wall that Somhairle guessed had not held a flame in years, because the first one smoked and stank before it caught and burned clean. It was dubhan in the lamps, of course; there was no coal issued to a drill hall that had stood empty a generation, and Somhairle watched the black smoke go up in a thin curl to the rafters and thought, not for the first time that autumn, how strange a thing it was that the fuel a man in the north had made to warm his own hearth now lit the room where his own people's old names were being taken down off a shelf to be used against, whatever the council chose to call it this month, his own people's children.

The last of the rolls was read as full dark came on outside, by lamplight that threw the four men's shadows long and doubled up the hall's stone walls, and when Liath's voice stopped at the final name, a company once raised at a town on Slighe a' Ghuail whose muster-ground, the clerk found on checking, was now a rail siding with three sheds and a signal box, the room held a silence that none of the four men seemed in any hurry to break. It was Somhairle who broke it at last, because someone had to, and because command, he was beginning to understand, meant chiefly that whichever silence nobody else would break fell to you.

"How many companies stand, once the dead towns are struck out?"

The clerk added a column that had lain untotalled since the reading began and gave him the figure. It was not a large one, set beside what the roll had once named, ninety years before, when the whole of the kingdom's strength had stood behind Cogadh nam Beann; but it was more than Somhairle had commanded at any one time in his years with the Board's enforcers, and he found, hearing it said aloud in that cold hall by lamplight that smelled of the fuel a Tuathach had made, that it settled on him with a weight he had not braced for. The number did not frighten him. What weighed was that it was his, from that evening, to answer for, in a way that the depot and its barrels and the road below the western Seann Skell had never quite been.

"Then we begin with those," Liath said, and closed the topmost ledger, and Somhairle understood that the reading was over and the rest, whatever it turned out to be called, was now his own to carry.

Before the clerk began to pack the ledgers away, Somhairle asked to see the list of the towns still standing, and read it through himself by the last of the lamplight, slower than Liath had read it aloud, because a name on a page took longer to settle in the mind than a name spoken once and moved past. He knew most of the towns for what they were now rather than what the roll called them, market towns grown fat on the coal trade that had since gone south, fishing harbours that had never had much cause to think of the western hills at all until a company was named for them ninety years before and left, since, to gather dust; and he found himself doing, without quite meaning to, the same sum the clerk had done all afternoon with his careful column of struck-out names, setting each town against what he remembered of it from his own years riding the coal roads, and asking of each, silently, whether the men it sent him now would know, or care, what their grandfathers' company had once been called.

"You've a corporal of your own with you," Liath said, watching him at it. "Bring him in tomorrow and let him hear the names as you heard them today. A man who has heard a thing said once in a lamplit hall carries it differently to his own company than a man only handed a paper."

Somhairle thought of the corporal who had ridden down with him, a steady, unremarkable man named Iain Dòmhnallach who had served two years under him at the western Seann Skell without once, so far as Somhairle knew, asking why any order he was given had been given, and wondered whether that was the kind of man the old rolls wanted brought in to hear them, or whether it was precisely the kind that did not need to be, having never in his service shown any sign of asking the questions a hall full of ninety-year-old names was meant, Somhairle supposed, to answer before they were asked.

"I'll bring him," he said, and left it there.

## II. Of the Bench in the Passage

Martha Greaves kept the feast that year in a hired carriage on the northern road south, with a rug over her knees that did little against the cold coming up through the floorboards and a basket at her feet holding bread and a cold fowl that Ellen Marsh had pressed on her at the last that she had not yet found the heart to eat. She had left Doire ghlas two days before an Nollaig, when Athchuinge Dheireannach an Tionail was at last agreed word for word after a sitting that ran past midnight, and had travelled since by whatever the season offered: a carrier's cart as far as the boundary of the shires, a dubhan coach from there, and this last hired carriage, its wheels loud on the frozen ruts, for the stretch into the capital that no coach ran on an Nollaig Eve.

She had never in her life kept the day travelling, and she did not, in the small hours somewhere south of the boundary stones with the carriage jolting and her breath showing white even under the rug, allow herself to think overmuch about what her mother would have said of a woman who did. The Dia-thìrich kept no feast that day at all, which was one small mercy in a season that had offered her few, since it meant the roads she travelled were not crowded with those keeping it properly, at home, by a fire, as she should by rights have been keeping it herself.

The petition rode in her lap the whole way, in a leather folder Peter Hale had made her promise to guard as though it were the crown, which she had thought at the time a young man's overstatement and had come, somewhere past the second night without proper sleep, to find less funny than he had meant it. It asked one thing only, in words Tional nan Tuathach had turned over for the better part of a fortnight until every clause said exactly what it meant and no more: that the western pits be let alone to work out their own seams, since by every honest reckoning the coal in them would be spent within a handful of years regardless of what any board or council did about it now, and that the kingdom, having already the whole of the south's new fuel to warm it, might find in its own patience a cheaper peace than in any company it could raise to enforce the alternative.

She came into Cathair dhearg in the grey forenoon of an Nollaig, with frost lying white and undisturbed on the parapet of the bridge because so few feet had crossed it that morning to wear it away, and paid off the carriage driver, who wished her the day's blessings in the human way out of what she took for simple habit and then looked faintly embarrassed at himself for it, as a man does who has said a thing to the wrong company. She found the council's building without difficulty, having been given careful directions by Peter Hale from a visit of his own some years before, and was shown by a porter, once she had stated her business and waited while he went to ask whether her business was in fact wanted, to a bench in a stone passage off the main hall, where she was told the council would see her when it saw fit and not before.

More times than she could easily have counted, from her years managing the mill at Doire ghlas and her years since in Tional nan Tuathach's own chair, she had sat waiting on hard benches, and had thought, before this morning, that waiting was a thing she had grown past minding. The cold of the stone under her had little to do with it, nor the ache of two nights without a proper bed still in her back; it was the particular smallness of sitting in a passage that was not built to hold anyone for long, with nothing on the walls to look at but a row of framed grants she did not trouble to read and a single high window that showed her nothing of the sky but a strip of flat white cloud. Sitting there, she thought of the morning twelve years before when Thomas Rook had read Daniel Frayne's death into Tional nan Tuathach's own minute and the room had stood for the space of a breath and sat down again, and how strange it was that the office that morning had handed her without her asking for it should end, or at least come to this particular passage, on a bench built for men who did not expect to be kept waiting and had never once, in the building of it, thought to ask whether a woman from the north might someday be.

She waited the better part of an hour. The bench was cold enough that she kept her cloak about her the whole time, and the passage carried, faintly and by turns, voices from the chamber at its end, none of them clear enough to make out a word, only the rise and fall of men taking turns to speak and be answered, which told her nothing except that the council was, in fact, sitting, and had not, as she had half feared through the last two days of travel, adjourned for the feast after all and left her to have come all this way for a locked door. Twice in that hour she took the petition out of its folder, her hands wanting a thing to hold besides their own stillness, and twice she put it back unread, since she had written half of it herself and could have said it from memory standing on her own doorstep at Doire ghlas without troubling to look.

A clerk came out at last, a young man with the particular brisk courtesy of someone who has been told to be courteous and has not yet had time to make it his own, and asked her name. She gave it, and he wrote it on a small paper he carried, and went back in, and she heard, a moment later, her own name said through the door in a voice not his, in a shape she did not at first know for hers at all: the surname set first, in the way of the island's own houses, and the whole of it given the turn of the tongue that made every foreign name that passed through this building sound, by the time it reached the room, a little more like the island's own. She sat a second longer than she meant to before she understood that it was her they were calling, and stood then quickly, gathering the folder and her cloak together in a way that felt, to her own eye if to nobody else's, less composed than she had meant to arrive.

The chamber, when she came into it, was smaller than she had pictured it in the two days of travelling here, and warmer, with a dubhan stove set in an alcove at one side throwing a heat she felt at once after the cold of the passage, so that for the first few moments she was more aware of her own numbed hands beginning to prickle back to life than of the room. It took her a breath or two to see it properly: a long table, twelve chairs about it, and in every chair a face turned toward the door, toward her, with the particular stillness of people interrupted in the middle of business they had expected to finish uninterrupted. She knew none of them by sight save one, Anna nic Phàil, whom she had met once at a market charter's signing three years before and who gave her now, across the width of the table, the smallest incline of the head, not quite a greeting, more an acknowledgement that the room held one other person who had not always sat in rooms like this one.

She was the first speaker of Tional nan Tuathach, in all the years since Walter Hale had first gathered the northern towns to speak with one voice, to walk into this chamber at all. Nobody told her so; she understood it crossing the floor, from the feel of a room that did not often see a stranger cross it, the twelve faces following her the whole short distance from the door to the place, some six feet short of the table's head, where the same clerk who had called her name now stood waiting with a look that told her, plainly enough, that this was as far as she was meant to come. She learned afterward that she had understood rightly, and was not surprised.

"You may read it," said the man at the table's head, an old man she did not know, his voice carrying easily in a room built, she thought, for exactly that kind of voice.

She read it standing, holding the leather folder open in both hands because her hands, still not quite recovered from the cold, did not entirely trust themselves with a single sheet. She read it through without hurrying, though every instinct in her wanted to hurry, to be done and out of the room and back on the cold road north where she understood, at least, the weather and the company; she read it the way Peter Hale had made her practise it twice in Tional nan Tuathach's own hall before she left, slowly enough that no clause outran its own sense, pausing where the sentence paused and nowhere else. The room heard her out in a silence she could not read at all, twelve faces giving back nothing she could carry home and tell Tional nan Tuathach she had seen, no nod, no frown, nothing but the ordinary attention of people listening to a thing they had already, she suspected, been told the substance of before she ever crossed the bridge that morning.

She came to the last line, the offer of patience in place of a company, and let it stand a moment in the warm air of the chamber before she folded the paper closed and stepped forward the last few feet to lay it on the table, in the space the old man at its head cleared for it with one hand without otherwise moving.

"Received," said the clerk at her side, and wrote it down.

That was the whole of the answer the room gave her that morning: one word, said by a man who was not one of the twelve, and the small dry sound of a pen crossing paper. Nobody at the table spoke to her directly, then or after; the old man at its head inclined his own head slightly, in a gesture that might have been thanks and might only have been the signal that her business was finished, and the porter who had shown her in was already at her elbow to show her out again, as though the room's whole business with her had been measured beforehand to the minute and now, that minute spent, must close over the gap she had made in it and go on to whatever had stood next on its morning's list before she came.

Crossing back to the door, she thought of the letter she would have to write Tional nan Tuathach when she came home, and of how little there would be to put in it beside the one word and the small dry sound that had followed it. A room capable of measuring a stranger's whole business to the minute, she thought too, though she did not let it slow her step, was not a room that had been surprised by anything the petition asked of it, which meant that whatever answer the western towns were eventually to have, it had very likely already been settled, in some other room than this one, before she had ever climbed into the hired carriage two mornings before.

The cold struck her at once through the door before it had even fully shut, back in the passage, and there she let herself, for the first time since Doire ghlas, feel the whole distance she had come to hear one word said once. Weeping had never in her life answered anything, and she did not begin now; but she stood a while by the cold bench she had waited on, not sitting again, only standing, with the empty folder under her arm and her own breath white in front of her, until the porter, waiting with the particular patience of a man paid to wait, asked whether she wished him to find her a carriage back north, and she said that she did, and let him.

The petition she never saw again. It went, she supposed, wherever a filed thing goes in a room she had been in only the once, into some clerk's press among the year's other business, to be found, if it ever was found, by whoever next had reason to open that drawer and ask what Tional nan Tuathach had wanted, that an Nollaig, and what the council had answered it. She thought of it, on the long cold road back, less as a paper than as the one word the room had spent on it, turned over so many times in the two days of travel that had brought her here and the two more that would take her home again that by the time she came in sight of Doire ghlas's own lit windows, late on the second night after, she had almost stopped hearing it as a word at all, and heard instead only the sound the clerk's pen had made, crossing the paper, once, and then lying still.
