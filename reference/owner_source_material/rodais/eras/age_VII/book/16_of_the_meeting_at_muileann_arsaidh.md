# Of the Meeting at Muileann àrsaidh

*DE 16*

> **Part Three: Of the Holdouts of the Western Hills**
>
> *B' fheàrr leinn a bhith fuar còmhla ri ar nàbaidhean na blàth leinn fhèin.*
> We would sooner be cold with our neighbours than warm alone.
>
> — the delegates of Àth naomh, DE 16

## I. Of the Pit Captain's Daughter

In the western hills they still tell it that Calder of Àth leathan went down to his own pit for the last time in the dark of an Gearran, three weeks before he died, and would let no one carry his lamp for him though his hands by then shook too much to trust with a naked flame. He had cut coal at Àth leathan for six-and-thirty years, boy and man, and captained the pit for twenty of them, and the black cough that took him in the end was no stranger to that village; it had taken his own father before him, and it would take others after, and nobody in the hills thought to call it anything but the price of the seam.

Ruth was with him when the last of it came. She had kept his tally-books since she was twelve, first because her hand was steadier than his after a day underground and later because it was simply understood between them that the books were hers to keep; and she sat by his bed those last nights with the ledger open on her knee more from habit than any thought that the work in it still wanted doing, while her mother, dead herself these nine years, was spoken of only once, near the end, when Calder said her name and then said nothing else that Ruth could make sense of. He died a little before first light on the ninth day of the month, in the small stone house above the pit-head that had been his father's before him, and the village buried him two days after in the plot behind the chapel where the Àth leathan dead had been laid since before anyone living could say.

It was at the pit-head, not the chapel, that the choosing was made, on the evening of the burial, with the light going and the wind coming up cold off the hills. There was no guild left in the west to make it formal; Comann nan Gualadairean had dissolved itself four years before, and the western pits, small and poor and half-forgotten by any board that might once have cared, had never troubled the guild's rolls much in any case. What there was instead was thirty-one hewers of Àth leathan standing about the pit-head in their working clothes because they had come straight from the last shift, and old Angus Rennie, who had cut coal longer than any man there but Calder himself, saying what everyone had already half decided among themselves in the two days since Calder took to his bed for good.

"He kept the books and he kept the peace," Angus said, "and he knew every seam in this hill better than any man breathing, saving one." He kept his own eyes on the pit-head's winch as he said it; every other eye in the crowd turned to Ruth. "I'll not stand up here and pretend I know another man fit to take his place at short notice. But I know who kept his books alongside him five years and more, and knows the seams near as well as he did, and I'll stand behind her if the rest of you will."

Nobody spoke against it. The hewers of Àth leathan had, Ruth understood afterward, no settled notion at all of a woman standing where her father had stood; what they had was a memory of her running messages down the pit road with her father's dinner in a cloth as a child, and of watching her keep his books through two winters when his eyes had begun to fail him, and too little time, with the light going and the ground cooling under their feet, to think of anyone else to ask. Ruth said yes because there was nothing else to say, standing at the pit-head in her father's own worn coat, which she had put on that morning without thinking because it was cold and it was there, and because a man handed her, without a word, the small brass lamp that had been Calder's own since his own father died, its case dented from thirty years of being carried down and up again. She took it because there was nowhere else for it to go.

Sleep would not come to her that night. She sat up instead with the ledger open before her, as she had a hundred nights before, and found that for the first time in her life there was no one to hand it to when the figures were entered and the page was done.

## II. Of Sarah Dunmore's Kitchen

Word of the meeting at Muileann àrsaidh had been going round the western hills since the turn of the year, carried by carriers and by the hewers who still walked between the pit towns on Sundays to see kin, and by the time Ruth set out on the hill path in the first week of am Màrt, ten miles by the short way over the shoulder of the moor, she went as Àth leathan's delegate without having asked to be one or having been asked in any form she could afterward have pointed to. It was simply understood, the way the choosing at the pit-head had been understood, that the pit captain went where the pit towns' business was done, and that this was pit towns' business if anything in the hills was.

She had never before walked so far alone. The path ran up over open ground where the wind had nothing to break it and down again through a stand of birch where the last of the winter's snow still lay in the shadow of the trunks, and she came into Muileann àrsaidh a little after noon with her boots wet through and her father's lamp, unlit, swinging from her hand because she had brought it without quite deciding to and had not liked to leave it behind once it was in her pocket.

Sarah Dunmore's house stood at the foot of the town's one street, a stone house no larger than its neighbours, with a good peat fire already going in the kitchen grate when Ruth came to the door and a smell of broth that told her the house had been feeding people for some days before she arrived. Sarah herself, a broad, plain-spoken widow of some thirty years past her own husband's death under a fall of stone at the Muileann àrsaidh pit, had put her kitchen at the meeting's disposal without being asked twice, on the reasoning, as she gave it to Ruth within an hour of her arrival, that a kitchen fed people and a hall did not, and that men who had walked ten or fifteen miles over hill paths in the wet would talk better with something hot in front of them than they would in a cold room with nothing but a table.

There were seven of them crowded round that table by the time the light began to go: two from Muileann àrsaidh itself, an old hewer named Fraser and a younger man called Meikle who did most of the town's writing; a heavy, careful man named Drummond who had walked over from Cnoc thais that morning on Tom Varley's errand, Varley himself being kept at home by a knee that had never mended right since a fall the winter before; a thin, watchful woman called Nan Ross who spoke for the few families left at Tobar dhearg of the hills, where the one pit barely kept forty hewers in work; and Sarah Dunmore herself, who was no delegate of anywhere but sat at the head of her own table as though the question of whether she belonged in the talk had never once occurred to her.

Ruth said little that first evening. She was the youngest at the table by a dozen years and more, new to the captaincy by less than a fortnight, and she had come, she told herself, to listen before she spoke; but she listened hard, and what she heard, under the talk of prices and the talk of the south and the talk of what Tional nan Tuathach might or might not do, was that none of the six people in that kitchen had any clear notion yet of what they wanted the towns to agree to, only a clear and common notion of what they would not agree to, which was to fold their pits and buy their fire from the south like every other town in the north had done.

"We're none of us saying we won't sell to the south's fuel-men, mind," Fraser said, late in the evening, with the fire down to embers and Sarah moving quietly about the room banking it for the night. "Only that we'll keep cutting what we cut, and sell it to whoever in the north still wants coal, and not be told by any charter that we've no business doing it."

"That's a thing worth saying," Ruth said, the first full sentence she had put into the room all evening, "but it wants saying plainer than that, or it'll be argued over as long as it's talked and never once written down." She felt every face at the table turn to her, and pressed on before she could think better of it. "My father kept books thirty years. A thing not written down plain enough to read back is a thing that gets remembered five different ways by five different people inside a twelvemonth."

Sarah Dunmore, from the hearth, laughed once, short and approving. "There's Calder's own daughter talking," she said. "Well. If it wants writing plain, who's for the writing of it?"

Nobody answered for a moment. Then Drummond nodded slowly across the table at Ruth, and Fraser after him, and Ruth understood, with something between pride and a plain cold weight settling in her chest, that the pen, like the lamp, had been handed to her before she had thought to reach for it.

They talked past midnight, though little more of it went into any settled shape that night. Meikle set down on a scrap of paper, for his own memory more than the meeting's, the figures Drummond had brought from Cnoc thais: what a sack of dubhan cost delivered to a northern door against what a sack of their own coal cost cut and carted, and how the second sum had crept up year on year as fewer hands stayed to cut it, until it stood now not far below the first. Nan Ross said little beyond agreeing that Tobar dhearg's forty hewers would go on cutting whatever the meeting decided, since forty hewers with nothing else to do were no argument at all; and Sarah, setting bread and a jug of milk on the table without being asked, said once that she had buried a husband under this town's coal and would sooner see her neighbours burn it than see the pit stand idle over his grave, which nobody in the kitchen thought needed answering. Ruth slept that night in a box-bed off Sarah's own kitchen, with the fire's last heat coming through the wall behind her, and lay a long while listening to the house settle before sleep took her, turning over in her mind the shape of a paper that did not yet exist.

## III. Of the Pit-shed and the Agreement

The kitchen held them well enough for talk, but by the second day word had gone round the town and more had come than Sarah's table would seat: hewers from Muileann àrsaidh itself who wanted to hear with their own ears what was being said in their name, two more men down from Cnoc thais on Tom Varley's second errand once his knee let him send them, and, on the third morning, a boy on a borrowed pony from Tobar dhearg with a note in Nan Ross's own hand asking that nothing be signed before she'd had the chance to read it over once more herself, which raised a laugh even from Nan when it was read aloud, since she was sitting at the table not three feet from where the note was opened.

They moved the talk to the pit-shed above the town, a long low building of stone and turf where Muileann àrsaidh kept its winches and its spare timber and, in the corner nearest the door, a bench worn smooth by three generations of hewers changing their boots before a shift. It smelled of tar and damp rope and old coal-dust, and it held, once the benches were cleared and set in rows, near forty people at a squeeze, which was more, Ruth thought, looking round it on that first morning, than she had ever seen gathered in one room in her life.

She wrote the agreement's terms on three sheets of Meikle's paper over the course of that day and the next, crossing out more than she kept, while the room argued each line before it was let stand. It was slower work than she had thought book-keeping had prepared her for; a line she thought plain enough to need no more discussion could still draw an hour's argument from men who had different notions of what "the seams" meant, whether it took in Tobar dhearg's single pit as well as Cnoc thais's dozen workings, or whether "any household in the north that will buy" meant houses in the south too if a southern buyer somehow wanted northern coal, which nobody thought likely but several thought worth saying plainly all the same.

The hardest of it, in the end, was the leaving out. A man from Muileann àrsaidh's own row of hewers, a squat, red-faced man called Dodds, stood up on the second afternoon and wanted a line put in naming the fuel-fitters of the south by name, and the charter that shut Tuathaich hands out of the new trade besides, so that whoever read the agreement after would know exactly whose doing the five towns' trouble was. Ruth let him talk himself to a stop before she answered, because she had learned already, in two days of it, that the room argued better with a thing than against a man. "Name them," she said, "and the paper stops being about coal and starts being about grievance, and a grievance is a thing folk argue with. Five towns cutting their own coal and selling it plain is a thing folk can only agree to or leave be." Dodds sat down without much grace, but he signed the sheet three days later along with the rest of Muileann àrsaidh's hewers, which Ruth took, privately, for the better half of an answer.

What stood, in the end, in the hand Ruth had written it in, was shorter than any single hour of the argument that had shaped it. The five towns of the western hills — though only four were named in that first writing — would go on working their seams as they had always worked them, taking up no man's charter and asking none, and would sell their coal to any household of the north that came to buy it, at whatever price buyer and seller could agree between them, and to no household that did not come freely asking. It said nothing against the fuel from the south, and nothing for it. Of Tional nan Tuathach, which none of the four towns present had asked to be part of the meeting, it said nothing at all, and none proposed to write to it now. Its last line, which Ruth added herself and read aloud to the shed before anyone else saw it, promised that the towns signing it would stand by each other in the keeping of it, for as long as any of them had coal left to sell.

Fraser signed for Muileann àrsaidh, Drummond for Cnoc thais, Nan Ross for Tobar dhearg once the boy had ridden the finished sheet back to her and she had, true to her note, read it over twice before putting her own mark beside her name. Ruth signed last, for Àth leathan, in the same hand she had used for her father's tally-books, on a morning in the third week of am Màrt with the pit-shed's one small window letting in a grey light that had not yet decided whether it would rain, and felt, setting her name under the others, that she had put more of herself into those three short sheets than into anything she had done in her life before that week.

## IV. Of the Town that Would Not Be Warm Alone

Àth naomh sent no one to the meeting at Muileann àrsaidh that am Màrt, and for the better part of a month afterward the agreement stood signed by four towns and spoken of by the fourth as though it were finished business. It was in an Giblean, with the pit-shed's turf roof dripping steadily under a fortnight of rain, that two delegates came over the hill path from Àth naomh on a wet Tuesday and asked, without much in the way of preamble, to be let sign what the others had already signed.

Àth naomh had the least to offer of any of the five: a handful of old shafts worked thin, a temple and a citadel from an older age that the town's few hundred souls now kept more from habit than any wealth to defend, and coal enough, by its own delegates' plain accounting, to warm perhaps two winters more before the seams gave out entirely. It would have cost the town nothing, several in the shed pointed out, and might have spared it a hard winter or two, simply to go over to the south's fuel as every other northern town but these four already had; and it was the elder of the two delegates, a stooped, grey-bearded man named Ferguson who had walked the hill path with a stick in either hand, who answered that argument standing in the middle of the pit-shed floor with the rain loud on the roof above him.

"We could burn dubhan same as any town in the north," he said. "We've thought on it, don't doubt that we have, thought on it hard through two cold winters running. But we'd be burning it alone among our own hills, with every neighbour we've got still cutting coal at the seam, and I'll tell you plainly what we decided, the whole town of us, over it." He looked round the shed, at faces he mostly did not know, and said the thing as though he had carried it the whole ten miles from Àth naomh rehearsing exactly how he would say it. "We'd sooner be cold with our neighbours than warm alone."

Nobody in the shed answered that at once. Ruth, sitting near the front with the signed agreement still in its folder on her knee, found that she had nothing to add to it that would not have made it smaller, and said only, after a silence that went on long enough to be its own kind of answer, that there was room on the sheet for a fifth name, and would he care to write it himself or have her write it for him. He wrote it himself, in a hand less steady than Ruth's own but legible enough, and his companion after him, and by the time the two of them set off back over the hill path that same wet afternoon, unwilling to be a night's board and lodging on a town no better off than their own, the agreement of Muileann àrsaidh carried the names of all five.

By an Cèitean, with the worst of the spring rain past and the hill paths dry enough to walk without ruining a pair of boots in an afternoon, the five towns had become, in the mouths of everyone in the western hills and a good many people well beyond them, simply the holdout towns, and Ruth Calder, one-and-forty years old and less than three months a pit captain in her own right, had become, without any second choosing to mark the change, the person whose name was given when anyone from outside the hills asked who spoke for them. She had not asked for that either. It came to her the way the lamp had come to her, and the pen after it, because there was no one else at hand when the asking was done, and because she had shown, in the writing of three short sheets, that she could put a thing down plainly and stand behind it after.

Tional nan Tuathach, when word of the agreement reached it, did neither of the two things the five towns had half expected it might do. It did not send a delegate north to add Tional nan Tuathach's own name to the sheet, as one or two in the pit-shed had hoped it might; and it did not send word condemning the towns for standing apart from a trade the rest of the north had gone over to whole. It sent nothing at all, so far as Ruth or anyone in the hills ever learned, beyond a bare line in its own minute-book, copied out for her by a carrier some weeks later at her own asking, that noted the towns' agreement had been reported to Tional nan Tuathach and that Tional nan Tuathach, having heard it, saw no cause to act upon it either way. She read the line twice, standing in her own kitchen at Àth leathan with her father's lamp on the shelf where she had set it the night of his burial, and understood it for what it plainly was: the sound of a door being left exactly as it stood, neither opened nor shut, by people who had decided that the five towns' trouble was, for the present, none of theirs to have an opinion about.

## V. Of the Two Shafts at Cnoc thais

Tom Varley's knee mended slowly through the summer of that year, and it was not until an t-Samhain, with the first hard frosts already whitening the moor grass in the mornings, that he was fit again to walk the length of his own pit workings at Cnoc thais without a stick; and it was in that same month, once he could stand a full day underground, that he set two dozen of his hewers to opening again a pair of shafts on the hill's northern face that had lain shut, by every account the town could give of them, since before An Dealachadh, when the men who dug them had been of another people entirely and the coal they left behind had been thought too thin and too far in to be worth a second working.

It was thin, when Varley's men reached it, and it was harder cutting than the town's main seams, and the first cartload brought up from it, in the last week of the month, burned with more smoke and less heat than any coal Cnoc thais had cut in living memory. Nobody in the town complained of it. It was set that same week on a dozen hearths across Cnoc thais, in houses that had been buying their fire in from Muileann àrsaidh at a price the winter made harder to bear each year, and the town's own account of it, which reached Ruth by way of a letter from Varley himself a fortnight later, said only that the coal was poor, and that it was theirs, and that a poor fire of one's own cutting warmed a house no worse than a good one bought dear from a neighbour.

Varley wrote, too, that the two old shafts had given up more than coal in the opening of them: a broken pick-head of a make none of his hewers knew, and, in the second shaft, a length of rotted timber shoring still holding the roof up after however many years it had stood there, which his men had left untouched rather than risk the roof coming down on the strength of curiosity alone. He asked, half in earnest, whether Ruth thought it worth writing to Leabharlann Muileann chaol about the pick-head, and answered his own question in the same letter before she could: that a chronicler's clerk two days' ride away would take more interest in an old iron head than any of the five towns could spare the time to satisfy, and that the shaft wanted working, not wondering over, with the winter coming on and Cnoc thais's own hearths waiting on it.

Ruth read the letter at her own table at Àth leathan on an evening in the first week of an Dùbhlachd, with the year's business behind her and the next year's not yet begun, and found in it, more than in anything that had happened since the pit-shed at Muileann àrsaidh, the whole of what the five towns had agreed to be. It was not a victory, Varley's letter said as much as it said anything; it was thin coal from an old working, burning worse than the coal it replaced, in houses that would rather have had better. But it was the hills' own, cut by the hills' own hands, out of ground the hills had never given up, and Ruth understood, reading it a second time by the last of the evening light, that this was the whole of the bargain the five towns had made with each other in am Màrt and would go on making, winter after winter, for as long as any seam among them held coal enough to be worth the cutting.

She sat a long while at the table after the letter was folded away, with her father's lamp burning low on the shelf and a fresh sheet of paper set out in front of her, blank, because Fraser had written from Muileann àrsaidh that week asking whether it would not be worth a letter to one or two of the northern towns still undecided about their fire, laying the five towns' case out plain the way the agreement had been laid out plain, in case there were households elsewhere in the north still minded to buy coal from a neighbour rather than dubhan from the south. It was, Ruth thought, turning the pen over once in her fingers before she began, exactly the kind of asking her father would have turned to her without a second thought, the way every asking since the pit-head in an Gearran had come to her without her once being asked whether she wanted it; and she understood, dipping the pen at last, that there would be a great many more such letters before the seams at Àth leathan or Cnoc thais or any of the five towns ran thin enough to end the need of them, and that she, and no one else, would be the one sitting at a table some cold evening each year of it, finding the words.
