# Of the Eleven Books

*DE 17*

## I. Of Cathair gheal

Tobar dhearg of the hills was the least of the five holdout towns, and knew it, and made no complaint of it. It had one pit and forty hewers, a red well the Dia-thìrich had dug long before An Dealachadh and the Tuathaich had kept digging after, and a track down through the western hills that a cart could just take if the carter walked at the horse's head the whole way. What the pit raised was poor coal, sooty and quick to crumble, no match for the seams of Muileann àrsaidh or Cnoc thais; but it burned, and the walled town of Cathair gheal on the coast below had no coal of its own at all, only boats. So twice in the month, when the weather served, a cart went down from Tobar dhearg loaded with sacks, and came back loaded with fish salted in barrels, and the arrangement had held so long that nobody in either town could have said which had thought of it first.

Ellen Marsh came down to Cathair gheal on the carrier's cart that brought the fish north, in the raw first days of an Gearran, with her satchel on her knees and her notebook wrapped in oilcloth against the spray off the harbour road. She had made the journey a dozen times in as many years, for one reason or another, and always for the same true reason underneath the others: there was, in that walled town of white stone with the sea on three sides of it, an old woman named Annie Drew who had been a child at An Dealachadh, and who had said, the last time Ellen sat with her, that she did not think she would keep another winter.

She had kept it. Ellen found her sitting up in the kitchen of her daughter's house by the harbour wall, wrapped to the chin though the fire was well built with Tobar dhearg's coal, her hands gone to knuckle and vein, her eyes still quick. She was ninety-one that winter, the oldest soul in Cathair gheal by some years, and the only one left who had been more than an infant when the parting came.

"You're earlier than I looked for," Annie said, by way of greeting, "or I'm later than I looked for. One or the other."

"The fish cart wanted an early tide."

"It always does." Annie nodded at the stool by the fire, which had plainly been set there for her before Ellen arrived, and Ellen sat and unwrapped the notebook and found the place where the last visit had left off, a third of the way down a page, in her own small hand: *she was five, or near it. her mother would not let her look back at the smoke.*

"You'll want the rest of that," Annie said.

"If you're willing."

"I said I'd give it you before I die, and I mean to keep to that whatever else I don't keep to." She was quiet a moment, working her hands together in her lap, and then she began, in the humans' tongue, which she kept to by long habit and never troubled to leave for Dia-thìris even with those, like Ellen, who had both equally. Ellen wrote as she talked, in Dia-thìris, since that was the hand she wrote fastest and truest, and stopped her now and again only to ask a name again, or a distance, or which of two roads.

It was not a long telling, in the end, though it had taken eleven years to get the whole of it, a little at a time, out of a woman who did not care to dwell on it and would not be hurried past it either. The smoke over the fires they had left burning to make the parting look sudden, though it had been three weeks in the planning by the time the household stirred that night. The stones set new along the road, white and raw, not yet grown over, that her father called the boundary though there had been no boundary the week before. A soldier at the last of them who had given her mother bread out of his own satchel and would not meet her mother's eye while he did it. The long walk after, and the fires lit along it each evening smaller than the one before, until by the fourth night there was coal enough only for a handful of families to share one fire between them, and the rest went cold, and nobody spoke of that afterward either, not for years, not until an old woman sat by a stranger's fire built of Tobar dhearg's crumbling coal and found she had, after ninety winters, one telling left to give away.

"That's the whole of it," Annie said, when she had done. "You've the bones of it from me twice over already. This is only the flesh."

"It's the flesh I wanted." Ellen read the last page back to her, low, so as not to wake the sleeping cat that had climbed into Annie's lap somewhere in the telling, and Annie listened with her eyes shut and said, at the end, that it would do, and that Ellen had got the soldier's bread right, which was the part she had always feared losing between one visit and the next.

"That's the last of them, isn't it," Annie said then. "You've been round the whole coast for it. There's none left older than me this side of Muileann àrsaidh, and she's simple, poor soul, hasn't known her own name since the fever three winters back."

"You're the last," Ellen said. She did not pretend otherwise; Annie was not a woman it did any good to spare.

"Well." Annie stroked the cat once, the length of its back, and looked into the fire a while. "I've kept my word, then. See you keep yours."

"I mean to."

"Mean isn't do. But I'll not live to see which, so you may tell me what you like." She said it without bitterness, the way she said most things, as a fact laid down and left to lie, and Ellen closed the notebook over the last page and did not answer that, because there was no true answer to give her that was also a kind one.

She stayed the night, as she always did, in the loft above the kitchen, and in the morning she walked down to the harbour before the fish cart was loaded and stood a while at the wall in the cold light, looking out past the white walls to the grey water. A boat was coming in from the night's fishing, low and slow, and up on the hill road behind the town a cart was coming down from Tobar dhearg with its load of sacks, the carter walking at the horse's head. She watched the two of them come toward each other, the boat and the cart, as they had come toward each other, she supposed, a thousand times before her, and thought of Annie Drew asleep in the house behind her with the cat still warm in her lap, and of the notebook wrapped in oilcloth in the satchel at Ellen's own feet, and of how little either the boat or the cart, going about their trade, cared for the telling folded inside it.

## II. Of the Eleventh Book

The schoolroom at Doire ghlas, by the time Ellen came home, had gone back nine years to being nothing but a schoolroom, the tables in their rows again under the alphabet painted round the walls in both hands, the stove drawing well, no smell of paste in it any longer to speak of. But in the low cupboard at the back, under lock, stood ten books, bound in plain grey board by her own hand over eleven winters, each lettered on the spine with a year and nothing more, and it was to these that Ellen went first, before she had so much as taken off her coat, to lay the notebook from Cathair gheal on top of them and stand looking down at the row.

She had begun the work almost by accident, the year she came to teach at Agnes Pike's old school, when an old man of the parish had stopped her in the lane to correct something she had told the children about An Dealachadh, a small thing, a date got wrong by a generation, and had gone on, once started, to tell her the true year and a good deal besides that she had never read in any book because it was written in none. She had gone home that evening and set it down before she forgot the half of it, and the next week had asked another, and the week after another, and had not, in eleven years, found a way to stop, though she had several times found reasons she ought to.

What she had, by the winter that closed the seventeenth year, was eleven books: the tellings of some two hundred old men and women of the north, taken down as near their own words as her pen could keep pace with, town by town, coast to hill, An Dealachadh and the years straight after it, the first winters, the boundary stones, the names of those who had not lived to cross them. No two tellings agreed in everything. She had learned early to let that stand rather than smooth it, and had written, in the front of the first book, a line to say so: that where two witnesses differed she had set down both, and let the reader judge, as a court would.

She worked through the first week of an Gearran binding the eleventh volume herself, at her own table by lamplight, with the flat bone folder Daniel Frayne's eldest binder had given her nine winters before, when the two of them had sat up late in this same room over Tional nan Tuathach's own sixty-three books and Ellen had asked, more from curiosity than any plan, to be shown how the stitching went. The binder had shown her, and left her the folder after, saying that a body who kept books ought to know how to mend one, and Ellen had used it every winter since on her own small work, though never so fine as the binder's own.

She had thought, more than once through those years, of asking the binders of Muileann leathan to do this last one properly, since they were near and willing and it would have taken them an evening where it took her three, but had always come back to doing it herself. It seemed to her, though she could not have said quite why to another person, that a book made of other people's own words asked to be sewn by the same hand that had taken them down, and not passed on for finishing to someone who had never sat by any of those fires.

When it was done she laid it at the end of the row, and it was, by an inch's difference in the grey board, exactly the same as the other ten, and the row of eleven stood the length of a hand-span on the cupboard shelf, and held, she reckoned without troubling to count it exactly, the better part of two hundred lives that would otherwise have gone into the ground with nothing to show that they had been lived at all.

She wrote the letter to an Leabharlann the same evening, at the same table, in a fair hand she kept for such things and did not often need. Four sentences held the whole of it. It set down what An t-Aon Leabhar Deug were and how they had been gathered, over how many years and from how many mouths, and asked, in the plain form such requests took, that Leabharlann Muileann chaol receive them and keep them beside its own record, as a thing belonging to the shires as much as any royal minute or council return. Nowhere did it ask that the books be believed over the written record where the two crossed; the whole of the asking was that they be kept where a person who wanted to know might come and find them, the way a person could come and find the reign of any king. Ellen read it over twice, found nothing in it she wished to soften, sealed it, and sent it north the following week by the library carrier who came through Doire ghlas each month with the shires' returns, An t-Aon Leabhar Deug wrapped together in oilcloth and tied with the same waxed thread the binders of Muileann leathan had taught her to use.

## III. Of the Letter Laid on the Table

The answer was five months in coming, which did not surprise her, since she had asked five months' worth of a plain answer and had not, in her heart, expected one at all.

It came in an Lùnastal, on a hot still evening when the children had long since gone home and Ellen was alone in the schoolroom totting the term's slates before she put them away for the summer. The carrier brought it up from the harbour road himself rather than leaving it at the school gate, a courtesy he did not often trouble with, and stood a moment at the door turning his cap in his hands before he gave it over, as if he had already guessed, by its weight or its seal, that it was not the kind of letter a man handed across a threshold and walked away from.

It was in Raghnall mac Mhuirich's own hand, which she knew from the flyleaf of every one of her own books, since he had written a line in each on the day it was carried in, acknowledging its receipt, always the same few words. This was longer. Her request, he wrote, and An t-Aon Leabhar Deug with it, had been laid before the keepers of an Leabharlann in the spring, read entire, and set upon the table of the council's business, where it remained, not refused. The question the request raised, as he set it out, touched the books themselves hardly at all, since he did not doubt they were honestly made; it turned instead on what standing an Leabharlann ought to give to witness that came by the mouth and not by the pen, set down at second or third hand by one recorder's own judgment of what to keep and what to leave. This was a question Comhairle na Rìoghachd had not yet given its view on, in this matter or in any other that had come before it in like form, and until it did, Neach-gleidhidh an Leabharlainn had no more authority to shelve eleven books of spoken telling beside the shires' own returns than he had to strike a line through any of those returns on his own word that they were wrong. Sorry to send so little for so much labour, he hoped she would believe the labour was not lost for being unanswered, since a thing once written did not become less true for waiting.

Ellen read it twice at the window, where the light was better, and then sat down at the nearest table with it still in her hand.

She was not, in the ordinary way, a woman given to sitting idle over a letter. But there was, in the reading of it, a particular kind of stillness that came over her, not anger exactly, or not only anger, more the stillness of a sum that has been added correctly and come out wrong. She thought of Annie Drew's hands working together in her lap, and of the soldier's bread that Annie had feared losing between one telling and the next, and of the ninety winters it had taken to bring that one small true thing to a page, only to have it weighed now, by a kind man in a good hand, against a standard it could never by its nature meet, since a soldier's bread given at a roadside leaves no return for any clerk to enter.

She did not doubt that Raghnall mac Mhuirich meant every word of his sorrow, nor that he had done, within the room he had, as much as the room allowed; she had heard, from more than one quarter, how the man had found money out of his own small fund to keep a girl clerk in dry stockings when the council would give him none for the purpose, and she did not think a man of that stamp wrote *not refused* as a way of refusing. That was, in its way, the worst of it. There was no one person in the whole chain of it to be angry at. The letter simply said, in its careful sentences, that An t-Aon Leabhar Deug would lie on a table until a council that had not been asked to look at them chose to, and that no one, not the keeper, not the council, not Ellen Marsh herself, could say when that might be, or whether it ever would.

## IV. Of the Census Clerks

She wrote her reply the same night, before the stillness had time to cool into something more governable and less exact.

She did not write in anger, or not in any anger a reader could have pointed to in a single line; she was too careful a hand for that, and too old a teacher of grammar to let a sentence run away from her meaning. But she wrote, plainly, that she did not think the standing of spoken witness was so untried a question as the council supposed. She reminded the keeper — though she did not think he needed reminding, and said so — that when Cunntas nan Làmh was last taken in the shires, the clerks who came north to count the northern towns had counted nothing, but had taken the figures of every town from Tional nan Tuathach's own mouth, written them down on their own authority with no other proof asked or given, and that this spoken tale, and no other kind, was the figure entered afterward in the kingdom's own returns and used by Tional nan Tuathach in its dealings with the council to this day. She had seen the page herself, she wrote, in Tional nan Tuathach's own minute-book, the year the figures were read into the room and the two words written under them that were the whole of what the room had thought fit to say. If spoken witness had stood good enough, without question, to fix what a shire owed and what it was owed, she did not see by what rule it stood too uncertain to be shelved, unweighed against nothing, beside a written return that no one had ever asked to prove.

She wrote that she would not press the council to believe An t-Aon Leabhar Deug over anything an Leabharlann already held. She asked only, again, that they be kept where they could be found, and left the rest, as she had the first time, to whoever came after her with the patience to read them.

Nobody saw the letter before she sent it, not even Martha, who would have read it with approval and said so and perhaps, being Martha, have found some way to make it the mills' business as well as her own; Ellen wanted no more behind it than her own name. She sealed it and gave it to the carrier the next market day, and went back to the term's slates, and did not, in the weeks after, ask whether an answer had come, because she had already had the only answer there was going to be, and a second letter would only have dressed the same one differently.

## V. Of the Parsing

The children came back to their benches the first week of an t-Sultain, brown from the summer and restless at their letters, and Ellen, who had spent eleven years deciding what was worth a class's morning and what was not, took Raghnall mac Mhuirich's letter out of her satchel on the second day and read it to them entire, standing at the front of the room with the sun coming in low and yellow through the tall north windows.

She did not tell them, at first, what it was about, only that it was a real letter, lately received, and that she wanted their ears on the words and not the matter, since the matter was hers to carry and the words were a lesson any of them might use. She read it through once without stopping, and then began again from the top, slower, and stopped this time at the end of nearly every sentence to ask a child to say what it did: here was the subject, here the thing done to it, here a clause that leaned on another and would fall without it, here a word chosen, she said, because it let a man say a great deal while appearing to promise nothing at all.

"What does 'not refused' mean?" she asked, at the sentence that held it, and a girl near the stove, quick with her tongue and quicker with her temper, said at once that it meant *no*.

"It doesn't say no."

"It means no all the same, missus."

"Then why doesn't it say so?"

The girl thought about that longer than she thought about most things, turning her slate over in her lap. "Because," she said slowly, "if he said no, you could ask him why. And there's no answering 'not yet.'"

Ellen let that stand a moment before she went on, because it was, she thought, as exact a piece of parsing as she was likely to get out of any of them that year, truer to the letter's working than half the grammar she had meant to teach from it. She showed them the long sentence in the middle, the one that turned on itself three times before it came to rest, and had them find where the turns were, and asked whether a shorter sentence could not have said the same thing, and whether, if it could, there might be a reason a careful man chose not to say it shorter. A boy at the back, who rarely had much to offer on any morning that did not involve sums, said that his father had told him once that a long fence took longer to climb than a short one, whatever height it stood, and the room laughed, and Ellen let them, and did not correct him, because he had not, in her judgment, said anything that wanted correcting.

She did not tell them, that morning, what had become of the request, only that it concerned some books she had made herself over many years, and that the letter was the answer she had had about them; and when the quick girl by the stove asked outright whether the books would go to an Leabharlann or not, Ellen said, in the same plain voice she had used all morning on subjects and clauses, that she did not know, and that not knowing was itself a thing worth being able to say plainly, since a great many grown people spent a great deal of ink trying not to.

The lesson ran past its hour, and she let it, since the day was long and the harvest not yet calling any of them away. When at last she sent them out to the yard she folded the letter back into her satchel, along with the fair copy of her own reply that she had kept for her own record beside the eleventh book, and stood a while at the window watching them scatter across the grass in the last of the low sun, loud and unhurried, as children are who have not yet been told that a table is a place where a true thing can be laid down and left to lie.
