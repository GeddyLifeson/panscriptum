# Of the Hundred Nights

*DE 5*

## I. Of the Barrels at the Slip

The twenty barrels came up the east coast at the end of an Gearran in the hold of a salt-boat of Àth bheag, which had carried salt down to Seann Skell of the south and had nothing to bring home. Her skipper took them because the engineer of Comhairle nan Taighean-solais paid the freight in coin and in advance, and because nobody told him what was in them. He found out off the southern cape, when the sun came out for an afternoon and warmed the staves. By the time he brought her round under the eastern cape and in to the slip at Baile ìseal his crew were sleeping on deck with their coats over their faces, and he told Peigi nic Ascaill, who stood waiting on the wet stones with her bag at her feet, that he would carry pigs for her next time, live ones, but not this.

"It keeps the weather out," Peigi said. "Put your head in a coal-cart and tell me which you'd sooner."

"The coal-cart."

"So would everyone. Help me get them up the slip."

Baile ìseal is a small place. It was some ninety souls that year, in low houses of grey stone along one street that runs down to the slip, with the dunes going away north towards the ford at Àth bheag and the cape going away south into the sea. The tower stands out at the end of the cape, a couple of miles below the town, where Torcall Clachair had it raised in An Aois Choigreach, sitting in a chair at the foot of the headland because he was too old by then to climb. Long before the tower the households of that coast kept a fire on the cape in turn, to bring their boats home in a storm, and the old people of Baile ìseal will still tell you that the light is there to bring boats home, whatever Comhairle nan Taighean-solais has painted over its door. The keepers of the light were the town's watch as well. Sea-thieves had come ashore on that coast within living memory and burned the salt-sheds at Àth bheag, and the town had no other watch but the woman in the lantern-room with a glass.

Peigi had the barrels carted out along the cape by a carter of Baile ruadh, whose family had carried the keepers' coal and meal since the light was lit, and who charged her twice his rate for the smell. He set them down in the yard at the tower's foot, in the lee of the coal-store, twenty black casks in two rows. The chalk on their heads had run in the salt-boat's hold, but the mark could still be read on most of them: a square with a flame inside it, four strokes and a curl. The carter asked what it was.

"The lights' mark," said Peigi.

"The lights have got no mark."

"They have now," she said, and paid him.

She had chosen Baile ìseal in the coach going home from Seann Skell at the year's end, with her pocket-book on her knee and the five towers written down the page. Tobar dhearg was nearest the works, and the whole south coast would have known by the next morning that the engineer of the lights was burning the new stuff under its tower, and so would the house of Brìde on the height above the harbour. The three towers of the north were far, and cold, and their keepers were not people she knew. Baile ìseal was as far from the capital as a tower can be and still be on the island. It kept Na Seann Spioradan, and had no house of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail within a day's walk to take offence; and its old keeper, retired, still lived at the landward end of the yard wall. She had met him twice, years before, on her rounds, and remembered that he had corrected her arithmetic.

Her masters' answer had found her at the capital in am Faoilleach, and she carried it folded inside her coat and had read it enough times to have it by heart. Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, the secretary wrote, saw no objection to a trial of the engineer's burner in the yard of Taigh-solais Baile ìseal, at the engineer's own charge and in her own time, on condition that the light was not touched and the keeper's duties in no way hindered. There was a second paragraph. The secretary had observed that twenty barrels of fuel had been entered to its account at Seann Skell by the engineer's hand, and would be glad to learn under what head.

Peigi had not answered the second paragraph. She meant to answer it with a hundred nights.

The keeper met her at the tower door that afternoon. Seonag nic Thòmais was some years younger than Peigi, broad in the shoulder and red with weather, with the keys of the tower on a strap across her chest and coal-dust ground into the knuckles of both hands. A copy of the secretary's letter had come to her too, and she had it in her apron pocket, and she took it out and held it up without unfolding it, the way a man at a fair holds up a paper that proves the cow is his.

"The yard's yours," she said. "That's what it says. The stair's mine."

"I've no wish to go up your stair."

"That's as may be." Seonag looked past her at the barrels. "That light's burned coal since before my mother was born. Murchadh kept it thirty-one years and I've kept it eight, and there's never been a night it went out. Whatever you've got in those, it doesn't come past the door."

"It won't," said Peigi. "I'll want a man to keep the log. Somebody who knows a light and isn't me. My masters won't take my word for a hundred nights, and it's right not to."

Seonag considered this. Something moved at the corner of her mouth.

"Murchadh," she said. "He's nothing to do all night but sit at his window and tell me I'm burning it too high. You can give him something else to look at."

## II. Of the Spare Lamp and the Log

Murchadh mac Coinnich was seventy-one that spring. His knees had gone, and he walked with two sticks and sat with both legs stretched out before him, but his eyes were as good as they had been when he first climbed the stair as a boy from Baile ruadh, and his hand was square and exact. He had the cottage at the landward end of the yard wall, two rooms and a peat fire, with a window that looked straight up the tower to the lantern. He heard Peigi out without a word, filling his pipe and not lighting it.

"What does it cost?" he said at the end.

She told him: a third less than coal, for the same hours of light, at the works' price; and the price of coal still climbing, and the price at the works falling.

"Who makes it?"

"A company of shipmasters at Seann Skell."

"That's who sells it," said Murchadh. "Who made it first?"

"A man in the north. A Tuathach. They call the stuff by his name."

"Dubhan," said Murchadh. "The carrier from Àth bheag talks of nothing else. He says it's made out of the muck the pits throw up, and it stinks like a burnt boot." He struck a light for his pipe at last. "Well. I'll keep your log. I'll write down what I see and not what you'd like to see, and I'll rule the book myself, and you'll pay me the lights' rate for an under-keeper, since that's what you're making of me."

"Agreed."

"And you'll not argue with what I write."

"I'll argue," said Peigi. "You needn't change it."

He looked at her for a while through the smoke and then nodded, as if that was the answer he had wanted.

The spare lamp was in the coal-store under sacking. It was the tower's storm-lantern, a great box of iron and glass with a silvered bowl at the back of it, which the keepers hung on the gallery rail on the nights when the main grate was drawn and cleaned of its clinker, so that the cape was never dark. It had a grate of its own the size of a man's hat. Peigi took the grate out and threw it on the heap, and in its place she set the burner that the fitters at the works had made to her drawing over the midwinter: a broad round wick in two rings, one inside the other, with a brass drum below it to hold the fuel and a pipe coiled twice round the foot of the chimney, so that the heat of the flame would warm the stuff on its way to the wick. The fuel came out of the barrel thick and slow, like treacle in the cold, and in a cold lamp it would not climb a wick at all. Warmed, it ran like oil. She had worked that out on the bench in the fourth shed at the works in the spring, with the master of the company watching her and not knowing what he was watching.

She had a stage built for it in the yard of old ship's timber from the beach, twenty feet high with a ladder, so that the lamp stood above the yard wall and showed to the sea over the lip of the cape. From the water it would be a second light below the tower's, low and a little to the landward side. She measured the angle three times and had the stage shifted a yard to the north, and the carpenter from Baile ìseal, who had built boats for forty years and never shifted anything a yard for a woman, did it without a word, because the keeper had told him to.

Murchadh ruled the log on the afternoon of the first day, at his table by the window, with a straight-edge and a pot of the keepers' ink. He gave each night one line across two pages. The headings were his own: the night and the day of the month; wind; weather; lit; out; wick; smoke; and at the far right, with the most room, *what the boats said*.

"The boats'll say nothing," said Peigi. "They'll not know it's there."

"They'll know," said Murchadh. "And they'll have opinions. You've never lived in a fishing town."

She lit it first on {{date:VI-0011}}, at dusk, with the wind light from the west and a clear green sky over the land. She warmed the coil with a spirit-cup, as she had practised at the works, and when the stuff began to run she touched a spill to the wick. It took, and crawled round the rings, and stood up in the chimney yellow and a little smoky; and then, as the coil warmed and the draught drew, it lengthened and brightened until it was nearly white, and the silvered bowl behind it caught it and threw it out over the wall. Peigi came down the ladder and walked out to the lip of the cape to look at it from as near the sea as she could get.

Above it, the tower's own light burned at the top of the stair: a coal flame in its great grate, red at the heart and ringed with that dark coloured edge that only the island's coal gives, and all of it made large by the glass. Below it, and to one side, her lamp burned yellow-white and steady, smaller, plainer, and in the clear air nearly as far-reaching. From where she stood she could see both. She stood there until her feet were cold.

When she came back Murchadh had lit his own lamp and had the log open.

*Night the first,* he had written. *Wind W, light. Clear. Lit at dusk. Wick took well. Smoke: some, black, going off to the E. Smell: bad. Boats: none out.*

"Smell's no heading," said Peigi.

"It is tonight," said Murchadh.

The burner clogged at two in the morning. The flame went down on one side and then the other, yellow, sullen, and a coil of black smoke went up the chimney and blackened the glass inside. Peigi went up the ladder in her coat with a lantern in her teeth and found the wick crusted over with a hard black char, like the crust on a burnt pot, which the stuff laid down as it burned. She trimmed it with the scissors and it came up again. At four it crusted again. She trimmed it again. At sunrise, when she put it out, she had slept an hour on a chair in Murchadh's kitchen, and her hands were black to the wrist.

*Out at sunrise,* Murchadh wrote. *Wick crusted twice, trimmed twice. Engineer up the ladder with scissors at two and four. Said a word I will not write.*

## III. Of What the Boats Said

The first fortnight was a fortnight of the wick. Peigi strained the fuel through sailcloth into the drum to take out the grit and the lumps, and it crusted less. She warmed the barrel it came from by the coal-store wall on the sunny afternoons, and it crusted less again. She had the tinsmith of Àth bheag make her a second pair of wick-rings, so that one pair could burn while the other was soaked clean in a pan of the stuff itself, and on the tenth night the wick burned from dusk to dawn with one trimming. Murchadh wrote *trimmed once* under *wick* without comment, and she saw him look at it afterwards with his head on one side.

On the eleventh night a squall came in off the sea after midnight, hard cold rain driven almost level, and the chimney-glass, which was hot as a kettle, cracked from top to foot with a noise like a pistol. The flame staggered and smoked and did not go out. Peigi had three spare glasses. She put one in with the rain running down her neck, and in the morning she had the tinsmith make a hood of sheet iron to go over the top of the lantern and keep the rain off the glass. After that the glasses held.

*Night the eleventh,* the log says. *Wind SE, strong, squalls of rain. Glass cracked at one. Lamp did not go out. New glass in by the engineer, who was wet.*

The nights grew shorter, and the lamp burned less of the stuff each night; Peigi counted it, a finger-mark on the dipstick in the drum each dawn, and wrote the figures in her pocket-book beside the tower's coal. She knew the tower's coal as well as she knew her own wage. Seonag's lad, a boy of Baile ruadh who carried coal up the stair in two buckets forty times a night on a bad night, told her one morning how many buckets had gone up, and after that he told her every morning, without being asked, and she gave him a coin at the month's end, which the keeper did not see.

The coal in those years was poor. It came from the bottom of the failing vein, dear and slaty, and it burned dull and left a great deal of clinker behind. When Murchadh first climbed the stair it had burned with a green edge and a blue heart, he said, and on a still night you could see the colours from the sea two miles off. Now the edge had gone muddy, and in the last year Comhairle nan Taighean-solais had written to all five keepers that the grate was to be kept low on clear nights and fed high only in thick weather. Seonag had read that letter out to Murchadh in his kitchen and then put it in the fire.

The fishing boats of Baile ìseal went out to the long lines in the spring, a dozen of them, open boats with a lugsail and four men, and they came home at dark or after. Murchadh had been right: by the fourth night they knew the lamp was there, and by the tenth they had opinions. He wrote them down in the wide space at the right, in their own words, as nearly as he could get them, with the teller's name when the teller would give it.

*A lamp in a yard,* one said. *Like a star fallen in the nettles,* said another. *Too low. No use to a ship in the open sea.* *Too white; it hurts the eyes.* *Where's the colour gone.* *It smells from half a mile out when the wind's off the land, you'd find the cape by your nose.* An old man who had not been to sea for twenty years came out along the cape on purpose to tell Murchadh that he would not have steered by it in his day, and Murchadh wrote that down too, with the old man standing over him to see that he spelled his name right.

Late in am Màrt the salt-boat came back up the coast and her skipper walked out to the cape to look. He stood at the foot of the stage with his hands in his pockets for a long time and said at last that it was a better light than it was a smell, and that was something. Murchadh wrote it in.

Peigi slept in the day in a room of the keeper's cottage that Seonag let her have, because there was nowhere else in the yard, and ate at the keeper's table, and the two women were civil to each other across the bread. Seonag did not come near the stage. In the evenings she came down the tower door to the yard at dusk with the keys on her chest, and stood a breath in the doorway, looking out to sea and not at the lamp, and went up again; and the tower light would come on above, red, and then fuller, as the lad fed the grate. In the mornings she came down black to the elbows and went to her bed without a word. Once, in the first week of an Giblean, Peigi heard her on the stair in the small hours, going up and down between the coal-store and the lantern with her own buckets because the lad was sick, and counted forty-four journeys before she fell asleep.

On the forty-first night the haar came in.

It came off the sea in the afternoon, grey and wet and thick as fleece, as it does on that coast in the spring when a warm air comes over the cold water; and it lay high on the cape, as a haar will sometimes, with a clear ribbon under it along the face of the water. The tower's light burned up inside it. From the slip you could see a red smear in the grey where it should have been, and nothing else. Peigi's lamp burned below it, in the clear ribbon, and from the slip you could see it plain.

Calum mac Iain's boat was the last in that night. He had gone out farther than the others after the lines, and the haar had found him on the way home. He told the story afterwards at the slip, where Peigi heard it, and he told it more than once, so that the log has two forms of it. In the first he was steering for the smear in the haar, which is all a sensible man could do, when he saw a light under it, low and white, and took it for somebody on the rocks with a lamp and came in to see who was drowning. In the second he knew very well what it was, and had been steering by it for half an hour, and would do it again. Either way he came round the cape with the white light on his right hand and the smear above it and found the slip without touching a stone.

The next morning he walked out to the yard and looked at the lamp for a while, burnt out and black-glassed in the daylight on its stage, and then went to Murchadh's window.

"You'll not write that," he said. "Don't let Seonag see it."

*Night the forty-first,* the log says. *Wind E, light. Haar, thick from the afternoon, lying high. Tower light lost in it from the slip. This lamp seen under it. Wick trimmed once. Boats: Calum mac Iain came in by this lamp and not by the tower. Asked me not to write it.*

Seonag read it all the same. She read the whole log at the end of every week. Murchadh left it open on his table on purpose.

On the eve of Bealltainn the young people of Baile ìseal lit their fire on the dunes north of the town, as they did every year, and put out the hearths of the houses and lit them again from it the next morning. A girl of the town came out along the cape on the morning of the feast with a shovel of the fire in a pail, as her mother had carried it out to the tower in Murchadh's time and her grandmother before that, and the lad took it up the stair to the grate. On the step of the stage she stopped, and looked at Peigi's lamp, and asked whether it was to have some too.

"It lights from a spill," said Peigi.

"It's to have some," said Murchadh from his window.

So the girl climbed the ladder with her pail, and Peigi, who thought it nonsense and knew better than to say so, took the spill and lit it from the Bealltainn coals, and put it in her pocket-book for that night, and lit the lamp with it at dusk. Murchadh wrote *lit from the Bealltainn fire* under *lit*. That was the fifty-ninth night.

## IV. Of the Seventieth Night

The gale came on the seventieth night out of the south-east, off the open sea. The glass had been falling all day in Murchadh's kitchen, and the swell had come in under a fine sky long before the wind did, so that the old men at the slip knew what was coming and kept their boats in. Two did not. They had gone out before dawn to lift lines they had shot the day before, far out to the south-east, and they were not back by noon, and by the middle of the afternoon the wind was up and the rain behind it.

By dusk it was a whole gale. The sea came up the cape in sheets and the spray went over the yard wall and rattled on the coal-store roof like shot. Peigi and the lad had lashed the stage to the wall with the carter's ropes in the afternoon, and she had wired the chimney-glass into the lantern at the top and bottom, and under the hood; and when she lit the lamp at dusk the flame lay over flat in the chimney, and smoked, and roared, and stood up again. She stayed on the ladder with it until she was sure. Then she went down and sat at the foot of the stage in her oilskin with her back to the timber and the dipstick in her lap, and Murchadh opened his window a hand's breadth against the weather so that he could see both lights at once, and sat at it with the log.

The trouble in the tower began a little after midnight. Peigi saw it from the yard before she understood it: the red of the tower light went dull, and then dark red, and then brown, like a coal dropped out of a grate on to the hearth. Smoke was coming out of the lantern's vents at the top, and blowing flat, and the wind was driving it down on to the gallery and round the glass.

She learned afterwards what had happened, from the lad. The roof of the coal-store had lifted in the first of the gale and let the rain in on the heap, and the coal that went up the stair from nine o'clock on was wet. The grate would not draw on it. The wind came down the vent at the top of the lantern as it will in a south-easter, the one wind the vent was never built against, and drove the smoke back into the room; and the poor coal of those years, wet, lay in the grate and smoked and sulked and would not blaze. Seonag raked it and fed it and raked it. She opened the vent and the wind came down it harder. She shut it and the grate died under her hands. The inside of the glass went black with soot. From the sea, the light of Baile ìseal was a red smudge, and then a brown one, and for the best part of two hours, from the second hour after midnight to the fourth, there was hardly any light at all.

Peigi went to the tower door. It was shut, and she could hear Seonag above, shouting to the lad for dry coal from the back of the heap, and the wind in the stair like a beast in a pit. She put her hand on the latch.

"Keep off my stair!" Seonag's voice came down the stone. She could not have seen who it was. She knew.

Peigi took her hand off the latch and went back to the stage.

Her own lamp was burning. It burned low and yellow when the gusts came, and long and white between them, and it smoked, and the smoke went off towards the land in a black rag; and every half-hour she went up the ladder and trimmed it, and Murchadh at his window called the time out to her and wrote it down, and every half-hour the lad came down out of the tower door with his buckets and looked at it and went up again with the dry coal. The drum held. The wire held. The hood held. The chimney-glass steamed and cleared and steamed. And all the while it showed out over the lip of the cape to the sea, low and plain, a second light below the tower, and for two hours the only one.

The first of the two boats came round the cape at about the third hour. Peigi did not see her. She saw a lantern on the water to the south, going up and down in the seas at a great distance, and then going behind the point, and she did not know until the morning that it had been Calum mac Iain's brother's boat. The second came in an hour after, with a torn sail and a man with a broken arm, and her skipper said at the slip in the morning, before he had his boots off, that he had not seen the tower since midnight and had steered for the white light under the cape because there was nothing else to steer for, and had not known what it was, and did not care.

At the fourth hour the tower light came back. Seonag had got dry coal from the back of the heap under the tarpaulin, and wedged the vent half-open with a bar so that the wind could neither blow straight down nor shut it, and at last the grate drew. The red came up through the soot, dull and then fuller, and by the first grey of dawn it was burning as it should. Peigi's lamp burned on below it until sunrise, and she put it out as the sun came up out of the sea to the east, far off, where Ceò Mhanannain lies along the edge of Muir Mhanannain; and then she sat down on the step of the stage because her legs would not hold her.

Seonag came down the stair when the sun was up. She was black from her hair to her boots, and her eyes were red, and her hands were cut about the knuckles from the rake. She stood at the tower door and looked across the yard at the lamp on its stage, dark now, with its glass black and its drum wet, and at Peigi on the step.

"It's lower than my light," she said.

"It is."

"A light that low's no use to a ship in the open sea."

"No."

Seonag looked at it a while longer.

"It didn't go out," she said, and went into the cottage and shut the door.

Murchadh had written the night up before either of them came in. His hand is as square that morning as any other in the book.

*Night the seventieth. Wind SE, a whole gale from dusk, rain. Lit at dusk. Out at sunrise. Wick trimmed every half-hour. Smoke: much, blown away to the land. The tower's light failed from the second hour after midnight to the fourth, for wet coal and a down-draught; the keeper kept at it. This lamp did not fail. Boats: two came in by it. They said nothing about it. They were too wet.*

## V. Of the Hundredth Night

After the seventieth night the lad came down out of the tower every evening at dusk, sent by the keeper, to ask whether the lamp was lit; and when it was, he went up again and said so. Seonag herself did not come.

A roofer came from Baile ruadh in the week after the gale and put the coal-store to rights, and a letter came from the secretary at Cathair dhearg in answer to the keeper's report of the night, asking for particulars of the wet coal and the down-draught, and saying nothing of the yard. Seonag gave Peigi the letter to read at breakfast. It was the first thing she had given her.

Peigi began to write her report in those weeks, a page or two in the day before she slept, at Murchadh's table while he sat by the fire. She had every night in her pocket-book beside the tower's: the fuel burned by the finger on the dipstick, the coal by the lad's count of buckets, the hours from dusk to dawn, the price of each at the works and at the pit-head. She did the sums three times, and Murchadh did them once in his head and found that she had carried a figure wrong in the second, and she did them a fourth time. At that rate the barrels would last the hundred nights and some over. By the end of the trial she had burned eleven of the twenty and some of a twelfth.

The nights shortened. By the start of an t-Ògmhios the lamp was lit late and put out early, and in the few hours between the sky in the north never went all the way dark, but lay green and pale along the edge of the land; and on the clear evenings, far out over the sea to the east, Ceò Mhanannain could be seen lying on the water where it always lies, grey, at the very edge of sight. Peigi had never lived where she could see it. On the calm nights, with the lamp trimmed and the drum full and nothing to do until the next trimming, she sat at the lip of the cape and looked at it. The straight way east had never been found through it. She thought, not for the first time, that a lamp is a strange thing to set against a sea like that, and that the people of Baile ìseal had been setting one there since An Aois Ailein.

The boats had stopped saying things. In the wide space at the right of the log, through the last weeks, the line goes on night after night, *boats: nothing*, and *boats: nothing*, and once *boats: Calum mac Iain asks when it's to go up the stair*, which Murchadh wrote down without a word and Peigi read without one.

On the ninety-sixth night Seonag came down out of the tower door at dusk and crossed the yard to the foot of the stage, where she had not stood in three months. She looked up at the lamp for some time, lit, the flame standing tall and nearly white in its chimney. Then she asked how the coil round the chimney foot was made, and how many turns it had, and why two and not three; and whether the drum would take a bigger charge; and how often the wick was changed in a week of bad weather; and what the stuff did to brass. They were an engineer's questions, and Peigi answered them one by one, as exactly as she could, with the lad standing by with his buckets forgotten in his hands. At the end of it Seonag nodded.

"The stair's still mine," she said.

"I know it is."

"Good," said Seonag, and went up to light her own.

The hundredth night was fair, with a light wind from the west and a long clear dusk. Peigi lit the lamp herself, with a spill, at the hour Murchadh called; she warmed the coil and touched the wick and watched it climb and stand, as she had watched it ninety-nine times before, and came down the ladder and stood a while at the foot. The tower's light came on above it, red, and fuller, as the lad fed the grate.

She trimmed the wick at midnight and at two. There was nothing else to do. She sat on the step of the stage with her coat round her and her back to the timber, and did not sleep, and watched the two lights go out over the sea, one high and one low, and the short night go grey in the north and then pale along the eastern water. When the sun came up she climbed the ladder and put the lamp out.

Murchadh had the log open on the table when she came in, and the pen in the ink. She had thought, in the night, that she might ask to write the last line herself. She held her tongue. It was his book.

He wrote it slowly, and blotted it, and turned the book round on the table so that she could read it.

*Night the hundredth. Wind W, light. Clear; Ceò Mhanannain on the sea to the E, as always. Lit at dusk. Out at sunrise. Wick trimmed twice. Smoke: some. Boats: said nothing about it. They have got used to it.*
