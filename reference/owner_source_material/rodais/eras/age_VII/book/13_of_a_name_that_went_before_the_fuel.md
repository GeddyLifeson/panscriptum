# Of a Name that Went before the Fuel

*DE 11 – 13*

## I. Of the Slip-box

Sìleas nic Rath came to an Leabharlann in the wet spring of the eleventh year of Linn an Dubhain, a niece of a copyist at Àth dhìreach with a fair hand and no family living close enough to keep her from a post so far up the valley. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh had asked the council for a clerk three times in as many years and been answered twice with silence and once with a letter regretting that the office's charge did not run to it; in the end it was Raghnall mac Mhuirich who found the money, out of the keeper's own small fund for binding, and told the council afterwards rather than before. Sìleas was two-and-thirty that spring, narrow-shouldered, quick with a pen, and she arrived at the old building on a wet Tuesday with a single box tied with string and a letter of introduction she never had cause to show, because Beathag met her at the door before she had raised her hand to knock.

"You'll want dry stockings before anything else," Beathag said, and that was the whole of her welcome, and Sìleas took it for kindness, which it was.

She was set, that first month, to nothing grander than copying the shires' returns into the year's ledger in a hand the council's clerks could read without complaint, work any careful child could have done and few had the patience for; but she had, from the second week, a habit of her own that nobody had asked of her and nobody thought to stop. She kept, in a cigar-box begged from Ailig mac Thormoid, who had no more use for it than she had for the cigars that had come in it, a set of slips, one to a scrap of paper, on which she wrote down every spelling she met of the word that was now on every second letter that came up the library road.

There was no one spelling to meet. The council's own minute, the autumn before, had set it down twice in two lines as *dubhan* and *dùbhan*, the second with the grave accent moved a place, as though the clerk who wrote it had not been certain where the weight of the word should fall and had guessed twice rather than once. A letter from the maor of Cathair mhòr wrote it *dùban*, dropping the h entirely, which Sìleas thought at first a slip of the pen until she found it done the same way three letters running from the same clerk's hand, and understood it for a settled habit rather than an accident. Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own minute-book, which came up twice a year for an Leabharlann's copying, wrote it once, in an t-Ògmhios of that year, as *An Dubhan*, with the article and a capital letter, as though the word had become, in that one entry, less a kind of fuel and more a name for a thing there was only one of; and a fortnight later, in the same book and the same clerk's hand, it went back to the small letter and no article, as though the writer himself had thought better of it, or simply forgotten what he had done a fortnight before.

"It's not the spelling that wants marking," Beathag said, the first time she found Sìleas at the box after the day's copying was done, sorting slips by the little pile they made rather than by any order a stranger could have followed. "Any fool can misspell a word. It's that they're spelling it at all, and not the man's name. Read me the maor's letter again — the one from Cathair mhòr, in am Faoilleach."

Sìleas found it and read: "'The price of dùban at the fair here has held above the coal ceiling the court set for it, there being no ceiling yet set for dùban, the court holding it no fuel of the old kind and so no matter for the old order.'"

"There," Beathag said. "A year ago that letter would have said Dubhan's fuel, or the black stuff a Tuathach makes, or some such circling of a thing the writer had no settled word for. Now it has a word, and the word has parted from his name. He is going out of the sentence, the way a man's name goes out of a coin once the coin has been struck long enough. I want every letter marked, from now, the first time it happens in each hand, so we can say when. I don't think why is a thing that can be dated."

Sìleas kept the box faithfully after that, and by the time the box would hold no more slips without a second box beside it, which was some time in the fourteenth year and so beyond the telling of this chapter, she had marked the turn in forty-one hands out of the sixty or so that wrote regularly to an Leabharlann, each at its own month, none of them together, as though the word had come up out of the ground in a hundred kitchens and market squares at once and needed no one to carry it from one to the next.

She wrote her own small note at the front of the box, in the careful hand she kept for anything she thought might one day be read by someone other than Beathag, saying only what the box held and by whose asking; and under it, because she could not quite stop herself, a line that was hers alone and went into no ledger: that a word which does not need a man's name behind it any longer has become, whatever the court of the fair or the council's minute chooses to call it, a thing of its own. Beathag, finding the note some weeks later while looking for a slip out of order, read it twice, put it back exactly as she had found it, and never said a word to Sìleas about it at all, which Sìleas took, rightly, for the nearest thing to praise the chronicler knew how to give a clerk not yet three months in her post.

## II. Of the Letter from Caol shean

In the same spring a letter came from Scoiltean nan Draoidhean at Caol shean, addressed to Raghnall mac Mhuirich as keeper and copied, by his own note in the margin, to Beathag as chronicler, because it concerned, the school's letter said, a matter an Leabharlann might in time wish to have on its shelves rather than in a schoolman's private word.

The school at Caol shean had long been near empty for the better part of two generations, its halls built for three hundred pupils and holding, in most years since the humans' time, fewer than forty; and the letter, which ran to four close-written pages before it came to its point, spoke first and at some length of the school's own poor fortunes before it turned to the matter at hand, in the manner the letter itself called an old habit of scholars, of saying the discouraging thing before the useful one, so that the useful one should not be mistaken for good news undeserved.

The point, when it came, was a word. The school's masters, discussing among themselves — the letter did not say over what occasion, though Sìleas guessed, reading it aloud to Beathag by the window that afternoon, that it had been over supper, there being a lightness to the sentence that no committee minute ever carried — had found themselves in want of a word for a particular kind of thing: an attempt that looked, at the time it was made, like one more failure among many, and proved afterwards, when nothing else had come of the years around it, to have been the one attempt that mattered. The masters had turned the word over among themselves for the school's own case, which had tried and failed at a dozen small revivals over the generations and did not yet know, the letter said with what Sìleas took for a schoolman's dry honour, whether its own latest effort at taking pupils again would prove another failure or the one that held; and out of that turning they had made, from the old root for a first working and the word the north now used for the fuel, a single coined term: *dubhan-tùsail*, a first-attempt-that-was-the-one, which they offered to an Leabharlann, the letter said, for one reason alone: that a word made in a small room ought to be sent somewhere it might be kept even if it was never used.

"They want it in the record," Raghnall said, when Sìleas brought it to him. He turned the page over, as though the back of it might have more to say than the front. "For a word about their own school."

"They want it kept," Sìleas said. "I don't think they much care whether it's used."

Raghnall looked at her a moment, the way a man looks at someone who has said, without meaning to, a truer thing than he had said himself. "No," he said. "I expect that's exactly it." He copied the word into the day's page in his own hand, under the entry that had already noted the letter's coming, and did not use it again that year in anything he wrote. It sat on in an Leabharlann's word-list where he had set it, unused and uncorrected, the one entry on that page that nobody troubled to strike or to build upon.

## III. Of the Line and the Coal Line

Up at Muileann chrom the next year Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn's own minute-book, the one Sìleas had marked for its wandering article, was making a smaller change than any word of Dubhan's, and one that touched Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own name for its own road.

The line from the capital to An Sloc Mòr had been called, since it was laid, Slighe a' Ghuail, in every timetable Comhairle nam Mèinnean had printed and every mouth that used it; and in the spring of the twelfth year Comhairle nam Mèinnean's clerks, drawing up the new season's card, printed it for the first time as only *an t-slighe*, the line, with no coal in it at all. Nobody at Comhairle nam Mèinnean wrote to say why, and no order that Sìleas ever found in the returns explained the change; the timetable simply came up to an Leabharlann in an Cèitean with the old name gone from its own cover, though every entry inside it still spoke, as it always had, of trains and hours and the fare by the mile.

It made no difference to anyone who rode the thing. The drivers at Muileann chrom, who had never in living memory called their road anything but Slighe a' Ghuail, went on calling it that in the yard and at their own firesides, whatever Comhairle nam Mèinnean's printer set on the card; and the carriers who used the road, and the farmers whose fields it ran through, and the clerks at Cathair dhearg who booked freight on it, all went on saying coal line as they always had, so that within the year Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own new name existed nowhere but on the printed cards themselves, a name with no mouth to carry it, while the old name it had tried to set aside went on being spoken by everyone who had any daily business with the road at all.

Beathag, reading the new timetable when it came up with the spring's papers, laughed once, shortly, and set it on the pile with the rest.

"They can print what they like," she said. "A road keeps the name the men on it give it, not the name Comhairle nam Mèinnean puts on the card. I'd sooner trust the drivers." She looked at Sìleas over the pile. "Mark that too, if you're marking such things. Not the word for the fuel this time. The word for what the fuel is taking away."

## IV. Of the Last Roll

Comann nan Gualadairean dissolved itself in an Cèitean of the twelfth year, in the hall it rented at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr above Cuan chrom, by a vote its own last minute recorded as unanimous among the eleven masters who came to cast it, out of a roll that had once numbered more than sixty.

Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh, who had been master eleven years and buried, by his own reckoning given at the pit-head at Muileann dhearg the autumn before, more of the trade than he had ever bound to it, brought the guild's last goods to an Leabharlann himself, on a hired cart, in the first week of an t-Iuchar: its rolls of binding going back to the guild's chartering in the early years of the restored custom, its sick-roll, thin now, with the names of the last dozen hewers it still paid a shilling a week to for the black cough that had ended most of their working lives, and the oath-stone itself, a flat grey block no bigger than a hearthstone, worn smooth on its upper face where two generations of thumbs had rested while a boy or a girl swore the guild's old words over it, words that had promised, among other things, that the swearer would never cut coal for any hand but the guild's own and would die, if it came to dying, with the guild's black on their own.

Sìleas met the cart in the yard and signed for the goods herself, Beathag having gone that week to Àth dhìreach to see her sister, who was unwell; and it was to Sìleas, not to the chronicler she had come to see, that Tormod handed the rolls one by one, naming each as he gave it over, as though a thing so long kept ought not to change hands without at least that much ceremony.

He was three days coming, and had stopped, she guessed from the mud on his coat, more than once on the road to rest a chest that no longer let him ride a full day without it; but he would not sit while the goods were handed over, and stood the whole of the unloading with his hat off in the rain, though she asked him twice to come in out of it and wait by the fire in the porters' room while the men carried the heavier things.

"I've stood at worse handings-over than this," he said, when she asked a third time. "I'll stand at this one too, and then I'll come in, and gladly."

"You'll want a receipt," Sìleas said, when the cart was empty, and felt the word wrong in her mouth as she said it, a thing too small for what had just been handed over.

"I want nothing of the kind," Tormod said. "I want it kept, and I want it said, when anyone asks, that it came here whole and not begged for. That's receipt enough for me." He looked at the oath-stone, which stood now on the yard's flagstones between them, too heavy for either of them to have carried far alone, waiting for the two men Raghnall had sent down to bring it in. "I've sworn eleven masters on that stone in my time, and had eleven sworn on it themselves before that. I don't know what it's for now, in a room with nobody to swear on it. But it oughtn't to be broken up for a doorstep, which is what would happen to it at Achadh dhomhain inside five years, now the hall's sold."

He gave her, last of all, a single folded letter, addressed to no one in particular, which he asked her to see copied into an Leabharlann's record rather than filed loose among the guild's other papers, because he said he had written it to be read, not kept.

Sìleas read it that evening, by the last light at her own small desk in the room she had been given off the old building's stair, and copied it herself into the day's page before Beathag came home, in a hand as close to Beathag's own settled hand as she could make it, because it seemed to her, reading it, a thing that should sit in the record as though the chronicler herself had taken it down. It was not a long letter. It said, among lesser things about what the sale of the hall had fetched and the disposal of what small funds remained, that the guild had outlived the mountain's patience; that a trade bound to a thing in the ground could not outlast the vein by more than a generation or two of pretending otherwise, and that the guild, in dissolving itself before it was dissolved by want, had done the one dignified thing left to it to do.

Beathag read the copied page the morning after she came back from Àth dhìreach, standing at the window with the light behind her the way she always stood to read anything she meant to think hard about, and said nothing for a long while.

"You copied it well," she said at last.

"I tried to make it sound as you would have made it sound."

"You made it sound as he made it sound, which is better." Beathag folded the page back into the ledger and set the ledger on the pile with the others. "There's not a hedging word in the whole of it. Most men, writing the end of a thing they'd given their life to, would have found some way to say it wasn't really ending, or asked to be remembered for it, or promised better days for it yet. He didn't trouble himself with any of that. He said the thing was over and gave the reason and stopped." She looked at Sìleas a moment. "That's a rarer gift than you'd think, in a chronicler's clerk as much as in a guild master. Keep it, when your own time comes to write things down."

## V. Of the Bath-house below Muileann ruadh

Among the guild's smaller goods, listed at the foot of Tormod's own inventory almost as an afterthought, was a bath-house the hewers had built two generations before over Fuarain Theth Muileann ruadh below Muileann ruadh, for the ease of men whose chests the coal-dust had ruined; a low stone building over three basins fed straight from the ground at a heat no fire needed to raise, kept by an old woman of the town on the guild's small wage and open, until that year, to none but hewers and their families on the guild's own card.

The house passed, with the rest of the guild's holding, to the custodians of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail at the southern Seann Skell, the guild having named no other body fit to take a charge it could no longer keep; and the custodians, who owned already the greater bath-houses of the south coast and had never had much use for a small one so far inland, opened it that same summer to any who could pay the old woman's modest fee, hewer's card or none.

It was in an Lùnastal of that year, by the letter the keeper of the house sent up to an Leabharlann at Raghnall's own asking, that a Tuathach family came to the basins for the first time in the house's history: a woman of Doire ghlas, brought south by a married daughter for a chest complaint the northern doctors had no more to offer against, and the daughter's own two children, who had never in their lives been let past the door of any house of the old faith on any errand at all. The keeper's letter said only that she had taken the woman's coin, which was good coin and no different from any other, and shown her to the basin as she would have shown anyone, and that the woman had stayed the better part of an hour and come out saying something to her daughter in a tongue the keeper knew nothing of, though she took it for thanks by the way it was said, and it made the daughter laugh and put an arm round her mother's shoulders in the cold of the yard outside.

"She asked no leave of the custodians," Raghnall said, reading the letter to Beathag and Sìleas both, "and none was asked of her. I don't know that either side thought of it as a thing needing leave at all. The water doesn't know whose chest it's easing."

"Write it down as she wrote it," Beathag said. "Not a word more."

## VI. Of the Damp at Seann Toll

Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh did not long survive the guild he had dissolved. His own chest, which had troubled him for a dozen years before that summer and troubled him worse after it, kept him from the road for most of the autumn that followed; but in am Faoilleach of the thirteenth year, against the plain advice of a doctor at Cathair mhòr who told him a river town in winter was the worst place a man with his cough could choose to visit, he went down once more to Seann Toll, to see, he told the doctor, how Sreath nan Gualadairean did in its first hard frost, and would hear no argument against it.

He was three days at the Row, by Gilleasbaig Ruadh's own account sent up afterwards to an Leabharlann at Beathag's asking, walking the frozen street each morning as he had walked it the winter before, and taking, on the third evening, a fever that settled in his chest within a day and would not lift. Gilleasbaig sent for the nearest doctor, who was at Muileann chrom, half a day off by the river road in the worst of the weather, and sat with him himself through the two nights that came between the sending and the doctor's arrival, in a house of the Row no better built than any other, with a single dubhan lamp burning low on the sill because the fire, Gilleasbaig's letter said, gave less heat than the lamp and Tormod had asked, more than once in his wandering, that the lamp be kept lit and the fire let go out, though he never said why and Gilleasbaig never asked.

He died a little before dawn on the sixth day of an Gearran, in the Row he had walked his people into, among people who were no longer, by any charter, his to answer for. Gilleasbaig Ruadh's letter, which reached an Leabharlann a fortnight later, the river road being what it was in that season, said only that he had gone quietly, that he had spoken little in the last day beyond asking twice after the oath-stone's safekeeping and being told, both times, that it was safe; and that the Row had buried him themselves, in the ground at the edge of the flood-plain that Seann Toll kept for those it had no older claim on, because there was no guild left to claim him and no family near enough to send for in the time the weather allowed.

Beathag entered his death in the day's page herself, in her own hand, the last entry Sìleas ever saw her make without asking the clerk to make it first and copy it after; and she wrote beside the bare date, which was all the entry strictly needed, the one line from his own letter that Sìleas had copied the summer before, that the guild had outlived the mountain's patience, and under it, in a hand smaller than her usual hand, as though she had thought better of writing it large: that he had not.

The hall at Achadh dhomhain, which the guild had sold before it dissolved itself, changed its trade on {{date:VI-0040}}, three weeks after he was buried. Its buyer, a dealer of the town, had the long room cleared of its benches and set two dubhan pumps in the doorway where the masters had once come in to sit, and a tank in the yard behind; and because the lintel over the door was one great stone, and cutting it out would have brought the front of the house down with it, the lintel stayed where it was, with the guild's oath cut into it in letters a hand high, above the pumps. The carters who filled their barrels there read it every time they looked up, those who could read, and the ones who could not had it read to them once and did not ask again.
