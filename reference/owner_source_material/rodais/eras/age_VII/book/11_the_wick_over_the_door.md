# Of the Wick over the Door

*DE 9 – 10*

## I. Of the Roads Before the Lamps

The old mining roads of the kingdom had been made for coal, and coal had kept them, in the years when Comhairle nam Mèinnean's carts went out from every pit-mouth to every market town and a house on the road could live well enough selling beer and oats to the men who drove them. There were inns along every one of the roads, a day's walk or a day's drive apart, and a carrier who knew the roads knew the inns by name before he knew the towns they stood near: the Lion on the north-east road, An Sligeanach Fad' às on the east road, An Taigh-seinnse Òir and An Taigh-òsta Grianach each on its own way south, and a dozen smaller ones between, kept by families who had kept them for longer than any coal charter had run. They lit their yards and their taprooms with oil in glass, on a wick that wanted trimming every hour past dark, and a house was judged along the roads by how clean it kept its wicks and how far its light carried into the yard, so that a carrier coming in tired and late could see, from a mile off in the winter dark, whether there would be a fire and a bed for him or not.

In the ninth year of Linn an Dubhain, though nobody yet called it that, the light along the roads changed inside a single turn of the seasons, and it changed in every house at once, though no house changed on the same day as its neighbour. The Muileann mhin lamp-makers, who had been given leave the autumn before to set their own mark on the lamps they made, now sent them out along every road that a lorry could reach, small round-bellied things of brass and glass with a wick of their own kind that needed no trimming and burned steady in a wind that would have guttered oil to nothing. A house that put in three or four of them at the door and along the yard wall had light enough to see a cart come in from the top of the hill, and light enough, more than one landlord said, to count the coin by without a candle at the till. The lamps cost money that not every house had lying by, and the barrels of dubhan to fill them cost more again, carted out from the depots that were only then opening at the market towns; so the roads did not change together, and for a season and more a carrier travelling from the north-east to the south-west of the kingdom passed from a house lit like a market fair into one still lit as his grandfather's inn had been lit, sometimes within the same day's driving.

The carriers themselves said little about it at first, being men and women whose trade was to move goods rather than to remark on them. They were the ones who saw it whole, house by house, season by season, in a way that no single landlord and no clerk of Comhairle nam Mèinnean ever did, and it is chiefly from what they told afterwards, and from the account-books of the houses themselves, that the change along the roads is known at all. A carrier of the south-west road, asked years later what she remembered first of the new lamps, said only that she remembered the smell of them before she remembered the light, the tar-and-wet-dog reek that clung to a coat left too near the door, and that for a whole season she judged an inn's newness by how strongly it smelled rather than by how brightly it burned.

They judged the houses by other small signs too, that a clerk of Comhairle nam Mèinnean riding the same roads would never have troubled to note. A house that had put in the new lamps kept its stable-boy later, because a lorry could come up the road at any hour and a carrier who saw light from a mile off would drive on to reach it rather than stop short at a darker house nearer by; a house that had not put them in kept its old custom of shutting the yard gate at full dark, because there was no longer any light by which a late traveller could be seen coming, or trusted, once the gate was shut. A landlady who had hung the lamps might tell you, if you asked her over her accounts, exactly what week her trade had turned; a landlady who had not would tell you only that trade was much as it had always been, in a voice that did not always believe itself. The carriers carried both kinds of answer from house to house all that year, along with the grain and the barrels and the letters that were their ordinary trade, and a house's name came, before the year was out, to mean as much for its light as for its beer.

## II. Of the Yellow Lion

On the north-east road, two days' driving out of the capital where the road climbs onto the high moor, stood the house called An Leòmhann Buidhe, which had stood on that spot, by its own reckoning, since before the humans came. It was not a large house. It had a taproom, a kitchen, six rooms for travellers and a long low stable that had held more horses in its day than it held now that half its custom came by lorry, and its landlord was a heavy, slow-spoken man who had kept it from his father and meant to leave it to his son, and who had, until that year, never in his life put anything new into the house that the house had not asked for first.

He hung the new lamps in the spring, four of them along the yard wall and two either side of the taproom door, because a carrier had told him plainly, the autumn before, that houses without them would lose their custom to houses with them inside two years, and he was not a man to lose custom out of stubbornness. The lamps burned clean and steady and lit the yard farther than the old oil ever had, and within the month the taproom was fuller of an evening than it had been in years, men coming in off the road simply to sit where the light was good.

But over the door itself, where an oil lamp had burned since before his father's time, he did not take the old one down. He filled it, and trimmed it, and lit it every evening with the same care he had always given it, so that a traveller coming up the road at dusk saw, above the four new lamps burning steady along the wall, one small wavering flame that had to be watched and tended and could not be trusted to burn the night through on its own.

A carrier who lodged there that summer asked him why, when the new lamps cost him nothing to keep once they were filled and the old one cost him an hour of trimming every night of his life.

"My grandmother lit that one," the landlord said, "the night my grandfather came home from the sea he'd been given up for lost in, and she kept it lit every night after, in case he was late again. I don't believe the wick's been out a night since, bar the one when the glass cracked in a frost and we had it mended by morning." He looked up at it, where it burned above the new lamps' steadier light. "The new ones I keep for the road. That one I keep for the house."

He never gave a fuller reason than that, and none was asked of him again, but other houses along the road began to do the same in the months after, keeping one old wick burning among the new lamps, over a door or in a window that faced the road, and the custom spread faster than anyone could account for from a single inn's grandmother. Some of the houses that took it up had no grandmother's story of their own to hang it on, and lit a wick over the door only because the house up the road had one and looked the better for it; a carrier who lodged at three such houses in a week said that by the second he had stopped asking what each lamp was kept for, since the answer was as often "because the Lion does" as it was any tale of a father lost at sea. Within a year or two a wick lamp kept burning that way, of no more use than the memory it was lit for, or of no memory at all beyond the fashion of keeping one, was called along every road in the kingdom a Leòmhann, after the house where a carrier first thought to ask why, and the word outlived the reason for it in most of the mouths that used it.

## III. Of the Carriage that Needed No Horse

At the southern Seann Skell, where the shipmasters' works had grown in six years from a leased row of salt-sheds into the largest yard of its kind on the coast, the fitters who served under Peigi nic Ascaill had by the ninth year of the count learned to build the dubhan engine into almost anything that moved on wheels: lorries first, then a fishing boat's donkey-engine, then, that spring, a farm cart for a miller of the shire who wanted his grain in to market before his neighbours' horses were harnessed. It was Peigi who thought first of the carriage, or so the works always said afterwards, though she herself, when asked, only said that the Àrd-choimheadaiche had asked her outright whether a man might ride without a horse as well as a cart of grain might, and that she had not been able to think of a reason to tell him no.

Alasdair mac Ìomhair, Àrd-choimheadaiche of the order of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, kept a closed carriage of the old kind for his journeys among the houses of his order, drawn by a pair of matched greys that were, by that year, old themselves and failing in wind on the long climbs to the hill houses. The works fitted a lorry's engine under the carriage's own frame, boxed it in behind a panel so that it showed nothing from the road but a low hum and a smell, and set the driver's seat where the coachman's had always been, with reins for nothing now but a wheel. It took the fitters the better part of a month, working evenings on it besides their ordered work, and Joe Ashby, who had come to the works two years before with two other Tuathaich hands and stayed on as one of Peigi's best, did most of the fitting of the engine itself, though the works' book, when the carriage was shown, named the works and not the man.

On the day it was ready the custodian came down from Seann Skell's house of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail to try it, in his working robes rather than his formal ones, and stood a long while looking at it before he got in.

"It has no horse in it at all," he said.

"No, my lord," said Peigi.

"Then what does it eat, going up the hill to Tobar dhearg?"

"The same as a lorry eats. A barrel behind, and it goes as far as the barrel lasts, and no farther, unlike a horse, that can be fed from any field along the road if the barrel runs dry."

He considered that, and got in, and Joe drove him, the custodian sitting very upright with his hands folded and his eyes on the road the whole way. The road out of Seann Skell climbs for the first two miles before it levels toward the coast, a grade that the old greys had taken at a walk for years, resting twice, and the carriage went up it without slowing, so that the custodian, glancing out at a milestone he knew well, remarked only that he had not thought to see that stone again so soon. Joe said nothing to that, having been told before he ever put his hand to the engine that the custodian's habit was silence on a journey and that a driver who filled it with talk would not be asked twice, and the two of them went the rest of the road that way, the engine's hum under the floor the only voice in the carriage, until they came in sight of Brìde's house at Tobar dhearg, where he was expected that evening for the eve of a small feast that fell in an t-Ògmhios. The custodians and the novices of the house came out at the sound of the engine, having never heard one on that road before, and stood in a line along the steps to see him step down from a carriage that had come the whole nine miles from Seann Skell without a horse ahead of it.

"It is not forbidden," the custodian said, to no one in particular, looking back at the carriage as it stood ticking and cooling in the yard, the phrase he had used five years before of the fuel itself and had never since had cause to take back or to add to. "I do not know that it is blessed, either." He went in to the feast, and the carriage stood in the yard of Brìde's house overnight with a boy set to watch it, in case it should do something no one had thought to warn him of, and did nothing at all but grow quiet and cold, as any engine does, once its fire is out.

Within a few years the council kept such carriages of its own, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean after it, and a carriage that needed no horse ceased to be a thing anyone in the capital troubled to remark on; but it is remembered along the roads as the custodian's carriage first of all, the first one anyone had seen, and the fitters at Seann Skell kept, for a long while after, the panel from its first engine-box, with the mark Joe Ashby had chalked on the inside of it where no report would ever find it, and the mark was his own initials, and nothing more.

## IV. Of Am Buabhall Fortanach

There was no house on the south-west road better known, nor better loved by the carriers who used it, than Am Buabhall Fortanach, the Lucky Buffalo, which stood at a crossing of two roads near Doire shean where five could put up for the night in comfort and thirty could crowd the taproom on a market eve without anyone going without a seat. It had been built by the landlady's grandfather's grandfather in the years when the south-west road first carried coal down to the coast in any quantity, and it had prospered on that coal for four generations, through droughts and through the wars of a kingdom the house had outlasted twice over, and its account-books, kept in an unbroken line back to the house's founding, were the pride of the family that kept it, each ledger signed at its close by the landlady of the year and handed to her daughter or her niece when the house passed on.

By the ninth year of the count the house was kept by Oighrig nic Iain, who had taken it from her aunt six years before, a tall, dry-spoken woman with a scar on one forearm from a kitchen fire in her girlhood and a way of running her thumbnail along a column of figures that every carrier who had watched her cast an account knew and dreaded, because it meant she had found an error and would not let it stand uncorrected for politeness' sake. She had heard of the new lamps the same autumn as every other landlady on the roads, and unlike most of them she had not hurried. Her account-books told her, plainly enough, what the new light would cost her: the lamps themselves, brass and glass and the Muileann mhin mark on each one, more than she cared to reckon at first sight; the barrels of dubhan, carted from a depot two days off, at a price that the depot's own clerk admitted might fall in a year or might not; and against all that, no certain gain, only the word of carriers passing through that a house without the new light would lose its custom to a house with it, which was a thing said easily enough by people who did not keep the books.

"I will hang them," she told her cousin, who fitted lamps for half the houses of that road and had asked her three times that year already, "when I have seen one house on this road ruined by hanging them, or one house on this road ruined by not."

"You'll wait a long while for either," he said.

"Then I'll wait a long while."

Neither happened, in the year and more she waited, but her custom did not fall either, for Am Buabhall Fortanach was the kind of house that carriers went out of their way for whether its yard was bright or not, and she went on filling her oil lamps and trimming her wicks and casting her accounts by their light, while house after house along the roads about her put in the new light and told her, when they met at the fair or the market, that she was a fool to wait, or a wise woman to wait, according to how their own reckoning had gone that season. A carrier who put up at her house most weeks of that trade said afterwards that he never once heard her answer the talk with more than a shrug and a line about counting at the year's end rather than at the fair, and that he had taken her for simple stubbornness until he saw her books himself, and understood that she had never once, in all that waiting, stopped reckoning.

Her own book changed her, in the end, and neither a season's takings nor any carrier's word. In the autumn of the ninth year, closing the ledger for the year past as her aunt had taught her and her aunt's mother before that, she found, ruled up in her own hand at the year's foot, that the house's trade in oil, which had held steady or grown a little every year of her keeping, had fallen for the first time since the ledgers began, by a sum small enough that no single month's entry showed it plainly, but real once the year was cast up whole. It was the first year in four generations that the house's oldest trade had gone backward, and she sat a long while with the book open before her and the last of the day's oil light burning low on the sill, and then she wrote, beneath the year's total, a line that stayed in the book after: *the light is going out from under us, whether we hang the new lamps or not; better to hang them ourselves than have the road hang them for us.*

She sent for her cousin the next week. He came with six lamps for the yard and two for the taproom, and fitted them himself over three days, working from a ladder while she stood below him with the account-book still under her arm, as though she meant to enter the cost of each lamp the moment it was hung rather than wait for the week's end. On the second evening, with half the yard already bright and the other half still lit by the old lamps she had not yet let him take down, a carrier coming up the road in the dusk stopped his cart at the gate and sat looking at the house a long moment before he drove in, and told her, when she asked him what had held him, that he had thought for a moment he had come to the wrong inn, the yard being lit in a way he had never known it.

"You have not," she said. "Come in and I'll show you the account for it, if you doubt me."

When her cousin presented his account for the work and the lamps together she read it through twice, ran her thumbnail down the column as she always did, found no error in it, and paid it without a word of complaint, which her cousin said afterwards was the strangest part of the whole business, stranger even than the lamps themselves. She kept the bill pinned inside the cover of that year's ledger, where a curious carrier who asked to see the house's books could still find it, the whole cost written plain: a year's takings from the oil trade, to the shilling, spent all at once to be rid of the trade that had earned it.

The old oil lamp over the taproom door she did not keep burning beside the new ones, as the Lion's landlord kept his; she took it down the same evening the new lamps were lit, cleaned it, and set it on the shelf above the till, where it stood empty from that day, and where more than one carrier, seeing it there years after and asking after it, was told only that the house kept its reckonings where it could see them.

## V. Of the Two Houses and the Carriers' Song

Farther north, on the old coal road above Cuan chrom, the house called An Taigh-seinnse Mòr had done what few of the smaller inns could afford to do, and built rather than merely lit: a second hall, set back from the road behind the old one, raised over the summer of the ninth year and opened in an Dàmhair, lit throughout with dubhan lamps set into the walls themselves rather than hung from chains, so that the hall showed no lamp at all to a stranger's eye, only a steady even light with no flame anyone could point to. The landlord let the new hall for weddings and for wakes, at a price the old taproom could never have commanded, and that same spring, when Comann nan Gualadairean, its own hall at Achadh dhomhain put up for sale for want of members to keep it, asked whether it might hold its meetings there until it found a roof of its own, he let the hall to the guild as well, at less than he might have asked of a wedding party, because his father had hewed coal on that road before he ever kept an inn on it, and he said he would not charge a guild dying of the very trade his family had left for its own last meetings under his roof. The guild's rolls were thin by then and its meetings shorter than they had once been, but for two years and more the hall above Cuan chrom heard the old business read out under new lamps, by men whose fathers had cut coal by nothing brighter than a wick clipped to a cap.

The guild's master, an old hewer whose name the hall's own account did not trouble to keep, thanked the landlord at the first meeting held there, standing under lamps set into a wall his own trade had never built with, and said that a hall was still a hall whoever's light it burned. The landlord answered only that he hoped the guild would have the use of it a good while yet, though both men in the room that evening knew, from the guild's own thinning rolls, that the hope was more kindly meant than likely to be kept.

Farther east, on the south-east road near Cnoc dhìreach, a smaller house called An Taigh-òsta Grianach, went further than any inn on any road that year. Its landlady, widowed young and running the house alone with two grown sons, rebuilt it entire over the winter, lamps in every room down to the meanest, a pump of its own set into the yard wall where a carrier low on fuel could fill his barrel before he had so much as taken a room, and a sign painted new over the door that named, under the house's old name, what a traveller could buy there: *beds and fire*. No house on any road had sold fuel alongside a bed before that spring; within the year half the great houses did the same, but hers was first, and the carriers said of her, for as long as the house stood, that she had understood before any of them what a road ran on now, and had simply built her house to match it.

It is from the carriers, in the end, more than from any house's own book, that the fullest account of that year along the roads survives, for they alone travelled the whole length of them and saw every house in its turn, bright or dark, new-lit or old, and carried word of one house to the next faster than any clerk of Comhairle nam Mèinnean ever carried a report. In the tenth year, at a fair where carriers of half a dozen roads met as they did every spring to trade news and hire and complaint, someone set a verse to a tune the carriers already used for a droving song, about the wick kept burning over a door for no more use than the memory of it, and the tune caught before the words were even settled into their final order, so that within a season it was sung, in one form or another, from the north-east road to the south-west, by carriers who had never seen An Leòmhann Buidhe and did not always trouble to get its name right. It runs four lines and a turn, and says only that the old light and the new light burn side by side a while in every house before the old light goes out for good, and that a wise house keeps one wick a little longer than it needs to, so as not to forget too quickly what it burned before.

No one along the roads could say, by the time the tune was old, who had made the words first, and no house claimed the making of it, though more than one landlord hummed it without noticing he did, filling the new lamps by their own steady light with the old song about the wick going on in his mouth.
