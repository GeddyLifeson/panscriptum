# Of the Four Baskets

*DE 27*

## I. Of the Lot at the Southern Seann Skell

The twelve came up out of the one open face of the Sloc Mòr in the first days of an Dàmhair with four baskets between them, no more and no less than the year's cutting had ever come to since the Rite took the working over, and the maor's own cart carried the baskets down to Achadh dhomhain station roped under a tarpaulin against the rain, and from there they went south by the coal line that no longer carried coal, in a van kept for nothing else that week, to the southern Seann Skell, where the houses of Brìde's order had kept the lot on the same morning every year since a working was first given them.

Alasdair mac Ìomhair drew it that year as he had drawn it every year of his office, an old man now, past his eighty-fifth summer by his own account and past it by a season or two more by the reckoning of the clerk who kept his house's roll, and the priests who stood about him in the yard behind Brìde's house had taken, these last few years, to setting a low stool at his elbow without being asked, which he used or did not use as the morning's cold decided for him. That year he stood the whole of it. Four baskets and nine houses waiting on them: the two Seann Skells, Ros dhomhain, Cathair mhòr, Cnoc bheag, Seann Bhral, and three lesser houses of the coast whose own priests had walked or ridden two and three days to be there, each with a slip of paper bearing its house's mark folded into a cup that had once, before the Rite took to keeping its lots in a silver bowl brought out every year from the house's own treasury, been an ordinary kitchen basin.

He drew four slips from the nine, one hand into the bowl and the other steadying himself on the shoulder of the boy who stood there for the purpose rather than on the stool, a novice of fifteen who had been told only that morning that this would be his office from now on and had not slept the night before for the weight he had put on it. The four houses drawn that year were Cnoc bheag, Seann Bhral, the western Seann Skell, and, by a chance the priests standing about did not remark on though several of them thought of it, the southern house among them, so that of the four baskets one need travel no farther than across the yard.

"It is always the same four baskets," Alasdair said afterward to the young priest who had come down from Ros dhomhain that year to see the lot drawn for the first time, "and never the same four houses twice running, though I could not tell you the last time it fell as it fell today, with our own house among the four. I have seen this lot drawn, by my own count, six-and-fifty times, as a clerk of this house and then as its master, and I could not tell you either what governs it, beyond that it keeps to no pattern a man watching year on year could swear to." The young priest asked him whether he thought the coal itself, cut so few a basket at a time now that the vein was all but spent, would still be drawn for at all in ten years, or in twenty. Alasdair looked at him a while, in the way of a man deciding how much of an answer a question deserves, and said only that the mountain had given the houses of Brìde's order altar-coal since before either of them was born, in baskets that had once numbered forty and more, and that a man his age had learned not to promise a young man what the mountain would do next, having been wrong about it, by his own reckoning, more often than he had been right.

The yard behind Brìde's house that morning stood ankle-deep in the first hard rain of the season, and the priests who had walked from the lesser coast houses came in with their hems black to the knee and stood steaming a little near the kitchen door before the lot was called, so that the drawing, when it came, was done under a sky the colour of the coal it decided the fate of, with the silver bowl held out at arm's length by the novice so that the rain should not spot the folded slips before Alasdair's own hand went into it. One of the coast priests, a woman of a house so small it had never yet had the lot fall to it in living memory, stood through the whole business with her own eyes fixed less on the bowl than on the four baskets, set down on a trestle by the wall under a second tarpaulin, and said afterward to no one in particular that she had come three days' walk to see coal drawn for by lot that would once, in her grandmother's own telling, have been carted to the coast houses by the wagon-load and never missed.

The twelve hewers who had cut it were not there to see it drawn, the cutting and the drawing having been kept apart, by old custom, since before any priest now living could say why; but word came back to them at Achadh dhomhain, by the same post that carried the maor's ordinary returns, of which four houses had had the lot that year, and the old man among them, the one who had sworn the guild's oath once already in his life and had thought it the last oath a coal-cutter's trade would ever ask of him, said, on hearing that the southern house had drawn its own basket back again, that it was the kind of thing the vein did when it had a mind to be droll, and that he had stopped, some years before, trying to read anything further into it than that.

## II. Of the Lamps Lit Early

Sìleas nic Rath had the maor's return on the altar-coal on her own table at Muileann chaol within the fortnight, one line among the ordinary autumn returns, and she entered it in the ordinary column, four baskets cut and shared by lot among nine houses, the southern house among the four that year, in the same flat hand she gave the fair's takings and the regiments' postings; and she thought, setting the pen down, of baskets that had once numbered forty and more, shrunk to four, and let the thought go no further than the line already written, having long since made it a rule of her own office not to write in the open chronicle more than a return required of her.

It was some weeks later, in the third week of an t-Samhain, that she came to the day she had known since the spring was coming and had put off, without quite naming to herself that she was putting it off, for as long as the ordinary business of the season let her. The daylight that year failed earlier at Muileann chaol than any clerk of the Library could remember it failing, or so old Ailig the copyist said, standing at the reading-room window one afternoon with his coat still on, and Raghnall had given the same order he gave every year at this turning, that there would be no fire before the fire could be spared and none after dark at all, so that by the middle of the month the whole gallery kept its lamps lit from three in the afternoon and put them out again the moment the last light off the river gave enough to see a page by in the morning.

Sìleas lit her own lamp that afternoon at a little after three, the sky over the coal line already the colour of slate, and drew toward her the ledger she kept apart from the day's ordinary returns, the one bound in the plain grey boards Beathag had always used for it and that Sìleas, in the seven years since she took up the office, had never once thought to have rebound in anything finer. She had written the foot of every year's page in it since she became chronicler, a single entry each Dùbhlachd, and she had meant, sitting down that afternoon, to write no more this year than she had written the year before and the year before that: a line on the Treasury's roll, a line on the regiments' stations, a line on the state of the coal line and the state of the north.

She found, with the pen in her hand and the lamp burning steadily against a window already dark, that the line she had meant to write would not come whole, and that some other line had been forming itself in her, unbidden, through the whole of that autumn's ordinary business: through the maor's return on the four baskets, through the carrier's yard-talk of the year before that had first taught her the word seann-chunntas, through Ellen Marsh's civil letters that never once asked for anything the Library was willing to give, through the guard that stood at her own gate over a room she herself had never been let open. The line, at last, went down in the same plain hand she gave every other entry: that the kingdom's record was whole in every part but two: the war, which was sealed, and the north, which had not been asked; and she sat some while after the ink had dried, the lamp burning down a little in the draught from the window, before she judged the line fit to stand as it was and closed the ledger upon it.

She thought, closing the ledger, of Beathag, dead now a year at her sister's house at Àth dhìreach, and of the rule Beathag had given her the day she laid down the office: write what you are let, and write down what you are not. Beathag had never, so far as Sìleas's own reading of five-and-twenty years' entries could show her, written a line like the one now drying under her own hand, a line that named a silence rather than a fact; and Sìleas wondered, sitting a while longer than the ledger required, whether her old teacher would have judged it a chronicler's proper business to write of a silence at all, or would have held, as she had held about so much else, that a silence left unwritten was a silence better kept than named. She had no one left to ask. Raghnall would have told her, had she put the question to him, that a keeper's business was to keep what was given him and no more, which was Raghnall's own answer to nearly everything, and not, this once, the answer she wanted.

She did not show the line to Raghnall, though he asked her, crossing the yard the next morning, whether she had closed the year's account yet, as he asked her every year about this time; she told him only that she had, and he did not press her further, having long since learned, keeping a library five-and-twenty years, the shape of an answer a chronicler has decided not to give beyond what she has already given.

## III. Of the Regiments' Muster and the Còmhrag's Last Bout

The returns that reached her table through the rest of that month kept, as they always kept, no order she would herself have chosen. The nine regiments' winter musters, taken on the same three days at the nine towns where they had first been raised, showed three thousand and some three hundred men still on the rolls and three-and-twenty guns still serviceable, figures within a hand's breadth of the year before and the year before that, so that a clerk reading only the muster returns might have supposed, Sìleas wrote, that the kingdom kept nine regiments the way a house keeps nine chairs, out of old habit and against a need that no longer came to call for them; she set the thought down and struck it out again before the ink was dry, judging it nearer to a chronicler's own opinion than to the plain fact a return was meant to carry, and entered instead the bare figures and the nine towns' names.

The Còmhrag's own reckoning for that year, sent up from Ceann leathan by the fair-wardens with the gate's own takings, closed a business Sìleas had opened in the autumn: the ninth regiment's man, having won it the summer before, had stood again that season in a bout the wardens had added, at the crowd's own asking, for the year's champion against all comers, and had lost it in the third exchange to a hewer's son of Cnoc bheag, twenty years old and new to the field, so that the year's championship passed, in the fair-wardens' own dry phrase, "from the regiments back to the shires, as it had stood before the regiments came to it," a line Sìleas copied whole into her return, finding in it, though she wrote nothing of the finding, as plain an answer as the Còmhrag had ever given anybody to any question about which side of that year's account was truly winning.

## IV. Of the Telling at Doire ghlas

Ellen Marsh kept her own school at Doire ghlas through that same Dùbhlachd as she had kept it every winter for twenty years, in the one long room behind the meeting-house with a stove at either end and never enough turf for both, and on the evening of the shortest day she let the youngest children go early, as she let them go every year on that one evening, and kept back only the eldest dozen, the ones old enough to hold a pen steady and be trusted with a page of the eleventh book to copy without blotting it.

They copied by turns that evening, two at the table nearest the good stove and the rest waiting their turn with slates instead, while Ellen read out, from a book already twice copied and now going into its third hand, an account a fisherman's widow of Caol mhòr had given her nine years before of her own husband's last season before the war took the boats for its own use; and when the copying was done and the ink blotted and the eleventh book put away in the chest under the window that held all eleven, she banked the good stove and told the dozen, as she told them every year on this one evening, the telling that had no book of its own and needed none, having been carried whole in the mouths of the north since before any book was kept at all.

She told it as her own mother had told it to her, of a first canoe put out from a shore no one now living could name, carrying a small crew who had heard, or believed they had heard, that an isle of the blessed lay somewhere beyond the mist that hung always on the sea to the north and east, and who had gone into that mist not knowing whether they would come out the far side of it or nowhere at all; and she told, as her mother had told her, of the fire the crew were said to have carried with them in a clay pot packed in wet moss against the salt spray, tended turn and turn about the whole crossing through, because a canoe that lost its fire in that mist, the telling said, was a canoe that would not find the isle again, whatever the mist chose to do with it.

One of the eldest, a boy of thirteen whose own father cut peat for half the row and who had heard the telling every winter of his life without once, until that evening, thinking to ask anything of it beyond the asking a story is owed, put up his hand when she had done and asked her, in the plain way children ask a thing that has only just occurred to them, whose fire it had been, the one the canoe carried, seeing as the telling named only the steersman, Naomh Breandan, and none of those who tended the pot and the isle, when they reached it, was said to have had fires enough of its own already burning on its shore to guide them the last mile in. Ellen told him what her own mother had told her when she had asked the selfsame question at very near his own age: that the telling did not say, and that a fire carried a long way by people whose names the telling had let go was, in her own mother's judgment and in her own, no less a fire for that, and perhaps a better one, there being few enough things in the world that stayed the same fire the whole way through without some hand or other, along the road, laying a new claim to it.

The boy considered this a while, turning his slate over in his hands, and said that it seemed to him a hard thing, all the same, that a fire should be carried so far and kept so faithfully and never once, in the whole telling, be told whose it was; and Ellen, banking the last of the stove for the night and sending the dozen out into a darkness that had come down long before the kingdom's own almanac called it evening, told him only that she had thought the very same thing herself, most winters of her life, and had never yet been given, by telling or by book, an answer she trusted better than the question.

## V. Of the Pass-Table and the Shortest Day

By the last week of an t-Samhain the returns from the north had thinned to their ordinary winter trickle. The sixth regiment's pass-table at the boundary stones, kept under a canvas awning the maor's own clerks struck and pitched again each morning against whatever the weather did overnight, reported fewer crossings with every week the light shortened, the last of the autumn's carters having gone south before the roads closed and few enough Tuathaich troubling, once the days shortened past a certain point, to make a journey a printed sheet could still turn back at the clerk's own say-so. One clerk's return that quarter noted, in a line no order had asked him to add, that an old woman of Baile chrom had come to the table three mornings running to ask after a pass for a granddaughter gone south the year before to nurse in the capital's own infirmary, and had been told each morning that no pass had come up from the capital in her granddaughter's name, and had walked the long road home again each time without asking the clerk's own name or leaving hers; Sìleas copied the clerk's line whole, judging that a return which troubled, of its own accord, to record a woman's three wasted mornings had told her something the pass-table's bare count of crossings could not have told her alone, though she added no comment of her own beside it. The Moot's own minute-book, copied south as it was copied every quarter under Peter Hale's hand, added little that autumn to what she had already entered in some other column: the wool sold at its northern day, the tithe collected without protest, the eleven books still kept in the north and still not named in the Library's own catalogue, which listed, as it had listed for ten years, the tabled request and nothing beside it.

She wrote the last entry of the year on the one-and-twentieth of an Dùbhlachd, the shortest day the almanac gave the kingdom, in the same grey-boarded ledger and by the same lamp, lit that day, by Raghnall's own order, before the noon bell had finished ringing. The Moot's minute for the day, come up with the ordinary post that same morning, gave the shortest day one line, as the Moot's minute gave every year's shortest day one line, noting only that the Moot had met, that Peter Hale had read the minute-books' unbroken tally since Walter Hale's first meeting, and that the twelve had gone home before the light failed altogether; and Sìleas copied the line whole, as she had copied it every year of her office, into the day's page, and then, under it, wrote the entry she had been carrying, without quite knowing she carried it, since the four baskets first crossed her table two months before.

The coal, she wrote, was all but gone from the mountain that had given the kingdom its coin and its kings, and the trade that had grown up in its place ran now on a name that had once belonged to one man alone, out past the north-western capes since the autumn under a charter narrower than any leave the kingdom had granted since the Severance. The Dia-thìrich held the capital and held the sea. The Tuathaich held the north, and held with it their own Church, and a grievance five years old that neither side had found a word for until the north made one itself, and a telling, kept in their own houses on their own night, of Naomh Breandan's first crossing to an isle of the blessed that the kingdom which had twice put down their arms still called, when it troubled to call it anything, an old people's tale and no more.

She wrote nothing of what might come after, having no leave from the record and no gift of her own for it, and closed the ledger, that short afternoon, upon the same rhyme she had heard first, years before, from a Tuathach carrier standing in the Library's own yard with salt fish for the kitchen and no thought that a chronicler at an upper window was near enough to hear him, and had heard once or twice every winter since from some northern mouth or other, asked as a child asks a thing it has been told never to expect an answer to: black the coal, and black the dubhan, but whose is the fire. She set the words down as she had first heard them, in the north's own tongue and then in the tongue the kingdom kept its rolls in, and gave them, at the foot of the shortest day, {{date:VI-0157}}, no answer of her own, there being none in the open record, so far as her years of keeping it had shown her, that the kingdom had yet troubled to give.

Outside her own window the last of that short day's light had already gone from the coal line and from the yard where the sentry still stood his hour at the Library's gate, and Sìleas sat on a while with the closed ledger under her hand and the lamp burning low. Somewhere north of the boundary stones, she knew without needing any return to tell her, the same night had already come down over towns whose wool sold on its own day and whose dead the regiments' own stone did not name; and she sat with that plain fact and the rhyme together a while longer, banking her own small fire at last with the same hand that had entered every line of the year now closed, and found in herself, turning the two over together in the last of the lamplight, no word to set beside them that the open record had not already given her.
