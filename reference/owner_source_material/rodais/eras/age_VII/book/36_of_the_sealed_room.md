# Of the Sealed Room

*DE 24*

> Let the whole record of the war be shut against every reader for fifty years, and let the keeper hold the one key.
>
> — the council's order, am Faoilleach, DE 24

## I. Of the Key

The order came up to Muileann chaol in the first days of am Faoilleach, carried by a rider who had changed horses twice at Àth dhomhain and once more at the ford, and who told Raghnall mac Mhuirich, handing it over on the doorstep of the old building with the snow still on his shoulders, that he had been given no leave to wait for an answer, only to see it delivered and ridden back. Raghnall read it standing in the doorway, in the cold, because the rider would not come in out of it, and read it a second time at his own desk before he sent for Sìleas.

It was four lines long and said what it meant to say without any of the softening a council's clerk usually gives an order of that kind. The whole record of the Long War, from the first muster to the last, was to be put out of the reach of every reader, of whatever office or standing, for fifty years from that day. A room was to be found for it in the Library's own keeping, and the key was to be held by the keeper and by no one else, and the keeper was to answer to the council, and to no reader, for what was done with it. There was no reason given. There never is, in an order of the council's own hand; the reasons, when there are any set down at all, go in a minute the Moot's own clerks never see and this book may not read.

Raghnall had kept the Library five-and-twenty years by then, and had seen, in that time, one chronicler lay the office down and another take it up, and he read the order through twice and said, when he had finished, only that it was the first time in the kingdom's whole history that any part of the record had been shut against its own chronicler, and that he did not think Ailean Leabhar, who had said once that the Library settles nothing and only keeps, would have thought much of the distinction the council's clerk had just drawn between keeping a thing and keeping it from a reader.

"He said it settles nothing," Sìleas said. "Only keeps."

"He never foresaw an order asking the Library to keep a thing from its own reading-room."

She asked him, because there was as yet no clerk of her own in the room to ask it in her stead, whether he meant to argue the order, and he said no, in the same flat voice he had read it aloud in, and when she asked why not, since he had argued smaller things with the council before and won, he told her that a keeper who argued an order of this kind risked being answered with a second order naming a different keeper, and that he had rather keep the room himself, badly if it came to that, than hand the key to some clerk of the council's own choosing who might not trouble to keep it at all. She had no answer to that, and did not try to make one; she only asked, before she left his room, whether she might at least be told who at the council's table had proposed the sealing, and he said he did not know, and that the order, as she had already seen, gave no name for anything, not the reason, not the proposer, not even, in its own four lines, the war it meant.

There was a room at the end of the new wing's lower gallery that had held nothing for years but the broken binding-press nobody had troubled to carry down to the smith, and a scatter of tallow ends, and the smell, in wet weather, of the river coming up through the stone. Raghnall had it cleared in a day, the press dragged out and the tallow swept up and burned, and a mason sent for from the town to fit a door of oak two inches thick where a plank door had stood since the wing was built, with a lock the smith made new to no other key in the kingdom. It took the mason and the smith between them the better part of a week, working through the worst of the month's weather with a brazier for warmth and their breath standing white in the room even so, and Raghnall stood over the fitting of the lock himself on the last morning, because he judged, he told Sìleas afterward, that a thing built to keep a secret from the whole kingdom for fifty years deserved at least one pair of eyes on it who meant to keep the secret honestly and not merely competently.

Sìleas brought her own record to the door on the appointed morning herself, and would let no clerk carry it for her. It was the fuller record she had kept beside the open chronicle since the first year of the war, four boxes of it by then, close-written and cross-referenced in her own hand, everything the open record left out set down in it as plainly as she had been able to set anything down while the war still ran: the regiments' movements she had not been let publish, the numbers of the column's dead that the corporal's requisition for lime had only hinted at and that she had gone back afterward and tried, as honestly as the surviving papers allowed, to reckon for herself; three letters from Doire ghlas that Ellen Marsh had sent her under seal and asked her to keep rather than print, because the families named in them were still living and had not asked to be read by strangers; a single page, unsigned, that had come to her by a hand she never learned, setting out what a boy of the western hills had told a stranger at a market about the last night before Muileann ghlas, which she had judged too near the tellings and too far from proof to put in any chronicle bearing the Library's own name, and had kept anyway, because she judged also that a thing might be true and still unfit to print, and that a chronicler's business was to keep both kinds and know which was which.

She had carried the four boxes up from her own room herself, one at a time, on the two days before, and set them just inside the new door, and on the morning of {{date:VI-0128}} she stood in the doorway a while before she went in to lay the last of her own working papers on top of them, not because she doubted what she had decided to give up, she said afterward, but because a woman does not often get to stand at the door of the one room in the kingdom built to keep her own hand's work from her, and she thought it worth a moment's looking before she went in and did it.

Raghnall turned the key himself, in the new lock, with the smith and the mason gone and no one else in the gallery to see it done. It was a plain iron key, longer than most, and it turned with a sound neither of them had thought to ask the smith to make quieter, a single hard click that ran the length of the stone gallery and came back to them off the old wing's far wall before it died. Raghnall put the key on its own cord round his own neck, under his shirt, and has worn it there since, by every account this book has found, and he has been seen, more than once, by copyists who worked late enough to see him lock the reading-room for the night, to lay his hand flat against his own chest for a moment before he blew out the last lamp, as a man does who is making sure of something he has already made sure of a hundred times before and cannot quite stop checking.

## II. Of the List She Was Allowed

What stayed open, when the door had been shut on the rest, was less than a chronicler of thirteen years at the desk might have wished to keep and more, Sìleas has told her own clerk since, than she had expected the council to leave her. Three things: a list of the places the war had touched, town by town and post by post, with no number of dead set against any of them; a list of the regiments and fleets present at each place, by name and by date; and the counts she had already been permitted to give in the open chronicle before the order came, which stood as they stood and were not called back, though nothing was to be added to them from that day.

It was a narrow allowance, and Sìleas kept to the letter of it with a care her own clerk, a girl of nineteen new to the copying that winter, found at first hard to understand, since the girl could see for herself, reading the open list over Sìleas's shoulder some months into the work, that the same list, read carefully enough by a reader who already knew the places and the regiments both, told a good deal more than its own bare columns seemed to say. Sìleas did not deny it. She told the girl, the one time the girl asked her outright, that the council had shut the drawer, not the shape of the room the drawer stood in, and that a chronicler who kept an honest list of places and regiments, and let a reader's own wit do the rest, was keeping the letter of an order she had never been asked to keep in spirit as well, because no order had troubled to ask that of her, and she did not propose to offer it unasked.

The list, as it stands, runs to some forty places by name, from Àth leathan where the war had opened to Muileann ghlas where it had closed, each with its regiments set against it in the order they had come and gone, and against each place, where she had been let give it, the count the council had allowed before the sealing and not one figure more. Cnoc thais, taken in an Lùnastal of the second year, kept the number of holdouts the open record had always given for it. The thorn stand at Muileann ghlas kept the three-and-twenty the corporal's list had named. Nowhere on the whole open list did any number stand for the column's own dead, because no number had ever stood there before the sealing either, and Sìleas judged, setting the list to rights that first week, that an order telling her to add nothing was no bar to her leaving a blank space exactly where a blank space had always been, in case some later hand, fifty years on, cared to ask why it was blank and went looking, as she had gone looking herself, for the requisition that answered it without quite saying so.

She wrote, at the head of the open list, a single line she had not been asked to write and did not strike out when she had written it: that this was the whole of what the Library's own chronicle held open for the war years, and that the rest of the record existed, and was kept, and would be given to whoever asked for it honestly on the day fifty years should end. It was, she said to Raghnall when he read it over her shoulder, the nearest thing to a promise she was able to make to a reader she would not live to meet, and she thought a chronicle owed its readers that much honesty about its own gaps, whatever it owed the council about filling them.

Raghnall let the line stand. He told her, folding the page back into its place among the day's other returns, that he had once told a young chronicler new to the office that the Library keeps and does not chase, and that he stood by it still. Keeping a thing honestly, he had come in five-and-twenty years to think, sometimes meant telling a reader plainly that a thing was being kept from them, and where, and until when, rather than letting the gap in the shelf speak for itself and be taken, by some later hand with less patience than hers, for carelessness or for shame.

## III. Of the Cairn and the Tithe

The Tuathaich of the western hills had begun, before the frost was properly out of the ground that spring, to carry stone up to the field at Àth leathan where the first great battle had been fought three years before, and by the time the snow was gone from the higher ground they had a cairn there taller than a standing man, raised without any leave asked of the seventh regiment whose post lay not half a mile below it on the road. The regiment's captain, a Dia-thìreach of Cathair dhearg new to the post that year, wrote to the Board asking whether he was to move it, and was answered, some weeks later and in terms that told him a good deal less than he had hoped, that the Board saw no charge laid on him to move stones off a field the Crown had no present use for, and that he might use his own judgment in the matter so long as no arms were found under them.

He used his own judgment, and it was, by every account this book has found, a plainer thing than the Board's letter had troubled to be: he told his patrols to walk past the cairn as they walked past any other stone on that road, and to count the stones if they had a mind to, and to report nothing of it unless they found something under one worth reporting. The patrols have walked past it since, and it has grown, by ones and twos, every time they passed, until it stands, by the reckoning this book has had from a corporal who serves at that post, higher than any stone the regiment had ever troubled to raise for its own dead, though it carried no names, and no plaque, and nothing at all to say whose field it stood on or why.

A woman of the town, who had lost a brother to the fighting on the bank there and would not say, when asked, which side he had died fighting for, was seen more than once by the same corporal to walk out to the cairn alone in the early morning before the patrol came by, and to set one stone on it, never more than one, and to stand a while afterward with her hand flat against the top of the pile before she walked back down to the town. The corporal never asked her name, and never reported the visits, holding, as he told it afterward, that a woman who carried one stone at a time to a field where her own brother might or might not lie was doing nothing his captain's letter had told him to trouble himself over, whichever side the stone was meant to answer for.

At Cathair dhearg that same spring the Treasury carried the wartime tithe forward on the pumps for another year, as it had carried it forward every year since the war's first month, and no minute of the council records that anyone there so much as raised the question of letting it lapse. A tithe laid for a war's sake and never once revisited once the war was won is not, Sìleas wrote in the open chronicle that year, a thing this book need call by any harder name than the plain one; it is enough to set down that it was never struck out, and to let a later reader draw whatever conclusion the plain fact seems to want drawn.

In the same season the Treasury's clerks fixed the market-due, on every sale a town made into its own market and on every sale carried between one market and the next, at seventeen in the hundred, and set a clerk of their own in each of the sixteen chartered market towns to take it and keep the town's own roll of what was sold and by whom. Caol ruadh, whose charter the council had granted the year before, the sixteenth and the last such charter the kingdom would grant, had its first Treasury clerk that spring, a young man out of Cathair dhearg who lodged, his own letters home say, over the harbourmaster's own storeroom for want of any better room in a town that had never before had cause to house an officer of the Crown at all. The wartime tithe on dubhan the Treasury kept apart from the new market-due, on its own line at the pumps, as it had kept it from the first month of the war, so that a barrel of fuel paid twice over before it left the pump, once to the war that had made the fuel's price what it was and once to the market that sold it on, and no clerk of the Treasury's, so far as this book has found, ever wrote a word to say the two payments sat oddly together on the same barrel.

## IV. Of the Contracts

The Guild of Fuel-Fitters at Seann Skell took, that same spring, the contract for every engine the nine regiments kept and every hull the four fleets ran, and doubled its own rolls within the year to answer it. Peigi nic Ascaill signed the contract on the guild's behalf at Cathair dhearg on a morning in an Cèitean, in a room of the Board's own offices with a clerk on either side of her to witness it, and she told her own under-fitter afterward, in words the under-fitter thought worth keeping and set down in a letter home, that she had signed a good many papers in her working life and never one before that she was certain, signing it, would put more Dia-thìrich boys and girls into a trade her own charter had once shut its Tuathaich fitters out of, without a single line in it anywhere that named the Tuathaich at all. The charter rule held under the new contract exactly as it had held under the old: every fitter taken on to answer the regiments' work was Dia-thìrich, as the law had required since the charter's first year, and Peigi signed to it, her under-fitter said, with the same hand and the same care she gave any other clause, neither faster nor slower for what the clause meant.

The forges of Àth naomh in the west and of Caol ìseal on the north coast, which had smelted the bog-iron of their own districts since before the war began, took the Crown's contract that same season for the regiments' small arms and field-pieces, and every forge-town of the old vein country that could turn a hand to iron followed them into it within the year, so that by the autumn arms had become, in every market of the kingdom, the dearest common goods a household could buy, dearer than grain in a bad year and dearer, in the pit towns especially, than the fuel it was bought to burn. A smith of Àth naomh, writing to his own brother at Cathair dhearg that winter, said that he had turned out more musket-stocks in the six months past than he had turned out ploughshares in the six years before the war, and that he did not know, setting his tools down at the day's end, whether to call that a smith's good fortune or a kingdom's bad one, and had decided, by the time he sealed the letter, that a man in his trade was not the one who ought to have to choose between the two words for it.

At Caol ìseal the same trade came to a smithy that had cast little before the war but nails and pot-hooks and the odd gate-hinge for the farms round about, and its master, a woman who had inherited the forge from a father with no son to leave it to and had spent her first years in it proving to every customer in the district that a woman's hand could hold a hammer as true as a man's, told the Board's own inspector, when he came to see the new contract's terms met, that she had taken on four boys and a girl that winter who had never before been let near a forge fire, and meant to keep every one of them past the contract's own term if the trade would bear it, war or no war, because a hand once taught a trade does not forget it merely because the reason it was taught has passed. The inspector wrote her answer down more or less whole in his own return, adding nothing to it, which this book takes for as plain a mark of respect as an inspector's report is ever likely to give.

## V. Of the Fleets

Of the four fleets that the war had raised and the peace had not stood down, three had work that spring that the open record was glad enough to set down in full, having no cause to shut any of it away. The second fleet, working the eastern water off Baile ìseal through that summer, ran down at last, in the first days of an Lùnastal, the raiders who had taken the salt-boats off Àth bheag three years before and burned a third, and brought their two remaining hulls into Seann Skell under guard, their crews to the capital's gaol and their own account of themselves, such as it was, to a court that this book has found no full record of. Seonag nic Thòmais, keeper of the trial lamp at Baile ìseal's own lighthouse and mother, by then, to a daughter old enough to keep the watch with her on the worse nights, kept in her own log, alongside her plainer entries of wind and tide, an account of the chase that the second fleet's own captain thought worth copying whole into his return to the Board: that she had seen the raiders' sail from the lamp-room two hours before the fleet's own lookout raised it, by the set of a light she had learned, watching that coast for near thirty years, to know from an honest fisherman's by nothing she could easily put into words beyond the plain fact that an honest boat runs before a squall and a raider runs across it, and that she had lit the second lamp, the one kept unlit in ordinary weather and meant only for a warning, a full hour before the fleet came up, on her own judgment and without waiting to be asked for it by any signal from the water.

The fourth fleet, kept on the Tuathaich coast in the north-west since the war's last summer, searched through that same spring the bays off Cathair gheal where the raiders had been thickest in the war years, and found them, to the last cove, empty. The fishermen of Cathair gheal, questioned by the fleet's own officers as to where the raiders had gone, gave an answer more than one of the officers set down in almost the same words in their separate returns, as though the town had agreed it beforehand, though this book takes it rather for a saying already old among them than for any planned answer: that the raiders had left when the fleets came, and would be back when the fleets went, and that a fisherman of that coast had learned, over a good many years of both, not to trouble himself overmuch about which was doing at any one time, since the sea, in his experience, always found some use for an empty bay before long, whichever side filled it.

The third fleet, at Baile ghorm on the western strait, kept the tank-hulls it had built for the war's own carrying and turned them, that spring, to running dubhan up the coast to the Tuathaich depots at Doire ghlas and Caol mhòr, on a contract the Moot had asked for itself and the Moot's own purse paid the freight of, purse for purse, out of what little the Moot's fund had left over from feeding the war's own families three years before. It was, so far as this book has found, the first contract in the whole of the war's long accounting that the Tuathaich had asked for and paid for themselves rather than had laid upon them, and the Moot's own minute on it, brief as its minutes generally are, records only that the freight was agreed at a fair rate and that the third fleet's captain, a Dia-thìreach who had run the same water for the war's own carrying and asked, this once, to be told plainly what he was carrying and to whom, was told, and carried it, and made no complaint of the cargo that this book has found, though more than one Dia-thìreach captain of that same fleet, in the years before, had grumbled loudly enough about carrying dubhan north at all that his grumbling had gone into the Board's own returns.

## VI. Of the Morning After

Sìleas came to her own desk on the morning after the lock was turned at the ordinary hour, before the copyists were properly at their tables and before the market bell had rung in the town below, and opened the day's page as she had opened it every working morning for thirteen years, with the date set at its head in her own hand and the weather noted under it, because a chronicle that means to be read fifty years on, she had told her own clerk more than once, ought to be able to tell its reader, at the very least, whether it was raining. It had not rained that morning. She wrote that it had not, and then the day's first ordinary business, a letter from a mill at Muileann leathan asking after a copyist's fee, exactly as she would have written it on any other morning of the thirteen years before, and did not write, anywhere on that page or any page that followed it, one word about the room at the end of the new wing's gallery or the key that hung, from that day, under Raghnall's own shirt.

It was not, she has told her clerk since, that she had put the sealing out of her mind; a woman does not carry four boxes of her own work to a locked door and then forget, by the next morning, that she has done it. It was rather, she said, that a chronicle which stopped, even for a day, to say to its own reader, here is a thing we are no longer able to tell you and here is why, more than the bare line she had already set at the head of the open list, was a chronicle that had begun to write about its own troubles rather than about the kingdom, and she had watched enough chronicles in the Library's own stacks fall into that habit, in years when their keepers grew tired or frightened or merely old, to know it for a slow way of ceasing to be a chronicle at all while still filling pages. So she wrote the mill's letter, and the fee, and closed the page at the ordinary hour, and began the next morning's page the same way, the date, the weather, the day's first ordinary business, as if the room at the end of the gallery held nothing more than the broken binding-press it had held the winter before, and went on writing that way, morning after ordinary morning, for the years the rest of this book has still to tell of her.

Raghnall, for his part, was seen that same first morning to open the reading-room at the usual hour and set the fire and lay out the day's returns for the copyists exactly as he had laid them out every working morning since before Sìleas came to the Library at all, and if he laid his hand against his own chest that morning for the first time, no one who was in the room to see it thought it worth writing down, there being, that early in the thing, nothing yet in the gesture to mark it out from any other habit a keeper might fall into without quite meaning to. It was only later, once the habit had held for a season and then for a year, that a copyist or two began to notice it and to wonder, without ever quite asking him outright, what it was a man checked for so often in a key he had never once, by any account this book has found, mislaid.
