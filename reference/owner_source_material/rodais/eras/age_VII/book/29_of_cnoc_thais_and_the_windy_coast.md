## I. Of the Road They Cut

Tom Varley had reopened the two shafts at Cnoc thais with his own hands two summers before the war came looking for them, and he had known even then, hauling the first cartload of poor coal up into daylight with three other men and a borrowed windlass, that a seam a town cuts on its own account without a charter is a seam some day somebody with more men than argument will want back. He had said as much to Sarah Dunmore once, at her own kitchen table, and she had not disagreed with him; and he thought of that unremarkable evening a good deal in the last week of an Cèitean and the first of an t-Iuchar, when the word came up the hill paths, farm to farm the way word always came in that country, that the road below Cnoc thais was cut.

It was cut the way the road to Muileann àrsaidh had been cut the year before, a company dug in across the cart track where it left the last of the flat ground and began to climb, and a second company working along the ridge to close the hill paths one by one, so that within a fortnight a man could still walk out of Cnoc thais on his own two feet, by paths only the town's own people rightly knew, but nothing on wheels could come in or go out, and nothing at all could come in that the regiments below did not first choose to let by. Tom had seen a siege from the inside once already that year, at Muileann àrsaidh, from the wrong side of a wall with a boy's blood on his hands, and he had carried the wounded of it up a ridge path in the dark and left a woman he could give no better answer than silence. He had told himself, walking home from Muileann àrsaidh to his own shaft-head with that behind him, that he would not stand inside a second wall and watch it close a second time, and he found, when the road below Cnoc thais was cut in earnest, that he still held to it.

The town itself was smaller than Muileann àrsaidh and had held out longer than anyone below it in the low country had troubled to expect, for the plain reason that Cnoc thais had never been much of a town to begin with, a scatter of houses about two shaft-heads and a chapel too small for half its own dead, and a company sent to take a place that size does not, in the ordinary way of war, hurry. The regiment below spent the better part of a month doing nothing that Tom's own watchers on the ridge could make out as an attack at all: digging, mending a bridge the spring floods had taken, drilling in a field the townsfolk had sown that year with oats they would not now live to cut. Tom used the month as it was given him. He walked the two shafts with the men who had cut them beside him, old Angus Rennie's cousin among them, and worked out between them, in the evenings, by a single shaded lamp in the room behind the chapel that served the town for a council when it needed one, exactly how much powder the lower galleries would want to bring the roof down clean and how much less than that would only crack the timbering and leave a seam half open for whoever came after to clear.

"You'd know that better than any man here," Angus's cousin said to him, the second evening, meaning it kindly and meaning also, Tom understood, that the work of ruining a thing you had built with your own hands was not work any man should be asked to reckon twice over if once would serve. Tom did not answer that directly. He said only that he had gone down those shafts as a boy to another man's coal before he ever cut a foot of his own, and that a shaft, when all was said, was a hole in a hill and would still be a hole in a hill when the war that wanted it closed was three generations forgotten, which was less an answer than a thing he told himself to make the reckoning bearable, and both men in that room knew it for what it was and let it stand.

The council, such as it was, met most evenings in that same chapel room, five men and two women who held no charter for the holding of it beyond the town's own custom of asking the oldest and the steadiest to sit and hear a thing out before anybody acted on it, and Tom sat with them though he had never been asked to and nobody had ever thought to tell him not to. The talk those evenings ran the same ground over and over, the way talk will when a town is waiting on a thing it cannot hurry and cannot put off: whether to send the children down to kin in the low country while the road still allowed it, whether a message to Muileann àrsaidh's own remnant might bring help that did not exist to bring, whether the regiment below meant to starve the town out slow or take it quick, a question none of them could answer and all of them asked all the same, the way a man worries a loose tooth with his tongue for the plain reason that it is there to worry.

It was old Mairi Kessock, the eldest of the two women and a widow of a hewer dead these twenty years, who said, the third week of that month, the thing Tom found himself turning over longest after. She had lived through the Severance as a girl, or near enough to it that her own mother had, and she said that a town threatened by soldiers it cannot fight had, in her mother's telling of the old days, exactly two ends open to it: to be taken whole, houses and all, for whatever use the takers wanted made of it, or to leave the takers nothing worth the taking and go. Cnoc thais, she said, looking round the room with an evenness that Tom thought afterward had cost her a good deal to keep, was too small and too poor to be worth much taken whole, and that left, by her own reckoning, only the second end, whenever the soldiers finally troubled to come up and prove her right or wrong of it. Nobody in the room argued with her that evening, though nobody voted on it either, there being nothing yet to vote on; but Tom saw, watching the faces round the shaded lamp, that the room had, without a word more being said, already begun quietly making ready to leave rather than be taken, each in whatever way each household judged it could manage the leaving.

## II. Of the Firing

Cnoc thais fell in an Lùnastal, on a morning grey with the kind of low cloud the western hills keep long past the season the low country calls summer, and it fell, by every account the open record afterward gave and every account Tom himself carried away from it, without the town ever properly deciding to let it. The regiment came up the last of the road at first light, having found, two days before, a shepherd's path along the northern ridge that the town's own watchers had judged too steep for anything but goats and had, for that reason, left unwatched; and by the time word of soldiers on the ridge reached the chapel room where the council sat, the thing that wanted deciding had already, in the plainest sense, been decided for them.

Tom fired the shafts himself. He would not let Angus's cousin do it, though the man offered, twice, and Tom found afterward that he could not entirely say why he had refused the offer beyond a feeling, not fully reasoned, that a thing built by one pair of hands ought if it must be unbuilt to be unbuilt by the same pair and no other. He went down the first shaft alone in the grey hour with the powder already laid and the fuse cut to the length he had reckoned the night before, and stood at the shaft-head afterward with the second fuse in his hand and the sound of the first charge still working its way up through the rock underfoot, a sound less like an explosion than like a very large door being shut a long way off, and thought, watching the smoke come up thin and grey against the greyer sky, that he had heard that same sound described to him as a boy, by the man who first showed him a seam of coal by candlelight, as the sound a hill makes when it decides a thing is finished.

The second charge went the same way. By the time both shafts were sealed, timber and rubble packed down hard enough that no summer's digging would open them again without more men than the regiments below had any reason to spend on a played-out western seam, the soldiers were already coming through the town's one street, and Tom stood at his own shaft-head with the smoke still drifting and watched them come without any clear notion in his own mind of what he meant to do next, until Angus's cousin took his arm, not roughly, and said that a man who had just spent his morning burying his own coal had done the day's work the hills would remember him for, and that the rest of the day's work, whatever it turned out to be, could be done walking.

Mairi Kessock was not among those who went up into the hills that morning. Tom saw her, in the last moments before he and the others took the rabbit path, standing at her own door with a shawl about her shoulders and a look on her face that he read, at the time and again many times after, as a woman who had already made her own reckoning of the two ends she had named three weeks before and chosen, for herself and no one else, the first of them, being too old by her own account to walk a hill country she had not walked in forty years and unwilling to be the weight that slowed six younger men's going. She raised a hand to him as he passed, not a wave so much as an acknowledging of him, one person to another, and he raised his own hand back, and that was the whole of the parting between them, no more words spent on it than the moment could properly hold.

The open record, when it came to be written, gave the number of Cnoc thais's holdouts taken that morning as forty-one, a figure Sìleas nic Rath's clerks drew from the regiment's own muster of prisoners marched down to the low country in the days that followed, and it gave no figure at all, then or ever, for how many of the town's people had not been taken because they were, by the time the regiment reached the chapel room, already some hours gone up into the hills behind the town by paths the shepherd who had shown the soldiers one path had not, in the end, shown them all. Tom was among those not taken. He left the town by a path he had walked as a boy after rabbits and had not walked since, with Angus's cousin beside him and, within the first mile, four more of the town's own hewers who had judged, watching the soldiers come up the street, that a man taken at Cnoc thais that morning was a man who would not see his own hill again for a good while, if at all, and had chosen, each in his own time and by his own reckoning, to take the path instead.

## III. Of Those Who Went On

They did not stop in the near hills, where a dozen other small parties out of Cnoc thais and the last of Muileann àrsaidh's own scattered folk had by then made a kind of ragged half-camp among the shielings the summer herding used and abandoned every autumn regardless of any war. Tom had thought, the first night out, that the near hills would do, that a man could sit quiet enough among the shielings through the worst of the weather and wait to see what the peace, whenever it came, would leave standing for him to go back to. It was Angus's cousin, of all of them, who said otherwise, on the second night, with the six of them crowded round a fire small enough that its smoke would not carry far and a sky above them clearing, for once, to more stars than any of them had troubled to look at in a year.

"They'll not let the near hills be," he said. "This year or next, it makes no matter. They'll come up after the shielings same as they came up after the town, once some clerk in the low country works out there's men here still breathing that the muster roll never caught." He had no more certain a reason for the saying of it than any other man's guess that autumn, and he offered none, but the six of them sat with it a while in the firelight, turning it this way and that the way men turn a coin they cannot yet decide is counterfeit, and found, each in his own private accounting, that none of them could put forward a better guess to set against it.

Tom said little that night himself, though he had thought, before Angus's cousin spoke, that he might argue for the shielings after all, on the plain ground that a man's legs tire and a winter in open country asks a great deal of legs that had spent a life underground and not on a hillside. He did not argue it. He found, turning his own reasons over by the fire, that he trusted the other man's guess more than his own wish, and a guess trusted over a wish was, he had learned that year if he had learned nothing else, the surer thing to build a winter's walking on.

So they went on, past the near hills and the shielings and the last of the drove paths any Cnoc thais man had a name for, into ground that the town's own oldest hewers spoke of only at second or third hand, a country of low scrub and standing water and, at last, a wind that Tom found did not properly stop from the day they first felt it on their faces to the day, weeks later, that he stood at the edge of the sea and understood why every teller who had ever spoken of that coast to him as a boy had called it, without needing to say more, the windy coast.

The walking took the better part of three weeks, by Tom's own count of nights slept under whatever cover the ground offered, and it was not, he found, a country that made the walking easy for the reward of its emptiness. The scrub tore at boots already worn thin from a summer's siege-work, and the standing water, brown and cold and often deeper than a man's first step into it judged it to be, cost them one bad night when Angus's cousin went in to the knee in what had looked, in the failing light, no more than a puddle, and came out with a chill that kept him shivering by the fire two days running before it passed. They ate what the country gave them and what little they had carried, birds taken with a sling two of the party had thought to bring, and once, memorably enough that it was spoken of round the fire for a week after, a hind that one of the Àth naomh men, met on the path by then and fallen in with them, brought down with a single cast of a spear he had carried the whole way from his own dissolved town for no better reason, he said, than that a man walking into country he did not know felt easier walking into it armed with anything at all, even a spear no soldier on either side of the war would have troubled to call a weapon.

They crossed one another's paths with the other six parties by a kind of slow accretion rather than any single meeting, a fire seen at a distance one evening and approached cautiously by both sides until a shout in Dia-thìris settled what a shout in no tongue at all might not have, two families falling in together for a day's walk and then parting again toward different stretches of coast, word of a third party carried forward by whichever traveller had last spoken with them. Tom found, doing this slow arithmetic of meetings as the weeks went on, that he had come to know the shape of the whole scattered company, seven parties and something short of two hundred souls, before he had ever stood in the same place as more than a dozen of them at once, a kind of knowing at second hand that put him in mind, more than once, of Ellen Marsh's books of Tuathaich accounts, gathered the same slow way from people who had not set out meaning to be gathered into anything at all.

They were seven parties in all by the time word of one another reached them on that ground, seven separate handfuls out of Cnoc thais and the last stragglers of Muileann àrsaidh and, it was said though Tom never met the man himself, one small party out of Àth naomh that had come the longest way round of any of them, and between the seven parties they made, by nobody's exact count, somewhat short of two hundred souls sitting on ground that belonged, by any charter the low country kept, to nobody at all. The regiments followed them as far as a boundary stone at the edge of what the old province rolls called the shire of Seann Vell, a stone none of the seven parties had known was there until a patrol's officer, come up to within shouting distance of it one grey morning, called across that he was under orders to go no farther, the ground beyond being outside any shire the Board's writ ran in, and that his own commander wanted no part of a war fought on ground his own maps did not properly own.

It was that same officer's report, copied afterward into the open record and from there, in time, into every account the low country ever kept of that autumn, that gave the seven parties the name they carried thereafter in every paper that troubled to name them at all: those of no shire, a phrase the officer had reached for, Tom thought later, hearing it repeated back to him by a trader who had heard it from a soldier who had heard it from the officer himself, because a man writing a report wants a name for the thing he is reporting, and the ground those seven parties sat on had, in the plainest sense the officer's own maps could give him, no name of its own to lend them. The phrase said nothing of Cnoc thais and nothing of the western hills either, and Tom, hearing it named back to him for the first time by a stranger's mouth at a stranger's fire, felt the particular smallness of a man who learns that the ground he stands on has been written down by somebody who has never stood on it at all.

## IV. Of the Fleet Sent South and the Boats That Burned

What word reached the windy coast of the rest of that autumn's business came slowly, and came sideways, carried by traders who came up the coast path from the south with salt and needles and, now and again, a barrel of dubhan oil to sell to fisherfolk who had burned fish-oil in their own lamps since before any of the seven parties' grandfathers were born, and who took, in exchange for their goods, whatever news the traders judged worth the telling. The fisherfolk themselves took the seven parties in without much asking, in ones and twos and small households, the way Tom had heard the north coast generally took in whoever a hard season washed up on it, and he and Angus's cousin and the others of the Cnoc thais six came, before the month was out, to a widower named Calum Odhar who fished a two-man boat out of a cove Tom never did learn a proper name for, and who gave them the byre behind his own house to sleep in and asked, in the whole of the first fortnight, exactly one question of them, which was whether either could mend a net, and neither being much use with a net, set them instead to hauling driftwood for the winter's fires and troubled them with nothing further.

It was one such trader, a woman named Cairstine who walked the coast path twice a year and had done so, by her own account, since before the war had a name, who first told Tom and the others, sitting over a fire in Calum Odhar's own byre with the wind working at the thatch above them, that the fleets in the south had been thinned to keep the north-west shut, and that the raiders of the eastern water, who had troubled nobody in living memory while the fleets stood full, had come out from their own islets that same autumn and found the southern water emptier than it had been in a generation. She told it plainly, the way Tom came to understand was her whole manner of telling anything, sitting cross-legged on a creel with her pack still on her back because she meant, she said, to be gone again before the tide turned, and she took a long pull from the cup Calum Odhar's own daughter set by her before she went on, as though the telling were thirsty work and wanted wetting partway through.

The second fleet, Cairstine said, had been raised that same season at the southern Seann Skell, out of the harbour towns and the Rite's own boatmen, to hold the eastern and southern water while the first fleet and the fourth kept the north-west shut against nothing that anyone on the windy coast could rightly say deserved the shutting; and the raising of it had not been quick enough, or not full enough, or simply not lucky enough, to keep two salt-boats out of Àth bheag from being taken clean out of the water one grey morning and a third burned to the waterline within sight of its own harbour mouth, the crew of the burned boat put ashore, some days after, at the light of Baile ìseal, more by luck than by any rescue the second fleet could rightly claim credit for. The fleet did run one of the raiders down in the end, off that same light, a fortnight or more after the burning, and brought her in with what was left of her crew for the court of the sea to deal with in its own time and its own way; but Cairstine, telling it, gave the running-down less weight in the telling than the burning that came before it, in the way a teller weighs a thing by what it cost rather than by how it ended, and Tom, hearing it, found himself thinking less of the raiders or the fleet than of a woman he had never met, put ashore at a light he had seen only from a boat's deck once as a boy, with nothing left of her own boat to show for a season's work but the clothes she stood up in.

He asked Cairstine, when she had done with the telling, whether the fleet's thinning and the raiders' coming were spoken of, in the low country, as one thing or two, and she said she could not properly answer that, not being a woman the low country's own clerks troubled to ask; but she said that a fisherman of Baile ìseal, from whom she had the tale in the first place, had put it to her plainly enough over a cup at his own hearth, that a kingdom which sent its ships to shut a door on nothing much would some day find another door, one it had not thought to watch, standing open behind it, and that this was not, in his own telling of it, a new thing under any sky, only a thing kingdoms went on forgetting each in their own turn.

Calum Odhar, who had sat through the whole of Cairstine's telling without a word, said only, once she had gone on her way north the next morning with her pack lighter of goods and heavier, he judged, of nothing at all, that a man who fished a two-man boat out of a cove with no name learned early not to trouble himself over which of a kingdom's doors stood open or shut, since the sea took no more notice of a kingdom's doors than it took of a kingdom's charters, and a fisherman's whole trade was learning to read the one door the sea kept, which no fleet anywhere had ever yet managed to shut for a single season together. Tom thought that a fair answer, turning it over afterward, and thought too that it was the kind of answer a man could only give who had never had a shaft of his own to fire, or a book of names taken down at a trestle table that he could not stop, some nights, from counting over in his head.

## V. Of the Edge of the Sea

Tom kept, through that autumn and into the first hard weather of an Dàmhair, a private reckoning of his own, not written down anywhere, since there was no paper on the windy coast that any of the seven parties troubled to carry and no ledger a man could rule columns in even had he carried one, but held instead the way a man holds a tally he means to keep straight in his own head against the day somebody asks him for it. He counted, that autumn, the men who had come out of Cnoc thais with him and were still, by his own eye, breathing and whole; he counted, separately, the ones the regiment had taken at the town and marched south, whose names he knew and whose faces he could still call up plainly enough by firelight; and he counted, last and longest, a column that had nothing to do with Cnoc thais at all, a woman's book of names taken down at a trestle table two seasons before, and a field left unploughed below Àth leathan that he had stood at the edge of once, at a sentry's leave, and looked at a long while without finding an answer his own eyes could bring back with him.

He did not find, that autumn, any word of Ruth Calder on the windy coast, nor any reason to think a word of her would ever reach so far a shore, and he had by then stopped expecting one in any place he came to, the way a man stops expecting rain in a season that has already, by every sign the sky gives, gone dry for good. Angus's cousin asked him once, mending a net neither of them was much use at, whether he still meant to send word north to the hill farms the way he had that first fortnight at Muileann àrsaidh, boy to shepherd to farmhouse, and Tom said he did not, this coast having no chain of boys and shepherds a message could travel by, and no farmhouse within it that had ever heard Ruth Calder's name to begin with; and having said it aloud he found it settled a weight in him that had ridden there unspoken the length of the walking, not so much an ending as a putting-down of it he had carried the length of the walking without once setting it aside to rest his arms.

What he found instead, in the last clear evening before the weather turned for good, was the sea, and he stood at the edge of it on a strand of black shingle with the wind coming off the water hard enough to take a man's breath if he stood square into it, and the light going down grey into grey without any proper sunset to mark the day's end, the way the low country's own evenings marked it with colour and this coast, by every account the seven parties had of it, never troubled to. Calum Odhar had told him, the week before, that the windy coast kept no calendar of feast days the way the low country did, the weather here being too much its own master to be told when a season was meant to turn, and that the fisherfolk marked their year instead by the boats that went out and the boats that came back, a reckoning Tom found, turning it over, not so very different from a hewer's own way of marking a year by the coal that came up and the coal that did not.

He stood there a while, with Angus's cousin some way off along the shingle doing the same thing in his own silence, and thought, watching the water come in flat and grey and endless, of a burn in spate below a pit-head, brown and quick with snow-melt, going on the same before, during and after a battle as if it had somewhere better to be than any of the men fighting beside it; and he thought that the sea before him now was the same water, in the end, only grown too large for any man to hear it the way he had heard that burn, and that a man could stand at its edge his whole life and never once persuade it to answer a question he had not thought to ask it plainly. The wind did not let up. It had not let up, by his own reckoning, since the day the seven parties first felt it on the far side of the near hills, and Tom Varley, watching the light go out of a grey sky over a grey sea on ground that belonged, by any charter the low country kept, to nobody at all, found he had stopped expecting it to.
