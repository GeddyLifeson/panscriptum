# Of the Motion Allowed to Die

*DE 25*

## I. Of the Thing Off the Cape

The fourth fleet's log for that year, kept as every fleet's log was kept, in a hand that set down wind and sail and little else, carries one entry that its own captain thought worth underlining, twice, before he sent the book south with the winter's returns. On a clear night off the north-western cape, with no sea running to speak of and the moon most of the way to full, the watch on the *Bratach Ghlas* reported a thing longer than the hull under their own feet moving under the water off the starboard bow, showing neither fin nor spout that any man aboard could swear to, and gone before a second man could be fetched to see it. The captain wrote what his watch told him and no more, and then, because a log is not a place for a captain's own fancies and he knew it, added underneath, in the same hand: *called it a whale.*

It was not the first such thing seen off that cape. The Tuathaich fishermen of the windy coast had a name for whatever it was, and a telling to go with the name, that ran back at least to the years when Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill kept the royal chronicle, for she had set down a sighting of it from the fishermen's report, and she was the first chronicler of the restored kingdom; the telling said only that the thing came seldom, and always in fair weather, and that it had never yet been known to do harm to any boat that let it pass unremarked. The fourth fleet's own men, being Dia-thìrich and mostly from towns that had never had cause to fish that coast before the war stationed them there, knew none of this, and called it a whale because a whale was the largest thing any of them had a name for, and because a log, once a captain has written a word in it, is generally left to stand.

Nothing came of the sighting. No boat was lost that year off the north-west cape, and no fisherman of the windy coast troubled to ask the fourth fleet what its log said, there being little, by then, that the two sides of that water had fallen into the habit of asking each other about anything at all.

## II. Of the Motion Anna Brought

Anna nic Phàil had kept a copy of the Moot's last petition in her own writing-case for four years and more by the time she brought her motion, at last, before the twelve, and she could have told anyone who asked her, though nobody did, the exact words of its closing clause without once looking at the paper, having read it over to herself so many evenings at her own table in Cathair dhomhain that the words had worn a groove in her the way a cart-track wears a groove in a soft road. *The kingdom, having already the whole of the south's new fuel to warm it, might find in its own patience a cheaper peace than in any company it could raise to enforce the alternative.* She had watched, in the years since Martha Greaves carried it into the chamber, one small mercy after another pass the council's table and be granted or refused on its own account, a capping here, a register there, a released man handed over at a boundary stone, and had come to understand that the petition, the whole of it, asked payment rather than patience for a season the petition had not foreseen, and would need a new clothing before the room would so much as look at it straight.

She wrote the motion herself, through the early months of that year, at her own table by her own lamp, in the plain hand she used for every paper she meant the council to take seriously, and she wrote it three times before she judged it fit, cutting from the second draft a whole page of what the war had cost the western towns in named figures, granaries filled and pit-heads flooded and a stone with one-and-sixty names on it at Cathair mhòr, because she had sat at that table long enough to know that a room grows deaf to a column of figures faster than to almost anything else a petitioner can bring it, and that the figures, however honestly reckoned, would only give the twelve a thing to quarrel over instead of a thing to answer. What she kept, in the end, was short: that the Tuathaich towns had borne the war's cost in flooded seams and burned farms and men not come home, that the kingdom's own Treasury carried a tithe laid for the war's sake two years after the war had ended, and that a sum, once, out of that same tithe, paid to the towns that had borne the worst of it, would cost the kingdom less than the war itself had cost and would settle, at least in part, an account that every year left standing only grew harder to close.

She brought it to the council in an t-Ògmhios, on a morning warm enough that the dubhan stove in its alcove stood cold and unlit for once, and the twelve sat, as they always sat, about the long table with the light coming down grey and even from the high windows, Somhairle mac Lachlainn among them now these two years, a soldier's stillness about him that Anna had come, in that time, to read as attention rather than indifference, though she could not always have said which of the two it truly was. She read the motion standing, as the room's custom required of any member bringing new business, and she read it slowly, the way she had read the petition to Martha Greaves's own draft years before it ever came to this table, pausing where the sense paused and nowhere else, because a room that has decided in advance what it thinks of a thing will hear a hurried voice as an apology and a slow one, sometimes, as a fact it cannot so easily set aside.

"You have heard it," said the old man at the table's head, when she had finished and laid the paper down, in the same voice that had told Martha Greaves, four Christmases before, that she might read. "Does any member speak to it?"

Anna spoke to it herself, since custom allowed the bringer one further word before the table's own business took it up, and she kept that word shorter even than the motion: that she did not ask the council to say the war had been wrong, only that a kingdom rich enough to raise nine regiments and four fleets in twenty months was rich enough to spare, once, a sum smaller than a single season's tithe for the towns that had not raised them; and that she brought it now, and not before, because two years of watching what the peace was turning into, capping by capping and contract by contract, had taught her that a thing left unasked past its season is rarely asked at all. She sat down when she had said it, and the room, for the length of perhaps ten breaths, said nothing back to her at all, twelve faces giving her exactly the silence Martha Greaves had once described to her over a cup of tea at Doire ghlas as the room's whole answer to anything it had not yet decided how to refuse.

It was the master of the Treasury who spoke first, a heavy, careful man named Uisdean Dubh whom Anna had sat beside at that table for six years without ever once hearing him raise his voice, and he asked, in the flat tone he asked every question in, whether the mover had costed the sum she proposed against the Treasury's own roll for the year, since a council that granted a payment without a figure attached to it granted, in his experience, a habit rather than a mercy, and habits, once granted, were harder to unmake than the wrongs they were meant to answer. Anna had expected the question and answered it plainly: a sum, she said, was for the Treasury's own clerks to set, being their trade and not hers; her motion asked only that the principle be granted, that some amends were owed, and left the reckoning of how much to the room better fitted to reckon it. Uisdean Dubh wrote this down, or wrote some other thing down, in the small notebook he kept always at his elbow, and said nothing further that morning, and Anna, watching him write, could not have told anyone afterward whether she had answered him or only given him a longer sentence to carry back, unresolved, to whatever room he next carried his notebook into.

## III. Of the Eight Days

What she had not expected, and what she found hardest afterward to set in any order she could report to Martha Greaves, was how little the eight days that followed resembled a refusal of any kind she had met before at that table. No member spoke against the motion. Nobody rose, in that sitting or the one that followed it three days later, to say that the western towns had brought the war on themselves by holding their pits against the Board's own ruling, though she had braced for exactly that argument from at least two of the twelve and had an answer ready for it that she never, in the end, had cause to give. Nobody said that the Treasury's roll could not bear it, though Uisdean Dubh's question had seemed, that first morning, to open the door to exactly such an answer. The motion simply sat, on the table's own business-paper, at the foot of each day's list, carried forward morning after morning under the small clerk's notation *held over*, the same two words she saw written against it on the second day and the fourth and the sixth, until she came to recognise the phrase the way a woman recognises a neighbour's knock, by its shape alone before the door is even opened.

She spoke to three of the twelve privately in those eight days, in the corridor outside the chamber where such talk was neither forbidden nor encouraged, only tolerated the way a draught under a door is tolerated, too small a thing to be worth the mason's trouble of stopping it. The first, a woman of the eastern fisher towns whom Anna had counted, before that year, among the table's fairer members, told her that she saw the justice of the thing plainly enough and would vote for it gladly when it came to a vote, which was, Anna understood only some days later, not quite the same promise as a promise to see that a vote was called. The second, a man of the coal-line towns whose own seat depended, Anna knew without being told, on merchants who had done well enough out of the war's freight to want no new tithe laid anywhere near their own ledgers, said only that the times were not easy for asking the Treasury for anything new, and left the sentence exactly there, without saying which times he meant or when, in his own reckoning, easier ones might come. The third was Somhairle mac Lachlainn himself, whom she caught at the chamber door on the sixth day much as he had once caught her at it, two autumns before, and asked him plainly, since he alone among the twelve had once commanded the very regiments the motion's figures would have been measured against, whether he thought the sum too great.

"I think," he said, after a pause long enough that she wondered whether he meant to answer her at all, "that I have read a great many rolls of names in the last four years, and that I have never once been asked, reading them, whether the towns they were raised from could bear the cost of the raising. Why a roll of the dead should be easier to ask a kingdom to pay for than a roll of the living, I don't know. That it has been, I know well enough." He said nothing further, and Anna, walking back to her own lodging that evening past the mint where Gille-Easbaig Bàn's dies still struck the plain seal into coin pegged, now, wholly to a fuel a Tuathach had made, found she could not decide whether what he had given her was an answer, a confession, or only the same kind of silence dressed, this once, in more words than the others had troubled to use.

## IV. Of the Line Allowed to Die

On the eighth day the motion did not appear on the business-paper at all. Anna saw its absence before she saw anything else that morning, scanning the short list a clerk laid at each member's place as the twelves' custom was, and she asked the clerk, a young man she had seen carry that same list to that same table for the better part of three years without once, so far as she knew, being asked to explain an omission from it, where the motion for amends had gone. He told her, in the brisk apologetic voice of a man repeating something he had been told rather than something he had decided, that the master of the Treasury had asked that it stand over to the autumn sitting for want of the figures the mover had declined to bring, and that the request had been granted the evening before, in a short session Anna had not been told was sitting, by a vote of the members present, of whom she had not been one.

She understood, hearing this, with the particular clarity that comes to a woman only after the thing she has feared has already happened rather than before it, that a motion stood over to a sitting nobody troubles to call is a motion granted every appearance of patience and refused every substance of a decision, and that the room had found, in the space of eight days and without a single member ever once saying plainly that the Tuathaich towns should have nothing, a way to give them nothing that left no single hand answerable for it. She asked whether the autumn sitting had in fact been set a day, and was told that it had not, the Treasury's figures being, the clerk understood, still some way from ready. When they might be ready she did not ask him, having learned enough that week to know the question would only be answered with another true thing that answered nothing.

The Moot's own minute on the matter, when word of it reached Caol mhòr some weeks later and Thomas Rook's successor set it down in the book that had run unbroken since Walter Hale's wet afternoon, gave the whole of what had happened in one line, in the plain hand the minute-book had always used for a thing too large for the room to say more about: *Leigeadh leis bàsachadh.* Allowed to die. Anna read a copy of that line herself, sent her by Martha Greaves under a short cover note that asked nothing and accused nobody, and found that it said, in three words, everything her own two years of drafting and cutting and reading slowly at a long table had failed, in the end, to say in any number of words at all.

She went back over the sittings in her own mind afterward, more than once, in the way a woman goes back over a fall to find the exact stone her foot had caught on, and could not, however she counted it, find the single vote that had killed the thing, because no single vote ever had. She counted instead the faces: the woman of the eastern towns who had promised a vote and never once, in eight days, asked that one be called; the man of the coal-line towns who had spoken of hard times and let the phrase stand for an answer; six more of the twelve who had said nothing to her at all, in the corridor or at the table, and whose silence she had at first taken, in her own hopefulness, for simple caution and had come, by the eighth day, to read for what it plainly was; and Somhairle mac Lachlainn, who alone of them had given her an answer with a shape to it and had, in the end, no more moved to call the vote than any of the rest. Twelve chairs, and not one hand raised against the motion, and the motion dead all the same. She thought, counting them over that last time, that a room could look away from a thing together more thoroughly than any single member of it could ever have managed to look away from it alone.

## V. Of the Chair Laid Down

Martha Greaves heard of the motion's fate at Doire ghlas, from the same letter of Anna's that had carried the minute's four words to her, and read it standing at her own kitchen table with the morning's mail still in her other hand, and found, reading it, that she felt less anger than she had expected of herself and more a kind of tiredness that had, she understood only slowly, been gathering in her for the better part of two years without her once giving it a proper name. She had sat as speaker of the Moot since the winter Daniel Frayne died, seventeen years by then, through the fuel's whole rise and the whole of the war and the whole of the two years' peace that had followed it, and she found, folding Anna's letter back into its envelope, that she no longer wanted, in any way she could put a plain word to, to be the one who wrote the next letter asking a room that had just let this one die to look at anything else the Moot might bring it.

She told nobody of the decision for a fortnight, turning it over at her own table much as Anna had turned over the motion's own wording at hers, and when she did tell someone it was Peter Hale, in the delegates' room over the net-lofts at Caol mhòr on an ordinary sitting day, with the fire of driftwood and turf doing as little for the far end of the room as it always had. In the same voice she had used to read Ellen Marsh's registers and the census figures and every other hard thing the chair had asked her to read aloud over seventeen years, she told him plainly that she meant to lay the chair down that year, and that she thought him, of every delegate she had sat among, the fittest to take it up, having carried the rebound minute-books through half the war on his own back and having, besides, a great-grandfather's name that the room's own oldest members still remembered without being told whose grandson he was.

Peter Hale did not answer her at once, and when he did it was not to refuse, though Martha had half expected refusal from a man past fifty who had never, in all the years she had known him, put himself forward for anything the Moot had to give. He asked her instead whether she thought the chair worth taking up, after what the council's own table had just shown the whole north it thought of anything the Moot troubled to bring it, and Martha, who had asked herself the same question at her own kitchen table more than once that fortnight, told him what she had come, by then, to believe: that a chair worth nothing to the room across the boundary stones was worth a good deal more than nothing to the room it actually sat in, since the Moot's own minute-books, unbroken since Walter Hale's wet afternoon, were the one record on the whole island that had never once, in ninety years, needed the council's leave to be kept honestly, and a man willing to keep them honestly for another seventeen years was doing work that mattered whether or not any table south of the stones ever troubled to read it. Peter Hale sat with that a while, in the way Martha had seen him sit with hard things before, and said at last that he would take it, if the Moot itself, and not only its outgoing speaker, judged him fit for it, which she thought, hearing him say it, as good an answer as the chair had ever been given by anyone who had held it.

She sat one last sitting that autumn, in the same room over the net-lofts, with the same trestle down the middle and the same plain high-backed chair of ship's oak that Thomas Rook had once laid a cushion on for a man too ill to sit in it, and read out, as her last act in it, the year's register of the dead and the maimed with its new column of released names from the boundary stones added at the foot, and then the short business of the day, a roof at the school at Doire ghlas, the price of wool at the fair, a complaint from a mill at Muileann leathan about the coal-line's freight rates, ordinary things that the room had gone on discussing, she thought, listening to herself read them out for the last time, through a war and a siege and a sealed archive and now a motion allowed to die, because a people does not stop needing its roofs mended and its wool priced merely because the larger account it also keeps refuses, this year, to be settled. When the business was done she said, in the same voice she had used for the roof and the wool, that she laid the chair down, and that the Moot's own custom, unbroken since its first speaker's death, asked it now to choose whoever it judged fittest to take it up; and the room, after a silence shorter and warmer than any the council's own table had ever given Anna nic Phàil, chose Peter Hale by acclamation, without so much as a show of hands being called for.

She went home to Doire ghlas the next morning on the ordinary carrier's cart, her own baggage no more than it had been the year she first came to the mill as a young woman, and found, coming up the last rise before the town with the mill's own smoke rising grey against the hill behind it, that she did not know yet what a woman who had been the Moot's own voice for seventeen years was meant to do with her mornings now that the voice was no longer asked for, and thought that she supposed she would learn it the way she had learned everything else in that chair, one ordinary day at a time, without being told in advance how the learning was to go.

That same autumn, while the motion for amends still sat, unsummoned, on whatever shelf the council's own clerks had at last found for it, three lesser accounts of the war's long tail were closed with rather less ceremony than either the motion or Martha's own chair had been given. At Seann Warr the fifth regiment, quartered there since the war's first summer under canvas and borrowed roofs, raised through that season a barracks of dressed stone on ground the town gave it freely, judging, as its own delegates told the maor plainly enough, that a regiment likely to stay was worth more to a harbour town's trade settled than tented. At Inis uaine the eighth regiment's watch on the eastern dubhan tanks, set there against fire in the war's second year and never yet stood down, was made lasting by a council order that gave it, for the first time, officers' houses in the town rather than quarters borrowed year by year from the tank-yard's own sheds, and the Seann Spioradan house of that town, whose prayers had never before had cause to name any company of soldiers at all, added the regiment to its Sunday round. And at Ceann mhòr, in an Dùbhlachd, the fourth regiment marched out of the old magistrate's house it had held since the war's first winter and went home at last to Caol bheag, its own colours cased for a journey the men had waited four years to make; the Moot asked, as it had asked once before and been answered with nothing, that the house be given over to the town it stood in, and this once, the council's answer came within the season and was granted; the Moot's own letter had asked the house for the town, and the order gave it instead to the maor who governed the town in the kingdom's name, so that the house passed, in its plain wording, from one hand of the Crown to another, and the town that had housed the regiment through four years of quartering found itself no nearer owning the old magistrate's house than it had been the morning the fourth regiment first marched in under it.
