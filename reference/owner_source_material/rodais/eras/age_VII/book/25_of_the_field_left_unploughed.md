# Of the Field Left Unploughed

*11 an Giblean, DE 21*

## I. Of the Road into the Hills

Tom Varley did not see the field go quiet. He knew afterward, from men who had stood nearer the burn than he had that last hour, that there had come a point, past the middle of the afternoon, when the kingdom's companies had stopped pressing the narrow ground below the pit-heads and let the western line draw off up the steeper slope without following it far, as a man lets a beast go that has already cost him more than it is worth to hold; but he was himself, by then, a mile up the hill path with the first of the carriers, and what told him the fighting had eased was not any sight of it but the sound, the burn's own noise coming up clearer behind him as the other noise that had covered it all day began, by degrees, to fall away.

He had thirty-one men with him on the path by the time full dark came down, Cnoc thais's own company thinned to less than half its morning number and the carriers of three other towns besides, and among them nine who could not walk unhelped, laid on hurdles lashed from pit-props and carried two and four to a hurdle by men who had themselves been on their feet since before light. It was not a path built for carrying anything heavier than a creel of peat, narrow where it crossed the shoulder of the hill and broken by a scree slide not yet a year old that took the best part of an hour to get the hurdles over, each one handed down man to man over the loose stone with a care that Tom would not, before that day, have thought a body in that state of tiredness still capable of. He carried a corner of the first hurdle himself the whole way, his own hands raw from the pit-prop's splintered edge before they were an hour on the path, and did not set it down at any halt longer than it took to shift his grip and take up the weight again.

He did not know, going up that path in the dark, what had become of the towns' own company at Àth leathan itself, whether it had come off the ground by the road or been cut from it, and he did not ask any of the men walking beside him, since every man on that path had troubles enough of his own that night without being made to answer for another's. He thought of Ruth Calder only once on the whole climb, at the top of the scree slide, when the man walking nearest him, one of Tobar dhearg's eleven, said her name aloud to no one in particular, in the tone a man uses for a name he means to fix in his own memory rather than to ask a question with, and Tom said nothing back to it, because he had nothing to say that the saying of it would have improved.

One of the nine on the hurdles, a boy from Àth naomh no older than the one who had slept against a coal tub in his father's coat the night before the battle, died between the scree slide and the head of the glen, quietly enough that the two men carrying his corner did not know it until they set the hurdle down at the next halt and found nothing in the boy's face that answered when they spoke to him. They carried him on regardless, since there was no ground on that path fit for the digging of anything and no time that night to look for any that was, and Tom took the news of it standing with his raw hands still on the hurdle's rail, and said only that they would see him properly buried at Cnoc thais, and set his own shoulder back under the weight to spare the two men who had carried the boy the whole way from having to carry him the last mile alone as well as the first.

The moon came up clear of the eastern ridge somewhere past the middle of that walk, throwing the path into a light that was worse, in its way, than no light at all, since it showed a man his own shadow going on ahead of him over ground still too broken to trust to a shadow's judgement, and more than one of the party went down on a loose stone that the daylight would have shown for what it was and the moonlight did not. Nobody complained of it. A complaint, Tom thought, watching a woman of Muileann àrsaidh pick herself up off her knees without a word and take up her end of a hurdle again, was a thing that wanted breath to spare for it, and breath, on that climb, was the one thing the whole party had least of to give away.

They came down at last into the head of the glen above Cnoc thais a little before the second hour of the morning, the moon by then well up and giving light enough on the pale ground to walk the last stretch without lanterns, and found the town's own people waiting for them at the first houses with what fires could be kept going and what beds could be found, women of Cnoc thais out in the cold in their shawls with lamps held up to see the hurdles by before the carriers had properly reached the first wall. Tom set the wounded down in the order the walk had taught him, the worst hurt first into the houses nearest, and only when the last hurdle was inside a door and off his own hands did he let himself sit down on the low wall by the well, and found, sitting, that his legs would not at once agree to hold him up again when a woman came to ask whether he wanted anything brought.

He wanted nothing brought that a well-house at two in the morning could give him, and said so, civilly enough, and sat on a while longer with his back against the cold stone, watching the lamps go from door to door as Cnoc thais took in more of its own than it had beds for, and thought, not for the first time that night, of the list Ruth had kept at her trestle table above the pit-shed office, four hundred names and more in a hand gone thick with cold by the sixth evening, and wondered how many of those names would want striking through before the week was out, and how a list once made was ever properly unmade again once the thing it counted had stopped being countable.

## II. Of the Pits Given Up

Word of what had been done at Àth leathan itself, once the fighting there had ended, came to the hills in pieces over the days following, carried by whoever had cause to travel the roads still open and was willing to say what they had seen: a carter from Muileann àrsaidh who had gone down for salt and come back with more than salt to tell; a woman of Àth naomh whose husband, spared the battle by a broken leg got a fortnight before, walked the five miles to Cnoc thais on a stick to hear for himself whether his own town's company had come off the field, and stayed to tell what he had learned along the way.

By these pieces, set together as well as anyone in the hills could set them, it was understood within the week that the regiments had not troubled to hold Àth leathan itself once the western companies had drawn off from it, there being, as more than one teller of the tale observed, nothing left standing in a pit village of a few hundred souls that a company of fourteen hundred had any use for beyond the pits themselves, and those the Board's own order dealt with inside the fortnight: the shafts sealed at the collar, and water let into the workings below by whatever means the Board's engineers judged surest, so that the seams Ruth Calder's father and his father before him had worked would not, by the time the water found its own level in them, be worked by any hand again in a lifetime any man then living could expect to see out.

Tom heard this last part from the carter, standing in Cnoc thais's own yard with the man's cart still loaded and the carter in no great hurry to unload it, since he had, by his own account, more to say than salt to deliver, and he heard it without much surprise, having reckoned, walking up the hill path that first night with the burn's noise still in his ears, that a pit taken by the Board and not by the towns was a pit the Board would not leave working long, whatever became of the men who had worked it. It was the flooding of the shafts, more than any word of the dead or the taking of the ground itself, that seemed to settle something in the hearers of that tale wherever it was told through the following weeks, a finality to it that the fighting itself, for all its cost, had somehow not carried in the telling: a battle, however it went, was a thing that had been fought and was over; a flooded shaft was a thing that could not, by any labour the hills still had in them, be unflooded again.

The southern pits given up with Àth leathan's own, at Muileann mhòr and the smaller workings along the same seam that the towns had never properly garrisoned at all, went the same road within the same fortnight, by the same order, so that by the close of am Giblean there was not a working face left open anywhere south of the line the retreat had drawn, and the hewers of five towns who had come down to Àth leathan carrying lit lamps at noon found themselves, those of them the hillside had let go home again, hewers with no pit left south of Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais to be hewers in.

## III. Of the Returns at the Western Seann Skell

Somhairle mac Lachlainn read the first return of the battle at his own desk in the depot below the western Seann Skell, four days after the day it named, the paper having come to him by the same road the arms trains had run west by, and found in it a good deal less than he had wanted and a good deal more, he judged, than the towns themselves would ever have from any office of the kingdom's about what the day had cost the other side of it. Thirty-eight of the kingdom's dead, named and schedule'd company by company in a clerk's careful hand; the pits secured, the field held; nothing beyond that of what the return called, in the single sentence it gave the matter, "the enemy's own losses, not ascertained."

He had commanded nothing on that ground himself, his own charge these three months past being the depot and the road behind it rather than any company sent forward to the fighting, and he read the return, sitting at a desk that had been a shipmaster's counting table before the Board took the building over, with the particular attention of a man reading an account of a day he had not lived through but would, from that morning, be answerable for the ordering of what came after it. It named no captain of the towns among the enemy's dead or taken, though it named, in a line he read twice before he was certain he had read it rightly, one Ruth Calder, of Àth leathan, formerly noted on the Board's own list of the towns' spokesmen, as among those "unaccounted for" after the day's fighting, a phrase he had seen used before, in other returns of other years, for men who had gone into a wood or a river and never come out of either, and had learned, from those earlier returns, to read as neither a death nor a denial of one, only as the furthest a clerk's pen was willing to go on a matter it had no ground under its own feet to stand on.

The order for the seventh regiment came down from the council within the same week, raised from the levies of the west coast towns that had sent little enough to the earlier musterings, and Somhairle spent the better part of four days at the drill ground behind the depot seeing it stood up from nothing: a colonel's commission handed to a man he had met twice before and trusted on the strength of those two meetings and no more, a quartermaster borrowed from the depot's own stores who complained, rightly, that he could not properly outfit a regiment and keep the depot running on the same stock of boots, and something over six hundred men, more than a third of them never under arms before that spring, mustered in a yard that had held, the autumn before, nothing grander than the harbour watch's spare gear.

He gave the seventh its charge himself, standing before the drill ground's assembled companies on the morning they marched, in words a clerk behind him set down for the depot's own record and that Somhairle himself, reading the fair copy some days after, found had rendered him a good deal more eloquent on the matter of holding a road than he remembered feeling at the time: to keep the road from the Seann Skell to Àth leathan open for whatever traffic the Board's business required of it, to hold the field itself against nothing more than its own emptiness, since no one at the depot, that week, expected the western companies to come at it again with the pits already flooded and the hills behind them offering better ground to hold than any field below them ever could. The regiment went up the road on the second morning after, and Somhairle went back to his desk and the returns still waiting on it, and did not, in the weeks that followed, go up that road himself to see the field the seventh now held, having, by then, a depot and a growing muster to answer for that left him little enough leisure to visit ground his own returns told him plainly enough was empty.

The seventh's first patrol report came back within the fortnight, in the same plain hand the depot's clerks used for every return that crossed Somhairle's desk that spring: the field secured, no arms or dead found above ground, the pit-heads standing but unworked, the burn fallen now to its ordinary summer level and running clear. He read it standing at the window that looked out over the depot's own yard, where a second draft of the seventh's reserve was being drilled that same morning by a sergeant with a voice that carried the whole width of the yard, and thought, not for the first time that spring, that a field left as this one had been left, unploughed and unclaimed by either side that had fought over it, was a stranger thing in its own way than a field taken and held, since a field taken at least answered to somebody's purpose, and this one, by every report that came to his desk, answered to nobody's at all.

## IV. Of the Asking at the Hill Farms

It was the third week after the battle, the flooding of the pits by then settled news in every house of the hills and the seventh's patrols a fact of the road that carters and drovers had learned to expect and steer wide of where the ground allowed it, before Tom Varley judged Cnoc thais's own wounded settled enough, and its dead buried enough, that he could give a day to the thing he had put off doing since the second morning after the battle, which was to find out, if it could be found out at all, what had become of Ruth Calder.

He went about it the only way he knew how to go about anything: on foot, farm by farm, up every glen and along every drove track between Cnoc thais and the pass below Muileann àrsaidh where the hill country proper began, asking at each door the same plain question he had asked at the last, whether anyone of that house, or any neighbour of it, had seen a woman of Àth leathan's own build and colouring, in the days after the battle or since, on any road or path that led up out of the low ground. He had a description ready that did the woman no particular justice, he knew, having tried three times over the first two farms to say what made Ruth Calder herself and not merely a woman of her general age and height, and given it up each time for want of words that would carry the thing across a farmhouse doorstep to people who had never met her.

The answers he got, over that long day and the half of another he gave to it before Cnoc thais's own business called him back, were none of them answers he could use. An old woman at the first farm above the glen had seen a party of Àth leathan's people pass along the drove track on the evening of the battle itself, going up rather than staying by the pit-heads, but could not say whether any woman had been among them, her own eyes, she told him plainly, being no longer equal to telling one shape from another at any distance across a darkening field. A man two farms further on had heard, from a nephew who had heard it from a carter, that a woman answering some such description had been seen on the road toward the windy coast in the week after, among a party that had gone on past the hills rather than turning for any of the five towns, but could not say the nephew's name with any certainty when Tom pressed him for it, nor find the carter again to ask him straight. A third house had heard nothing at all, and said so without troubling to soften it, the man of it adding only that a great many souls had gone up into that country in the weeks since the battle and that no one household, keeping its own beasts and its own dead to bury, could be expected to have kept a tally of every stranger's face among them.

He came back to Cnoc thais on the evening of the second day with nothing in his hand that a court of the kingdom or a chronicle of the north would have called an answer, only a scattering of half-seen shapes and half-remembered nephews that might, laid end to end by a more hopeful man than Tom judged himself to be, have made some kind of road for Ruth Calder to have walked out of that country by, and might, with equal justice, have made no such road at all. He did not, telling it over to himself that night by the fire Cnoc thais's own people had kept going for the wounded, let himself settle on either reading over the other. He had asked, at every door the day allowed him, the only question he had it in him to ask, and had got, at every door, an answer that told him a good deal about how little any single household in that broken country could be expected to know of any other soul's road through it, and nothing at all, in the end, about the one road he had gone out that day meaning to learn.

He set the pen down that Ruth herself had used at her trestle table, packed since the battle among Cnoc thais's own gear along with her book of names, and did not open the book, that night or for some while after, to see how far down its four hundred entries her own hand had got before the list stopped needing keeping. There would be time enough, he told himself, going in at last to see to Cnoc thais's own wounded before he let himself sleep, for the book to be opened and read properly, by whoever in the hills was left with the heart to do it; what the day had taught him, walking farm to farm with a description that did no justice to the woman it was meant to call to mind, was only that the country above Àth leathan had grown, in three short weeks, a good deal larger and a good deal less known to him than he had thought it, the whole of his life before that spring, a man could grow to know a stretch of hill ground he had walked since he was a boy.
