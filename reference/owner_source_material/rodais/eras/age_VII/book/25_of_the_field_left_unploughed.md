# Of the Field Left Unploughed

*DE 21*

## I. Of the Carriers on the Ridge Path

Tom Varley did not see the battle end. He saw it break, which is a different thing, and he saw it from the wrong side of a drystone wall with a boy of Tobar dhearg bleeding through both hands pressed to his own thigh, and after that he saw very little that a man could afterward set down in an orderly way. The burn was in spate below the pit-heads, brown and quick with the last of the snow-melt, and it was the burn he remembered most clearly once the day was done: the sound of it under the other sounds, going on the same before, during and after, as if the water had somewhere better to be than any of them.

They had held the southern pits until the light began to go, longer than anyone at the pit-shed table had thought they would, and then word came down the line from company to company, not an order so much as a thing everyone understood at once the way a flock understands weather, that the southern ground was not to be held any further, and the hewers of five towns began to come back off it in the dusk in the way men come back from a thing that has gone against them, walking fast in twos and threes, turning now and again to look behind at nothing in particular.

Tom took the wounded. It was not a duty anyone gave him; he found himself doing it the way he found himself, most days of his life, at the head of whatever needed a head, and by full dark he had six men and the Tobar dhearg boy on hurdles and handcarts and, in one case, on the back of a Cnoc thais man built broad enough to carry his own cousin the better part of two miles without setting him down. They went up by the drove path along the ridge, the same track Cnoc thais's forty had come down by in am Faoilleach with their lamps lit at noon, and this time no lamp was lit at all, because a lit lamp on that path that night would have told the whole of the low country exactly where to look.

The boy from Tobar dhearg was called Rab, though Tom did not learn that until they were an hour up the path and the boy, half out of his senses with the cold and the loss of blood, began saying it over to himself, quietly, as though it were the one word left to him that still meant anything sure. Tom knelt by the handcart twice in that hour to press the dressing tighter, cloth torn from his own shirt-tail since the proper dressings had gone with the men still fighting, and both times the boy looked at him without seeming quite to see him and said his own name again, Rab, Rab, and Tom said it back to him, which was the only medicine he had to give.

They did not stop at Àth leathan. That was the hardest thing Tom did that night, harder than the carrying: to come within sight of the pit-shed roofs where four hundred men had slept on straw two months before, and to keep walking past them into the hills, because word had already reached him, passed hand to hand up the column of carriers, that the southern approach was cut and the town would not hold a night's stand against what was coming up the road behind them. He looked once at the dark shape of the pit-shed roof against a sky just beginning to lighten in the east, and thought of Sarah Dunmore's oatmeal carts and the boy who had portioned it out one bowl to a name, and did not let himself think further, because there was a cart-wheel stuck in a rut just then and six other men waiting on him to set his shoulder to it.

By the grey hour before true dawn they had come up over the saddle between two hills and down the far side toward Muileann àrsaidh, where fires had been lit, small and shielded, in the lee of the walled pit-heads, and women came out along the last half-mile of the path to take the hurdles from the carriers' shoulders without being asked. Sarah Dunmore herself was among them, in a coat thrown on over a nightgown, and she took one look at the Tobar dhearg boy's leg by the light of a shaded lantern and sent a girl running for the woman who set bones and stitched wounds for half the western hills, and said nothing to Tom at all beyond a hand laid once, briefly, on his arm as he went by her with the next hurdle, which was thanks and welcome and a question all in the one touch, and which Tom had no words to answer that night and did not try to find any.

He counted them in at the pit-head gate the way Ruth had counted them out at the trestle table two months before, though his list was shorter and kept in his head and not on paper, because there was no paper to hand and, he thought, setting the last hurdle down inside the wall as the sky went from grey to a thin cold white, perhaps no great wish in him to see the number written where anyone could count it against the number that had gone down to Àth leathan.

Six on hurdles, one on a cart, one carried the last mile on a cousin's back, and himself walking, which was nine souls got up off a field that had held nine hundred, and he knew, doing that sum in his head at the gate the way he had done Sarah's oatmeal sum two months before, that it told him nothing at all about the wider field, since a man who carries the wounded off a battle carries only those near enough his own place on it to be carried, and the rest of the field, wherever it lay and whatever it held, was no more open to him that night than the far side of the sea. He had heard, going up the path, two other parties of carriers ahead of him and behind, their voices carrying strangely in the wet air the way voices do on a hill at night, and did not know, and would not know for some days, how many souls those parties had brought off between them, or from how far along the line they had gathered them.

The woman who set bones came within the hour, an older woman named for nothing but her trade in every mouth on that hillside, Bonesetter, and worked on Rab's leg by lamplight at Sarah's own kitchen table while Sarah held the boy's shoulders and Tom, having no other use just then, held the lamp steady and watched a stranger's hands do to a stranger's boy the sort of careful violence that saves a leg or does not, and found himself, watching it, thinking less of the boy than of every other hand at that moment doing the same work on some other kitchen table in some other hill town, work he could not see and would only hear of, in pieces, over the days that followed.

Ruth's name was on neither list, in his head or on any paper. He had last seen her before first light the day before, at the pit-shed table with her own book open, telling off the companies by town as they formed on the road below the pit-heads, and had not seen her again once the light came up and the morning's work began in earnest. He told himself, standing inside the Muileann àrsaidh wall with the last of the wounded seen to and the dawn fully come, that she was one woman among nine hundred and the field was wide, and a man does not see everyone on a field that wide in a single day's fighting, and this was true, and it did nothing at all to loosen the particular tightness that had settled behind his breastbone somewhere in the small hours and had not left him since.

## II. Of the Pits Taken and the Water Let In

What happened at Àth leathan after the hewers went out of it, Tom Varley did not see, because by the time it happened he was two hills away with a wall between himself and the road, seeing to the last of the wounded and to Sarah Dunmore's fires. It came to him afterward the way most of that spring came to him, in pieces, carried up the hills by men who had themselves had it third-hand from a carter or a shepherd or, once, from a Dia-thìrich quartermaster's clerk who talked more than his orders allowed to a woman selling eggs at the western Seann Skell market and did not know or did not care that the woman's cousin kept sheep on the Àth leathan road.

The town was taken without a shot fired in it, or so every account that reached the hills agreed, a strange thing to Tom, turning it over afterward: that a town four hundred men had gathered to hold could pass from one hand to another in an empty street with nobody left inside it to say either yes or no to the taking. The regiments came up the road two days after the battle, the accounts said, and found the pit-shed doors standing open on their straw, the byres empty of the cattle that had been driven off into the hills days before by anyone who thought to do it, and the chapel bell still hanging in its little tower, untouched, because nobody on either side that week had found it in himself to ring a bell for any reason at all.

The order to flood the pits came from the Mine Board at Cathair mhòr, and it came, by every account Tom heard, within days of the town's falling, as though the order had been drawn up and left ready in a drawer against exactly this day and wanted only the day's arriving to be sent. The shafts at Àth leathan were old, older than the Board's own charter, cut into a seam that had fed three generations of the town's hewers before it fed the war that ended their holding it, and the engineers who came up with the order did their work in an afternoon: the pumps that had kept the lower galleries dry for sixty years were stopped, and the water that the hills had been shedding all that wet spring, the same brown quick water that ran in the burn below the pit-heads, was let down into the workings by a sluice cut for the purpose, and the Board's report, which Tom heard of long before he ever saw a word of it, gave the flooding as complete within the week.

Tom had gone down those shafts himself as a boy, visiting a cousin's family at Àth leathan the summer he turned eleven, and he remembered them chiefly for the cold and the smell of wet stone and a hewer, his cousin's father, showing him a seam of coal by candlelight with the particular pride of a man showing off a thing he has spent his whole life inside of. He thought of that seam now, drowned, dark water standing where the candlelight had been, and found that the thought did not move him the way he might have expected it to a year before. He had reopened his own two shafts at Cnoc thais the summer before last with exactly this in his mind, that the coal under the western hills was the hills' own and answered to nobody's charter but the hewers who cut it, and he had known, taking that first cartload up out of the ground, that a day might come when somebody with more men than argument decided otherwise. It had come. He found he was less surprised by the water in the Àth leathan shafts than by how little, in the end, the surprise of it cost him, set against everything else that week had already cost.

What he could not get past, turning the reports over at Sarah Dunmore's kitchen table in the evenings that followed, was the emptiness of it: a town taken by nobody, from nobody, its pits drowned by an order signed in an office two days' ride away by a man who had never, so far as anyone in the western hills could learn, set foot within a mile of the place. Sarah said, the third evening, stirring a pot that had less in it than either of them would have wished, that it was the emptiest thing she had heard of in a long war already thick with empty things, and Tom, who had by then heard the word field left unploughed passed from mouth to mouth among the men who had come up from the south with him, though nobody yet knew quite what lay under the phrase, said only that an empty town was easier held than a full one, which was true, and was also, he knew even as he said it, no kind of answer to what Sarah had actually asked.

She pressed him on it a second time, the fifth evening, with the kitchen quieter than it had been all week and the last of the wounded moved to beds found for them among the town's own houses. What she wanted to know, she said, setting two bowls down and sitting rather than standing over the pot as she had every night before, was not whether the pits were flooded, which every carter on the road had told her twice over by then, but whether flooding them served any purpose beyond the purpose of telling the hills plainly that the seams were done for good and no agreement, however generous, would ever open them again. Tom turned that over a while before he answered her, because it was, he saw, the better question of the two he had been asked that week, sharper than his own about the field, and he said at last that he thought it served exactly that purpose and no other, since a flooded shaft cost the Board more to pump dry again than the coal in it would ever be worth to a kingdom that no longer burned coal for anything but its altars, and that a Board which meant only to hold the seams against some future need would have sealed the entrances and left the water out, not let it in. Sarah nodded at that, slowly, and said that she had thought as much herself and had wanted to hear it said by somebody who had gone down those shafts as a boy, so as to know the saying of it cost him something and was not merely a guess offered cheap.

## III. Of the Returns at the Western Seann Skell

Somhairle mac Lachlainn had been three years learning to write a report that said less than he had seen, and by the second week after Àth leathan he had learned it well enough that the returns crossing his desk at the western Seann Skell read, to a man who did not know how to read them, like the dullest paperwork any army had ever generated: musters, rations issued, a bridge repaired, a company's boots replaced. He knew how to read them, and what he read in the returns that fortnight was a victory that had cost more than the figure at the bottom of any single sheet would say, and a countryside behind his own lines that had gone, in the space of a battle, from watchful to silent in a way he did not entirely trust.

The order for the seventh regiment reached him on the fourth day, carried down from Cathair dhearg by a rider who had changed horses twice and looked it. It was raised, the order said, from the levies of the west coast, fishermen and small tenants and the sons of the river-port families who had never before been asked to stand a muster, and it was to be quartered at Seann Skell, the port nearest the western hills, with instructions Somhairle himself had a hand in drafting once the order's bare bones reached him: to hold the road behind the army and to hold, in particular, the field below Àth leathan, until such time as the council's further pleasure was known.

He raised it in a week, which was faster than any regiment before it had been raised in the whole course of the war, and he raised it, he told the returns and told nobody else, because the west coast had men enough idle at that season, between one fishing and the next, to fill the rolls without stripping a single farm of its labour, and because a regiment recruited on the coast and not from the capital's own companies was a regiment whose loyalty, in a countryside as unsettled as this one had lately become, he judged less likely to be tested by a cousin or a brother found on the other side of a wall. He kept this second reason out of any return, the kind of thing a commander thought and did not set down, the same discipline, though he would not have put it to himself in these words, that had a driver on the coal line signing for weight and not for goods.

The regiment's first task, once mustered, was simple enough to state and dull enough in the stating that the return recording it ran to four lines: patrols on the Àth leathan road, morning and evening, to keep it open for the army's supply and to report anything found upon it. Somhairle read the first week's patrol reports at his own table in the requisitioned merchant's house at Seann Skell, by a dubhan lamp that burned steadier than any coal flame he had known as a young man, and found in them exactly what the order had asked for and nothing more: the road clear, the weather wet, a cart met and let pass, a farm gate found open and shut again.

It was the second week's reports that gave him the phrase that stayed with him longer than any other that spring. A patrol sent out on the fifth day past the town, along the road that ran below the old pit-heads and on toward the hills proper, returned with nothing to report but the field, and the officer writing it up, a young man new to his rank and not yet schooled in the discipline of saying less than he had seen, wrote more than the form strictly required: that the field where the battle had been fought lay as it had been left, that no one had come to it since, and that it had not been ploughed, though the season for the first ploughing was well begun on every other field the patrol had passed that morning.

Somhairle read that line twice. He had seen, in the three years since he had first marched a company toward the west, a great many things he had chosen not to set down, and he had grown, without quite meaning to, careful of the things other men chose to set down when he was not looking over their shoulder to stop them. The field left unploughed was, on its face, no more than an agricultural fact, the kind of detail a farmer's son turned soldier notices because farming is the trade he was bred to before soldiering was. It sat with Somhairle longer than that, through the evening and into the night, because he understood, better perhaps than the young officer who had written it, exactly why no farmer within five miles of that field had put a plough to it, and he found himself unwilling, turning the return over by lamplight, to write the reason into any report of his own, and equally unwilling to let it pass from his mind as easily as the form he was filing it under seemed to want him to.

He sent the same officer out again three days later with instructions, given verbally and not in writing, to walk the field ground rather than the road beside it, and to bring back word of what, if anything, the ground still held. The officer went, and came back within the day, and what he brought back Somhairle wrote into the return in the fewest words the form would bear, and no fewer, because there are things a report can carry and things it cannot, and he had by then three years' practice in telling one from the other.

The musters that same fortnight gave him a second figure he found himself turning over as often as the first. The seventh's rolls, drawn from the coast towns and the river-port families, ran to some four hundred and twenty men by the second week, short of the full regiment the order had called for, and the shortfall, when he asked after it through the maor of Seann Skell rather than through any officer of his own, came back to him as no more than the ordinary business of a fishing season not yet done and boats still out that a levy could not properly reach until they came home to harbour. He accepted this answer because there was no other readily to hand and because pressing harder on a levy already raised faster than any before it seemed to him, weighing the whole of that fortnight's business, more likely to cost him goodwill on the coast than it would buy him bodies for the muster roll. He wrote the shortfall into his own return to Cathair dhearg as a seasonal matter, to be made good by midsummer, which was true as far as it went, and left out of it the maor's further remark, offered over a glass of something the maor's own cellar had not paid the wartime tithe on, that a coast town asked to fill a regiment's ranks against its own hill neighbours might be forgiven for finding its boats a week longer at sea than the weather strictly required.

## IV. Of the Answer Nobody Could Give

Tom Varley began asking after Ruth Calder on the third day at Muileann àrsaidh, once the last of the wounded were settled and Sarah Dunmore's kitchen had stopped needing every pair of hands in the town at once. He asked first the men who had come up the ridge path with him, one by one, in the flat unhurried way a man asks a question he has already asked several times and expects, each time, the same answer he has had before. None of them had seen her past the grey hour of the morning, when the companies were still forming on the road below the pit-heads and her own book was still open on the trestle table, her thickened pen going down the last names before the light was properly up.

He sent word out from Muileann àrsaidh to every hill farm within a day's walk, not by any formal order, since he held no rank that could give one, but in the way word travels among people who have known each other's families three generations back: a boy sent here, a cousin asked there, a message left with a shepherd whose flock ranged close enough to the old battle-ground that he might, in the course of an ordinary week's work, see something worth the telling. Has anyone seen Ruth Calder, the message ran, in one form or another, carried by a dozen different mouths into a dozen different farmhouses over the course of that week, and the answer, when an answer came at all, was always some version of the same thing: that nobody now working that ground since the battle had seen a woman of her description, alive or otherwise, and that the battle-ground, by the regiments' own patrols and by the few shepherds bold enough to walk near its edge, appeared to hold nothing that anyone had found worth reporting.

A woman from a farm below the ridge, whose husband had gone down with the Cnoc thais men and come back with a wound in his shoulder that Tom himself had dressed on the path, sent word that she had heard, from a niece married into a family nearer the town, that some of the field's own dead had been carried off in the days just after the battle by people from the hills who came down at night and went again before light, and that if Ruth Calder had fallen there, she might have been carried with the rest and be lying now in ground no list on either side would ever hold a name for. This was not an answer either, though it was closer to one than anything else Tom heard that week, and he found himself, hearing it, unable to decide whether it eased the tightness behind his breastbone or drew it tighter, and settled, after turning it over two full days, on the second.

He asked, too, at the pit-shed table, once word came that a handful of Àth leathan's own people, those who had not come north with the hewers, had begun slipping back down under cover of dark to see what remained of their own doors and byres, and had themselves been asking after faces they knew and not finding them. One such, an old woman who had kept hens at the edge of the town and gone up into the hills with a cousin's family rather than stay to see what the regiments made of her yard, sent word by the same chain of boys and shepherds that she had seen, the morning after the battle, before the regiments came up the road, a party of women from the hill farms out on the field with handcarts of their own, moving among the fallen in the grey light, and that she had not looked long enough, or near enough, to say whose faces she had seen among either the carriers or the carried, because a woman alone at the edge of a battlefield the morning after learns quickly not to look too long at anything. Tom heard this and thanked the boy who brought it and gave him nothing further to carry back, because there was nothing further to ask that the old woman's account had not already, in its own way, answered as fully as it meant to.

He walked out himself on the eighth day, alone, along the ridge path he had carried the wounded up, and down the far side toward the field, going as near it as the seventh regiment's patrols allowed, which was not very near, a sentry turning him back at a stone dyke a quarter-mile short of the ground with a civility that Tom, for all his anger at the turning back, could find no fault with; the man was young, and cold, and doing exactly what his own officer had told him to do, no more and no less. From the dyke Tom could see the shape of the field, a long slope running down from the pit-heads to the road, empty under a grey sky, no plough mark on it anywhere that he could make out at that distance, the grass beginning already, in the way grass will, to come up green over ground that had held, three weeks before, nine hundred men and more with their carriers.

He looked at it a long while, until the sentry, not unkindly, told him the light was going and the patrols came round again at dusk and would not be so civil to a man found standing at the dyke after dark. Tom thanked him, which cost him something to do and he did it anyway, and walked back up the ridge path in the failing light with no more of an answer than he had carried down it, and no less, and told himself, coming over the saddle with Muileann àrsaidh's few fires showing small below him, that a field left unploughed keeps whatever it keeps whether or not a man stands at its edge asking it to give an account of itself, and that he would go on asking all the same, because asking was the one thing left to him that Ruth Calder's own book, four hundred names taken down in a clean careful hand, would have understood as work worth doing, and he was not yet ready to set it down.
