# Of the Shipmasters' Works

*DE 4*

## I. Of the Salt-Sheds

Peigi nic Ascaill smelt the works before she saw them.

She had come down from Cathair dhearg in the second week of am Màrt, two days in the carrier's coach with a crate of lamp-glass on the seat beside her, and on the second afternoon, where the road tops the last rise above the southern coast, the wind came up off the sea and brought her a smell she had no name for. It was like tar, and like a wet hearth in the morning, and under both was something sour and sharp that made her eyes water. The carrier, a broad man from Muileann òg who had driven the coast road half his life, put his sleeve over his nose and said nothing until they were down among the first houses. Then he said, "That's them. The shipmasters. You can smell them from Tobar dhearg when it blows from the west."

Below them lay Seann Skell of the south, the great port of the southern coast: its harbour inside the long mole, the grey roofs going down to the water in steps, and on the height the house of Òrd Bhrìde with its lamp already lit in the west window against the dusk. Beyond the harbour, on the east side where the salt-pans had been, stood a row of long low sheds with tarred roofs, and at the end of the row a new chimney of red brick, still in its scaffolding, with smoke coming out of it at a slant.

Nobody had built anything so tall on that shore in the memory of the town. It was not yet as tall as it would be by the year's end, and already the gulls went round it.

Peigi had read about it at her desk in the capital. The company of shipmasters, Comann nan Sciobairean, had taken a lease of the old salt-sheds in the winter; the salt trade had long gone east, and the town was glad to be paid for sheds that had stood empty since the humans' time. On {{date:VI-0008}} the company had set up its works there, the first works for the making of dubhan anywhere on the island outside the north. That was what the letter to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais said, in a clerk's round hand, and it said no more. It did not say who had shown them the making, or where they had it from.

She had also read the surveyors' report of two years before, which had come to an Leabharlann and to her own masters and been filed without a minute. Three clauses: it burns; it stinks; it is not coal. She had copied the three clauses into her pocket-book and underlined the first.

She was thirty-six that spring, a narrow woman with ink on her second finger that no soap would take off, and she had been engineer to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais for six years. In those six years she had watched the price of the lights' coal go up every quarter, and every quarter she had written to say so. The five towers of the island burned coal-lit flame, the flame with the dark coloured edge that only the island's coal gives, and the coal came dearer and poorer each year from the failing vein. At Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg, the first of the five, the keeper had begun to bank the fire on calm nights to save it. Peigi had not written that to her masters. She had told the keeper she had not heard it.

She took a room at an inn on the quay where the landlady fed her fish and asked her no questions, and in the morning she walked round the harbour to the sheds.

The master of the company met her at the gate. He was an old salt-shipper with a white beard cut square and a coat too good for the place, and he knew who she was before she said it. He took her hand in both of his and said the whole town knew the lights' own engineer had come, and that the company was honoured.

"I've come to see it made," Peigi said.

"And so you shall," he said, "so far as there is anything to see."

He walked her through the first of the sheds. It was full of heaps: slack and spoil, the grey and black waste that the pits throw out and the heaps of the pit towns are made of, brought down the coast in the company's own hulls. Men were shovelling it into barrows. The floor was black and the light came in grey through the high windows, and the dust stood in the light like smoke. In the second shed the waste went into iron retorts, six of them in a row on brick furnaces, and there the heat met her at the door like a hand on the chest. Men in leather aprons tended the fires. The smell she had met on the hill was here at its root, and she breathed through her mouth.

The third shed was locked. There was a new padlock on it the size of a man's fist.

"The making-shed," said the master of the company. "Where the stuff is finished. You'll forgive us. Every house has one room it keeps."

In the fourth shed the finished dubhan stood in barrels, rows of them, new oak bound with iron, and at the far end a man had one open and was drawing it off into a can. It came out thick and black and slow, like treacle in cold weather, and it shone. The master had the man fill a small lamp from the can and light it on a bench, and it burned with a yellow flame, steady and a little smoky, and the smell of it was less than the smell of the shed.

Peigi watched the flame for a long time. She held her hand over the chimney and felt the heat, and turned the wick up and down, and held a sheet of her pocket-book behind it to see how the light fell. Then she asked the question she had come to ask.

"Where did you learn the making?"

"You'll take a glass of wine," said the master of the company.

He took her up to a room over the gate where there was a fire of driftwood and a table with a cloth on it. The wine was from the south-west coast, red and good, and he poured it himself. He talked about the harbour, and the price of hulls, and how the coal ships had had nothing to carry these five years, which was true. His father, he said, had carried coal for A' Chompanaidh of the humans from the west coast to the capital and been paid in human silver. Then he asked after the keeper at Tobar dhearg, whom he had known as a girl.

When she asked the question again, he filled her glass.

When she asked it a third time, he smiled and said, "We are shipmasters, engineer. We go a long way along the coast and we hear a great deal. We have a paper for the lease, and a paper for the harbour-due, and the maor has seen both. There's no law on the making. You'll find nothing wrong with us."

"I'm not looking for anything wrong."

"Then you'll find nothing," he said, and filled his own glass.

She went down to the yard afterwards and stood in the wind to clear her head. Men were rolling barrels down a plank onto a cart for the quay. Most were Dia-thìrich of the town, in the blue smocks of the harbour. Four or five were not.

She knew them for Tuathaich before she heard them speak, by the cut of their hair and the way they stood apart at the water-butt; and when one of them called to another it was in the humans' tongue, and the answer came back in Dia-thìris, as if the two tongues were one road with a ford in it. It was a rare thing in the south to see Tuathaich working at all. They do not come south, the council's old minute says; and here were five of them at the far end of the island, in a shipmasters' yard, with black to the elbow.

One of them was fitting a new tap to a barrel at the end of the plank. He was a young man, lean, with his sleeves rolled above the elbow and a burn scar on the back of one wrist, and he worked the tap into the wood with small turns of a wrench, listening to it. When it was in he tried it, closed it, wiped it with his thumb, and went on to the next. He did not look up. Peigi watched him fit three taps. Not one of them dripped.

"Who is that?" she asked the foreman, a Seann Skell man.

"That one? Ashby. Joe Ashby. From Caol mhòr, up north." The foreman shrugged. "They're good hands, the northern ones. They know the stuff."

"How do they know it?"

The foreman looked at her, and then at the gate-house window, and said he had barrels to see to.

Three more days she stayed. She saw the retorts filled and the spoil go in grey and come out black, and she saw the locked shed opened twice, each time for a man to go in with a lamp and come out again, and the door shut behind him. She counted the barrels in the fourth shed, and the carts on the quay, and the hulls in the harbour with the company's mark, a black ring, painted on their bows, and wrote it all down. On the fourth morning she took the coach back to the capital and wrote her report on the way, with the book on her knee and the coach lurching.

*The fuel burns clean enough for a lamp,* she wrote. *It may serve for the lights. The works will not say how it is made, and I think they will not say it to us or to anyone. They have Tuathaich hands who know the stuff. I asked where from and was given wine.*

She thought about striking the last line out, and left it.

## II. Of the Making-Shed

Her masters read her report and filed it. Nobody wrote to her about it. In an t-Ògmhios she found a reason to go south again: the lantern at Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg wanted new glass, and the glass-blowers of Seann Skell, who blow from the white sand of their own shore, were the best on the island and half the price of the capital's. She saw the glass ordered in a day. Then she walked over to the works.

The chimney was finished. It stood above the salt-sheds like a finger raised, and its smoke went up straight on still days and lay along the shore on windy ones, and the fishwives of the harbour said the herring tasted of it. The town had taken a petition to the maor about the reek. The maor had read it and answered that the company paid its harbour-due. That was the end of the petition.

The works had doubled since am Màrt. There were twelve retorts now, and two new sheds, and a jetty of its own where a hull with the black ring on her bow was unloading spoil into barrows. The master of the company was away up the coast. The foreman let her in without asking him, because she had been there before and it was a hot day and he was busy.

She did not go near the locked shed. She went and found the Tuathaich.

There were nine of them now. They worked the retorts at the far end, the hottest end, in shifts, and they ate together at noon in the shade of the jetty wall, bread and cold fish and a can of tea boiled over a dubhan lamp. Joe Ashby was among them. He had grown a beard since the spring, and his scar had a new one beside it.

Peigi sat down on an upturned barrel near them with her own bread. It was not a thing an engineer of the lights did, and the men went silent. After a while one of the older ones said something in the humans' tongue that made two of the others laugh. Joe did not laugh.

"He says," Joe said to her, in Dia-thìris, "that you've come to learn the making by sitting on it."

It was the first time she had heard him speak. His Dia-thìris was the north's, with the long vowels shorter, and quick.

"I came to ask about the taps," Peigi said. "I watched you in the spring. You fitted three and none of them dripped. The lights have barrels that drip at every tap on the island."

He looked at her for a while. Then he took a tap out of his apron pocket and passed it to her, and showed her how the thread was cut finer than the carpenters cut it, and how he wrapped it with tarred cord, and why the cord went on with the thread and not against it. It took him a long time to explain, because he did it with his hands more than with words, and she asked him to do it twice. The others went back to their bread.

"Where did you learn that?"

"Caol mhòr. There's boats at Caol mhòr. You learn taps on boats or you bail."

"And the dubhan? Where did you learn to work the retorts?"

"Here," said Joe.

"And before here?"

He took the tap back from her and put it in his apron pocket.

"Before here," he said, "I fitted boats at Caol mhòr."

That was all, and she did not ask him again. Peigi was no fool. She could see that the men at the jetty wall had stopped eating to listen, and that the foreman at the retort-house door had turned his head. Whatever there was to be told, it was not going to be told on an upturned barrel at noon to a woman from the capital with ink on her finger. She was not even sure that Joe Ashby knew it. He might have come south for the wage, as men go anywhere for a wage, and been set to the retorts because he was quick with his hands. The Tuathaich at the wall might have been hired off the quay for all she could tell. The company's hulls went up and down the whole coast.

In the afternoon one of the retorts burst.

She heard it from the yard, a crack like a mast going, and then the shouting. By the time she reached the retort-house door the men had the furnace raked out and were throwing sand on the floor, and a Seann Skell man was sitting against the wall with his arm held out in front of him and the skin of it gone white and wet. The stuff had come out of the split seam of the retort in a black spray. Joe Ashby had him by the shoulder and was pouring water over the arm from a bucket, slowly, and talking to him in Dia-thìris the whole time about nothing: the weather, the herring, the fishwives. The foreman had gone for the doctor.

Peigi looked at the retort. It had split along the lower seam where the rivets were, and she could see why: the iron had been heated too fast on one side, where the furnace door was, and had not had time to follow on the other. She said so to the foreman when he came back. He looked at her as if she had spoken in the humans' tongue.

"Bank the fire on the door side," she said. "Or put a baffle in. Anyone who has tended a tower furnace could tell you. The heat wants to go round."

The foreman said he would tell the master when he came back.

She went back to the inn and drew a baffle for the retort furnace on a sheet of her pocket-book, with the measures, and sent it round to the works in the morning with a boy, unsigned. Then she went back to the capital with the glass for Tobar dhearg packed in straw on the coach roof, and she did not know until the autumn that the company had fitted her baffle to every furnace in the row.

## III. Of Litir an Àrd-choimheadaiche

In an t-Samhain she was in the south a third time, for the new glass was being set in the lantern at Tobar dhearg and she would not leave that to anyone. It rained for a week. On the evening appointed she walked into Seann Skell along the cliff path, with the gale at her back and the rain coming over her shoulder, to hear Litir an Àrd-choimheadaiche read in Brìde's house.

Everyone knew it was to be read. The letter had been talked of in the south since the summer. Alasdair mac Ìomhair, Àrd-choimheadaiche of Òrd Bhrìde since before Cathal was crowned, had been asked by the custodians of four shires whether the new fuel was lawful to Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, and had taken the whole of the summer and the autumn to answer. Some said he was waiting for the council. Some said he was waiting for the works to fail. The fishwives said he was waiting for the smell to go away.

On {{date:VI-0009}} his answer was sent to every house of the order on the island, to be read aloud at the evening lamp, and at Seann Skell he read it himself.

The great house of Òrd Bhrìde at Seann Skell is the largest house of the order anywhere, and the oldest part of it is older than the kingdom: a long hall of grey stone with a roof of heavy slate, and at the far end, up three steps, Brìde's altar, with the coal flame burning in its iron cup as it had burned since before the humans came. It was the only fire in the hall. The rain beat on the high windows and the wind came under the door, and the people stood close in their wet coats and steamed. There were more of them than the hall was built for. Peigi stood at the back against the wall, where she could see the altar over the heads.

The coal on the altar was poor. She could see that from the back of the hall. The flame had the coloured edge still, the dark blue and the green that the old coal gives; but it was low in the cup and it burned unevenly, and every so often it spat. The custodians fed it with tongs from a small box, a lump at a time, and the box was not full.

Alasdair mac Ìomhair came out from the vestry in his grey robe with the letter in his hand. He was sixty-two that year, tall and stooping, with a long face and a voice that did not need to be raised. He stood beside the altar, where the light fell on the page, and waited until the hall was still. Then he read.

The letter was not long. It greeted the houses in Brìde's name. It said that a fuel was now made on the island out of what the pits cast away, by a man's hand and without the vein; that the custodians had asked whether the order might suffer it; and that he who wrote had taken counsel and prayed at the altar and read what the order had written since An Ceangal. Then it gave the ruling.

"It is not forbidden," read Alasdair mac Ìomhair, "since it is not coal. And it is not blessed, since it is not coal."

He lowered the page. For a breath nobody in the hall moved, and the rain came on the windows. Then a custodian in the front row, a young man from one of the eastern houses, said aloud, "That's the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it," said Alasdair mac Ìomhair.

There was a stir through the hall like wind through barley. Somebody near Peigi laughed, and stopped. An old woman in front of her said to her neighbour, very clearly, "Then my lamp has no blessing on it and I'll have to put up with it," and the people round her smiled, because many of them had dubhan lamps at home already, bought off the works' carts that summer. They had wanted to know whether they had done wrong. The Àrd-choimheadaiche had told them that they had not done wrong, and that they had not done right either, and that the vein did not come into it.

Peigi thought it was the cleverest thing she had heard a priest say. She thought, too, that it left every house on the island free to go on as it liked, and every custodian free to quarrel about it for years. She said so to Alasdair mac Ìomhair himself, half an hour later, which she had not meant to do.

He had come down the hall after the reading to speak to the people, as the custodians did, and she had not moved fast enough to get out of his way. He stopped in front of her and asked, pleasantly, whether she was not the engineer of the lights, who had been seen at the works; and she said she was; and before she could stop herself she said that the letter was very clever and would be argued for years.

"I hope so," said the old man. "A letter nobody argues is a letter nobody reads." He looked down at her with his long face. "You have come about the lights."

"I've come about the glass at Tobar dhearg."

"You have come about the lights," he said again, as if she had not spoken. "Your towers burn the island's coal, and the island's coal is failing, and you have come to see whether the new stuff will burn in a lantern." He looked back up the hall towards the altar, where the custodians were feeding the flame a lump at a time from the small box. "The houses will ask me next whether a sea-light may burn it. They will ask whether a flame of Brìde's may be put out for it. I should be glad of more time than I had for this one."

"Would you forbid it?"

"I have just told the whole island," said Alasdair mac Ìomhair, "that I forbid nothing that is not coal." He smiled a little, and went on down the hall to the people waiting for him.

The letter was argued, as he said it would be. Within the month the school of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh at Caol shean had written that a fuel made from the vein's own waste could not be called wholly *not coal*, since the spoil had lain against the seam for a thousand years; and the houses of Òrd an t-Seabhaig round Cnoc bheag, who had never owned the Àrd-choimheadaiche's right to rule on anything, wrote that they had not asked his opinion and were glad to have it all the same. The letter was read in every house of the order that winter and copied into the books of most of them, and it was quoted at supper-tables in the south for a long time. At the works, where they had heard that Alasdair mac Ìomhair was to rule on the fuel and had feared the worst, the master of the company opened a cask of the south-west wine for the whole yard, Tuathaich and all, and the fishwives said that the smell that night was the smell of a company that had been let off.

## IV. Of the Twenty Barrels

On {{date:VI-0010}} the works sold their first year's making.

Peigi had not meant to be there. The glass at Tobar dhearg was set and the lantern lit behind it, the keeper had signed for it, and she had her seat taken on the coach for the week before midwinter. Then the gale came back and the coast road flooded under the cliff at the ford below Tobar dhearg, and the coach did not run for three days. She spent the three days at the inn on the quay, writing letters to the capital that could not be sent, and on the fourth she walked round the harbour to the works because she had nothing else to do and could not keep away.

The harbour was full of the company's hulls, riding at the mole with the black ring on their bows, and between them the fishing boats of the south coast were rafted three deep, waiting to load. The skippers had come in from all along the shore, from Tobar dhearg and the coves to the east of it and from as far as the western end of the coast, for the word had gone up and down that the works would sell before the midwinter and that dubhan was cheaper than coal by a third. The south-coast boats had burned coal in their galley-stoves and their riding-lamps since the humans' time. For five years now they had been burning driftwood and fish-oil and going cold, and more than one had gone on the rocks for want of a lamp that would stay lit in a gale. So they came in for dubhan, and they came in numbers.

The barrels came down out of the fourth shed on a slide of greased planks and went onto the boats by tackle from the jetty, two at a time, swinging out over the water with a man on the guide-rope. On the quay a clerk of the company sat at a table under a sailcloth with his ledger and his box of money, and the skippers stood in the rain in a line to pay him, in coin and in fish and in promises. Up the slope from the quay a row of carts stood with their tails to the works, and these were for the capital: dubhan for the lamp-houses of Cathair dhearg, bought by the barrel on the lamp-houses' account, to light the poorer streets that had been left dark for three winters to save coal.

Peigi stood by the jetty wall and watched the carts load. She counted them, because she always counted things. There were eleven carts, six barrels to a cart.

Above everything the chimney went up into the rain. Its smoke lay along the shore to the east, grey on grey, and in the thick air it looked taller than it was. It was the tallest thing on that coast. From the quay it stood higher than Brìde's house on the height above the harbour; and the fishwives, who had never forgiven it, said it was put up where it was so that the Àrd-choimheadaiche would have to look at it every morning.

At noon the master of the company came down onto the quay in his good coat to see the last carts off, and found her.

"The engineer," he said, and took her hand in both his again. "Stormbound with us. We are honoured twice." He looked at the carts. "Your baffle, I'm told. On every furnace. We've not split a retort since the summer." He did not say who had told him, and she did not ask.

"I'm glad of it," Peigi said.

"The company is grateful. If there were ever anything the company could do for the lights."

She had not known until he said it what she was going to do. She thought afterwards that she had known since the spring, since the lamp on the bench in the fourth shed, and had only been waiting for somebody to say it to her so that she could answer.

"Twenty barrels," she said. "On account, for the lights. For a trial."

The master of the company looked at her for a while with his head a little on one side, the way the gulls look. He knew as well as she did that an engineer of Comhairle nan Taighean-solais could not buy a barrel of lamp-glass without a letter from her masters, let alone twenty barrels of a fuel those masters had filed without a word. He knew too that she had no such letter, for if she had, she would have shown it to him in am Màrt.

"A trial of what?"

"Of whether it will burn a sea-light through a winter."

"And your masters have agreed to this trial."

"My masters," said Peigi, "will agree when they have the figures."

He laughed, a short bark, and called the clerk from under the sailcloth. The clerk wrote it in the ledger while the rain dripped off the edge of the cloth onto the page: *twenty barrels to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, on account, for the engineer*. Peigi signed it. Her hand was steady. Her name went down in the company's book below the skippers of the south coast and the lamp-houses of the capital, in the same black ink, and she noticed that there was coal-dust in the ink, and did not know why she noticed it.

"Where will you have them sent?"

She had not thought of that either. The towers were at the island's ends: Tobar dhearg on the southern cape a few miles along the shore, Baile ìseal far away on the eastern cape, the three others in the north. Tobar dhearg was the nearest and the easiest, and at Tobar dhearg every soul who worked the light would know by the next morning that the engineer from the capital was burning the new stuff under their tower, and so would the house of Brìde on the height. She thought of the keeper's face.

"Hold them for me," she said. "I'll write."

"Twenty barrels held for the engineer," said the master of the company. "Joe."

Joe Ashby came out from the fourth shed with his sleeves rolled and a lump of chalk in his hand. He had heard the whole of it; she could see that he had. He went along the row by the shed wall and counted off twenty barrels and rolled them out of the line, one at a time, onto the planks under the eaves where the rain did not reach. On the head of each one he chalked a mark. She went to look at it when he had done. It was a lantern: a square with a flame inside it, drawn in four strokes and a curl, the same on all twenty.

"That's the lights'," he said.

"The lights have no mark."

"They have now," said Joe Ashby. He put the chalk in his apron pocket, next to the taps, and went back into the shed.

The last of the carts for the capital went up the slope at the fall of the dark, their lanterns lit with the stuff they carried and swinging on the tail-boards. The fishing boats cast off from the mole one by one and went out past the light on the end of it into the rain, and the smell of their galley-stoves came back across the water, tar and wet hearth and something sharp; the smell of the works, going out along the coast in every boat. Peigi stood on the quay until the last lamp was out of sight round the headland. Then she went back to the inn and wrote to her masters.

It took her until past midnight. The letter said that she had bought twenty barrels of dubhan on her masters' account, without leave, at the works of Comann nan Sciobairean at Seann Skell of the south; and she gave the price, which was a third less than the price of coal for the same hours of light; and she said that she asked their leave to run a trial of the stuff in a spare lamp, in the yard below one of their towers, for a hundred nights, with a man to keep the log. If they would not give leave, she would pay for the barrels herself out of her wage, and they could stop it from her quarter by quarter.

She read it over. It named no tower. Of the chimney it said nothing, nor of the locked shed, the Tuathaich at the jetty wall or the wine. On the last line of the letter she wrote the date and signed it; and out of the window, over the harbour, the smoke of the works went up into the dark where she could not see it, and she could smell it still.
