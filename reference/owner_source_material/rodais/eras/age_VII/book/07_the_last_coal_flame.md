# Of the Last Coal Flame

*DE 6*

## I. Of the Warrant of Tobar dhearg

At Tobar dhearg they tell it from the keeper's side, since it was the keeper who walked. The custodians of the south coast tell it from the altar, and the fitters who came after tell it from the stair; but the village on the red well says that nobody else was near enough to see the thing whole, and that Ciorstaidh nic Artair never told it twice the same way, and so they tell it for her.

She was in her sixty-ninth year when the order came, and she had kept Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg for twenty-two of them. Before that she had kept it under her father for as long as she could remember. He had taught her to trim the grate before she could reach it, standing her on a salt-box, and to read the colour of the fire the way a fisherman reads a sky. On a night in an t-Ògmhios, twenty-two years back, a boat of Seann Skell had gone onto the reef below the light in a south-westerly, and he had gone down with a line to it, and the sea had taken him off the rocks. She went down after him with the same line. She brought four men in, one by one, and the fifth could walk. Comhairle nan Taighean-solais sent her his warrant in a month, with a letter regretting the loss of a servant of long standing, and that autumn she had a post driven into the reef with a bell on it that the swell rang. While she kept the light no boat had gone aground there. The village could tell you that before it could tell you anything else about her.

The tower stood out on the cape below the village, a stone drum older than anyone's grandmother, and on its top, inside the lantern-glass the humans had set there in the days of A' Chompanaidh, the fire burned in an iron grate. It had burned coal for more than a hundred years. It burned the island's coal, broken small, which when it catches shows every colour in the break and gives a flame that no other fuel gives: bright at the heart, and at the edge dark and coloured, like oil on water, green and violet and a red that is nearly brown. Seen from the sea it was a warm light, low and a little unsteady, and the south-coast boats knew it from any other light on the island by that, and some of their old men said they could tell it from a star at twenty miles.

The coal came by sea from Seann Skell in sacks. For three years it had come short. The master of stores wrote each quarter that the lights stood first on the kingdom's list after the altars, which was true, and that the lights' allowance was what Comhairle nam Mèinnean could spare, which was also true, and Ciorstaidh had learned to bank the grate on the short nights of summer and let it roar only in the long ones. She kept the tally of every sack in her own book beside the log. The druids of the village, who by old custom kept the light's log for Brìde's sake, wrote the weather and the ships; she wrote the coal, in a small hand, with the price when she could get it.

On {{date:VI-0014}} Comhairle nan Taighean-solais sat and had read to it the council's order that the five lights of the coast should be turned to dubhan, the first works of the kingdom to be so turned, and that the coal they burned should go to the altars. The copy came down the coast to Tobar dhearg eleven days later in a leather case, carried by a clerk who had never been to the south coast and was sick on the fish-cart from Seann Skell. He gave it to her at the tower door and stood waiting, as if she might have a reply.

She read it standing. It was short. The lights of Baile ìseal and Tobar dhearg, being nearest the works at Seann Skell, were to be turned first, in the summer; the others as the fitters could reach them. The engineer of the lights was being sent to fit the burners. The keepers would keep their warrants, their wages and their houses.

"It says nothing of the grate," Ciorstaidh said.

The clerk said that he believed the grate would be taken out.

"And the fire in it?"

He did not know. He had not been told about the fire.

She gave him bread and a cup of the well water, which he looked at doubtfully because it ran reddish from the rock and always had, and sent him back up to the village to find his cart. Then she climbed the stair, which had a hundred and nine steps and a rope along the wall worn to the colour of a chestnut, and sat in the lamp-room with the letter on her knee until it was time to feed the fire.

## II. Of the Custodians' Asking and the Deputy

The custodians of Tobar dhearg were three, an old man and two younger women, and their house stood by the well at the top of the village. They were of Òrd Bhrìde, as nearly all the south coast was, and they held the light's log as their predecessors had since before the humans came, when the beacon was a fire in a ring of stones on the cape and the log was notches on a stick. They had known the order was coming before the clerk was sick on the fish-cart. The Àrd-choimheadaiche at Seann Skell had known it before the order was read.

The old custodian came down to the tower the next evening. He was a slow walker and she saw him on the path a long while before he reached her.

"The Àrd-choimheadaiche will not bless the new burners," he said, when he had his breath. "You know his letter."

Everyone on the south coast knew the letter. Alasdair mac Ìomhair had written it to every house of Òrd Bhrìde two years before, when the shipmasters' works first smoked at Seann Skell: the new fuel was not forbidden, since it was not coal, and not blessed, since it was not coal. It had been read out in every house, and argued over in every tavern that had a druid in it, and the fishermen had made a rhyme of it that was not repeated in front of custodians.

"He will not forbid them either," Ciorstaidh said.

"No. But the fire in the tower has been coal since A' Chompanaidh lit it, and before that it was a shore-fire of driftwood and wrack. It was Brìde's fire on this cape before there were clerks to own it. We have asked him that it not be let go out on a clerk's say, like a lamp in a kitchen. We have asked that it be carried to her altar at Seann Skell, and burn out there, where the flame first came from."

She looked at him a long time. The fire behind her ticked in the grate.

"And what does he say?"

"He says yes. He has written to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, and they have written back that they have no objection, provided the light is not dark a single night." The old man folded his hands. "They will send a man for the lantern. They offer one of their own clerks, or the Àrd-choimheadaiche will send a young custodian of the house there. It is ten miles, keeper, and in the heat."

"It is nine and a half," Ciorstaidh said, "and I have walked it every quarter for twenty-two years to argue for coal."

She said nothing more that night. The next morning she wrote to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, in the same small hand she kept the coal in, that she thanked them for the offer of a deputy and would not need one; that the flame was hers by her warrant until the burner was lit; and that she would carry it herself or it would go out on the cape in its own grate, and they might choose. The answer came in a fortnight and was a line long. The keeper was to arrange the matter with the Àrd-choimheadaiche as she saw fit.

The village says she was angry for a week after, and that nobody could tell with whom. The custodians thought it was the lights' masters. The clerk who had to carry the next letter thought it was himself. Her under-keeper, a Baile gharbh lad of nineteen whom she had taken on four years before because he could climb the stair twice without stopping, thought it was the coal, because she went at the grate that week as if it owed her money. He was the only one of them who ever said so to her face, and she told him he was nearer than the rest.

## III. Of the Tank at the Stair's Foot

The engineer of the lights came in the third week of an t-Ògmhios, off a works boat from Seann Skell that came round the cape in the early morning and stood off the landing-stones below the light, and the whole village came down to watch her land.

Ciorstaidh had heard of Peigi nic Ascaill, as every keeper had. She was a woman under forty who had run a dubhan lamp for a hundred nights in the yard below Taigh-solais Baile ìseal, on the eastern cape, and had it hold in a gale when the tower's own coal flame blew out. Every keeper on the island had had that story by letter within the month, and every keeper had an opinion of it. Seonag nic Thòmais at Baile ìseal had refused, it was said, to let the thing up her stair at all, and now had it at the top of her stair by the council's order and was not speaking to anyone who had signed it. Ciorstaidh had rather liked the sound of Seonag nic Thòmais.

The engineer came up the landing-stones in a man's coat with the sleeves turned back, and stood at the foot of the tower and looked up at it with her head on one side, and the first thing she said to its keeper was that the stair would do.

"At Àth dhearg it won't," she said. "I've the drawings. The tank would stick at the second turn like a cork. But yours is wide. The humans built this one first and hadn't learned to be mean with stone yet."

"The tank?" Ciorstaidh said.

It was coming off the boat. It was a drum of riveted iron, taller than a man's waist and as wide as a well-head, and it took eight of the village men with poles and ropes to bring it over the landing-stones, and the boat's crew stood on the gunwale and shouted advice. After it came the burner in a crate, and the pipes in a bundle, and the glass chimneys in straw, and then the barrels. There were forty barrels, black, and they were rolled up the path and stood in a row against the tower's wall where the coal-sacks used to stand, and the smell came off them in the warm morning and went up the cape.

Ciorstaidh had smelt dubhan before. By then everyone on the south coast had. The works at Seann Skell sent it out in its barrels to the fishing boats and the lamp-houses, and it burned on the quays at night in the new lamps, and the whole harbour smelt of it in a wet wind. Up close it was worse. It was a thick smell, like a burnt pan and something from under a pit-heap, and it caught in the back of the throat.

"You'll stop noticing," said Peigi nic Ascaill, who had watched her face.

"I doubt it."

"You will. I did." The engineer looked at the barrels without much love. "It burns, it stinks, and it is not coal. Comhairle nam Mèinnean's own surveyors wrote that four years ago, and nobody has improved on it."

They took four days over the fitting. The tank went up the stair on ropes, with the village men sweating under it and Ciorstaidh walking behind with a lantern, because the lamp-room was dark while the fire was banked for the day and she did not trust any of them on the turn. It sat in the room below the lamp-room, where the coal had been kept in the humans' time, and a pipe went up from it through the floor. The burner stood in the lantern-glass beside the grate. For four nights the grate burned as it always had and the burner stood cold beside it, a brass thing like a ship's bell turned over, with its row of wicks, and Ciorstaidh fed the coal and did not look at it.

On the third evening the engineer came up the stair after supper and sat on the step of the lamp-room while the keeper fed the fire. It was the longest day of the year or near it, and the sun was not down till late, and the grate was banked low for the short night.

"You keep it low," Peigi said.

"The coal comes short. Three years now. I bank it in summer. The boats know the light's lower in summer, and they know why." Ciorstaidh knocked the grate. The flame came up, and the colours came up with it along its edge, the violet and the green. "They say they can tell it from a star at twenty miles."

"Can they?"

"The old ones say so. I think they tell it from a star because it's where they've always seen it." She sat back on her heels. "Your lamp at Baile ìseal. The one that held."

"It held."

"And the tower's went out."

"In the gale. The draught came down the chimney and the coal was damp. It happens." The engineer looked at the fire. "Murchadh mac Coinnich kept the log for me. He was keeper there once. He was sorry it went out. Into the log it went, that the tower's went out and mine didn't, and then he wrote that he was sorry. He didn't sign that line."

Ciorstaidh said nothing to that for a while. Then she said, "Mine doesn't go out."

"No," Peigi said. "I've read your log. Yours doesn't."

They sat together without talking, and the light went down over the sea to the west, and the fire grew in the glass as the sky darkened, until from the cape it would have been the brightest thing on the whole south coast. Below, somewhere under the lamp-room, the dubhan in the tank ticked as it cooled. The engineer went down at the dark, and Ciorstaidh stayed and fed the grate until her under-keeper came up at midnight to take the second half.

They lit the burner on the fourth night for a trial, beside the coal, an hour only. It came up white. There was no other word for it. It was a steady, hard, white light without an edge, and it made the coal flame look like the fire in a cottage hearth, and it threw the shadows of the lantern-glass's iron bars out across the cape so that the whole village could see them lying on the grass. The boats that were out that night saw it and came in to ask what had happened. The engineer turned it down after the hour, and the grate burned on alone, and the old custodian, who had been at the foot of the tower all the while, went back up to the village without a word to anyone.

She had the day of the carrying set with the Àrd-choimheadaiche by letter: {{date:VI-0015}}, a day with no feast on it, so that nobody could say it had been chosen for a sign. The burner would be lit that evening for good. Until then the grate would burn as it always had. The engineer went back to Seann Skell on the works boat to do what she could at Baile ìseal, where the burner had been lit the week before and was already being blamed for everything, and she promised to come back the day before the carrying to see the burner in, and to walk with the keeper if the keeper would have her. The keeper said she might suit herself.

## IV. Of the Road to Seann Skell

She did not sleep the night before, and had not expected to. All night she sat up in the lamp-room with the fire and let her under-keeper sleep through the whole of his watch, which he held against her afterwards, and she fed the grate with the last of the quarter's coal, a sack and a half, and let it burn as high as it would burn on a summer night, higher than it had burned since the coal began to come short. The boats that were out saw it and wondered, and some of them knew.

In the grey before dawn she took down the lantern. It was a closed lantern of brass and thick glass, a storm lantern of the humans' make with a little door in its side and a pot at its foot for a handful of coal, and it had hung in the lamp-room for as long as the tower had stood, for carrying the fire down to the landing-stones on nights when a boat came in blind. She had carried it down that stair with her father's line coiled over her shoulder. She filled the pot with the best of the coal she had kept back, broken small, and she took a coal from the grate with the tongs and laid it on the others, and closed the door, and watched through the glass until the new coals caught.

Then she let the grate go.

That is the part the village does not tell, because she did not tell it, and there was only the under-keeper to see, and he had been asleep. He woke to find the grate dark and cold enough to put a hand in, and the keeper sitting on the step of the lamp-room with the lantern on her knees, and the sun coming up out of the sea to the east. He said afterwards that she looked the way his grandmother had looked the morning after his grandfather was buried, sitting straight on a hard chair with her good shawl on. She told him to get the burner going the moment the sun was off the sea that night, and not a quarter-hour later, or she would hear of it at Seann Skell. He said she would hear of nothing else. She told him to mind his tongue.

The engineer had come on the evening boat and slept in the keeper's house. She was at the tower door with bread and a flask of the red water when the keeper came down the hundred and nine steps with the lantern held out a little from her body, the way you carry a thing that is hot or that someone might want to take off you. The flame burned inside the glass with the colours along its edge. The two of them set off up the path to the village.

It was a hot morning with a haze on the sea, and the whole of Tobar dhearg was out. They stood at their doors along the one street and round the red well. Nobody spoke to her, since she plainly did not want to be spoken to, and the children who ran after her were called back. At the well the three custodians of the village fell in behind, the old man in the middle, and so did a good many others, and when she went out of the village on the road north-west there were thirty people walking behind the keeper and her lantern, and the engineer in her man's coat among them, and the fish-cart following empty in case anybody fell down.

The road from the cape to Seann Skell runs along the coast behind the dunes, then inland round the marshes where the burn from Baile gharbh comes down, then out along the shore again to the town. It is nine and a half miles. It was a road she knew stone by stone. On it she had walked to Seann Skell every quarter for twenty-two years to wait on the master of stores and argue for coal, and walked home again with less than she had asked for.

She walked it that day without stopping, except twice at wells to drink. She did not hurry, and she did not give the lantern to anyone. At Baile gharbh the fishermen's wives were at their doors, and one of them came out with a cup of milk and held it up to her without a word, and she drank it and gave the cup back and went on. At the ford over the burn a carter coming the other way with a load of salt pulled his horse into the reeds and took off his cap and stood there holding it until they had all gone by. A child asked in a loud voice what the old woman was carrying, and its mother said *Brìde's fire*, and the child said, *but it's only a lamp*, and nobody laughed.

Past the marshes the road came out onto the shore again and the town was in sight: the roofs of Seann Skell along the bay, and the masts in the harbour, and the long grey roof of Brìde's house on the rise above the quays, which was the greatest house of Òrd Bhrìde on the island. And to the left of the town, down by the old salt-sheds, stood the chimney of Obair-dhubhain Seann Skell, the shipmasters' works, which was the tallest thing on the coast by then and could be seen from Baile gharbh on a clear day. It was putting out smoke that morning, as it put out smoke every morning, and the haze from it lay along the shore in the heat, and the smell of it came to meet them on the wind a mile before they reached the town.

Ciorstaidh looked at it once and walked on towards it. The engineer, who was walking at her shoulder by then, said afterwards that she had been ready for the keeper to say something about the chimney, and would have understood whatever it was, and that the keeper said nothing at all and only shifted the lantern to her other hand.

At the town's edge a young custodian of the house was waiting for them, a lad not much older than her under-keeper, sent down by the Àrd-choimheadaiche to meet her. He bowed to the lantern first and then to the keeper, and offered to carry it the last half-mile up the hill, since that was the steep part. She looked at him until he coloured, and then she thanked him, and asked him to walk ahead and show the way, since the streets had changed since last she came. They had not changed at all. He walked ahead.

## V. Of Brìde's Altar

It was late in the afternoon when they came up to Brìde's house, and the sun was on the west side of it, and the doors stood open. The people who had walked from Tobar dhearg stopped at the steps and did not go in, since the hall was for the custodians that day, and the townspeople who had come up to see stood with them. The engineer went in, because the Àrd-choimheadaiche had asked for her by name, and stood by the door with her coat over her arm.

The hall of Brìde's house at Seann Skell is long and narrow and cool, with a stone floor worn into shallow dips by the feet of those who have stood there, and a roof of dark timber held up by posts that were ships' masts once. At the far end, on a step of three stones, is the altar, which is a block of grey stone with a hollow in its top, and in the hollow burns Brìde's flame, which is fed with the vein's coal in measure and from it the order lights its first lamp of the year on the feast of Brìde. That day the altar-flame had been let burn low, and the hollow beside it was clear.

The custodians of four shires were standing in two rows down the length of the hall, one on each side, facing inward, in their grey. There were more than sixty of them. Some had come a long way. The custodians of Seann Bhral had walked from their town, and the houses of the farther shires had come by cart the day before and slept on the hall floor. They stood in their rows and did not speak, and they did not turn their heads as she passed.

Ciorstaidh nic Artair walked down the hall between them with the lantern held out before her. Her boots were loud on the flags. The engineer said that it was the only sound in the place, and that she had never heard boots so loud in her life, and that she could hear the old woman's breath as well, because it had been ten miles in the heat and she was sixty-eight.

At the foot of the altar the Àrd-choimheadaiche was waiting. Alasdair mac Ìomhair was a thin man in his middle sixties, with a long face and his grey hair cut short in the custodians' way, and he stood aside from the altar as she came and made room for her on the step. He did not offer to take the lantern. The custodians of Tobar dhearg, who had told him a good deal about their keeper by letter, may have warned him not to.

She went up the three stones and set the lantern down in the clear hollow beside Brìde's flame. She opened its little door.

The flame inside leaned towards the air, as a flame does when a door is opened on it, and seemed for a breath about to go out, and steadied. It burned up in the open with the colours along its edge, a little higher than it had burned in the glass, and it gave the altar step a warm light that was different from the light of the altar-flame beside it. Then it settled down to burn the coal that was left in the lantern's pot.

Nobody told her to go, so she stayed. She stood at the side of the altar step with her hands folded, and the custodians stood in their rows, and Alasdair mac Ìomhair stood across the step from her, and nobody spoke, and the coal burned.

It took longer than anyone had thought. The custodians had been told by the house, or had told each other, that it would be a matter of an hour. It was the best of the coal, broken small, and she had packed the pot as tight as it would go, as she packed the grate in winter. After the first hour the young custodians at the back of the hall began to shift on their feet. After the second, the light through the west windows had gone from yellow to red, and the long shadows of the ranks lay across the floor, and one of the Seann Bhral custodians, an old woman, sat down on the floor where she stood and nobody reproached her. The engineer stood by the door the whole time. She said later that she had meant to stay a quarter of an hour, and found at the end that she had not once thought of going.

The Àrd-choimheadaiche did not move, and neither did the keeper. What he thought of it he never set down in any letter that is open to an Leabharlann, and the custodians of the south coast have never agreed about it among themselves. Some say he prayed. Some say he was counting, and meant to write the time to Comhairle nan Taighean-solais. The keeper said afterwards to her under-keeper, when he asked, that the Àrd-choimheadaiche had stood on the altar step for three hours watching a lamp go out and had not once looked at the door, and that she could not ask more of any man than that, and would not have got it from the clerks of the lights.

At the end, in the last of the light, the fire in the lantern's pot went down to a few coals, and to one. The flame stood up off it very small, and the colours went out of its edge one after another, the red and then the green, until it was only blue. Then it shrank to a blue bead the size of a barleycorn, sitting on the coal, and it went on sitting there, a long time, while the hall got darker round it.

Then there was nothing on the coal.

A thin thread of smoke went up from the pot, straight in the still air, grey, and rose until it met the roof and hung there under the dark timber. Down the right-hand row one of the custodians let out a breath as if he had been holding it the whole while, and someone else coughed, and it was over.

Alasdair mac Ìomhair stepped forward then, and put out his hand to the lantern's little door, and looked at the keeper, and she nodded. He closed it. He said the words that are said at Brìde's altar when a lamp that has burned the vein's coal is let go out, which are short and old and are not written down outside the houses; and then he said, in the ordinary voice of a man talking across a table, that the flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg had come home to the altar it was first lit from, and gone out there, and that he thanked the keeper who had carried it.

"The order does not bless the new light," he said. "Nor does it forbid it. I have said so, and there is no more it can say. This it can bless." He laid his hand flat on the lantern, which was still warm. "This it blesses."

Ciorstaidh nic Artair said, "Thank you, lord," and then, "I'll have my lantern back, if the house doesn't want it."

He looked at her a while, and then he picked it up off the altar step and gave it to her, and the custodians broke their rows.

## VI. Of the Light Seen from the Quays

She would not stay in Brìde's house that night, though a bed had been made for her, and she would not go home on the fish-cart in the dark. She went down with the engineer to the harbour, where there was a lodging-house that took in keepers who came to argue for coal and had taken her in for twenty-two years, and the landlady gave her the room she always had, which looked south-east down the coast.

The harbour was full that night. Word had come down from Brìde's house ahead of them, and the fishermen had come in early, and the quays were crowded under the new dubhan lamps, which the works had set up along the harbour-front the year before and which lit the wet stones white and made the whole quay smell of the burnt pan. Down in the taverns along the harbour they were singing.

The song was new that night. The fishermen of the south coast, who had made a rhyme of the Àrd-choimheadaiche's letter, made a song of the carrying, the same evening; the harbour says it was first sung in a tavern under the quay wall by a skipper of Tobar dhearg whose father had been one of the four men brought in off the reef twenty-two years before, and that he made it as he sang it. It had a great many verses before the night was out, and by the end of the summer every harbour of the south coast had its own. Some were about the keeper, and some about the chimney of the works, and some were not fit to be sung in front of custodians. The verse that stayed, and is sung wherever the song is sung, is the first one, and it was the only one the skipper sang twice.

Ciorstaidh nic Artair heard it from her window. She had the lantern on the sill beside her, cold now, with its little door shut. Out beyond the harbour, far down the coast to the south-east over the dark of the marshes, where the cape stood out into the sea, a light had come up at sunset on the stroke, as she had ordered. It was small at that distance, and white, and very steady, and it did not flicker when the wind came off the sea. It had no colour at its edge.

The engineer, who had come up to say good night and had stayed at the door, said that the lad had lit it on time.

"He has," said the keeper. "The boats will see that at thirty miles."

"They will."

"They'll not know it from a star." She watched it a while. "They'll get used to it. You said I would."

Below the window, in the tavern under the quay wall, the skipper of Tobar dhearg sang his first verse again, and the whole room sang it with him, and it came up to her through the warm night, over the white lamps on the stones:

> *Dh'fhalbh an lasair às an tùr,*
> *tha solas ann, ach chan ann leinn.*
> The flame has gone out of the tower;
> there is a light there, but it is not ours.
