# Of the Engines Laid Up and the Pit Quarter Emptied

*DE 10 – 11*

## I. Of the Long Siding

By the tenth year of Linn an Dubhain there were nine coal engines at the yard of Muileann chrom, and in the space of one autumn all nine went into the long siding by the river and none of them came out again.

It was not done at a stroke. Through the summer the Board had run them where it could, on the shorter hauls up to An Sloc Mòr and back, while the single dubhan engine took the long run to Cathair dhearg twice in the week and grew, in the yard's mouth, from a thing to be pointed at into a thing to be reckoned with. A coal engine wanted a fireman as well as a driver, coal by the ton got up from the pit and carted to the yard and shovelled twice over before it burned, and water besides, taken on at every second stop; the dubhan engine wanted a man to open a valve. By harvest the Board's clerk had stopped writing coal orders for the shorter hauls at all, because there was no longer coal enough coming up from An Sloc Mòr to be worth ordering by the ton, the pit itself running three days in the week where it had run six.

Eachann mac Ruairidh drove the dubhan engine those months, as he had all that year, and watched the coal engines come in from their last runs one behind another through an t-Sultain and an Dàmhair, until by the feast of Samhain there were nine of them standing cold in the long siding with their fires drawn and their tenders empty, in a row under the rain, and no order came to light them again.

The Board did not say the word laid up. The notice on the shed door, when it came at the end of an Dàmhair, said that the coal engines of the yard were stood down pending the Board's further order, and that the men who had driven and fired them would be entered for instruction on the new engines, at the driver's rate while they learned, as Eachann had been the spring before. It did not say what would be paid to a man who did not learn, nor what was to be done with the engines standing in the siding, and the rows read both silences the same way.

Some of the older men put their names down. More did not. Two firemen younger than Eachann, brothers named Somhairle and Uilleam Frisealach, came to him at the shed door the week the notice went up and asked him plainly what the new engine was like to drive, since he was the only man at Muileann chrom who could tell them from having done it a year, and he told them as well as he could: that there was less to it and more to it both, less strength wanted and more watching, that a man came off shift cleaner than he had ever come off a coal engine and slept worse the first month for want of knowing why. They put their names down together. Their father, who had fired for thirty years and was near the end of driving, did not, and said nothing to either of them about it, which the rows understood, having heard the same silence in Eachann's own house the year before.

The instruction, when it came, fell to Eachann, since the Board had no one else at Muileann chrom who had driven the dubhan engine a full year and knew its temper. He took the Frisealach brothers out on it through the slack hours of a fortnight in an Dàmhair, and found them apt enough, being young and used to trusting a gauge over a feeling; the harder teaching was with the older men, four of them who had put their names down against their own better judgement because a driver's wage was a driver's wage whatever pulled it, and who came to the footplate each with thirty years of a coal engine's habits already set hard in their hands.

One of these was a man named Dòmhnall Ruadh, who had fired and then driven on Slighe a' Ghuail since before Eachann was born, and who stood at the new engine's controls the first morning turning the valve this way and that with the same slow, testing pressure he had always used on a regulator, waiting, Eachann saw, for some resistance in it that would tell him how hard he might press; and there was none, the valve turning as easily open as shut, giving him nothing back to read. He drove it badly that week and worse the next; his hands carried a kind of grief they would not put down, whatever care he gave the work, and on the ninth day he came off shift, hung up his coat in the shed for the last time, and told the clerk to strike his name from the instruction list.

"I can drive anything with a fire in it," he said to Eachann afterwards, not unkindly, the two of them standing by the cold rank of coal engines while the rain came down. "Show me a boiler and I'll tell you its mood before it tells me. This thing has no mood. It only has a setting, and a man who reads moods all his life makes a poor hand at reading settings." He looked along the siding at the engines he would never drive again. "I'll go to the sidings, or the stores, or wherever they'll have a fifty-year-old man who knows every mile of this line and nothing of the thing that's replacing it. It's more than some are given."

Ruairidh mac Eachainn, Eachann's father, had by then given up his chair outside the middle row's door for the worst of the cold weather and sat instead just inside it, where he could still hear the yard through the open half of the door but keep his knees out of the wind. He heard the coal engines come in one by one through the autumn, and he heard, because the wind off the river carried it into the rows most evenings, when the last one came in and did not go out again. He did not go down to look at the siding. Eachann offered, more than once, to walk him down of an evening, and each time his father said he had seen a coal engine and did not need to see nine of them standing idle to know what idle looked like.

"They'll scrap them," he said one night in am Faoilleach, when the fire was low and the girls were asleep and Seonaid had gone through to the back room. "Or they'll leave them to rust in that siding until somebody with more sense than the Board sells the iron. One or the other."

"Maybe," Eachann said.

"I drove on that line before the kingdom had a timetable of its own," his father said. "Before your time. I fired for a man who had fired for a human, and he told me things about the old running I never saw the like of again — signals given by flag in fog, a fireman who could tell you the load of a train by the sound of the wheels on the points before it was in sight. All that's gone into the ground with men now, and it never went anywhere. It just stopped being wanted, the way this is stopping being wanted." He looked at the fire. "I don't grudge you the new engine, Eachann. That should be said plainly, since I may not say it again. What I grudge is the Board's silence. A thing that has carried men and coal and the kingdom's post for sixty years is owed better than a notice on a shed door."

Eachann did not answer that, because there was no answer that would not have sounded like an argument, and he did not want to argue with his father in am Faoilleach over a thing neither of them could mend. He banked the fire instead, and helped his father through to his bed, and went out to clean his boots in the cold of the yard, which he did every night whatever the day had been, the one habit of his that Seonaid still called vain.

In the spring the Board sent a clerk with a book to walk the long siding and write down the number and condition of each engine, and after that nobody at Muileann chrom saw the clerk again, or the book, or any order touching the nine engines at all. They stood there through that whole year and the next, and the grass came up between the sleepers under them, and children of the rows who had never fired a coal engine and never would climbed into the cabs to play at driving, until the paint wore off the footplates where their boots had stood. The oldest of the drivers who had put their names down for instruction and been turned away for age, a man named Ailean Frisealach, uncle to the two brothers, used to walk down to the siding of a Sunday and stand by the engines a while before he came home, and would not say to anyone what he did there, and nobody in the rows asked him.

## II. Of the Reek at An Taigh-seinnse Reòta

On the north road above Muileann fhada, a day's ride from the yard, stood An Taigh-seinnse Reòta, the coldest of the old coaching inns on that side of the country and, by its own landlord's telling, the last that still lit its rooms with oil and wick from end to end. Eachann had never seen the place himself; he heard of it from a guard who rode the carriers' coach on the north road and stopped there most weeks to change horses.

The landlord of the Reòta, a heavy, slow-spoken man named Murchadh Frisealach, no kin to the brothers at Muileann chrom though the name was common enough on that road, had written to the Board in am Faoilleach of that year to say that he would not put dubhan lamps in his rooms, because the reek of the stuff drove custom from an inn the way smoke drove bees from a hive, and he had seen it happen at three houses on the coast road and would not have it happen at his own. The letter was read out, so the guard said, at more than one fireside between Muileann fhada and the capital before it ever reached anyone at the Board with the standing to answer it, and for a season or two it was repeated as a kind of proof that a house could hold to the old ways if it had the will.

It did not hold. The lorries that ran past his door every day of the week brought him more custom in a month than the old coaching trade had brought him in a season, and every one of the drivers who stopped there asked, the first night, why the rooms stank of tallow and smoked worse than the yard they had just come from, and more than a few did not stop a second time. By the following winter Murchadh Frisealach had put dubhan lamps in the taproom and the two best rooms, telling anyone who asked that it was only for the drivers' comfort and that his own rooms upstairs kept the old lamps still; and within the year after that the old lamps were gone from the whole house but one, kept over the stable door out of what he called habit and everyone else called defeat. The guard told it as a joke against himself as much as against the landlord, since he had been one of those who complained of the reek the first night and had gone on stopping there anyway, for want of anywhere else to change horses on that road.

Eachann heard the tale from the guard one evening at the yard, over nothing more than the ordinary talk of men waiting on a delayed load, and thought little of it at the time beyond a moment's fellow feeling for a landlord who had written the Board a letter that changed nothing. It was only afterwards, in the autumn that followed, that it came back to him and stood for something larger than an innkeeper's stubbornness; but that is properly told with what happened at Muileann dhearg, and not before.

## III. Of the Last Face

Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh had been master of the Guild of Hewers for eleven years, and in all that time he had buried more of the trade than he had bound to it. He was a broad, grey man, not far off seventy, with a cough that had settled into his chest a dozen years before and never wholly left it, and hands that still knew a seam of coal by feel through a glove better than most young hewers knew it by lamplight. He had cut coal himself at Muileann dhearg as a boy, before the guild had thought to make him a master of anything, and it was to Muileann dhearg that the guild's last close business called him in an Dàmhair of that year.

The pit at Muileann dhearg had worked one face only for three years past, the rest of its galleries stood and flooding slowly from below, and the one face left had been thin and poor coal for most of that time, cut more because a town needs its pit to still be a pit than because the coal paid for the cutting. The Board's surveyor came up from Cathair mhòr in the first days of an Dàmhair, walked the face with the pit captain and two of the older hewers, and wrote in his book, which Tormod was shown afterwards and never forgave himself for reading, that the seam was spent below the value of working it, and that the pit should close.

It closed on {{date:VI-0032}}, on a morning of thin rain with the wind off the moor above the town. Tormod came up from Seann Toll the day before, on a carrier's cart with his own coat over his knees against the wet, because it seemed to him, though no rule of the guild required it, that the master ought to be there for the last shift of a pit that had made hewers of three generations of his own family before him, and he was not a man to reason himself out of a thing that seemed to him owed.

He stood at the pit-head from before light with the pit captain, a heavy, silent man named Gilleasbaig Ruadh, and watched the cage come up for the last time a little after the ninth hour, twelve men in it, black to the eyes, none of them speaking. They had known, going down that morning, that they would not go down again; the last coal had been cut two days before and the shift had spent its hours drawing the props and the rails that could still be sold for scrap, which is bitter work for a hewer, being the taking apart of what better men had built rather than the getting of anything new. The twelfth man out, a boy not yet twenty named Aonghas Dubh, carried up the last lamp used on the face, and stood at the cage door with it in his hand and looked about him for someone to hand it to, the way a lamp was always handed on at the end of a shift to whoever waited to go down next; and there was no one. The next shift had not been called, because there would be no next shift, and the boy stood there with the lamp until Gilleasbaig Ruadh, without a word, took it from him and hung it on the rack himself, in its place, where it stayed unlit.

Tormod watched that and said nothing for a long while, though of everything he saw that winter it was the thing he spoke of oftenest, to Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh's clerk among others, when the guild's own end came to be written down. He had seen pits close before, in his years as master, and had always thought the worst of it was the wages stopped and the families scattered; he had not before stood at a pit-head and seen a lamp with nowhere to go.

"There'll be work," Gilleasbaig Ruadh said, when the last man was up and the cage stood empty and swinging a little in the shaft-draught. "The Board says it. The river landing wants hands for the tanks, and the boats at Cill ghlas are hiring, some of them."

"Some of them," Tormod said.

"It's not nothing."

"No," Tormod said. "It is not nothing. I did not say it was nothing." He looked back at the pit-head, at the winding gear standing idle over the black mouth of the shaft, at the rain running off the roof of the engine-house where no engine now turned. "I have watched three pits close in my time as master, Gilleasbaig, and I have made the same speech at each of them, and each time it has been a smaller speech than the last, because there were fewer men to hear it. There is no speech left in me for this one. I am only glad I came."

## IV. Of the Walk to Seann Toll

The Board's word was that the families who remained at Muileann dhearg, some forty households by then, the rest having gone south or to the works before them in the years the pit had been failing, would be found places at Seann Toll on the Abhainn uaine, some five miles off by the river road, where the Rail Board's tanks and the dubhan works along the landing were taking on hands as fast as the river towns could send them. No cart was sent for the families' goods, the Board's letter having spoken of places found and said nothing of a removal paid for, and it was Tormod, hearing of it at the pit-head that same wet morning, who went from door to door in the pit quarter through the rest of that day and the next to say that the guild, though it had next to nothing left to give, would find carts for what could not be carried on a back, and that he would walk the road with them himself.

They went on the third day of an t-Samhain, forty households in a straggling line along the river road, with what they could carry and what four hired carts could hold besides, the children walking or riding on the carts by turns, the old carried when they must be. It was five miles, but the road was wet the whole way and the carts slow in the mud, and it took them from first light until well past dark. Tormod walked the whole of it, though Gilleasbaig Ruadh offered him a seat on a cart more than once and was refused each time, on the grounds, which he gave once and would not repeat, that a master who could not walk the road his people were walking had no business calling himself their master.

Seann Toll took them in without much ceremony. The town had land enough on its edge, low ground by the river that flooded in the worst winters and had never been built on for that reason, and the Board, which owned the land, had begun a row of houses there against just such a removal. They were not finished; the families lay that winter in the Board's sheds by the landing and went into the row as each house was roofed, the last of them in the high summer after. They were plain slate-roofed houses in a single street with a well at either end, no different in their building from the rows at Muileann chrom save that the walls were thinner and the chimneys smoked worse in a south wind. The town's own people, who called every new thing by the plainest name that came to hand, called it the Hewers' Row before the last house was roofed, and the name held, and within the year it was the only name anyone used for it, in the Board's own letters as much as in the town's mouth.

Few of the families found work on the river, as Gilleasbaig Ruadh had hoped. The boatmen of Seann Toll were their own close trade, river-bred for generations, and took on outsiders slowly if at all; two or three of the younger men were hired as deck hands over that winter and no more. Most went instead to the dubhan tanks at the landing, where the works wanted hands to roll barrels and mind the pumps and needed no river craft to do it, only a strong back and a willingness to learn a trade that had not existed when they were born. It was, as more than one of them said afterwards, not so different from the pit in the way it used a man, though the coal it moved was not coal and did not come up out of the ground under their own town.

Tormod did not stay at Seann Toll past the first fortnight, his guild's business calling him elsewhere and his chest not standing the damp of the river well, but he came back twice more that winter to see how the row did, and it was on the second of those visits, walking the street with Gilleasbaig Ruadh in a hard frost, that he said the thing Eachann mac Ruairidh, hearing it that same winter from the coach guard, turned over and came at last to understand.

"I keep thinking of the lamp," Tormod said. "The boy with nowhere to hand it. I have turned it over all winter and I think now it was not the pit closing that undid me at the pit-head. Pits close; I have seen it before and lived through the seeing. It was that there was no one coming down to take the lamp from him. A trade can lose its work and still be a trade, so long as the work is only gone for a season, or gone from one place and waiting in another. What I saw that morning was a trade with nowhere left for the work to go at all. The landlord on the north road who will not light his rooms with the new stuff — I heard of him this summer, and I thought him a fool, holding to an old thing out of pride. I think differently of him now. There is no trade left in his hands to hold together. He keeps one lamp lit that he need not, because somewhere in him he cannot bear to be the last man in the house who does not."

Gilleasbaig Ruadh had no answer to that, and did not try to make one, and the two men walked on down the frozen street of the Hewers' Row in silence, past the well and the first roofed houses, where forty households were to live that had not lived in that place a year before, until they came to the landing where the dubhan tanks stood in a row on their stone piers, and the river ran black and cold beneath them, going down, as it had always gone down, to the sea.

## V. Of the Druid House Left Standing

Above the emptied pit quarter of Muileann dhearg, on the rise where the winding gear could still be seen from the moor road, stood the druid house that had served the pit families since the town was young, a low building of the same grey stone as the pit-head, with its own small burial ground behind it running back toward the moor. When the last households went down the river road to Seann Toll, the house went neither with them nor into the Board's keeping, but to a caretaker, an old man named Pàdraig Iarna who had swept its floor and rung its bell for thirty years and had no family left in any of the towns the pit had scattered its people to.

The druid school at Caol shean, which held the charge of such houses as had lost their congregations, wrote to say that a teacher would come once a year to give what lecture the empty quarter still had ears for, and did come, in the autumn after, a young man who found four old people and Pàdraig Iarna himself waiting in the cold hall and spoke to them for the better part of an hour on a text none of them had chosen and none of them afterwards could say they remembered. He did not come oftener than once a year after that, there being little in the empty quarter to draw him back sooner, and the house stood the rest of each year with its door locked and its bell silent, kept only by the old man's daily round, the graves behind it tended as they had always been tended, the town below it going about its business at Seann Toll with no more thought for the hill than the hill, in its long patience, seemed to ask of it.

Eachann mac Ruairidh never went to Muileann dhearg himself, that year or any year after, his own road running the other way along the coal line to the capital and back; but he carried the tale of it, as the yard carried every such tale sooner or later, in the guard's telling and in Tormod's own words repeated at secondhand, and he thought of it, when he thought of it at all, alongside the nine cold engines standing in the grass of the long siding at Muileann chrom, each of them waiting, as the lamp at Muileann dhearg had waited on its rack, for a hand that was not coming to take them up again.
