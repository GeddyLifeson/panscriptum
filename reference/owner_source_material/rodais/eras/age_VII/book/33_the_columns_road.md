# Of the Column's Road

*DE 22*

Nobody at Cathair dhearg saw it start. That is the first true thing to say of it, and the last, since everything between is town report and hill telling, and the two do not always agree even on the weather. What An Clàr Foscailte gives is this: that in the last week of an Dàmhair a company of some four hundred came down out of the western hills where the holdouts had sat quartered a year and more under the regiments' watch, and went south-east across the middle of the island by roads that had carried nothing heavier than a drover's cart in living memory, and that six weeks later it stood on the fields by Muileann ghlas, a day's walk from the capital, with two regiments come down Rathad na Fèille to meet it. Why it went is not in An Clàr Foscailte, nor in any town's report, nor, if Sceulachdan nan Tuathach are to be believed on this one point, in the column's own knowledge past the man who led it, and he left no account behind him that anyone has found. The tellings give his name as Owen Barrow. An Clàr Foscailte gives no name at all, only "a column of the western hills," as though the hand that wrote it had decided, or been told, that a name was one thing too many to set down.

## I. Of the Ford at Àth bheag

Àth bheag stands where the old drove road crosses the upper water on its way down from the western hills to the plain, a burg of not much above two hundred souls with a mill, a smithy and a bridge too narrow for two carts abreast, and it had seen nothing of the war since the second summer's musters except the soldiers who came through now and again on the road to the Seann Skell garrison and never stayed the night. Its report to the Board, dated the thirtieth of an Dàmhair and copied afterward into an Leabharlann's file on the column, says that a company of armed men, not fewer than three hundred and by the reckoning of two who counted them from the mill loft nearer four, crossed the ford below the bridge in the grey of a Tuesday morning, in good order and without haste, and that they did not use the bridge, which would have brought them through the heart of the town, but forded the water a field's length below it, wet to the knee, and went on south without stopping.

The maor of Àth bheag, a man named Cailean mac Griogair who had held the office nine years and buried, in that time, two wives and one son to the winters the western hills give a small town on short rations, wrote his report the same evening by his own hand, and it is the fullest of the whole road because he wrote it while the thing was still new to him and had not yet learned, as later towns learned, how little a report of this kind was wanted to say. He had been woken before light by the smith's boy hammering at his door, and had come down to the bank himself in his nightshirt and a borrowed coat, and stood a good while at the field's edge watching the last of the column cross before he judged there was anything in it worth the walk back up to his own desk to set down. He set down the hour, the number as near as two men's counting could give it, the road they took and the road they did not, the state of the water (in spate from three days' rain, and cold enough that he wondered, watching them wade it, that no man of them cried out), and one thing more that the Board's copyist struck through in the file copy and that survives only because Cailean kept his own draft: that the miller's wife, watching from the loft with the two who counted, said afterward that she had seen no banner among them and no drum, only a man walking at the head of the column with nothing about him to mark him out from the rest except that the others left him the whole width of the road to himself, and that this, more than the guns some of them carried, was what had made her certain the company kept some errand of its own, and no design on the mill's stores.

Cailean added, in his own draft and nowhere else, that he had thought of going down to the ford himself and asking the man at the head of it plainly where he was bound, and had not done it, and could not afterward say why not, except that the width of road the column had left the man made him think a question put across it would not be answered, and a maor of Àth bheag who had spent nine years keeping the peace between drovers and millers without once having to draw a sword saw no profit in learning that for certain at a river crossing before breakfast.

Cailean's report went by carrier the next morning to the Board's clerk at the western Seann Skell, and from there, in the slower way such things travel once they are no longer news to the man carrying them, to Leabharlann Muileann chaol, where Sìleas nic Rath entered it in the file she had begun to keep of the column's road the week the first report of it reached her desk. She had asked, by then, for every town's account of it that could be got, and had been given leave to keep them, which was more than she had been given for the war four years before; the difference, she wrote once in a private note that never went into any file, was that a column marching in the open through country at peace was a thing a chronicler might be let see, where a war fought in the hills was not, and she did not know, setting that down, whether it comforted her or not.

Àth bheag's report ends with a line that the Board's own men, reading it afterward, took for a maor's caution and that Sìleas took, copying it in, for a plainer thing: that the column had asked nothing of the town, taken nothing from it, and left nothing behind but the wet marks of its crossing on the near bank, which the next rain washed out within the week.

## II. Of the Bread at Muileann mhin

A few days on, and a few miles by the road the column seemed to be keeping, though no report survives to say exactly where it slept those nights, Muileann mhin had its own turn: not the lamp-makers' town of that name east of the capital, but the small mill town of the same name under the western hills, a place of some two hundred souls. Its report to the Board, signed by its miller rather than its maor, who was from home that week at a wedding in Cathair mhòr, has a different temper from Àth bheag's, less the note of a man watching a company pass and more the note of a man doing sums.

Two men came into the town on the morning of the second of an t-Samhain, the report says, ahead of the column by some hours, and went to the baker nearest the bridge, a widow named Mòrag nic Fhearghais who had kept the ovens since her husband's death. The two men asked for bread, as much as the ovens held and as much again as could be baked fresh by noon, and asked the price, and paid it in full in the coin of the realm, counted out on the counter in front of three other customers who had come in for their own morning loaves and stood back, the report says, "not from fear so much as from a wish to see the thing done properly, as men will stand back from a fair bargain struck in the street." Mòrag, by the miller's account, asked the older of the two whether he meant to feed a garrison or a wedding, meaning it, she told him afterward, half as a joke to ease her own daughters' nerves at the size of the order, and he answered only that he meant to feed men who had walked a long way and would walk further, which was no answer at all and satisfied her, she said, better than one would have.

Mòrag baked through the morning with two of her own daughters to help her, and the column, when it came through a little after noon, sent no more than a dozen men into the town to carry the loaves away, the rest passing on the road below without turning aside, and the dozen who came for the bread paid again, a small sum over the first price, which Mòrag's own account, kept separately from the miller's and sent to the Board a week later at his asking, gives to the last farthing.

The miller's report does not say what the two men looked like, beyond that one was young and the other not, nor whether either was the man the tellings name; it says only that they spoke the humans' tongue between themselves and Dia-thìris to the widow, civilly, and that neither gave a name when Mòrag, by her own account, asked for one to set against the sale in her book. She wrote instead, where a name should have stood, "bread, to the western company, paid in full," and that is the line the Board's file preserves, copied twice more before it reached an Leabharlann, the wording unchanged each time, as though no clerk who handled it had found anything in it worth altering.

The miller's own closing line, which Sìleas kept without comment in her file, notes that the town's takings for that week showed no loss against the ordinary, the bread having been paid for at a fair price and the company having troubled nobody's stock beyond it, and that he set this down not to speak for the column, which was no business of his, but so that the Board should not suppose, from the mere fact of an armed company passing through a working town, that the town had suffered by it. Whether the Board read that line as the warden meant it, or otherwise, the file does not say; but a second hand, in a different ink, has added below the miller's signature a single further note, unsigned, that the loaves were good loaves and the widow well paid for them, which is either a clerk's private aside or, as Sìleas herself once wondered, a line the miller thought better to add without his own name against it.

## III. Of the Barn on the Capital's Edge

The country between Muileann mhin and the capital's own fields is the quietest stretch of the whole road, farms and hedgerows and the low rise of ground that a man from the hills would hardly call a hill at all, and it gave, in the fortnight the column crossed it, only one report of any substance, from a farmer named Aonghas mac Cuinn whose steading lay a mile off the road proper, close enough that a company passing would have seen the barn's roof plain against the last light, and far enough that Aonghas, by his own account, thought at first they would go by without troubling him.

They did not. A party of perhaps thirty came to his gate an hour after dark on a night of driving rain, in the second week of an t-Samhain, and asked, through the same young man who had asked at Muileann mhin, or one enough like him that the two reports, set side by side in an Leabharlann's file, read as though they might be the same man, whether the barn stood empty and whether they might have the use of it for one night against the weather. Aonghas, who had a wife and four children under his own roof and no love, he wrote afterward, for having armed strangers so near either, stood in his own doorway with a lamp held up between himself and the gate long enough to count the shapes waiting beyond it, and told them the barn was theirs for the night and nothing more, and asked no payment, thinking a company of that size would take what it wanted whether he named a price or not. His wife, listening from the passage behind him, asked once he had barred the door again whether he had done right, and he told her, by his own account, that he did not know, only that thirty armed men asking leave at a gate were thirty armed men who had not simply taken it, and that this seemed to him, that night, worth more than an answer to whether he had done right.

In the morning he found, weighted under a stone on the barn's threshing floor where the party had slept, a sum in coin that by his own reckoning came to more than a fair week's rent of the barn in any ordinary season, and a note, in a clear hand and in Dia-thìris, that read only: *For the barn, and thanks. We have asked nothing else and will leave nothing else asked of you.* The barn itself they had left swept cleaner, his wife said, than his own boy generally left it of a Saturday, the straw restacked and the door barred from the inside as they had found it, so that but for the coin and the note a stranger coming after might have thought no one had lain there at all.

Aonghas kept the note. It went, eventually, by way of the Board's local clerk, into an Leabharlann's file with the rest, and Sìleas, reading it the first time, found it the strangest paper the whole road had given her, stranger than the widow's uncompleted line at Muileann mhin, because it was the only piece of writing anywhere in the file that could be set down, without much doubt, to a hand inside the column, and it said, in the end, almost nothing: no reason, no name, no word of where the company was bound or why, only the plain courtesy of a debt paid and a boundary kept. She copied it into her file exactly as it stood, spelling and all, and noted beside it only the date and the finder's name, and did not, in all the years she kept the office after, ever find another line to set beside it.

The column crossed the last of the open country in the third week of an t-Samhain, keeping, so far as the scattered reports of small farms and a single toll-keeper at the edge of the fair country can be made to agree, a road that ran wide of every town of any size, as though whoever led it had studied the ground beforehand and meant to be seen only where seeing could not be helped. No report from that stretch tells of trouble, of begging, of theft, or of any word exchanged beyond what the day's business required; the column asked lodging where lodging was offered, paid what was asked and sometimes more, and went on, and the towns it passed, having nothing worse to report than a night's noise and a morning's quiet going, mostly did not trouble to report it at all until the Board, hearing by then from Àth bheag and Muileann mhin both, sent word down the whole line of the western road asking every maor to say what he had seen.

## IV. Of the Booths Taken Down

Ùna nic Ailein had kept the books of Cùirt na Fèille at Muileann òg for the better part of thirty years by the time the first word of the column reached the toll-house, and in all that time, through Riaghailt na Cairte and the granary votes and the long quiet stretch of the war years when nothing crossed Rathad na Fèille but the ordinary carts, she had never once been asked to close the year's books before the last market of an Dùbhlachd, and she found, when the asking came, that she did not much like being the one to do it.

It came from A' chiad rèisimeid's quartermaster, a brisk youngish man she had dealt with twice before over billeting matters and found, both times, civil enough for a soldier, who came up the toll-house stair on the seventh of an Dùbhlachd with an order under the council's own seal requiring the fair-ground at Muileann òg, its booths, its standings and its cleared ground, for the use of the first and third regiments from that day until further notice. Ùna read the order twice, the second time aloud, half to herself and half, she supposed afterward, to give the quartermaster the chance to say it was some mistake, and he did not take it, only stood at the foot of her desk with his cap under his arm and said that he was sorry for the short notice and that the order was not his to argue with any more than it was hers.

"Two regiments," she said. "On my fair-ground."

"On the whole ground between here and Muileann ghlas, if it comes to that," he said, "though I'll own the fair-ground's the flattest and driest piece of it, which is why it's yours the order names first." He had the grace, she thought, to look at his own boots saying it, as though the flatness and dryness of another town's market ground were not a compliment he much enjoyed paying.

"The market's set for the ninth," she said. "There's stalls booked from three towns."

"Then they'll book them somewhere else this year," he said, not unkindly, "or not at all. I've a thousand men and more coming down this road inside the week, and nowhere else in twenty miles flat enough to camp them and near enough water."

She went down herself on the ninth, the day the market should have stood, to see the booths taken down, because it was, she told the justice when he asked why she had not sent a clerk, the kind of thing the court's own book ought to have set down by someone who had seen it rather than been told of it, and she stood at the fair-ground's edge in a wind that had turned from the west that morning and watched men she had known for years, stall-holders and their sons, working under the quartermaster's own soldiers to strike the timber frames that had stood on that ground, some of them, since before her own time at the desk. Fionnlagh Dubh, who had sold ironmongery from the same standing for thirty years and whose father had sold it there before him, took his own frame down with more care than the soldiers gave theirs, numbering each pole with chalk before he stacked it, and told Ùna, passing her with an armful of them, that he supposed he would want the numbers again come spring, though neither of them, standing there, much believed it. Behind him the eel-woman was packing her tub onto a handcart with two of the soldiers lifting the heavier end for her, not unkindly, and further off a boy who sold ribbons and thread from a tray sat on an upturned crate with the tray still on his knees, watching the frames come down around him as though he had not yet decided whether there was any longer a market for a boy to sell ribbons in.

By dusk the ground stood cleared from the toll-house arch to the river bend, a long bare stretch of trampled grass and mud where a fair had stood every market day for longer than the court's own books went back, and the first companies of An treas rèisimeid came onto it before full dark, pitching their tents in rows where the ironmonger's stall and the eel-woman's tub and the cheese-sellers' shaded row had stood a day before, their fires lit against a cold that Ùna, watching from the toll-house window, thought looked, in the last of the light, very much like any market night the town had ever known, and nothing at all like one.

A' chiad rèisimeid came down the same evening, in better order and, Ùna thought, watching them from the same window, in greater numbers than she had believed a thousand men could be, filling the fair-ground and the water-meadow beyond it and spilling into the lanes above the mill, so that for those nights Muileann òg held more soldiers under canvas than it had held its own people the whole year round. The town's two inns filled with officers and the mill's own loft was given over to stores; a picket stood at the toll-house arch by the second night, civilly enough, and asked her name each morning going up to her own desk as though she had not kept that desk longer than the sentry had been alive, and she gave it each morning without complaint, judging it no great cost against the larger thing the whole camp was asking of the town without asking at all.

She kept Cùirt na Fèille open through it, as the justice bade her, hearing what small business came, a dispute over a strayed cow, a complaint against a soldier who had taken firewood without asking that the regiment's own officer settled before she need write a word of it down, and she watched, from her desk under the window, the road that ran south from the fair-ground toward Muileann ghlas fill and empty and fill again with companies going down to the fields there, where word had it that the column lay, camped in the open on the far side of the river from the town of that name, a day's walk from the capital's own gate and no farther, and showing, by every account that reached the toll-house, no sign of moving on.

She did not go to see it. Enough, she told the justice, watching the fair-ground go, and it was true enough as far as it went, though the truer reason, which she kept to herself, was that she did not want the picture in her own head to stand beside the fair-ground's own emptiness when she came to write the year's closing entry; and by the second week of an Dùbhlachd she knew there would be a closing entry to write, and that it would not read like any before it.

She wrote it on the last evening of the month the market should have run, sitting at the same desk where she had written the last cartload's sale nineteen years before, in the same clear hand, though slower now and with more pauses than that earlier page had needed. She set down the year's takings to the date the order came, which were ordinary enough, and the names of the three towns that had booked stalls and not had them, and the order, copied whole under the council's seal, and then she found, dipping her pen for the line that every year's book before this one had carried, the plain sum of the market's takings struck and totalled and the court's seal set under it, that she did not know what figure to write, because there had been no market, and a book cannot close on a sum that was never taken.

The pen stayed dry above the page some while. Outside the window the fair-ground's fires burned in their long soldiers' rows where the stalls had stood, and somewhere beyond them, past the river and the low dark shoulder of the land, other fires burned on the fields by Muileann ghlas that she had not gone to see and did not mean to, and the road between the two, Rathad na Fèille her own court's charter took its very name from, lay empty in the last of the light, waiting, though for what, and for how long, and with what end, was no more hers to know, sitting at her own desk with her own dry pen, than it was the miller's wife of Àth bheag's to have known, six weeks and near sixty miles before, watching a column with no banner and no drum go down into the water and up the far bank and on, south, without once turning to look back at the town it left behind it.

In the end she wrote nothing under the year's takings. She ruled the line the court's own form required, and left the space above it blank, and set the year's date under it, and closed the book, and it stands so in the court's own press at Muileann òg to this day: of all the years she has kept the desk, the only one with no closing entry.
