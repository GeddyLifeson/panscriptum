# Of the Boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich

*DE 5 – 6*

## I. Of the Engine in the Rope-Shed

In the summer that the trial lamp on the eastern cape ran out its hundred nights, some ninety miles to the west, at the other end of the south coast, a boatwright of Caol fhiadhaich bought an engine for the price of old iron. The boatwrights of the south-west tell it that way, as a bargain first and a quarrel after, and they begin it with the town.

Caol fhiadhaich stands at the head of its strait, a mile of broken water between the harbour wall and the skerries, where the tide runs one way under a wind blowing the other and the sea stands up in ridges. Its pilots had earned their bread there since An Aois Ailein, taking strangers through the rocks for a fee and an offering at the wolf-stone by the harbour mouth, as the houses of Òrd na Cloiche taught. Its own fishers had been the first to sail round into the southern sea, and the lane they opened still bore the town's name, Slighe-mhara Caol fhiadhaich. That was long ago. In the humans' time the fleet could not pay the harbour dues in marks and sold half its boats to A' Chompanaidh, and the men who had sailed them went into the pits. The harbour wall was built afterwards, out of the town's share of the western tolls, and was better than the fleet it sheltered. By the summer of this tale the boats of Caol fhiadhaich were some thirty open hulls and a dozen decked ones, under sail and oar, and when the wind came into the south-west and stayed there they lay along the wall for a week at a time and the town ate its own salt fish.

Sorcha nic Dhùghaill kept the yard at the west end of the harbour, under the rope-walk. She had been bound to the guild at sixteen, at Ros dhomhain, and had served her seven years on the slips there before she came home to take the yard from her mother's brother, who had built boats in it for forty years and died with an adze in his hand, or so he had always promised he would. She was forty-one in that summer, broad in the shoulder, grey early, and known along the coast for two things: her hulls did not leak, and she did not lower her price for anyone, not for the pilots and not for the priests of the Stone.

In an t-Ògmhios she went up to Ros dhomhain to buy copper nails of the guild's stamp and to see a timber merchant about larch. It was a long day on the mail-cart, which changed horses twice on the road, and a long day back. In a chandler's yard behind the coal quay, among anchors and stove-in water-casks, she found an engine.

It had come out of a harbour launch, one of the little craft that had once towed the coal ships off the quay when the wind was foul. The launch had been laid up eight years, since the price of coal passed what a launch could earn in a day, and it had been broken up for its planking. The engine sat on two baulks of timber in the nettles with a sheet of tarred canvas over it, and the chandler wanted it gone. Sorcha put her hand on the flywheel and turned it. It moved, stiff with old grease, and something inside answered with a slow clank.

"What do you want for it?" she said.

"What would you do with it?" said the chandler. "It eats coal. You couldn't feed it."

"I didn't ask what it eats."

He named a price for old iron. She paid it out of the nail money, and went home without the nails.

It came down the coast road a week later on a wain drawn by four oxen, and took two days to do what the carrier did in one. The children of the harbour walked beside it the last mile. It was set down in the rope-shed at the back of the yard, where it was dry, and there it stood for a month while Sorcha went round it with a lamp and a notebook and took it apart as far as she dared.

She knew nothing of engines. Neither did anyone in Caol fhiadhaich. What she knew was that the south-coast boats had been burning dubhan all that year in their stoves and their lamps, since the works at Seann Skell sold its first making to them, and that the stuff came west along the coast in barrels on the coasters and could be bought on the quay. It burned, it stank, and it was cheap. An engine was a fire and a wheel, she reasoned, and the wheel did not care what the fire was made of.

The smith of the harbour, Seonaidh Bàn, a man who spoke some dozen words a day and made each of them count, came and looked at the firebox and the grate. He went away, and came back two days later with a burner of his own making: a trough of iron with a row of holes along it and a pipe and a cock to feed it from a tank. He had copied it, he said, from the stove of a coaster out of Seann Skell on the southern coast that lay in the harbour that week, and made it bigger.

"Will it serve?" said Sorcha.

"It'll burn," said Seonaidh Bàn. That was all she got from him that day.

## II. Of the Unbored Keel

The engine was the easy part. The hard part was the boat.

Lachann mac Neacail came to her about it before she went looking for him. He had a decked boat of thirty feet, *An Ròn Dubh*, built in that same yard by her uncle twenty years before, and she was a good boat, heavy in the bilge and dry, and slow. In any foul wind she was the last boat home, and Lachann was tired of it. He was a lean man with a face burnt the colour of a kipper, and he had lost a brother to the strait. He came into the rope-shed one evening, looked at the engine on its baulks for a long while, and said: "If you put that in a boat, put it in mine."

"She'd have to be opened," said Sorcha. "Aft. Through the post."

"Then open her."

Every apprentice of the guild learned the rule in his first year, before he was let near an adze, and learned it in the old tongue as the first wrights of the fellowship had said it: *Cha tollar an druim*, the keel is not bored. A boat's backbone was laid whole and kept whole. You fastened to it, you scarfed it, you might shape it; you did not put a hole through it below the water. The rule was older than the guild's charter, older than the humans' coming, and it had kept a great many boats afloat. Sorcha had had it beaten into her on the slips at Ros dhomhain at sixteen, and she believed in it.

To put a shaft out through the stern of *An Ròn Dubh*, to turn a screw behind her, the post had to be bored and the deadwood under it, a long hole slanting down and aft, and a tube of copper set in it packed with oakum and tallow so the sea would not come in. There was no other road for the shaft to go.

She sat up two nights with the lines of the boat laid out on the loft floor in chalk, working out the angle. On the third morning she had *An Ròn Dubh* hauled up the slip and chocked, and sent for the long auger.

The other yards came to watch. There were three besides hers in Caol fhiadhaich, and all of them were members of the guild, as she was: old Donnchadh Ruadh, who had been bound in the same year as the guild's present master and never let anyone forget it; the two brothers of the yard below the church of the Stone; and a young woman, Raonaid nic Coinnich, who had served her time only the year before. They stood at the head of the slip with their arms folded and said nothing while Sorcha set the auger and began to turn.

It took her the whole of a long day. The post was oak, and hard. Twice she stopped to sharpen the bit. Twice she crawled under the hull with a lamp and a plumb-line to see that she had not wandered. Towards evening the auger broke through into the light behind the post, and a curl of pale oak came out on the end of it and dropped on the shingle. Old Donnchadh picked it up and put it in his pocket. He did not say why.

The engine went into the boat a fortnight later, bedded on oak and bolted through her floors. The tank for the dubhan went forward of it, and the smith's burner under it. The shaft went out through the new hole in its copper tube, and the screw on the end of it was a two-bladed thing that Seonaidh Bàn had cast in bronze from a pattern Sorcha had carved in pine, and it was ugly, and it was true.

They tried her on an evening near the end of an Lùnastal, with no wind at all and the harbour flat as a plate. Half the town was on the wall. Lachann was at the helm. Sorcha was down in the well with the engine, and Seonaidh Bàn was beside her with the cock of the burner in his fist. He opened it. The dubhan caught with a soft thump and a smell like a tannery on fire, and a black smoke rolled up out of the hatch and over the wall, where the people on it coughed and swore and did not move back. They waited. It was a long wait. Then the engine gave a groan, and a clank, and another, and the flywheel turned, and turned faster, and the shaft began to turn in its tube.

*An Ròn Dubh* moved. She went out from the slip with her sails furled and her oars shipped, pushing a little wave in front of her, and she crossed the harbour in a straight line to the wolf-stone and turned, slowly, and came back. Nobody on the wall cheered. They watched her the way they would watch a dog walking on its hind legs.

At the wolf-stone the pilots were sitting on their bench as they did every evening, waiting for a ship that might want them. The eldest of them, Ailig Mòr, who had taken more hulls through the strait than any man living, watched *An Ròn Dubh* go by within an oar's length, stinking and clanking, with no sail on her. He took his pipe out of his mouth.

"That'll frighten the fish," he said.

"It frightened you," said Lachann from the helm, and the pilots laughed, and Ailig Mòr put his pipe back in his mouth and said nothing more that night.

## III. Of the Guild's Letter

Word went up the road to Ros dhomhain faster than the engine had come down it. A timber merchant told a chandler, the chandler told a warden of the guild, and the warden told the master.

The letter came on {{date:VI-0012}}, on the carrier's cart with the mail and a crate of hens. It was addressed to the members of Comann nan Saor-luinge at Caol fhiadhaich, all four yards together, and sealed with the guild's seal, the adze and the ship. Sorcha had it from the carrier's hand at the yard gate and carried it unopened to the net-loft over the fish-store, where the four yards met when they had anything to meet about, which was seldom. She waited until they were all there. Then she broke the seal and read it aloud.

It was not long. The master of the guild, Pàdraig mac an t-Saoir, wrote that he had heard with grief that a member of the guild at Caol fhiadhaich had bored the keel of a boat below the water to take an engine; that the rule of the unbored keel was the first rule of the fellowship and older than its charter; that the work was unlawful for any member of the guild; that it was to cease in every yard of the town; and that a member who went on with it would be struck from the roll, and fined, and his apprentices unbound. Below his name the wardens had set theirs, eleven of them. The letter used *his* throughout. There had not been a woman among the guild's masters since its charter was taken.

When she had finished there was a silence in the loft. The fish-store below them smelled of brine. Somebody's dog was barking on the wall.

"Well," said old Donnchadh Ruadh. "You bored it."

"I bored it."

"Does it leak?"

"No."

"Then he's wrong," said Donnchadh, "and he always was. He was wrong on the slip when we were boys." He took the curl of oak out of his pocket, the one he had picked up off the shingle, and turned it in his fingers. "But he's the master. What'll you do?"

"There are six skippers waiting on my slip," said Sorcha, "and there's another engine in a yard at Inis chrom that I can have for the carting. I'll do the work."

"And the guild?"

She folded the letter and put it down on the table.

It was Raonaid nic Coinnich, the youngest of them, who said it first, because she had the least to lose and the longest to lose it in. "If they strike one of us they'll strike all of us," she said. "There isn't a yard in this town that hasn't had a skipper through its gate this month asking for an engine. We'll all be boring keels by the spring, or we'll all be building for the dead."

The two brothers of the yard below the church looked at one another for a long time, as brothers do, and nodded together.

Donnchadh Ruadh put the curl of oak on the table beside the letter. "I was bound in the same year as Pàdraig mac an t-Saoir," he said. "Fifty-one years. I'd like to have got to the end of it." He stood up, slowly, as a man does whose knees have spent half a century on slipways. "Write it, then, Sorcha. You've the best hand."

She wrote it that night, on the loft table, with the lamp smoking. It said that the boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich had read the master's letter; that they held the work lawful and would go on with it; and that since the guild held it unlawful for its members, they were members no longer, and returned their papers with this letter. The four of them signed it. Sorcha went home and took down from the wall of her house the paper of her binding, which had hung there in a frame for twenty-five years with the guild's seal on it and her uncle's name as witness, and she took it out of the frame and rolled it up with the others. In the morning it all went up the road on the carrier's cart with the mail and a crate of hens going the other way.

A month later the guild's answer came. The four yards of Caol fhiadhaich were struck from the roll, and each was fined, and the fine laid on Sorcha's yard was the heaviest, being more than the yard would clear in a good season. None of them paid it. The guild had no bailiff in the south-west and no ship to send one in, and the maor of the shire, when the guild wrote to him, answered that a guild's fine was the guild's business.

## IV. Of the Wind in the South-West

The fishers of Caol fhiadhaich still tell of the autumn that followed, and they tell it as the season of *An Ròn Dubh*, though by its end there were two engine-boats in the harbour and a third on the slip.

Before she went out the first time, Lachann did what every skipper of that harbour did before a new season: he went down to the wolf-stone at dawn with a fish from the last catch and laid it at the foot of the stone, as Òrd na Cloiche had taught the harbour, and one of the pilots said the words over it. That morning it was Ailig Mòr. He looked at the fish, and then over his shoulder at the boat with her stack and her smell, and said the words all the same, and not any faster than usual.

Then he went down the steps and into *An Ròn Dubh*, and took her through the strait himself.

He had never been through it under power, nor had anyone. He had spent sixty years reading the strait by the set of a sail and the pull of an oar, and at first he steered her as if she were still a sailing boat, waiting for a wind that did not matter any more. At the second skerry the tide caught her on the bow and she swung. Lachann shouted to the engine and Seonaidh Bàn opened the burner, and the boat drove on across the tide as if it were a millpond, and Ailig Mòr said something in the old tongue of the pilots that nobody in the boat understood, and did not repeat. Beyond the skerries he handed the helm to Lachann and sat down on a fish-box and lit his pipe.

"I'll take you back in," he said. "Don't try it on your own."

The first weeks were hard. The burner fouled, and had to be pulled out and cleared with a wire, in a seaway, by a man lying on his belly in the stink. The copper tube in the stern wept a little at the packing, and Sorcha had *An Ròn Dubh* up the slip twice to repack it, and lay awake in between. The dubhan came west in barrels on the coasters from Seann Skell on the southern coast, and the coasters came when they pleased, and one week in an Dàmhair there was none to be had in the town at any price, and *An Ròn Dubh* lay at the wall with her sails bent on like any other boat and her engine cold, and the pilots made remarks. But the boat caught fish. She could go out when the others could not and come in when they could not, and she did not wait on the tide at the skerries, and the fish she brought in were the freshest on the quay.

The day the town remembers came late in an Dàmhair. The fleet had gone east to the banks off the south coast by the old lane, where the herring were thick that year, twenty boats and more, with *An Ròn Dubh* among them, and they had fished two nights and filled. On the third morning the wind backed into the south-west and rose, and by noon it was blowing a full gale up the coast, straight in the teeth of any boat that wanted to go home to Caol fhiadhaich.

The sailing boats did what they had always done. They ran for the lee of the nearest headland to the east, a good day's sail from home, and lay there at anchor with their catches salting in the hold, as their fathers had lain there before them, to wait out the wind. It might be two days. It might be a week. The fish would be salt fish by the time it was over, worth a third of fresh.

Lachann did not run for the lee. He put *An Ròn Dubh* head to the gale, with a scrap of sail on her to steady her, and opened the burner, and went home.

It took him from noon until an hour after dark, into a sea that broke green over the bow and ran aft along the deck and down the hatch onto the engine. Twice the burner guttered and twice his boy, a lad of fourteen, lay in the water in the well and kept it lit with his body over it. Once a sea took the scrap of sail clean out of its bolt-ropes. The engine groaned and clanked and hissed where the water hit the hot iron, and did not stop. At every sea *An Ròn Dubh* lifted her bow and seemed to stand still, and then the screw bit and she went on, a boat's length, and another, along a coast where every man aboard knew the rocks by name.

In Caol fhiadhaich they had seen the fleet go east three days before and had not looked for any of it back until the wind changed. Sorcha was in the rope-shed at dusk, cleaning the auger by lamplight, when a boy from the harbour came running and shouting that there was a light in the strait.

She went out onto the wall. It was nearly dark, and the rain was coming across the harbour in sheets, and the whole mouth of the strait was white. Out in it, low down, a light was moving. It came on steadily through the broken water between the skerries, into the wind, not tacking, not drifting, rising and falling with the seas and always coming on, and behind it, when the wind let her hear it, came the clank and grunt of the engine.

Ailig Mòr was on the wall beside her. He had come out without his pipe. He watched the light come in past the second skerry, where the tide ran hardest, and past the third, and he did not say a word until it had turned in under the wolf-stone and was in the smooth water of the harbour. Then he said, "He did it on his own, the fool," and went back to the pilots' bench out of the rain.

*An Ròn Dubh* came alongside the wall with her deck swept clean and her boy shaking with cold and a hold full of herring caught the night before. The catch was carried up to the market at Doire shean on the road that same night, and sold at dawn, fresh, the only fresh herring on the coast, at a price the fish-wives of Doire shean spoke of for years. The rest of the fleet came home five days after, when the wind went round, with salt fish.

That winter there were more skippers in Sorcha's yard than she had room for on her slip. Some came to talk and some came with money. The other three yards of Caol fhiadhaich were boring keels by an Dùbhlachd, and old Donnchadh Ruadh fitted his first engine with his own hands before the year was out, grumbling the whole while that he was too old for it. In am Faoilleach of the new year the yards of Inis chrom and Doire shean, a mile or two along the coast, sent to ask how it was done, and Sorcha showed them, and by an Gearran three yards of the south-west coast outside Caol fhiadhaich were doing the same work, and they too had left the guild, or been struck from it, depending on who told it.

The engines were the trouble. There was no one on the island making them for boats. Every laid-up launch in every harbour of the capital country had its engine bought out of it that winter, and the chandlers of Ros dhomhain, who had been glad to be paid for old iron in an t-Ògmhios, asked three times the price by am Màrt and five times by an Giblean. Seonaidh Bàn made burners all winter, a dozen of them, each a little better than the last, and said nothing about any of them.

## V. Of the Court at Ros dhomhain

The summons came early in an Giblean. The court of Comann nan Saor-luinge would sit at Ros dhomhain on the twenty-third of that month, and the boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich were called to it to hear its ruling on the matter of the engines. It said nothing more than that. The four yards talked it over in the net-loft and agreed that Sorcha should go for all of them, since it was her auger that had started it.

She went up on the carrier's cart the day before, through the green of the spring. The road ran north-east, inland from the coast, through the fields of the shire, where the sowing was done and the lambs were out, and over the low hills into the capital country, and at evening she came down into Ros dhomhain with its counting-houses and its warehouses and its slips, and smoke over all of it. She slept at the carriers' inn by the market. She did not sleep much.

The guild hall stood above the slips, where it had stood since the wrights of Ros dhomhain took their charter in the first years of the kingdom: a long hall of stone with a roof of slate, and inside it a floor of oak worn into hollows by the feet of the apprentices bound there. Over the master's bench hung a board with the rules of the fellowship painted on it in the old tongue, and the first of them, in letters as long as a finger, was *Cha tollar an druim*. Sorcha had stood under that board at sixteen with her hand on the guild's adze and sworn to keep it.

The court sat at the fourth hour. The master was in his chair, with the eleven wardens on the bench on either side of him. Pàdraig mac an t-Saoir was a big white-haired man with the hands of a wright and the voice of a man who has spent his last twenty years talking to merchants. He did not look at Sorcha when she came in. The hall was fuller than she had expected: the masters of the Ros dhomhain yards in their good coats, a clerk of the merchants' houses, two men she did not know who sat together and took notes, and at the back a scatter of apprentices who had come to see the woman from the south-west who had bored a keel.

The master's clerk read the matter. The master read the ruling.

It was a careful ruling, and it took some time. The guild, he said, had considered the fitting of engines into boats at three sittings through the winter, and had taken counsel of its oldest members and of the charter itself. It found that the charter of the guild was given for the building of ships and boats and for the work of wood in them. An engine, the court found, was a work of iron, made by founders and smiths, and the setting of an engine in a hull, with the boring and fitting needful to it, was the work of an engineer. The fitting of an engine was therefore no shipwright's work at all, and lay outside the guild's charter. The guild had no rule over it, and the rule of the unbored keel did not reach it. So the boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich had broken no rule of the guild's, and they were readmitted to the guild, with all their papers and all their apprentices, and the fines laid on them were remitted.

He put the paper down and looked at her for the first time.

"The court has ruled," he said. "You may take your papers from the clerk, mistress. The guild is glad to have Caol fhiadhaich back."

Sorcha stood up. The whole hall had gone still. Through the open door came the sound of adzes on the slips below, and a gull, and she could hear her own heart.

She had thought on the cart, and in the inn, and lying awake, about what she would say, and had got no further than the first words. She looked up at the board above the master's head, at the letters as long as a finger.

"Master," she said, "in the autumn I bored the keel of *An Ròn Dubh* with my own auger, in my own yard, in front of three members of this guild. Your letter called it unlawful. You struck us from your roll for it, and you fined us. Now you tell me it was never your business. I bored that keel as a shipwright. I bedded the engine on oak as a shipwright, and I set the tube in the post, and I packed it, and it holds. If that's no shipwright's work, then I've no need of a shipwrights' guild. The boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich thank the court. We won't come back."

There was a noise in the hall then, of a good many people talking at once, and the master struck his bench twice with the flat of his hand. When they were still he asked her whether that was the answer of all four yards, or her own.

"All four," she said. "I have it in writing, if you want it."

"The court doesn't want it," said Pàdraig mac an t-Saoir. He looked at her a while longer. He was not a fool, and he had been a wright before he was a master. "You were bound under that board," he said.

"I was. Twenty-five years I kept the rule. I kept it until it kept the boats in the harbour."

The clerk wrote that the boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich declined the guild's readmission. The master rose, and the court rose with him, and it was over. The two men who had sat together taking notes went out quickly, and one of them, she learned afterwards, was a clerk of Comhairle nan Taighean-solais, and the other had come from the dubhan works at Seann Skell on the southern coast to see what the court would say about engines.

Outside the hall the evening was coming on, clear and cold, with a light wind out of the west. Sorcha walked down to the slips. The yards of Ros dhomhain were closing for the night. On the last slip, the one where she had served the first year of her time, a harbour launch lay chocked up with her planks sprung and her engine gone out of her; the hole in her stern where the shaft had once run was stopped with a wooden plug, and grass was growing out of the seam beside it. Sorcha stood and looked at it until an old watchman came along the slips with his lantern and asked her whether she wanted anything.

"No," she said. "I was looking at the post."

She went back to the carriers' inn, and ate, and asked the carrier what time he went south in the morning. Then she took out the yard-book that she carried everywhere, and under the day's date, below the price of the larch and the name of a skipper from Inis chrom who wanted an engine by Bealltainn, she wrote one line: *Court at Ros dhomhain. Declined.* She closed the book, and put out the lamp.
