# Of the Coal Line and the Salt Road

*DE 8 – 9*

## I. Of the Yard at Muileann chrom

The yard families of Muileann chrom lived in three rows of slate-roofed houses between the sidings and the river, where Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn had put them when it laid the new lines in the kingdom's thirteenth year of the old count. Comhairle nam Mèinnean counted them apart from the rest of the town, and in time they counted themselves apart. They married among the rows, they buried in their own corner of the burial ground below the mill, and they kept their own hours, which were the hours of the trains. A child of the rows learned to tell the coal engines one from another by the note of their breath before she learned her letters, and a man of the rows who could not tell you the gradient of every mile of Slighe a' Ghuail between the yard and the sea was thought a little slow.

Eachann mac Ruairidh was forty-three that spring and had driven coal on the line for twenty years. He was a short, broad man with a burn-scar across the back of one hand from a gauge-glass that had burst when he was a fireman, and he had the habit, which his wife said was the only vain thing about him, of cleaning his boots every night whatever the day had been. He lived in the middle row with Seonaid nic Dhòmhnaill, who had come to the yard as a girl from the drovers of Achadh mhòr, their two daughters, and his father.

His father was Ruairidh mac Eachainn, and he had driven for forty years. He had been a fireman on the first trains the kingdom ran up to An Sloc Mòr, when the rails were still the humans' rails and the timetable was still the humans' timetable, and he had been a driver on the same run before Eachann was born. In the year the yard stopped the trains for three days over the cut in wages, Ruairidh had stood on the line with the rest and had not been among the eleven dismissed on the fifth day, and he never said whether he had been lucky or careful. Now he sat on a chair outside the door of the middle row in all weathers but rain, with a blanket over his knees, and listened to the engines, and could tell you from the sound of a train leaving the yard whether the fireman knew his work.

In the last days of an Gearran, in the eighth year of Linn an Dubhain, which nobody then called by that name, Comhairle nam Mèinnean's clerk at the yard pinned a notice to the door of the engine-shed. Comhairle nam Mèinnean had bought an engine of the new kind from the shipmasters' works at the southern Seann Skell, and would try it on Slighe a' Ghuail. A driver was wanted to learn it. The notice said that the man chosen would be paid at the driver's rate during his learning, and it did not say what would be paid to anyone afterwards.

The older drivers read it and went back to their engines. Two of the younger ones put their names down and took them off again the same evening after talking to their fathers. Eachann read it three times, went home, cleaned his boots, and said nothing about it at supper. His father said nothing either, which was how Eachann knew he had heard.

"You'll put your name to it," Seonaid said, when the girls were in bed.

"Somebody will."

"That is not what I asked."

"Then yes," he said. "I will."

In the morning he wrote his name under the notice, alone, and the clerk took it down at noon.

The engine came up from the south in the middle of am Màrt. Slighe a' Ghuail did not run to the southern Seann Skell, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean would not wait for rails to be laid, so the works sent it by the old road on a low wagon with iron wheels, drawn by twelve horses. The horses were hired from the stables of Òrd Mhacha at Muileann chrom, which kept the best draught-beasts in the central country, and a priest of the order walked at the head of the leaders the whole two days, as the order's custom was whenever its horses hauled for the kingdom. It rained on the first day. On the second the wagon stuck in the ford below the town, and the priest stood in the water to his thighs speaking to the horses in the old words until they took it out. Half the yard went down to the ford to watch. Eachann went with them.

The engine was smaller than he had expected, a long box of dark-painted iron on eight wheels, with a cab at each end and no chimney. There was no firebox and no boiler, and no place in the cab for a fireman to stand. On its side, where Comhairle nam Mèinnean's number would go, someone at the works had chalked a word in the humans' tongue that nobody at the ford could read, and the priest of Macha wiped it off with his sleeve before the wagon came up the bank.

"What does it drink?" somebody asked.

"The black stuff," said a man who had been to the south. "Out of a barrel."

"And what does it eat?"

"Nothing. That is the whole trick of it."

The tank came three days after, on its own wagon, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's men set it on stone piers at the end of the long siding and filled it from barrels brought up by cart from the southern Seann Skell. It was the first dubhan tank Comhairle nam Mèinnean owned. The children of the rows stood under it and sniffed, and made faces, and came back the next day to sniff again. Dubhan in those years had a reek that nobody who smelled it ever forgot, something between a tar-boiler and a wet dog, and it clung to cloth. Seonaid said she could tell from the doorway what her husband had been doing all day, and in the end she made him leave his jacket in the wash-house.

Two fitters from the works came with the engine to teach it. One was a Dia-thìreach of the south coast, a thin, sour man who knew the engine as a priest knows a psalm and could not explain one word of it. The other was a Tuathach from Caol mhòr named Will Garside, who had come south to work at the shipmasters' works when they first took on northern hands. He spoke Dia-thìris with a northern burr and the humans' tongue with his own people, and he would shift from one to the other in the middle of a sentence when he was thinking hard. He was a patient teacher, and had Eachann take the engine's heart out and put it back on the first day, and on the second, and on the seventh, until Eachann could do it in the dark.

"You drove coal," Will said to him once, on the siding, while the rain came off the hills.

"Twenty years."

"Then you'll be wanting to feed it. You'll put your hand out for a shovel that isn't there. Every coal man does it. Leave your hand where it is."

Eachann did it for a week and then stopped. What he could not stop was listening for the fire. A coal engine talked to its driver the whole way: the roar when the door opened, the sigh of the safety-valve, the change of note on a gradient that told you before any gauge did that you were short of steam. The dubhan engine beat. It beat under his feet like a heart that had been running too long, steady and hard, and the beat went up when he opened it and down when he closed it and told him nothing else at all. For the first fortnight he drove with his head on one side like a dog listening at a door, and Will laughed at him and then did not laugh, because the listening was what made him careful.

By the middle of an Giblean he had taken the engine to An Sloc Mòr and back, empty, twice, with the sour fitter beside him and Comhairle nam Mèinnean's engineer, Niall mac Leòid, riding behind in a brake-van with a notebook. By the end of the month he had taken it without the fitter. The engineer wrote in his notebook that the man could drive it. He did not give Eachann any paper that said so, and Eachann did not ask for one.

His father came down to the siding once in all that month. He stood at the end of the platform with his stick and watched Eachann bring the engine in, and when it stopped he looked at it a long while, at the place where the chimney should have been, and at the cab where there was no fire.

"It's clean," he said at last.

"It is."

"A clean engine," his father said, and went home.

## II. Of the Engine that Carried No Coal

On Bealltainn, the first day of an Cèitean, Comhairle na Slighe-iarainn ran its first dubhan train on Slighe a' Ghuail from the yard at Muileann chrom to Cathair dhearg.

The fires of the feast had been lit on the hills about the town the night before, as they were every year, and the cattle of the hill farms driven between them for luck, and at dawn there was still smoke going up from three hilltops into a sky the colour of pewter. In the yard they filled the four tank-wagons from the tank on the piers by hand-pump, the men taking turns at the handle, and the reek of the stuff lay over the sidings so thick that the women of the rows shut their windows. There was no coal on the train. There was dubhan for the lamp-houses of the capital, and a brake-van at the tail with Niall mac Leòid in it, and that was all.

A few people came to see it off: the clerk, the yard foreman, the families of the rows who were not at work, the priest of Macha who had walked the engine up from the ford, and who said a blessing over it that was the blessing for a horse setting out on a long road, since no one had yet made one for an engine. Will Garside had gone back south a week before. Eachann's daughters stood on the wall of the rows and waved. His father was not there. His chair stood outside the door of the middle row with the blanket folded on it.

Eachann opened the engine, and the beat came up under his feet, and the train went out of the yard.

It is thirty-one miles from the yard at Muileann chrom to the goods-sheds at Cathair dhearg by Slighe a' Ghuail, and the line goes west the whole way, down the valley of the river and out onto the long slope of the capital's country. Eachann had driven it more times than he could count, and had never seen it. With no fire to feed and no fireman to watch, he had nothing to do with his eyes but look out of the window, and he found that the road he knew by its gradients and its signals and its places to take water had fields beside it, and people in the fields. At Cnoc ghorm the women of the line-side houses were hanging washing on the line behind their houses and did not look up; a coal train went by them six times a day, and they had not yet learned that this one was different. Past the bend below Cuan chrom the old coal road came down beside the line for a mile, and Eachann saw the roof of An Taigh-seinnse Mòr above it with a cart standing at the door. A little after that the line crossed a burn, and a boy fishing from the bridge dropped his rod and stood staring after the train with his mouth open, because it had no smoke.

That was what people saw, all the way down the line: no smoke. At every crossing where the road went over the rails there was someone standing to look, and they looked at the place above the engine where the smoke should have been, and then at the cab, and then at each other. A carter near Muileann mhin pulled his horse off the road and stood at its head, and as the train went past he took his cap off, though whether it was to the engine or to Eachann or to the day, he did not say.

Muileann mhin was where the lamp-makers were. That winter Comann nan Lòchran had begun to make lamps for dubhan, and had asked leave of the guild court to set on them the mark it had always set on its lamps, the flame over the hammer; the court was still arguing it. As the train came by the town's end, Eachann saw that the lamp-makers had come out of their shops and were standing in a row along the fence of the last field, some thirty of them in their leather aprons, to see the fuel their lamps would burn go by on its way to the capital. Nobody waved. One of them raised a lamp above his head as the engine passed, a new one, unlit, the brass bright in the grey light.

The goods-sheds at Cathair dhearg lay on the eastern edge of the capital, by the coal-yards, and the lamp-house men were waiting there with their carts and their barrels. They were men of the capital's poorer streets, where the lamps had been left dark to save coal six years before and had been lit again when the first dubhan came up from the south by road. The reek was nothing new to them, and nobody made faces at it. The dubhan was drawn off into their barrels by the tank-wagons' taps and loaded the barrels on their carts, and their foreman signed Niall mac Leòid's paper, and it was all done by a little after noon.

Eachann ate his bread in the cab. When he had finished, the lamp-house foreman came and stood by the step and looked up at him.

"Is it true there's no coal in her at all?"

"None."

"Not even to start her?"

"Not a lump."

The foreman shook his head. "My grandfather lit the lamps in Cathair dhearg when the humans put them up," he said. "Every night of his life he carried coal up a ladder. I carried coal up a ladder. My boy has never carried a lump of it, and he's twelve." He looked at the engine a while longer. "I don't know what I think," he said, and went back to his carts.

The train went home empty. There was nothing at the capital that anybody wanted carried to Muileann chrom, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean had not thought to find anything, and so the four tank-wagons rolled east behind the engine with their taps shut and nothing in them but the smell. On the long pull up the valley Eachann opened the engine to its full and felt the beat come up hard under his boots, and the engine took the bank below Cnoc ghorm without slowing, where a coal engine bringing the empties home would have been down to walking pace and the fireman sweating at the door. Eachann had not known it could do that. He sat with the knowledge for a while and did not know what he thought about it either.

He brought the train into the yard at dusk. The feast-fires were smoking still on the hills, and the smoke of them lay in the valley, and the yard lamps, which burned oil, were lit along the sidings. The families had gone in to their suppers. The foreman signed him off. Niall mac Leòid climbed down stiffly from the brake-van, shook Eachann's hand, said that Comhairle nam Mèinnean would be pleased, and went off to find a bed at the inn by the mill.

His father was standing at the end of the long siding, by the tank on its piers, leaning on his stick.

Eachann climbed down. For a while neither of them said anything. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the rows a child was crying and being hushed.

"She took the bank below Cnoc ghorm coming home," Eachann said, "and never slowed."

"I heard her come in," said his father. "From the house. I took her for the mill."

"Come up," Eachann said. "I'll take her down to the points and back. It's no distance. You can stand where the fireman stands."

"There's no fireman."

"Then stand where he would."

His father looked at the engine. In the half-dark it was only a long shape against the lamps, with no glow at the cab door and no steam at the valves, and nothing about it that moved.

"Forty years," Ruairidh said, "I had a fire at my knee. When she was hungry I knew it, and when she was full I knew it, and on the bank below Cnoc ghorm, going up in the dark with a full train, I could look at the fire and tell you whether we'd make the top. That was a thing I knew." He shifted his weight on the stick. "I'll not ride behind a thing I can't see into."

"It's the same line, Athair."

"It is," said his father. "I'll walk it."

He turned and went back up the siding towards the rows, slowly, with the stick, and Eachann stood by the engine until he had gone in at the door of the middle row, and then he went and cleaned his boots.

## III. Of the Salt Road

The summer went on, and the new engine ran to Cathair dhearg twice in the week and came back empty twice in the week, and the coal engines ran as they always had on the trains from An Sloc Mòr and stood idle more often in the siding between runs, because there was less coal to carry each season than the season before. In an t-Ògmhios Comhairle nam Mèinnean took a second fireman off the roll and did not put anyone on in his place, and a man of the first row who had been a fireman nineteen years went down to the river landing to see if the boats wanted hands.

In an Lùnastal a boy of the rows was killed. His name was Ailean mac Mhathain and he was fourteen, and he had been set to work with the shunting gang that summer because his father had broken a leg. The yard was moving the dubhan tank-wagons from the long siding to make room for a second tank, and the gang was moving them by rope and pinch-bar because the small coal engines were busy on the train from An Sloc Mòr and Comhairle nam Mèinnean would not use the new engine for shunting. A wagon ran on the wet rail further than they meant it to. The boy was between it and the next. They buried him in the corner of the burial ground below the mill on the day after, and the whole of the rows walked behind him, and Eachann, who had been on the Cathair dhearg run that day and was not told until he came home, walked with them. Comhairle nam Mèinnean's clerk wrote the death in the yard book as an accident in the changeover. The women of the rows said that in the old days the boy would have been listening for an engine and would have heard it, and that nobody had yet learned to listen for a wagon pushed by men; and that was the only thing ever said about it that anyone wrote down, for Seonaid put it in a letter to her mother at Achadh mhòr, and the letter was kept.

Of the salt the salt-carters of Àth bheag tell it this way.

The pans had been dug on the flats below the town in the kingdom's thirty-second year of the old count, and since then Àth bheag had salted the whole eastern seaboard and a good part of the country inland. The salt went inland the way everything had gone inland from that coast since before there was a kingdom: by the old drove road that runs south out of Àth bheag along the shore, past the tower of Taigh-solais Baile ìseal and the little town beside it, and then turns west away from the sea into the farm country. It went in carts drawn by oxen, six beasts to a cart, and the drovers of the east coast who owned the oxen took the salt in and brought back grain, and hides, and whatever else the inland towns had to sell. It was slow. A laden ox-cart makes ten or twelve miles in a day if the road is dry, and less if it is not, and on that coast it is seldom dry. But it had always been slow, and the drovers and the salt-masters had grown old together with it, and nobody had asked for it to be faster.

In the autumn of that year a salt-master of Àth bheag bought a lorry. He bought it from the shipmasters' works at the southern Seann Skell, where the fitters had begun to set their engines under carts, and it came up the coast road on its own wheels with a fitter driving and a barrel of dubhan roped behind the seat. The drovers came down to the ford of the Abhainn dhubh to look at it, and walked round it, and one of them kicked its wheel, and they went back to the alehouse and laughed about it for a week.

On the eleventh day of an Dùbhlachd it went out with its first load of salt. The salt-master's son drove it. It went down the drove road past Baile ìseal in the early morning, when the lamp was still burning in the tower, and the keepers came out on the gallery to watch it pass, and a man of Baile ìseal who was mending his boat on the shingle said afterwards that it made a noise like a hen-house on fire and went faster than a horse at the trot. By noon it had passed three ox-carts that had set out the day before. The drovers walking at the heads of their teams pulled over into the ditch to let it by, and stood in the ditch and watched it go on up the road, west into the farm country, with the salt-master's son sitting up high in the cab and not looking back.

"We knew then," an old drover of Àth bheag used to say, "and we did not know. We said to each other, one lorry. One lorry is a toy. And we went on to the next town at the pace of the oxen, and when we got there the lorry had sold its salt and gone home."

The lorry came to Muileann chrom two days later, with a second load. It was the salt-master's thought that if salt could come to the yard by lorry it could go on to the capital by rail, faster than any ox-cart could take it by the western roads, and Comhairle nam Mèinnean, which had been sending four tank-wagons home empty twice a week since Bealltainn, was glad to hear it. The salt went into a covered wagon behind the empty tanks, and on the next run Eachann drove it west, and brought it into the goods-sheds at Cathair dhearg, and a salt-merchant of the capital signed for it. It was the first load the new engine had ever carried towards the capital that was not dubhan, and the first time since the spring that it had pulled anything that paid.

Eachann told his father that night. "Salt," he said. "From Àth bheag. Came up by lorry, went down with me."

His father thought about it. "The drovers won't like that."

"No."

"They were an ox people," his father said, "the Àth bheag men. You could hear them singing to the beasts from the other side of a hill. My father knew some of their songs." He was quiet a while. "I don't remember any of them," he said.

That winter the drovers of the east coast began to sell their oxen. They did not sell them all at once. A man with three teams sold one, and then a second when the salt-master bought another lorry, and kept the last for the hay and the carting on his own ground. A man with one team kept it longer than was wise, and then sold it at a worse price than his neighbour had got. The beasts went to the farms inland and to the butchers of the market towns, and the carts stood in the yards with their shafts on the ground. In am Faoilleach of the ninth year the last ox-drove went out of Àth bheag with salt on the old road, four carts and twenty-four beasts, driven by an old man named Murchadh Bàn and his two grandsons, and it took five days to reach the inland towns in the rain; and when it came home the old man sold the oxen at the ford and did not buy others.

## IV. Of the Drovers at An Sligeanach Fad' às

Seonaid's brother was Uilleam mac Dhòmhnaill, and he had been a drover all his life, as their father had been. The drovers of Achadh mhòr were a different people from the drovers of the east. They were a southern family of families, who drove the oxen of the granary country and hauled its grain, and who had driven the cattle up from the east coast every autumn to the great fields of Achadh mhòr, where the stubble fattened them, and sold them on in the spring. They were wealthier than the eastern drovers and prouder, and a family of them had bought An Sligeanach Fad' às ten years before and built a second hall on it for the drove trade, so that it had become the greatest house on the old mining roads.

Uilleam wrote to his sister at the turn of the year. The letter was short, because he did not like writing, and Seonaid read it aloud at supper. There were no oxen coming from the east this spring, he said, because the eastern men had sold their teams; there was nothing to drive to the fields and nothing to drive away from them; and the grain of Achadh mhòr was going to the southern Seann Skell by lorry already, for the works there had begun to sell lorries to anyone who would pay. The family was meeting at the Sligeanach on the twenty-second day of am Màrt to settle what they would do. He did not say what that would be. He asked Eachann to come, if Comhairle nam Mèinnean would let him go, since Eachann knew engines.

Comhairle nam Mèinnean let him go for two days. He went south by the carrier's cart on the old road, twenty-one miles through the farm country in a thin rain, and came to the Sligeanach in the late afternoon of the day before the meeting.

It stood by itself on the southern road a little way out of Achadh mhòr, a long, low house of grey stone with a slate roof, and the second hall built out at the back of it, and behind that the ox-yards, walled, where two hundred beasts could stand at a time. It was the oldest house under Sìth an Rathaid, the law which says that no feud may be followed under an inn roof or within a spear's throw of its door, and the keepers of the waypoint houses had first set that law down here in An Aois Ailein. The carriers say that one is never nearer to An Sligeanach Fad' às than halfway, wherever one starts, and it has had that name from the saying for longer than it has had its stone walls.

When Eachann came in by the road that afternoon, the ox-yards were empty. There was a new thing in the forecourt, between the door and the road: an iron tank on stone piers like the one at the yard in Muileann chrom, only smaller, painted green, with a hand-pump beside it and a board nailed to the pump with the price chalked on it by the gallon. A lorry was drawn up at it, and a boy of the house was working the handle, and the reek of dubhan hung over the forecourt in the rain. Inside, the old hall was lit by the lamps it had always had, oil lamps with wicks on chains from the beams, and the fire was a fire of peat.

Uilleam met him at the door. He was a big, fair man, going grey, with a drover's hands and a drover's way of standing with his weight back, as if he had a beast on a rope in front of him.

"You smell like a lorry," Uilleam said.

"I drive one on rails."

"So I hear." He took Eachann's wet coat and hung it by the fire. "We're all going to smell like it soon enough."

The meeting was in the morning, in the second hall. There were some forty of them, the heads of the drover families of Achadh mhòr and their sons, and the landlord of the Sligeanach, who was one of them, and a man from the works at the southern Seann Skell in a good coat who had come with papers. Eachann sat at the back and held his tongue, for he was no drover and it was not his meeting; he listened, and afterwards he could tell Seonaid all of it.

It was not a long meeting. There were no oxen coming from the east, and there would be none next year. The grain would go to the southern Seann Skell and to the capital whether the drovers hauled it or not; the only question was whether it would go in their lorries or in someone else's. An old man named Aonghas Mòr, the eldest of the families, spoke for keeping the oxen one more year, to see. Nobody answered him. When he had sat down, the landlord said that he had put a tank of dubhan in his forecourt that winter, and that he had sold more fuel off it since Samhain than he had sold beds, and that if the families bought lorries he would sell them their fuel and their dinners at the same door, and the house would stand as it had always stood. The man from the works laid out his papers.

By noon they had agreed. Each family would sell its teams at the Sligeanach that day, to the butchers and farmers who had come for the purpose, and the works would take the price of the oxen against the price of lorries, the rest to be paid over three years from the grain. Uilleam put his mark to the paper with the others. His hand was steady.

The sale was held in the ox-yards in the afternoon. The rain had stopped and a low sun came in under the cloud, and the yards were full again for the last time, with the beasts of every drover family of Achadh mhòr, great red and brindled oxen with their horns tipped in brass, steaming in the cold. Buyers came from all over the granary country. Eachann stood by the wall with his brother-in-law and watched the lots go through.

Uilleam's were the last. There were six of them, one team, and he had raised the youngest from a calf. He went into the pen before the auctioneer called them and walked down the line of them with his hand on their backs, speaking to them in the words the drovers use to their beasts, which are older than the road; and then he came out and shut the gate and did not look at them again while they were sold. They went to a farmer from the hills west of Achadh mhòr, who wanted them for ploughing, and the price was a fair one.

"He'll work them," Uilleam said, when it was done. "That's something. He won't eat them."

"No."

"My father drove the east road forty years. His father before him. We had the cattle up out of the east every autumn, every year, since there were drovers." He stood looking at the empty pen. "Your father still won't ride the thing?"

"He won't."

"I wouldn't either," Uilleam said, "at his age." He turned from the pen. "But I'm not his age. Come and look at the lorry and tell me what I've bought."

The lorries stood in a row in the road beyond the forecourt, eleven of them, painted the same dark green as the tank, which was the colour the works painted everything. The man from the works had driven one up and brought the others with fitters in the morning. They were high, ugly, square-built things with open beds for the grain and a cab over the engine, and in the evening light, with the empty ox-yards behind them, they looked to Eachann like beasts of some kind themselves, waiting to be led. He went over Uilleam's with the fitter while the light lasted. He showed his brother-in-law where the engine's heart was, and how to know from the beat when it was labouring, and which of the fitter's answers to believe, and Uilleam listened to him as he had never listened to him in the twenty years since Seonaid had married into the rows.

When it was too dark to see, they came back across the forecourt. The landlord's boy was at the pump again, filling the lorries one after another from the green tank, and working the handle with his whole body, and the landlord stood beside him with a slate, chalking the gallons. On the wall of the forecourt behind him there was an older slate, fixed to the stone, where the house had kept the tally of beasts through the ox-yards for as long as anyone could remember, and the last figures on it were still there, in the landlord's hand, from the autumn. The landlord saw Eachann looking at it.

"I'll wash it off in the morning," he said. "I'll want it for the barrels."

Inside, the lamps on their chains were lit, and the fire of peat was built up, and the drovers of Achadh mhòr sat down together in the old hall to eat, as they had eaten there every spring on the day of the sale since the house was theirs. Somebody began one of the droving songs, and some of the older men took it up and some did not. Out in the road the lorries stood in their row in the dark, with the rain coming on again and running off their square cabs, and the reek of their fuel drifted in under the door every time it opened, and nobody got up to shut it.
