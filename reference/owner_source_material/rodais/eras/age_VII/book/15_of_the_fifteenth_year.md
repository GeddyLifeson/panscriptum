# Of the Fifteenth Year

*DE 15*

## I. Of the Northern Depots

The letter from Doire ghlas came up the library road in the last week of an Giblean, water-stained at one corner where the carrier had let his bag swing against a puddle, and Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh read it twice before she set it on the day's pile.

It was Martha Greaves's own hand, which had grown no neater in seven years of writing the Moot's minutes and had, if anything, grown faster and less patient, so that whole words leaned into one another like men crowding a doorway out of the rain. A dubhan depot had opened that month at Doire ghlas, she wrote, supplied by sea from the south, and word had come the same week of two more, at Caol mhòr and at Ceann mhòr, both stocked from the same boats. The figures followed as the Board's agent had given them to her: so many casks a fortnight, so much a cask, a scale of credit for any household that could not pay outright and would work it off at the mill. All this she set down as a minute sets a thing down, plainly, one fact behind another, and then, at the foot of the page, in a line apart from the rest as though she had thought about it before she let herself write it, came what the minute did not ask her to say. *The north now buys its fire from the south. I do not say whether this is a good thing or a bad one. I say only that it is the case, and that it was not the case a year ago.*

Beathag copied the letter whole into the day's page, the figures first and Martha's own line last, exactly as it stood, and did not add a word of her own beneath it. She had learned, in her years at the desk, that some sentences wanted nothing added to them, and this was one; anything she might have said herself would only have blunted the plain fact Martha had already sharpened for her.

Doire ghlas she knew a little, from letters if from nothing else: a mill town on the north coast, its people Tuathaich of the old northern congregations, whose great church under the grey oaks the north calls the heart of its faith. She had never been there and did not expect now that she would be. It came to her plainly enough from Martha's figures all the same: the casks coming off a boat at a quay that had never before unloaded anything but wool and salted fish, a queue of townsfolk with their own carts and barrows, a clerk with a scale of credit written on a board because half of them could not pay outright and the mill would stand for them if they could not; and somewhere in the queue, an old man or an old woman remembering the coal fires their own fathers had burned, coal that had never once, in all the years of the kingdom, come north of the boundary stones to warm a Tuathach hearth, because the vein was the kingdom's and the north had never held any part of it. Now the north was buying fire it had made no part of either, only bought it from a different hand.

She wrote nothing of this in the record. The carrying of it belonged to Martha's own line, and Martha's line was enough.

Three days later a shorter note came up from Raghnall mac Mhuirich's own desk, though he sat not six feet from her own, because he had taken to leaving her such notes when he thought a thing wanted saying and did not want to interrupt her at the page. It read only: *Ask Sìleas to mark the depots on the map in the front room. Three dots, close together on the coast. It will want marking again before the year is out, I think, though I could not tell you why I think it.*

She did not ask him why. His hunches about the shape a year would take, she had learned, were more often right than wrong, and he liked least to be asked to account for them before they had proved themselves one way or the other. She called Sìleas from the copying-room and gave her the note, and watched the girl's quick hand set the three dots on the old map that hung in the front room, close together on the coast as the note said, small and black and, for the present, no more than three dots among a hundred others.

## II. Of the Fifth of an Cèitean

The council sat on the fifth of an Cèitean, and what was done at that sitting reached an Leabharlann three ways within the week: the council's own minute, sent as a matter of course to every office of record; a private letter from a clerk of the Treasury who owed Raghnall a kindness from some years before and thought the chronicler's desk should not wait on the formal copy; and, last and slowest, the printed proclamation, posted up in the market towns and brought to the Library folded inside a farmer's almanac by a carrier who had torn it from a post at Cathair dhearg because he thought Beathag would want to see the thing as the crowd had seen it, creased and a little torn at one corner where someone's thumbnail had caught it pulling it down to read.

She read the clerk's letter first, because it came first and because it was the plainest telling of the three. He wrote that the council, sitting the whole of the forenoon and part of the afternoon on nothing else, had resolved two things at one stroke, as though the Treasury's masters had judged that two changes made on the same day would be easier for the kingdom to swallow than the same two changes made a month apart, each with its own weight of argument. The first: that the coin of the Rìoghachd, which since Fearchar Bonn's own day had rested on the stores of coal held against it, would rest from that day on the making of dubhan, the mint's reserve counted now in casks of the new fuel and not in coal at all. The second, moved by a councillor whose name the clerk did not think it his place to give, though Beathag guessed from the wording of the motion that it had come from one who had argued it before and lost: that the age of the kingdom, which had run since the Severance under its own count of years, was ended, and that a new count began from the day of the first working dubhan, fourteen years gone and more, so that the year now standing, which the mint's own ledgers and the council's own minutes had been calling the eighty-fifth of the old count, was to be entered thereafter as the fifteenth year of Linn an Dubhain, the era of Dubhan.

The clerk added, in a line he had crossed out and then, thinking better of the crossing, written again below it so that both stood: that the proclamation had passed by a show of hands with no count taken, which was not the council's custom in a matter of such weight, and that he did not know whether this meant the matter was thought too plain to need counting, or too awkward to want a number set against it that some other year might be held up and compared.

The printed proclamation, when it came, said nothing of the vote at all, only the two resolutions themselves in the flat language proclamations use, and, at the foot, the line that was carried to every market town on the same sheet: *from the day of the first dubhan the years shall be counted anew.* Beathag read it standing at the window with the creased sheet held to the light, the tear at its corner letting a thin blade of sun through onto the page, and thought that a single sentence had just done to fourteen years of her own record what the Library's reckoners had done, three generations before her time, to the ages before the Crossing, folding a stretch of lived years into a new count as though the years themselves had always belonged to it and only wanted the right hand to say so.

"They have moved the ground under every date I have ever written," she said to Raghnall, who had come to stand at her shoulder, though she was not sure until she had said it whether she meant it as complaint or as plain description, and found, turning the sentence over once it was out of her mouth, that she meant it as both at once and saw no reason to choose between them.

"They have," Raghnall said. "It was always dubhan that moved the ground. This is only the council saying so out loud, which it does seldom enough that I own I did not expect it from them this cleanly."

## III. Of the Fourteen Years Re-headed

The work fell to her alone, because it was a chronicler's work and no clerk's, however careful the clerk's hand; a clerk might copy a line, but only the chronicler who had written the fourteen years in the first place could say, of each one, where its true head now stood.

She began the evening the proclamation reached her, by a single lamp at the long table in the room behind the front room, with the fourteen ledgers stacked at her right hand in the order she had written them and a fifteenth, the year now running, open before her with its first four months already filled under the old heading. Sìleas offered to sit with her and hold the lamp steady, or fetch fresh oil when the first measure burned low, and Beathag let her, though there was little enough the girl could do beyond that; the turning wanted one hand and one memory, and Sìleas's memory did not go back far enough to be trusted with it.

It was not hard work in the doing, only slow, and slower than she had thought it would be when she sat down to it. Each ledger's first page carried, at its head, the year of the old count, *SE* and the number, in the same square hand she had used for fourteen years running because she had never in that time had reason to write it any other way; and beside each old heading she now set the new, *Linn an Dubhain*, the year in its place, first, second, third, and so down the stack to the fourteenth, which was the year just closed, and the fifteenth, which was the year she sat in as she wrote. She did not strike out the old headings. She had thought, before she began, that she might, and had decided against it somewhere in the walk from the front room to the long table, on no better ground than that a thing once written into the record was the record's own and not hers to unwrite, however the council chose to count it afterward; so she set the new heading beside the old, in a smaller hand, with a line drawn between them, and let both stand.

Between the sixth ledger and the seventh, some time past midnight by the state of the lamp, she stopped long enough to look back over what she had already turned, and found, reading the years in their new order, that she remembered them differently set out so. The sixth year of Linn an Dubhain had been, under its old heading, the seventy-sixth of the kingdom, a year she remembered chiefly for the cold spring and a fever that had gone through Muileann chaol's own streets and taken two children from the copyists' row; set now under the new count it was the year the coastal lights first burned dubhan, and she found she had to search her own memory for the fever and the cold spring, which had not moved an inch in the record but seemed, set beside the new heading, to have slipped a little further back than they had stood the night before.

"It is a strange thing," she said to Sìleas, somewhere past the ninth ledger, "to find that a year does not change by being renamed, and yet somehow does."

"Which is the truer year, then," Sìleas asked, "the one you lived, or the one the council has just given it?"

Beathag considered this longer than the question perhaps deserved, turning her pen between two fingers the way she did when a thing wanted more thought than the first answer she could have given it. "Both," she said at last. "I lived the year I lived, cold spring and fever and all, and no proclamation touches that. But I did not live it knowing what it was, and the council has just told the whole kingdom what it was, all at once, fourteen years after the fact. I expect that is what a proclamation is for. It does not change what happened. It only tells the ones who lived through it what they were living through at the time, which they could not have known, being in the middle of it."

She finished the fourteenth ledger a little before the light came up, and set it on the pile with the others, and sat a while with her hands flat on the table before she drew the fifteenth toward her, which wanted no turning, only a new heading at its head to match the fourteen behind it. She wrote *Linn an Dubhain, a' chòigeamh bliadhna deug* at the top of the open page, the fifteenth year of the Dubhan Era, in the same square hand as always, and found, setting the pen down, that her hand was not quite steady, though she could not have said whether it was the hour or the work.

At the foot of that same page, under the day's entries, which were themselves nothing more than the depot letter she had copied the week before and a note of a birth at Muileann chaol she had taken down for the town's own roll, she wrote one line more, in a hand smaller than her working hand, as though she meant it less for the record than for herself, though she let it stand once written and never struck it: *Cha b' fheudar dhomh riamh roimhe bliadhna a ghluasad 's mi beò innte.* Never before have I had to move a year I was living in.

Sìleas, reading it over her shoulder as she blotted the page, said nothing for a moment, and then asked whether it was meant to go into the fair copy that went up to the council each quarter.

"It can go in," Beathag said. "I've no wish to hide it. But copy it exactly as it stands, and don't smooth the grammar. I wrote it tired, and I'd sooner the council saw that I wrote it tired than have it think a chronicler writes such a thing at her ease."

## IV. Of the Plain Seal

The letter from the mint at Cathair dhearg came up a month later, in the first week of an t-Ògmhios, over the signature of its master but in a hand Beathag recognised before she reached the signature at all, because she had had one letter from that hand before, three years past, asking whether the Library kept any record of the old ink-recipe's origin, and had answered that it did not, only the recipe, which the mint already had.

Gille-Easbaig Bàn wrote now to say that the mint had cut new dies for the coin's seal, the first change to the seal since Fearchar Bonn's own day, and that the coal ink, ground from vein coal at a cost that had climbed every year of his own service at the mint until it stood, that spring, higher than the seal's whole worth in coin, was pressed into no die of the new coinage at all. The new seal showed the kingdom's mark plain, in the same silver as the coin, with no colour worked into it, and he wrote, in a line Beathag thought carried more feeling than the rest of the letter allowed him to show, that he did not know whether he was glad of it or sorry, having spent twelve years grinding an ink he would now never grind again, and having no pupil of his own yet to whom the losing of that knowledge might matter one way or the other.

He wrote last, as though it were an afterthought and not, Beathag thought, the true reason he had troubled to write to her at all rather than let the council's own notice carry the news: that Fearchar Bonn's first dies, the ones that had struck the kingdom's very first coin in the old counting-house at Cathair dhearg, and had been kept at the mint ever since, more relic than tool, past any use but the honour of having been first, were the council's to dispose of now that the seal they had cut was no longer the kingdom's seal, and the council had ordered them sent to an Leabharlann, to be kept there rather than melted, because a die that had struck the kingdom's first coin was thought worth more as a kept thing than as the little weight of silver it would fetch broken up.

They came up ten days later, wrapped in oiled cloth in a box no bigger than a loaf, carried by a mounted courier of the Treasury who would not leave them with anyone but the keeper himself and made Raghnall sign a paper before he would hand the box over, though what the paper said neither Raghnall nor Beathag could afterward agree, having both signed it without reading it closely in their eagerness to open the box.

The dies themselves were smaller than either of them had expected, two flat discs of worn steel, the king's old mark on one face of each so shallow with age and use that it wanted a slant of lamplight to read at all. Raghnall turned them over twice in his hands before he set them, at Beathag's own suggestion, in the glass case in the front room where the Hewers' oath-stone already stood, the two things side by side under one pane of glass, a stone worn smooth by two generations of hewers' thumbs and a pair of dies worn smooth by more strikings than any ledger had troubled to count, neither of them any use now to the trade that had made them.

"It is a strange sort of case we are building," Raghnall said, setting the last of the dust from his hands. "Everything that comes into it has just stopped being used."

"That is what a library is for, in the end," Beathag said. "Not only the things still in use. Someone has to keep the things that have stopped."

## V. Of the Two Letters

The high custodian's letter reached an Leabharlann in the last week of an t-Iuchar, sent, as his letters always were, over the seal of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail at the southern Seann Skell and in his own close, old-fashioned hand, which Beathag had come, over the years of her office, to read almost as easily as her own.

Alasdair mac Ìomhair wrote to the council, and sent a copy, as courtesy required, to the Library for its record, protesting the plain seal in terms he had plainly weighed with some care, for the letter carried none of the heat his earlier letters, on lesser matters, had sometimes shown. The coal ink pressed into every coin the kingdom had struck since Fearchar Bonn's day, whatever the mint's masters might now call it in their accounts, was a mark, he wrote: every hand that touched the coin, market-woman or minister, had touched it knowing that the kingdom's own wealth rested, in the last account, on the vein the Old Faith had always held sacred. A seal struck plain, in silver alone, with no colour of the coal in it anywhere, said to every hand that touched it thereafter that the kingdom no longer held that to be so, whatever the council's clerks might answer if asked outright. Withdrawal of the seal he did not ask. He asked only that the council say, in its own words and not through a mint-master's accounting, whether it meant by the change what he took it to mean.

The council's answer came within the fortnight, shorter than the custodian's own letter by more than half, over the signature of the councillor Beathag guessed, from the clerk's earlier letter of an Cèitean, had moved the proclamation in the first place, though the answer named no mover, only the council as a body. It said that the coin's seal had never, at any sitting of the council since the mint was first set up, been declared a holy thing by any resolution the council's own minutes could show; that the coal ink had been pressed into it because coal was, at the time, the surest reserve the kingdom held to stand behind its coin, and for no other reason the record kept; and that the council, in changing the reserve to match the fuel the kingdom now in fact used, had changed nothing it had ever said was sacred, because it had never said any such thing to begin with. The letter closed with a single sentence that carried, Beathag thought reading it the first time and thought again reading it the fifth, more weight than its own plainness seemed to want to admit: that the council held no vein and no fuel sacred, and never had, and that if the custodian's houses had taught otherwise these fourteen years, the teaching was theirs, and not the kingdom's.

She copied both letters whole into the day's page, the custodian's first and the council's after it, in the order they had come to her, and set no note of her own between them or beneath them, though she sat a long while at the table after the second was copied, the pen dry in her hand, before she blotted the page and closed the ledger for the night.

It was, she thought, setting the ledger back on its shelf among the fourteen others and the fifteenth still open beside them, one of the few places in the whole of her record, in all her years at the desk, where the kingdom had been asked outright what it held to be true, and had answered the question rather than let it stand unanswered as such questions were more often let stand; and it had answered, when it came to it, that it had never held what everyone had long taken it to hold, coal or council or custodian alike, and had never troubled, until a plain-seal coin and a custodian's letter forced the words out of it, to say so.

She did not write that thought into the record. It was only her own reading of a fact, and she had long held that a chronicler's readings belonged in her own head, not in the ledger, however much they might colour the choosing of what she set down and what she let pass. But she thought it again, walking back from the front room to her own small chamber under the eaves, the last light of an t-Iuchar still pale in the sky though the hour was late, and thought that a kingdom that had just said, in writing and for the first time, what it did not believe, might in time be asked what it did, and that she did not know, setting her lamp down for the night, whether that question, when it came, would be as easily answered as this one had been.
