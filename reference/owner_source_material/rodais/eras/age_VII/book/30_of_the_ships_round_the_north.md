# Of the Ships Round the North

*DE 21*

## I. Of the Regiments Called Again

The rolls that had lain in the council's chests since the Severance, brought out once already that autumn to arm the regiments that went west to Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais, were not yet put away when the second half of them was wanted, and by the middle of an Dàmhair the calling went on across the whole island rather than the west alone, so that a man might now be summoned to a war he had thought, until the summons found him, a quarrel of the pit towns and no business of his own province at all.

The sixth was raised first, at Cnoc bheag, the great river port of the Hawk houses in the north-central country, on the seventeenth of the month, and of all the regiments called that autumn it alone had its colours blessed before it marched, the Hawk order keeping still, in that one town, the custom that had gone out of fashion at the capital three reigns before. The priest who blessed them was an old man, by every account that reached the Library afterwards, and he blessed the cloth and not the men who would carry it, saying, as the custom asks, that Mòd's hunger fed on what marched under an unblessed flag sooner than on what marched under a blessed one, which the regiment's own clerk wrote down as said and did not trouble to explain further, it being no part of a clerk's office to explain a priest to a reader who had not been there to hear him. The colours, when they went north-west a week later to hold the road between the Tuathaich shires and the middle of the island, were new cloth on an old staff, one that had stood in the Hawk house's own keeping since a war none of the men now carrying it could have named. The blessing was done at first light on the quay steps, the cloth laid across the priest's own two hands and the whole regiment drawn up in three sides of a square about him with the river fog still lying on the water behind, and the townsfolk who had turned out to watch it, being a river port and used to the sight of soldiery coming and going on the Board's business, stood a good deal quieter than the Board's own returns of the day thought worth remarking, as men and women commonly do at the start of a thing whose end they have no way yet of guessing.

The regiment's own clerk, a careful young man new to the office, wrote in his first report that the ceremony had taken the better part of an hour on account of the tide, the colours needing to be blessed and the boats loaded on the same ebb, and that the priest had shown no sign of hurrying it for the tide's sake or anybody else's, which the clerk set down without complaint, having been told by his own captain, before ever they reached Cnoc bheag, that a blessing rushed for the convenience of a schedule was a blessing better not asked for at all.

Eight days after that the second was raised at Àth dhìreach, in the east, and set to guard the coal line and the library road together, the two roads running so near each other along that stretch that one company could watch both without doubling its marches. It was the first soldiery the town of Muileann chaol had seen in any living memory, and Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, who had kept the royal chronicle for five-and-twenty years before she laid the office down and asked nothing of her retirement but her sister's house at Àth dhìreach, watched the camp go up from the garden wall with the particular attention of a woman who had spent a working lifetime writing down what other people did and had never once, until that autumn, had it done under her own window.

She wrote to Sìleas nic Rath, her old clerk and the chronicler now in her place, a letter that said less than a letter from Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh might have been expected to say, on the reasoning, she told Sìleas plainly, that the office was no longer hers to fill with her own judgements, only her own eyes, which the office had never asked to be idle even when the pen that once served them had been laid aside. The camp was orderly, she wrote; the tents stood in rows a surveyor might have set out; the company's captain, a young man not long out of the Board's own service, had called on her within the first week to ask, civilly enough, whether her garden wall marked ground he needed for a picket line, and had accepted her answer, which was that it did not, without argument. She wrote that the town had given the company no trouble and the company had given the town none, and that this, in a year when other towns nearer the fighting could say no such thing, seemed to her worth the setting down, whatever anyone at the Library chose to make of it.

She added, at the foot of the second page, a line she struck through once and then let stand, the ink of the strike-through thin enough that Sìleas read the words under it without difficulty: that she had spent five-and-twenty years writing down what a kingdom did in its own name, and had come, in the six months since she laid the pen aside, to find she trusted her own eyes on a garden wall rather better than she had ever quite let herself trust the reports that used to reach her desk from towns she had never once had occasion to stand in. Sìleas kept the letter among her own papers, and it is one of the few private hands of that autumn that anyone kept by nobody's order but its writer's own habit of writing things down.

Away in the north-east, at Caol bheag, the fourth was raised on the second of an t-Samhain, and of the four companies mustered that season it was the fourth's road that ran longest and strangest before it ever came within sight of a field it might have to hold.

## II. Of the Voyage Round the North

Dùghall mac Iain was twenty-three that autumn and had been three years a soldier without, until the muster bell rang at Caol bheag on the last day of an Dàmhair, ever once expecting to be one anywhere but at home. He was a corporal because a sergeant who had known his father thought him steady, and steady he had been, in the way of a young man who has never yet been asked to prove it: he kept his kit in order, said little in the mess that he had not first turned over twice in his own head, and had, before that autumn, gone no farther from Caol bheag in the whole of his soldiering than the shire's own boundary stone, twelve miles out on the Ros chiar road, where the company sometimes marched of a summer to keep its legs in practice and its officers' reports supplied with something to report.

The order that came down named the fourth for the north-west, without saying at first how a company was to reach a coast that no road on the island ran the whole way to, and it was two days before the answer came down after it: they were to go by water, down the Abhainn ghorm to Ros chiar, and from Ros chiar round the north coast in whatever hulls the Board could hire on short notice, there being no fleet of the Crown's own idle enough that season to spare for the carrying of one regiment when four others wanted moving besides.

They marched down to the river on a grey morning with the frost still on the grass verge and the town's whole population turned out along the road to see them go, which Dùghall found harder to bear than he had looked for, the cheering of people he had known his whole life being a different thing entirely from the cheering he had imagined, in the idle way a young soldier imagines such scenes before he is given one, of a crowd that did not know him and so could cheer him without also, in the same breath, wondering aloud to each other whether he would come home. His own mother stood at the corner by the mill with her shawl pulled tight and said nothing at all as he passed, which he understood, without either of them needing to say it, as the greater kindness.

The barges that carried them down the Abhainn ghorm were coal barges once, by the look of the tar still black in their seams, refitted since with plank benches down both sides and not much else, and the river ran high and quick with the autumn rain so that the two days' journey the maps had promised was done in a day and a half, the barge-men steering more than rowing, and Dùghall, who had never in his life been afloat on anything larger than a fishing coble, spent the better part of that first day learning the particular misery of a barge deck in a following current, which pitches a man's stomach in ways no cart on a rutted road had ever taught him to expect.

The men of his own file passed that first day poorly, the way soldiers pass any day with nothing asked of them but to sit still and be carried, in fits of talk that ran out and started again over nothing much: a game with pebbles and a chalked board one of them carried folded in his kit for exactly such an idleness, an argument over whose home town brewed the better ale that neither side had any way of settling on a barge deck and so carried on for the better part of an hour regardless, and, once the light began to go, a song two of the older men knew between them and the rest picked up by the second verse, a harvest song their own mothers might have sung, which struck Dùghall, listening to it carry out flat over the water in the dusk, as a strange thing for men bound to a war to be singing, and a right one all the same.

His own corporal's charge, a file of nine, was young for the most part, three of them not yet twenty and none, Dùghall among them, ever before asked to do more soldiering than a summer's drill and the keeping of a quiet road. He found himself, without quite choosing it, the file's steadiest hand by simple virtue of being the one among them who had worn the corporal's own mark longest, a matter of eight months and no more, and spent a good part of that first night's watch answering questions from the youngest of them, a lad named for his own father and called by nobody the whole voyage but that same father's name, about what a battle was likely to be like, questions Dùghall had no truer answer to than the lad had himself, and answered anyway, as steadily as he could make his voice go, because a corporal who let his own voice shake in the dark was no use to a file with nothing else that night to hold steady by.

Ros chiar, when they came to it, was a walled town smaller than Caol bheag and built entirely, so far as Dùghall could see from the barge, around the business of getting things onto and off ships, its whole waterfront one long clutter of hoists and rope-walks and sheds smelling of tar and salt fish, and it was there, tied up three deep along the outer quay, that he had his first sight of the hulls that were to carry the regiment the rest of the way: squat, broad-beamed vessels with tanks built into their holds where cargo should have gone, hired, the quartermaster said, from whatever shipmaster on that coast owned a hull already fitted to carry dubhan and was willing, for the Board's money, to carry men instead for one voyage.

The Board's money was good money, and the shipmasters who took it asked no questions Dùghall's officers troubled to answer, but the men who crewed the hulls asked plenty among themselves, in the hearing of soldiers standing at the rail with nothing else to do but hear them, and Dùghall learned in this way, before any officer told him so directly, that they were going round the north cape and down the far side to a coast the fourth's own quartermaster had needed a chart to find, a coast whose towns the crew named as easily as Dùghall could have named the streets of Caol bheag, and spoke of, he noticed, in the plain flat voice of men speaking of somewhere they had traded in for years and expected no trouble in trading in again, whatever cargo they carried this particular voyage.

They put out from Ros chiar on the fourth of an t-Samhain into a sea already turning, the shipmasters said without much comfort in the saying, and it was a hard passage by any account Dùghall heard given afterwards, five days round the north cape in hulls built for dubhan and not for men, the tanks below decks giving off a smell that got into a man's kit and did not come out of it again for weeks, and the north cape, when they rounded it on the second night, showed Dùghall a sea he had not known the island's own coast could make: grey water running in ridges taller than the barge that had carried him down the river, and beyond it, for the better part of a watch, nothing at all to be seen in any direction but that same grey water going on to a horizon a man could not find the edge of. He was sick over the rail twice that night, along with the better part of his own file, and heard, above the wind, one of the older soldiers laughing at nothing in particular in a way Dùghall came in time to understand: a man keeping his own voice going so as not to have to listen too closely to the sea.

They lost no man on that passage, though a boy of the second company went overboard reaching for a line that had come loose and was hauled back in half-drowned and shaking, and one hull, separated from the others in the worst of the second night's weather, put into a cove on the far side of the cape and lost two days finding the others again, arriving at Ceann mhòr three days behind the rest with its whole crew and cargo safe but its captain, by every account, considerably older in the face than he had been when he set out.

## III. Of Ceann mhòr

Ceann mhòr stood at the head of a long inlet on the Tuathaich coast, a port town of some size by the standard of that country, its grey stone houses climbing the slope above the harbour in the close-built rows Dùghall had already learned, from what the older men of the company said of Tuathaich towns, to expect of that people's building, and its church, the largest structure the town owned, standing above the harbour where a Dia-thìrich town would have set its temple, which struck Dùghall, seeing it for the first time from the deck of the hull that carried him in, as a small and useless thing to notice and one he found himself noticing anyway.

The regiment was quartered on the tenth of an t-Samhain in the old human magistrate's house, a low, thick-walled building above the quay that the town's own maor, a Dia-thìrich man of the Crown's appointing named for that office alone, had kept as his own residence since the shires were first drawn, on the reasoning, handed down with the office itself, that a house built by the humans to hold the humans' law was as good a house as any to hold the Crown's, and better than most, being stone through and cheap to warm. The maor gave up the greater part of it without complaint that Dùghall's own captain reported hearing, and moved his own household into the two upper rooms the regiment had no use for, and it was noticed by more than one man of the fourth, in the way soldiers notice such things without being told to, that the maor's clerk, a Tuathaich woman who had served the office for years under three maoir running, addressed the captain in Dia-thìris without once stumbling over it and addressed her own townsfolk, when they came to the house on the office's business, in the humans' tongue exclusively, as though the two languages belonged, in her own ordering of the world, to two entirely different kinds of transaction and were not to be let mix.

The house, once four hundred men had been fitted into what had held one household and its servants, kept a smell about it for the whole of that winter that Dùghall came to think of as the particular smell of soldiery indoors, wet wool and boot-blacking and the tallow of too many lamps burning too long into too short a day, laid over whatever the magistrates who had built the place had meant it to smell of, and the maor himself, a heavy, unhurried man who had held the office since before Dùghall was born, took to walking the two upper rooms' own narrow gallery of an evening with his pipe, looking down into the courtyard at the drill his house had been given over to, and saying, to whichever officer happened to be crossing below him, some version of the same dry observation: that he had held this house forty years against damp, and the army had done in a fortnight what forty winters of the sea air had not managed, which the captain, by the third or fourth telling of it, had learned to answer only with a nod, there being no answer to a landlord's joke that does not also concede the landlord's point.

The Moot's protest reached the house within the fortnight, carried up from Caol mhòr by whatever carrier still worked that coast road in the season's worsening weather, a single sheet in Martha Greaves's own hand asking, in terms Dùghall's captain read aloud to the assembled company on the theory that men quartered somewhere ought to know what was being said of the quartering, that the fourth regiment be lodged at its own charge and not the town's, the town having neither asked for soldiers nor been offered the choosing of them. It was received, the captain's own note back to the Board said, and forwarded, and there the matter rested, as such matters generally did, the Board's reply, when it came near the year's end, saying only that the arrangement stood until the campaign's needs changed it, which answered the letter's words without answering anything the letter had actually been written to ask.

It was in those same weeks, though a good way farther west along that same coast, that the fleets off Cathair gheal took up the searching of the fishing boats that put out from that harbour every morning the weather allowed it, a measure the Board's order named necessary against the carrying of arms or men under a full boat's catch, and one the fishermen of that town, within a month of its starting, had put into a petition of their own, carried to the Moot at Caol mhòr by a delegation of six, and by the Moot on to the council with no more comment attached than the plain fact of its having been forwarded. The searching went on the same as before the petition and the same as after it, a boat held at its own quay of a morning for the better part of an hour while two of the fleet's own men went through its nets and its fish-hold both, and it was said afterwards, by the fishermen themselves and by nobody troubled to deny it, that not one weapon and not one man beyond the boat's own crew was ever found in the whole course of that searching, which the Board's returns record without remark and without, so far as the record shows, any change of order following from it.

The petition, when it came to be written, was drawn up at a meeting held on the quay, in the cold, before the boats went out one morning, the six men chosen to carry it agreeing between them on no more than the plainest facts a petition could safely carry: the number of days the searching had run, the number of hours a boat was commonly held, and the plain observation, set down without complaint attached to it because none of the six judged a complaint likely to travel any further than the facts alone, that a fish left too long in a held net spoiled before it ever reached a buyer, spoiled fish being spoiled fish whichever tongue the boat's crew happened to curse it in. It went to Caol mhòr folded inside a barrel of salt cod consigned, quite genuinely, to the Moot's own stores, on the reasoning of the man who carried it that a petition riding inside an honest cargo drew less notice at the searching than a petition riding in a man's coat.

Dùghall heard of it third-hand, from a corporal of the second fleet billeted for a night at Ceann mhòr on his way to somewhere else, who told it as a piece of dry entertainment over the two men's shared supper and nothing graver than that, a fish barrel and a folded letter being, to a man who had spent that autumn searching nets for muskets and finding none, the sort of story a soldier keeps by him for its comedy rather than its weight. Dùghall laughed with him at it that night, and thought little more of it at the time beyond the ordinary soldier's thought that another man's orders were another man's trouble and not his own, a thought he had occasion, before that winter was out, to think a good deal less easily than he thought it that first night.

Farther east again, on the sixteenth of the month, the eighth was raised at Inis uaine on the Abhainn chaol, to stand guard over the dubhan depots of the eastern ports against a burning the council feared more than it ever quite said aloud it feared, the holdouts having, by every report that reached the capital, neither the means nor, so far as anyone could show, the least intention of firing a depot the width of the island from any field they had yet fought on, which did not stop the eighth from being raised and posted regardless, on the reasoning, as sound in its own way as the reasoning behind the searching of Cathair gheal's nets, that a fear a council could not quite prove groundless was a fear it generally chose to arm against rather than argue with.

## IV. Of the Widow's House

The billeting of the company's men among the town's own houses, once the magistrate's house was full past any further stretching, was done by the maor's clerk from a list of the town's rooms to spare, a list she kept, Dùghall came to understand, with the same flat and even hand she gave to every other business of that office, naming which house had a room over its byre or a loft above its kitchen and how many a household could be asked to take without more hardship than the office judged fair to ask of it. Dùghall's own name came down against a house two streets back from the harbour, kept by a widow named Barbara Keir who had a loom set up in the room behind her kitchen and worked it, he came to learn, most of the daylight hours a fishing town's own work left her free for.

She opened the door to him the first evening with no more welcome in her face than the clerk's list had shown in its own writing, looked him over the way a woman looks over a stranger she has been told to house and not asked whether she wanted housed, and showed him the room she had made ready without a word in either tongue, only a hand toward the stair and, once he had climbed it and set down his kit, a plate of bread and salt fish left at the stair's foot without his hearing her come up to bring it. It was, he thought that first night, no worse a billet than a soldier had any right to expect in a town that had not asked for him, and he was three years too well trained in the plainer disciplines of soldiering to expect a hostess's warmth on top of a dry roof and a full plate, and told himself so, and slept.

She answered him readily enough in Dia-thìris, he found within the first week, a civil word for a civil word, the accounts of the billeting-due settled between them without friction on either side, and it was only some evenings later, meaning no more by it than the ordinary courtesy a guest owes a house not his own, that he tried the few words of the humans' tongue he had picked up from men of his own company with kin among the Tuathaich, and got, for his trouble, nothing at all: not so much as a shake of the head, only the same flat and even look she had given him at the door that first evening, as though he had not spoken. He heard her, within the same week, across the yard wall, giving that same tongue easily enough to a neighbour who had called something over about the weather, quick and unhesitating and without the smallest trip in it, so that he came to understand, before he ever properly asked himself why, that the withholding lay in whose ear the words were meant for, and nowhere in her own mouth.

He tried it again, once or twice, in the weeks that followed, telling himself each time that a man ought to be let learn the tongue of a house he ate bread in, and got, each time, the same flat civility that was not quite unkindness and was not, either, anything he could rightly call an answer, until he came, by the third or fourth trying, to understand it for what it was: a door she had shut on him alone, and kept open, plainly and without apparent effort, to every other soul in the town who was not wearing the regiment's own coat.

Why, he did not ask her. The question he turned over instead, those first weeks at Ceann mhòr, the way he turned over most things he had not been given orders about, slowly and without arriving anywhere he was satisfied to call an answer, got him only as far, most nights, as the plain fact that a woman who gave a soldier her own kingdom's own tongue for the plain business of bread and billeting-dues, and would not give him her own people's, had drawn a line somewhere in her own reckoning that was hers to draw and none of his to cross uninvited, whatever the drawing of it cost either of them. He went on eating her bread and sleeping under her roof through the weeks the billeting list kept them so, and she went on answering him in Dia-thìris exactly as far as the house's own business asked and no further, keeping the humans' tongue for the yard wall and the neighbour's voice and every ear in Ceann mhòr but his, and neither of them, in all that time, ever once said to the other what either of them understood the silence to mean.
