# Of the Two Surveyors

*DE 2*

## I. Of the Order and the Northern Road

The order came down to Seumas mac Eachainn in the second week of an t-Sultain, in the drawing-room at Cathair mhòr where the surveyors of Comhairle nam Mèinnean kept their tables, and it came folded twice under the seal of the mine office. He was twenty-four that summer and had been four years a surveyor. He had measured the flooding in the lowest galleries of An Sloc Mòr with a weighted line and a lantern, and had written in his book, as he was told to write, that the water stood at a pause. In all his life he had never been north of the capital.

The order said that it was reported from the north that a fuel was being made without the vein, and burned; that two of its surveyors should go into the Tuathaich country and learn whether it burned as men said; and that they should report under their hands to Comhairle nam Mèinnean at Cathair mhòr and, by a fair copy, to the council's clerk at Cathair dhearg. It was signed by the master of Comhairle nam Mèinnean and countersigned by a clerk of Comhairle na Rìoghachd, and that second name was what made the older surveyors look up from their tables when Seumas read it out.

"The council wants it," said Iain Ruadh mac Nèill. "Then the council can wait for it."

Iain Ruadh was the other surveyor named. He was fifty-seven, and the red that had given him his name had gone the colour of wet sand, except in his eyebrows, which still stood out on his face like two coals in ash. He had been a surveyor since before Seumas was born. In his time he had gone down every open shaft on the mountain and some that were not open, and he held that there was nothing on the island that could be learned about fire that the mountain had not already taught.

"It is pit waste," he said, "cooked in a pot by a man with time on his hands. They have been at it in the north since the humans went. It smokes, it cracks, it goes out. We will ride two days to watch it go out."

They rode for the capital the next morning, because the northern road began there, and lay a night at Cathair dhearg in a lodging-house near the bridge. Seumas's mother lived in the lanes below the bridge-foot on the south bank, and he went to her for an hour after dark. She asked him what the mine office wanted with the Tuathaich, and he said it wanted to know about a fire. She said the lamp-houses were talking of leaving half the streets dark for the winter, and that the lamp-lighter two doors down had not been paid since Lùnastal. He told her the price of coal was not his masters' doing. She said she had not said it was.

In the morning they rode out past Cathair dhomhain and its harbour, with the stubble of the barley on either hand. It was a mild grey day with the wind in the west and rain in it now and again, never enough to wet a man through. The road was the old one, broad, rutted by carts, walled here and there where the farms were Dia-thìrich and the fields were worth a wall. At Ceann gheal they watered the horses and ate bread and cheese standing up. After that the country rose into long bare ridges with the road going over them one by one, and the farms grew smaller and farther apart, and the walls gave way to banks of turf with gorse along them.

Iain Ruadh rode a big bay mare that knew the road better than he did and let her choose her pace. He talked when he wanted to and was silent when he did not, and Seumas learned by the middle of the afternoon that the silences meant nothing. What the old man talked of was the mountain: which galleries had been good in his father's time, which seams had gone thin, how the colour of a flame told a surveyor what a coal was and where it had been cut. Coal of An Sloc Mòr burned with a dark edge to its flame, blue and green and a red like a wound, and a man who had seen it would know it anywhere.

"That is what they cannot make," Iain Ruadh said. "They can make a thing that burns. Any fool can make a thing that burns. They cannot make the edge."

They lay that night at Tobar dhomhain, which was a well, a farm, a smithy and an ale-house, and the ale-house had one room for travellers with a bed in it and a bench. Iain Ruadh took the bed. Seumas lay on the bench under his coat and listened to the wind in the thatch and thought of the order in his saddle-bag and the second name on it.

They crossed the boundary stones in the middle of the next morning.

Seumas had known they were there, as everyone knew. Stones had been set on the north roads in the first year after An Dealachadh, where the Tuathaich country began, and no wall had followed them. He had thought there would be more to see. There were two stones, one on either side of the road, grey, shoulder-high, rough-dressed, with lichen yellow on their northern faces and nothing cut in them at all. A sheep was lying in the lee of the western one. It got up as the horses came near and walked a little way off and lay down again.

Iain Ruadh did not look at the stones. His mare did not slow. Seumas reined in, because it seemed to him that a man ought to, and sat for a breath or two between them, and then felt foolish and rode on to catch up.

"I crossed here the first time in my twenties," the old man said, without turning his head. "I thought there would be someone to ask my business. There never is."

North of the stones the road was the same road. The ridges went on. The farms were no poorer than the farms behind them, though they were built differently, with the house and the byre under one long roof and the doors painted, and the walls whitened with lime. At Àth fhionn a woman was lifting potatoes in a field by the ford, with two children picking behind her into a basket. Seumas called a greeting in Dia-thìris. She straightened and answered him in Dia-thìris, civilly, and when he had ridden past he heard her speak to the children in the humans' tongue, and the elder of the two laughed.

## II. Of the Harbour at Caol mhòr

They came down into Caol mhòr in the evening, with the light going copper over the sea to the west and the strait below them full of it. The town lay along the shore and up the slope behind, grey houses and white ones, and the harbour was crowded with boats come in for the night, their sails brailed up and their masts rocking together. There were three church towers that Seumas could count from the road, square and plain, and as they came down a bell began in one of them and was taken up by the second and then the third, not quite together, so that the sound came down the hill in pieces.

"Evening prayer," said Iain Ruadh. "They keep hours like a druid school."

They found a lodging at an inn on the harbour front whose sign was a painted boat. The landlord was a big soft-spoken man named Harker who spoke to them in Dia-thìris and to his wife in the other tongue, and who gave them a room over the tap with two beds and a window on the water. Seumas asked him, as they sat to their supper, whether he knew anything of a new fuel that was burned in the north.

Harker considered the question. "I know there's talk of one," he said.

"Have you seen it?"

"I've seen a good many things." He took their plates. "There's more fish if you want it."

That was the first answer, and it was the pattern of the rest.

They spent four days at Caol mhòr. They went first to the maor, the Crown's officer for the shire, a thin Dia-thìreach of the south who had held the post eleven years and plainly meant to hold it no longer than he must. He received them in a cold room above the harbour office with a fire of driftwood in the grate. He had heard of the fuel, he said. The Library had written to him about it. He had asked, and been told nothing. He doubted they would fare better; they were welcome to try, and he would give them a letter.

The letter got them into places and got them nothing there. They went to the harbour master, who showed them his book of dues and said there was no line in it for any fuel but coal and turf and wood. They went to a cooper's yard, a smith's, a rope-walk along the shore. On the second day they went to the market, because Iain Ruadh remembered that a pedlar had been said to carry the stuff there. The market was fish and wool and cabbages and a man mending pots; the pedlars had cloth and needles and cheap ribbons and tin whistles; and when Seumas asked after a black cake that burned, a woman selling eggs said that there was a man who had sold such a thing the winter before, and that he had not come this year, and that she did not know his name.

Everywhere it was the same. They were answered civilly and at once, in good Dia-thìris, and the answer was never a lie and never any use. A man asked a question in Dia-thìris would give it back smooth and turned over, like a stone out of the burn. Then, as the surveyors went away, they would hear behind them the other tongue, quick and low, and sometimes a laugh, and sometimes nothing.

"They speak it among themselves to keep us out," Seumas said on the third evening. He had drunk two cups of Harker's ale, which was strong.

"They speak it among themselves because it is theirs," said Iain Ruadh. "They speak ours to us because it is ours. On the whole island there is no better-mannered people. I have been coming here thirty years and I have never once been told a thing I wanted to know."

"You don't think it's there."

"I think a man in the north has cooked some pit waste, and the whole coast is pleased with him for it, and it will be out by the new year." The old man looked into his cup. "I think they are proud of him. That is why they will not show us."

Seumas lay awake that night and listened to the boats knocking in the harbour. He was a Dia-thìreach of the capital and a servant of the mine office, and he had not thought of himself, before this week, as anything a people would keep a thing from. He found he did not like it. Turning over towards the wall, he found also that he did not blame them.

On the fourth morning a boy came to the inn with a message. It was written in a good round hand in Dia-thìris, and it said that if the gentlemen of Comhairle nam Mèinnean wished to see a fire, the mill yard at Doire ghlas would be scouring on the morrow and they might come at the tenth hour. It was signed M. Greaves, manager.

"Who is this?" Seumas asked Harker.

"Martha Greaves," said Harker. "She has the big mill at Doire ghlas, the one Tional nan Tuathach built."

"How does she know we are here?"

Harker looked at him with a patient kindness that Seumas remembered for a long time afterwards. "Sir," he said, "everyone knows you are here."

## III. Of the Mill Yard at Doire ghlas

Doire ghlas lay a little under ten miles east of Caol mhòr, on a road that ran inland from the strait through small fields and then up among low hills with oak woods on them. The oaks were old and grey-barked and bent by the west wind, and the road went through the edge of them for a mile before it came out above the village. Seumas had heard it called a village and had expected a village. It was as big as Caol mhòr, and seemed bigger, because it was built close about a great grove of the grey oaks in the middle of it, with the houses and chapels and the mills set around the grove like men round a fire, and nothing at all built under the trees.

"They will tell you it is a village," Iain Ruadh said. "Let them."

The mills stood at the eastern edge, where a stream came down off the hill and had been led in a stone race along the backs of them. There were three, long stone buildings with slate roofs, and the noise of their looms came out of their windows and filled the road. Carts of fleece stood in the lane. A smell of wet wool and lanolin and something sourer hung over everything, and under it, when the wind turned, Seumas caught another smell that he did not know.

Martha Greaves met them at the yard gate herself. She was a square, grey woman in her fifties in a dark stuff gown with the sleeves pinned back and an apron over it, and she had ink on her right hand and a pencil through the knot of her hair. She greeted them in Dia-thìris, gave them each her hand, asked after their journey and their lodging and the health of the maor, and said that she had kept them no more than a quarter-hour, which was true.

"You'll want to see the scouring," she said. "Come this way. Mind the channel, it's hot."

The scouring-house was a lean-to along the yard wall, open on the yard side, with three great coppers set into a brick range. The raw fleece came in at one end, was boiled in the first copper with soda and stale urine, lifted steaming on forks into the second and the third, and went out at the other end grey and wet and clean to the drying lofts. Four women worked the forks, with their skirts kilted and their arms red to the elbow. The steam rolled up under the roof and out across the yard.

Under the coppers were the fires.

Seumas went down on one knee on the wet brick to look at the nearest, and Iain Ruadh, after a moment, lowered himself stiffly beside him. The fire-door was open. The fire inside was low and steady and very hot; Seumas felt it on his face at arm's length. It burned yellow, a heavy smoky yellow with red at the root, and the smoke went up the flue fat and brown and came down again in the yard as a smell. It was a smell of tar and bad eggs and something like singed hoof, and it got into the back of the throat and stayed there.

There was no edge on the flame. He looked for it, as he had been taught to look. There was none of the blue, none of the green, none of the dark red. It was a plain flame.

"How long has it been burning?" Iain Ruadh asked.

"Since the fifth hour," said Martha Greaves. "It will burn till the fourth hour of the afternoon, when we draw the fires. The girl banks it at noon with two more cakes."

"Two."

"Two."

Iain Ruadh said nothing more. Seumas knew what he was thinking, because he was thinking it himself. A fire of the mountain's coal that would hold three coppers at the boil from the fifth hour to noon would have eaten a scuttle an hour. He looked round the yard for a coal-heap and did not see one. Against the wall of the scouring-house, under a sheet of tarred canvas, there was a low stack of something dark.

"May I?" he said.

Martha Greaves lifted a corner of the canvas for him.

The cakes were about the size of a small loaf, flattened, and blacker than any coal he had seen, a dull black that took no light. They had been pressed in some kind of mould; there was a ridge round the edge of each where the mould had joined. He picked one up. It was lighter than coal of its size and a little greasy, and it left a black bloom on his fingers that would not rub off on his trousers. He smelled it. It smelled of the fire, fainter.

"Where is it made?" he asked.

"In the north," said Martha Greaves.

"By whom?"

"By the man who makes it." She let the canvas fall. "You may take that one with you. It will tell you more than I can."

"Is it sold?"

"We buy it."

"From whom?"

She looked at him. She had grey eyes and a broad plain face that had been handsome, and was still good to look at, and there was nothing in it at all that he could read. "From the man who sells it to us," she said. "Will you take some tea, gentlemen? The kettle is on in the counting-house."

They took the tea. The counting-house was a small room at the corner of the yard, with a desk and two ledgers and a clock that ticked loudly and a window on the grove, and there was a fire in the grate there too, of peat. Martha Greaves poured for them and sat down at her desk and talked to them for half an hour about the price of fleece, and the rule of Tional nan Tuathach for its mills, and the carts on the long road to the fair at Muileann òg, and the weather, and the harvest, and the health of the Tuathaich minister of Doire ghlas, who was ninety and had baptised her. She spoke of the fuel only when they asked, and then she told them what they could see for themselves: that it had heated the coppers since the spring, that it cost less than coal, that the women disliked the smell and had stopped mentioning it.

When Iain Ruadh asked her directly how many mills of the north were burning it, she said she could answer only for her own mill.

When Seumas asked her, at the last, whether the surveyors might be let see the place where it was made, she put down her cup.

"Mr mac Eachainn," she said. She said his name the Tuathaich way, with the stress in the wrong place, and he did not think she did it by accident. "You have seen it burn. You have held it in your hand. Take it home and burn it on your own hearth. What else is there that your masters would want to know?"

He did not answer. He found he did not know what to answer. She waited a civil while, and then she stood up, and so they stood up too.

She walked them to the gate. On the way across the yard they passed the scouring-house again, and the women at the forks did not look round. At the gate Martha Greaves gave them each her hand again, and wished them a good road, and said that she hoped they would find Caol mhòr comfortable, and that Harker kept a fair house. Then she went back into the yard, and the gate was shut behind her, and the noise of the looms closed over everything.

They rode back towards the strait in the long light of the afternoon. The cake was in Seumas's saddle-bag wrapped in a page of the maor's letter, and every time the wind came round behind them he could smell it.

"Well," said Iain Ruadh at last. "It burns."

## IV. Of the Three Clauses

On {{date:VI-0003}} the two surveyors sat down in the room over Harker's tap to write their report, with the window open on the harbour and a jug of ale between them and the cake from Doire ghlas on a plate on the sill.

They had burned half of it the night before in the grate of that room, with Harker's leave and to his wife's great displeasure. It had taken a long time to catch. Once caught it had burned for three hours and a half, low and hot and yellow, while they sat on their beds with their coats on and the window open and timed it by Iain Ruadh's watch. The smell had gone down the stairs and into the tap, where a fisherman had made a remark in the humans' tongue that set the whole room laughing. In the morning there was a little grey ash in the grate, less than Seumas had thought, and a black bloom on the ceiling above it the size of a man's two hands.

The order required that they report under their hands. Iain Ruadh, as the elder, had the right of the first draft, and he took it. He wrote for most of an hour in a small, square, old-fashioned hand, and then pushed the paper across.

Seumas read it twice.

It was a good report as reports of the mine office went. It set out the order and the road and the dates. The names of those they had called on at Caol mhòr were there, and what each had said. It described the mill yard at Doire ghlas and the coppers and the fire under them. It gave the hours of burning and the size of the cake and the weight of the ash. And it ended with a paragraph which said that the surveyors had not been shown the making of the fuel, nor the place of its making, nor told the name of the maker, nor the quantity made; that its nature and its lasting could not be judged by one mill and one cake; and that it must be accounted unproven.

"It is true," Iain Ruadh said, watching him. "Every word."

"It is true," Seumas agreed.

"Then sign it."

Seumas did not sign it. He sat with the paper in front of him and looked out of the window at the boats. A man was baiting lines on the deck of the nearest, and a gull stood on the rail beside him and waited. After a while Seumas said, "We were not sent to learn how it is made."

"We were sent to learn whether it burns as men say."

"And it does."

"We saw one fire, boy. In one yard. Shown us by a woman who told us nothing and meant to."

"We saw three fires. We burned one ourselves." Seumas put his finger on the last paragraph. "If this goes to Cathair mhòr, they will read the last word and no other. Unproven. They will file it and forget it, and next year someone will ask again, and someone will ride up here again, and be given tea."

Iain Ruadh was silent a long time. The gull on the rail stretched its wings and settled them.

"And what would you write?"

Seumas took a clean sheet. He knew the words already; he had been carrying them since the scouring-house, when he knelt on the wet brick and looked for the edge of the flame and did not find it. He wrote them in Dia-thìris, in the plain hand the mine office taught its clerks, below the heading and the order and the names:

> *Loiscidh e, tha fàileadh dheth, agus chan e gual a th' ann.*
> It burns; it stinks; and it is not coal.

Then he copied Iain Ruadh's account of the road and the town and the yard and the hours below it, word for word, and left out the last paragraph.

Iain Ruadh read it through. He read it a second time. He picked up his cup and found it was empty and put it down.

"They will think we were drunk," he said.

"They will read it."

"They will read the first line," said the old man, "and no other." But he took the pen. He signed first, as the elder, in his small square hand, and Seumas signed under him. Then they made the fair copy for the council's clerk, and signed that too, and Iain Ruadh folded both and sealed them.

When it was done he took from his saddle-bag the little tin that every surveyor of the mountain carries, with a thumb of coal from An Sloc Mòr in it for the matching of samples, and laid the coal on the last embers of the cake in the grate. It lay there a while and then caught. The flame came up round it thin and bright, and along its edge ran the colours of the mountain, blue and green and a red like a wound, so that for the time it burned the whole dull hearth had a rim of light on it.

"There," the old man said, and watched it until it was ash. "That is what I mean by an edge."

## V. Of the Unlit Lamps

They rode south two days later and came to Cathair dhearg on the evening of the second day, with rain. Iain Ruadh went on alone the next morning by the road east to Cathair mhòr, with the copy for Cathair mhòr in his saddle-bag, and Seumas stayed in the capital to put the fair copy into the hands of the council's clerk, as the order said, and to wait upon the council in case it wished to ask him anything.

He put it into the clerk's hands the same day, in a small panelled room off the passage below the council chamber, and the clerk, a courteous young man with a cold, gave him a receipt for it and wrote his lodging in a book. Then Seumas waited.

He waited nearly the whole of an Dàmhair. He lived at his mother's house in the lanes below the bridge-foot, and walked up every second morning to the panelled room to ask whether he was wanted, and every second morning the courteous young man, whose cold got better and then worse again, told him that he was not wanted yet. In between he walked the town. He walked out to the quays of Ros dhomhain and watched the coal-boats that had once come in loaded going out light. He walked round the precinct walls and past the temple of Macha, where the two horses of the rite were led out in the afternoons to graze the temple green. Walking was all there was to do with the days, and in the evenings he sat by his mother's fire, which was of turf and driftwood now, and told her about the grey oaks at Doire ghlas and the three bells at Caol mhòr, and did not tell her about the cake.

On {{date:VI-0004}} there came a letter for him from Cathair mhòr, in Iain Ruadh's small square hand.

It was short. It said that the old man had come safe to Cathair mhòr and delivered the report, and that the master had read it at his table in Iain Ruadh's presence and passed it to the chief clerk, and that the chief clerk had filed it in the press among the surveys of that year. There had been no minute made of it. There had been no question asked. It said that Iain Ruadh had been sent the week after to measure the water in the lower galleries again, and that it stood where it stood, and that he had written that it was at a pause. It ended: *They read the first line. I told you so. I. R.*

Seumas read it by the window of the panelled room, where the clerk had given it to him with his daily word that the council did not want him yet. When he had finished he folded it and put it inside his coat. The clerk asked, out of courtesy, whether it was bad news. Seumas said it was not news at all.

As he came down the steps into the lane there was a woman on the lowest step with three children beside her, the eldest a girl of ten or eleven holding the youngest on her hip. The woman had a paper in her hand. Seumas knew her; it was the wife of the lamp-lighter two doors from his mother's, and the paper was a petition, written out for her by the scribe at the bridge-foot for a copper, and it asked that her husband, who had lit the lamps of the south bank for nineteen years, be given other work of the town's, any work, since the lamps he lit were to be lit no longer. A porter of the council house was telling her, not unkindly, that the petitions of the lamp-houses went to the lamp-houses and not to the council, and that the lamp-houses had already said no.

"They sent me here," the woman said.

"Then they were wrong to," said the porter.

Seumas stood on the step above them. He thought of the receipt in the clerk's book, with his lodging written beside it. He had nothing in his hand to give her but Iain Ruadh's letter, and it was no use to anybody. After a while the woman put the petition away in her shawl and took the youngest child from the girl and went off down the lane towards the bridge, and the other two followed her.

He walked home late, after dark, the long way, because he did not want to go in.

The lamp-houses had made their order that day. It was pinned up at the bridge-foot, and the watch had it by heart: the lamps of the main streets, the precinct, the bridge and the quays to be lit as before; the lamps of the lanes on the south bank, and of the streets behind the tannery and the fish-market, and of the lower town along the river, to be left unlit until the price of coal should fall. Seumas had seen the lamps of his mother's lane burning every night of his life when he was in the capital. He walked down the lane now and counted them, because he was a surveyor and it was the thing he knew how to do.

There were eleven iron posts in his mother's lane, and eleven dark heads on them, with the rain running off the glass. There were nine in the next lane, and fourteen in the one after, and six in the steep little lane that went down to the water-stairs, where he nearly fell. He counted them in his head and then, when the numbers grew too many, in the notebook he carried for the galleries, writing by feel in the dark: lane, lamps, lane, lamps. Behind the fish-market there were thirty-one. Along the river road there were fifty-two, and the river beside them was black and loud with the rain.

From the river road he could see the bridge. It was lit from end to end, as the order said, twenty lamps in two rows, and each of them had a coal flame in it, and each flame had the dark edge of the mountain's coal round it, blue and green and a red like a wound, so that the whole bridge hung over the black water with a coloured light on it. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and it was burning coal that the poorer streets of the capital could not have.

He stood on the river road and looked at it for a long time.

While he stood there he heard a shout from the far end of the bridge, and then running feet, and then the rattle of the watch. A man came off the bridge towards the south bank at a run, out of the light and into the dark of the lanes, and was gone. Two of the watch came after him as far as the bridge-foot and stopped there, where the lamps stopped, and one of them spoke a word to the other and they went back. A little later Seumas passed them at the bridge-foot, under the last lit lamp, where one of the watch had his book open against the post and was writing in it by the coloured light. A carter coming home had been knocked down on the bridge and his purse taken, and the thief had gone into the south-bank lanes, where there was no light to follow him by. The watchman wrote that down, and the hour, and put the book away.

Seumas went on into the dark. At his mother's door he took out his notebook and, by the light of her window, added up the lamps he had counted. It came to a hundred and forty-three. He wrote the figure under the column and drew a line beneath it, as he would have done for any measure of the mine office's, and then he stood for a while in the rain with the book open in his hand, with no one to send it to.
