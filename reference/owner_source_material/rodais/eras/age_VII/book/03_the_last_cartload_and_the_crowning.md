# Of the Last Cartload and the Crowning

*DE 3*

## I. Of the Last Cartload

Cùirt na Fèille sat in the upper room of the toll-house at Muileann òg, over the arch where the fair road came in, and in winter the room smelled of wet wool and lamp-soot and the river. The court kept the fair's measures and heard the fair's debts, and on market days through the year it kept the town's as well, because there was no other court in Muileann òg and nobody had seen a reason to make one. Its clerk in those years was Ùna nic Ailein. She was not yet thirty. She had come to the desk at seventeen to cut quills for old Seonaidh Crùbach, and when his eyes failed she had read the day-book aloud to him every evening until he died, and after that the justice had given her the key of the press without asking the council's leave, which was how most things at Muileann òg were done.

On {{date:VI-0005}} it had rained since before light, a thin rain driven sideways off the plain. The market was small, as it always was in am Faoilleach: a few carts of turnips, two drovers with lean beasts, a woman selling eels from a tub. Ùna had the window open a hand's breadth for the light and kept her ink-horn under her palm against the wind.

A little before noon the coal cart came in under the arch.

She heard it before she saw it. Every child in the town knew the sound of Uilleam mac Dhùghaill's cart, because the near wheel had a split felloe bound with iron and it rang on the setts twice to every turn. He had carried coal down Rathad na Fèille from the yards at Cathair dhearg for thirty-one years. His father had carried it before him, when the loads came to the fair from the wagons of Comhairle nam Mèinnean and a cart went back and forth four times in a market week. In Ùna's first years at the desk Uilleam had still come every market day. Then it was every other. In the autumn before last, when Comhairle nam Mèinnean cut its sales and the price at the fair doubled, the court had fixed a ceiling, and Uilleam had sold under it twice and then stopped coming at all until the court looked away.

He stopped under her window. The load was covered in sacking, black with rain. He climbed down slowly, one hand on the shaft, and looked up at her.

"Is he in?" he said.

"He is in."

"Then I'll sell it whole, and I'll sell it here, and you can write it."

Fionnlagh mac Gille-Chaluim, justice of the fair court, came down to the arch in his old brown cloak and stood in the rain to see it done, as the rules asked. He was past seventy and had kept the court for twenty years. He lifted a corner of the sacking and looked at the coal underneath: small stuff, most of it, the size of a hen's egg and smaller, and some slack at the bottom that would be lost in the grate. There was no great lump in it anywhere. He dropped the sacking back.

"Whose yard?"

"The capital's. The last of what they'd let me have."

"Price?"

Uilleam told him. Ùna, above them, wrote it down before the justice answered, because she knew he would not argue. It was more than a third over the ceiling that was still painted on the board by the door.

The buyer was Màiri nic Leòid, who kept the brew-house by the ford and had a copper to boil. She had come up the street under a sack of her own with her son behind her pushing a barrow, and she counted the coin out on the step of the toll-house with the rain running off her hood onto the stone. When the boy had shovelled half the load into the barrow he took it off and came back for the other half, and Uilleam stood by his horse and did not help him. The horse was old too. Its breath smoked.

When the cart was empty Uilleam came up the stair and stood dripping in the doorway of the court-room with his hat in his hands. He had never come up before. The carters paid their dues at the window below.

"Put it in," he said. "The whole load, and to her, and the price. And put it that it was the last."

"I'll write what was sold," Ùna said. "How do I know it was the last?"

"Because I'm telling you. The yard won't sell by the cart any more. They'll sell by the sack at the gate, to anyone who walks up, and dear, and a man with a cart can't make a living of sacks." He turned his hat round once. "Put it that I said so."

She wrote the sale in its line, as every sale of coal-fuel had been written in that book since the fair was first held: the day, the seller, the buyer, the load, the price, the justice's mark. Then, in the margin, in smaller letters, she wrote: *The carter says this is the last load sold at this court by the cartload. The yard at Cathair dhearg sells now by the sack.* She turned the book round so that he could see it. He could not read. He looked at the marks a long while all the same.

"That's it, then," he said, and went down.

The justice came up after him and read the margin with his finger. He did not tell her to strike it. He went to the door, and took his knife, and scraped at the painted ceiling on the board until the figures were a grey smear.

"No sense keeping a price nobody sells at," he said. "Write that too."

She wrote that too. Afterwards, when the market had packed up and the lamps were lit, she turned back through the book to see how many cartloads there had been. In the first years of the fair they filled a page a day. Ten years back they filled a page a week. She found Uilleam's name on the last seven, one under another, like a man going down a stair.

Uilleam mac Dhùghaill sold his cart that spring to a man who carried grain from Muileann ghlas, and kept the horse, and did not come to the fair again that anyone at the court remembered.

## II. Of the Sack and the Lump

The day-book of the fair court for that year is in an Leabharlann now, and a reader who turns its pages can watch the coal go out of it.

In am Faoilleach there is the cartload. Through an Gearran and am Màrt there is no coal at all; the town bought what it bought at the capital, those who could get there, and those who could not burned turf and furze and cold wood from the hedges, and the court was asked twice to settle quarrels over hedges. In an Giblean a man from Cnoc leathan came with eleven sacks on a pack-horse and sold them one at a time from the step of the toll-house, which the court allowed, since the rules said nothing against it, and Ùna wrote the eleven sales in eleven lines. After that the sacks came irregularly, three or four in a market day, carried by whoever had got them. The price in the book goes up by a little every month. Nobody in the book complains of it. The court had already given up the ceiling, and there was nobody to complain to.

What the book does not show is the town. Ùna walked home every evening through the lanes below the toll-house, where the weavers' cottages stood in two rows back to back, and she saw the chimneys smoking less. The women took their bread to the baker's oven instead of their own. Old men sat in the brew-house late because Màiri nic Leòid kept the copper going and the room was warm, and some of them paid for one cup in an evening and made it last till she put them out. In the rows where there were children the doors were kept shut by day, which nobody in Muileann òg had done in Ùna's lifetime. The spring was mild and wet. People said it was lucky, and it was.

On Bealltainn a boy came into the court from one of the small houses of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail in the hills to the west. He was a custodian's boy, in a grey tunic, and he had walked since before dawn. He had money in a knotted cloth and asked, with great care for his manners, for a piece of coal.

"How much?" Ùna said.

"A piece. For the altar. For tonight." He held his hands apart to show her, about the size of a loaf. "Our custodian says the fire of Bealltainn must be lit from a coal of the vein, and we have none left in the house, and the pits send no more to houses of our size."

There was no coal in the market that day. Ùna sent down to the man from Cnoc leathan, who had one sack left over from the week before and had kept it back to burn at home; he came up grumbling and opened it on the floor of the court-room, and the justice turned the pieces over with his foot until he found one big enough, a black lump with a seam of colour in its broken face that took the lamplight and showed a flash of blue and red when he turned it. It was the kind the old people called a good coal, the kind that had once come by the wagon.

The boy paid. It was more than a cart-horse cost at Lùnastal the year before. He wrapped the lump in the cloth the money had come in, and put it inside his tunic against his chest, and went off down the west road at a trot.

Ùna wrote the sale. Then she sat with the pen in her hand. The rules of the court said that coal-fuel was entered by the load, the sack or the basket, and this was none of them. She wrote: *One lump, for an altar.* It is the first such entry in the book. There were four more before the end of that year, and in each the buyer gives the name of a house of Deas-ghnàth a' Ghuail, and in each the justice picked out the piece himself.

"They'll be buying it by the lump for the altars when there's none to be had for the grate," he said to her that evening, when she read him the day's lines. "I've seen that coming for ten years. It's a strange thing to see it come."

She asked him what else he had seen coming.

"Nothing I'd put in a book," he said.

## III. Of the Crowning at Cathair dhearg

In the summer word went round the fair road that Mairead's reign had ended, and that there would be a crowning at Lùnastal, the first in fifteen years. The court shut for the day of the feast by the justice's order. Half the town went up to the capital to see it, and Ùna went with them.

They went up the day before in the carrier's wagon, twelve to a bench, and slept in the carrier's loft in the lane behind the tannery, and were in the streets before light. The fair road was full all the way up. There were families walking with their children on their shoulders; there were two priests of Òrd Mhacha from the house at Cnoc òg with a bundle of green boughs; there were drovers with no beasts, and a man selling cold mutton pies from a board hung round his neck, who had sold out before they reached the bridge. The long light of the day before the feast lay yellow across the barley. From the brow of the last hill they could see the smoke of Cathair dhearg over the river, less of it than Ùna remembered.

What follows is told as she wrote it that night in a letter to the justice, who had stayed at home. The letter was kept among the court's papers and came to an Leabharlann with the day-books.

"We stood nearly five hours," she wrote, "in the street below the temple, the one that goes up from the bridge, and I could see the temple steps between a woman's hat and a lamp-post. Nobody sat. There were soldiers of the palace in the street to hold a lane open, and they were as tired as we were by noon, and gave the children water from their own flasks.

"The regalia came out first. Òrd Mhacha keeps them, as you know, and we saw how little anyone else has handled them, for the priests carried them down the steps as if they were carrying a sleeper. There was a circlet, and a mantle of red wool that trailed on the stone, and something in a box that I could not see. People said different things about what was in the box, and I will not write them.

"Then the horses. I had not thought there would be real horses. A priest led out a grey mare and another a black horse, from the stable on the south side of the temple yard, and they were brought to the foot of the steps and stood there, one on each side. The grey was restless and the black was not. People round me were saying their names, Liath Macha and Dubh Sainglenn, and a woman behind me told her son they were not the same horses as at the last crowning, only the same names, and he asked how she knew, and she said her mother had told her the grey was the restless one then too.

"Cathal came down between the horses. I was too far off to see his face, only that he walked slowly. The priests set the mantle on him at the foot of the steps and the circlet on him at the top, and between the two the grey mare put her head down and pulled at her rope and the priest had to lean on it. Nobody laughed. I think some wanted to.

"He did not speak. I had expected him to speak. The priests spoke, and the chief of them read the old words of the rite, which I could not hear for the crowd, and then turned to the street and said in a loud voice that the kingdom had its king, and the street shouted, and the black horse threw up its head at the noise. Then Cathal went into the temple and the doors were shut, and that was all we saw of him. People stood a long time after, as though something more would happen. It did not. By and by they began to go home.

"I stood beside a man at the lamp-post the whole afternoon. He told me, when we had stood there long enough to talk, that he cut dies at the mint, and that he would be cutting the new king's dies before the month was out. His name is Gille-Easbaig Bàn. His hands are black at the nails, which he says is the ink and no washing takes it off. I asked him was it hard to cut a king's face when you had only seen it at a distance from behind a lamp-post, and he said the face is the easy part, the council sends a drawing; the hard part is the seal.

"He told me the seal of every coin is pressed with an ink ground from coal dust, as it has been since Fearchar Bonn struck the first coin of the kingdom, and every die-cutter since has been a pupil of Fearchar Bonn's workshop, and he is the first who is not. He learned his trade at the silver-smiths' in the eastern hills. So he must make the ink himself as Fearchar Bonn's men made it, from a recipe on a card that hangs by the grinding-stone, and the recipe says it must be good coal of the vein, with the colour in it, ground fine and bound with gum. He said the mint sent to Comhairle nam Mèinnean for coal of that sort and got back a price. He said the price for the little pot of ink the new dies want is as much as the mint pays him in a year. I asked him was that true or only a way of speaking. He said he had worked it out on the back of their letter, and it was true.

"I asked him why they did not use some other ink, since the coin would be as good. He looked at me as though I had asked why they did not crown the king in a barn. Then he said, 'Because a clipped coin has to show the colour at its edge. That's how you know it's the kingdom's.' I said that if coal went on the way it was going there would soon be no colour to show. He said that was the council's business and not a die-cutter's, and he would cut what he was told. Then the woman with the hat moved, and I could see the steps better, and we did not talk any more.

"Walking back to the loft at dusk I went by the streets below the tannery, where the lamps have been left dark since the autumn before last. The lamp-posts are there still, with the glass in them. Someone had hung a green bough from Lùnastal on one. It was too dark to see what was on the others. We go home in the wagon tomorrow. I have written it all down so that you will know it as well as if you had stood there, which I think is better than standing, for my feet are raw."

She sent the letter by a drover going south that night and came home herself the day after. The justice had it read to him twice.

## IV. Of the New Dies and the Ruled Line

The mint's day-book for that month is short. It says that on {{date:VI-0007}} Gille-Easbaig Bàn delivered the dies for the coinage of the reign, face and seal, and that they were proved and found good. The coal ink for the seal was ground in the mint from coal bought of Comhairle nam Mèinnean, and the clerk set down the price, which is what Gille-Easbaig had told Ùna at the lamp-post. The first coins struck from the new dies showed the colour at the edge. No one else is named. In all that book it is the first entry in which the die-cutter is not called a pupil of anyone.

The new coin came down Rathad na Fèille in the purses of carriers and drovers through an t-Sultain, and by the time Fèill Muileann òg opened in an Dàmhair half the coin that crossed Ùna's table had Cathal's head on it. She held one of the first up to the window. It was bright, and a little thinner than the old, or she thought so; and when she turned it on its edge the thin dark rim showed where the seal-ink had been pressed, and when she tilted it in the light there was a thread of colour in the black, very small, blue going to red.

The fair ran eleven days that year. It was thinner than any year Ùna had kept its books. The coal-sellers' row, which in the old days had run the whole length of the lower field from the ford to the elm with its wagons in a line and its men black to the elbow, had four booths standing in it, and two of those sold turf. Cnoc leathan let fewer beds. The fire-watch walked the stalls every night as it had done since the burning, with buckets and a bell, and found little enough to watch; the booths were dark by nine for want of fuel to keep a brazier. But the cattle came, and the salt, and the cloth, and the court sat from first light every day in the upper room with its window open onto the noise, and Ùna wrote until her hand ached and the justice slept upright in his chair by the stove after noon and woke when anyone raised a voice.

On the northern day the Tuathaich goods came in.

It had been so since Daniel Frayne's first year in the speaker's chair of Tional nan Tuathach: one day of the fair given to goods of the north, sold through Dia-thìreach factors under the makers' marks. The factors stood at their booths along the upper field with the bales behind them, and the Tuathaich who had brought the bales down stood behind the factors and did not speak unless they were spoken to. There was grey twill from Doire ghlas with the white thread at the selvedge, and heavy cloth from Baile chiar, and knitted things in baskets, and fish salted in kegs from the northern coast. The buyers from the south walked the booths and fingered the cloth and asked the factors the price. The factors turned round and asked the makers, in the northern tongue, and then turned back and said the price in Dia-thìris, a little higher. Ùna had watched it done for ten years. It went quickly when it went well.

A little after noon the factor Coinneach Liath came up the stair of the toll-house with a man behind him.

The man was a Tuathach. He was of middle age, square and brown, in a jacket of Doire ghlas twill gone thin at the elbows, and he carried a creel on his back with a sacking cover lashed down over it. He stood in the doorway. Coinneach Liath came to the table, and leaned on it, and said that he had a sale to enter and did not know how to enter it.

"What goods?" said Ùna.

"Fuel."

"Coal-fuel goes in by the sack or the basket. Or the lump." She turned the book to the page. "Which?"

"It's not coal."

The justice woke. He looked at the factor, and then past him at the man in the doorway, and said, "Let's see it."

The man set the creel down on the floor and unlashed the cover. Inside, packed close like peats, were cakes of something black: flat and squarish, a little bigger than a man's hand, rough on the faces, with a dull shine where they had rubbed on each other. The man took one out and held it up. Ùna saw that his palm was grey with the dust of it.

"What is it?" said the justice.

The man answered him in Dia-thìris, slowly and correctly, the way northern people spoke it at the fair when they wanted no mistake. "It's made from the waste. What the pits throw out. It's burned in the north for a year and more."

"Made by whom?"

"Made in the north."

"Does it burn?"

The man looked at the stove. The justice nodded, and Ùna got up and opened the stove door, and the man crouched and put the cake in on top of the embers of the turf, and they waited. For a while nothing happened. Then the edges of the cake began to glow, a dull red with no flame, and a thick yellowish smoke curled out of it and up the flue, and some of it came back into the room. It had a smell that Ùna had never smelled in her life: sour, oily, like a tannery and a pitch-pot together. Coinneach Liath put his sleeve over his nose. The justice did not. He held his hands out to the stove door and kept them there.

"It's hot," he said, after a while.

"It's hot," the man agreed. "It lasts."

"And it stinks."

"It does."

The justice sat back. "Well," he said to the room, "it's not coal. What's it called?"

The man shrugged. "In the north the women call it the black cake. Some call it after the man who first made it." He looked at Ùna's pen. "Black cake will do."

The factor named his price for the creel, which the man had told him in the northern tongue on the stair. A buyer was waiting at the foot of the steps, a woman of Muileann ghlas with a bakehouse, who had watched the smoke come out of the court-room window and come up to see what it was. She bought the creel whole. She paid in the new coin, and Ùna saw the dark rim on the pieces as they went across the table into the factor's hand, and from the factor's hand, when the factor had taken his part, into the Tuathach's. He looked at them no longer than it took to count them. He put them in his jacket.

Then there was the entry to make.

The day-book of the fair court is ruled by hand at the start of each year, one page for each kind of goods the fair has sold before, and the lines run across from the day to the price, and at the head of each page the kind of goods is written in the clerk's best hand. Ùna turned through them: grain, cattle, sheep, horses, wool, cloth, salt, fish, timber, stone, iron, coal-fuel. She went back to coal-fuel and looked at it: the cartload in am Faoilleach, the sacks, the five lumps for altars, and then nothing since the start of the fair. The justice watched her. So did the factor, and the Tuathach in the doorway, and the baker's wife of Muileann ghlas with her creel, who wanted to be gone.

"Put it under coal," said Coinneach Liath. "It's the same trade."

"It's not coal," said the justice. "We've just said so. And the man says it isn't. And I'll not have the clerks of the pits reading my book and finding their coal sold by the creel at half their price, and coming down here to tell me I've made a fool of their ledger."

"Then put it under sundries."

Ùna did not answer him. She took the ruler out of the drawer, and the lead, and turned to the back of the book, where there were clean leaves kept for the next year. She ruled a page as the pages at the front were ruled, across and down, and at the head of it she wrote *black cake*, and under that, smaller, *of the north*. Then she dipped the pen and wrote the sale in its first line: the day, the factor as seller, the maker not named, the buyer of Muileann ghlas, one creel, the price, and a space for the justice's mark.

She turned the book so that the justice could make his mark. He looked at the page a long time. The black cake in the stove had settled and was burning low and red, and the smell of it lay in the room.

"There's only the one line on that page," he said.

"There's only been the one sale."

He made his mark. The Tuathach picked up his empty creel and put the straps over his shoulders. At the door he stopped and looked back at the page from where he stood, though he was too far off to read it, and then he went down the stair into the noise of the northern day, and Ùna did not see him again at that fair. She had not asked his name. The rules of the northern day did not ask for it.

When the fair was over and the booths were down and the field was mud, she went back through the book to reckon the year, as she did every year in the last week of an Dàmhair, with the lamp lit early. The page of coal-fuel held the cartload, a run of sacks, and five lumps for altars. The page she had ruled at the back held one line. She sat and looked at both for a while in the lamplight. Then she wrote at the foot of the year, under the totals, in the place where the clerks of the fair court had always written the year's weather and the state of the roads: *Coal sold this year by the cart, by the sack, by the lump. A new fuel sold once, from the north.* She blotted it, and shut the press, and turned the key, and put out the lamp.
