# Dia-thìris texts

Here are four conversations and seven tales as they are spoken and told on Dia-thìr, each set line by line beside its English. The first three tales are old, and are told up and down the coasts and the glens; the last four are the island's own legends: of the Keeper's riddle, of the Old Ones, of the coal-blood and of the Tuathaich. A short note under each title says what part of the grammar it shows.

## Aig a' Chala — *At the Harbour*

Conversation. Polite 'sibh' greetings, 'Is mise...' (copula) for giving a name, the perfect with 'air' + verbal noun (tha mi air tighinn), and the dependent past after the negative (chan fhaca mi). Also the feminine genitive after a compound preposition: ri taobh na h-eaglaise.

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Eilidh: Dia dhuibh! Ciamar a tha sibh? | Eilidh: Hello! How are you? |
| Tormod: Tha gu math, gu robh math agaibh. Ciamar a tha sibh fhèin? | Tormod: Well, thank you. How are you yourself? |
| Eilidh: Tha mi gu dòigheil. Is mise Eilidh. Dè an t-ainm a th' oirbh? | Eilidh: I'm fine. I'm Eilidh. What's your name? |
| Tormod: Is mise Tormod. Tha mi air tighinn à Cathair dhearg air a' bhàta. | Tormod: I'm Tormod. I have come from Cathair dhearg on the boat. |
| Eilidh: Fàilte gu Seann Dunn! An e seo a' chiad turas agaibh an-seo? | Eilidh: Welcome to Seann Dunn! Is this your first time here? |
| Tormod: 'S e. Chan fhaca mi a-riamh cala cho brèagha. | Tormod: It is. I have never seen such a beautiful harbour. |
| Eilidh: Tha an cala beag, ach tha na h-iascairean còir. Càite a bheil sibh a' fuireach? | Eilidh: The harbour is small, but the fishermen are kind. Where are you staying? |
| Tormod: Tha rùm agam anns an taigh-òsta ri taobh na h-eaglaise. | Tormod: I have a room in the inn beside the church. |
| Eilidh: Tha fios agam air. Thigibh còmhla rium, agus seallaidh mi dhuibh an rathad. | Eilidh: I know it. Come with me, and I'll show you the way. |
| Tormod: Gu robh math agaibh, Eilidh! | Tormod: Thank you, Eilidh! |

## Aig a' Mhargadh — *At the Market*

Conversation. The object of a progressive in the genitive (a' sireadh arain agus èisc, from aran and iasc), the future for an offer or decision (gabhaidh mi), the passive with 'chaidh' (chaidh a dhèanamh), 'is toil leam', and 'dà' + singular noun.

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Calum: Madainn mhath! Dè a tha sibh ag iarraidh an-diugh? | Calum: Good morning! What would you like today? |
| Anna: Madainn mhath. Tha mi a' sireadh arain agus èisc. | Anna: Good morning. I'm looking for bread and fish. |
| Calum: Tha aran ùr agam. Chaidh a dhèanamh sa mhadainn. | Calum: I have fresh bread. It was made this morning. |
| Anna: Gabhaidh mi dà lof, ma-thà. Dè a' phrìs a th' orra? | Anna: I'll take two loaves, then. What's the price of them? |
| Calum: Trì buinn airgid. Agus dè mun iasc? Tha scadan agus bradan agam an-diugh. | Calum: Three silver coins. And what about the fish? I have herring and salmon today. |
| Anna: Is toil leam scadan. Gabhaidh mi dà scadan, mas e ur toil e. | Anna: I like herring. I'll take two herring, please. |
| Calum: Seo sibh. An e sin a h-uile càil? | Calum: Here you are. Is that everything? |
| Anna: 'S e. Seo an t-airgead. Gu robh math agaibh! | Anna: It is. Here's the money. Thank you! |
| Calum: 'S e ur beatha. Latha math dhuibh! | Calum: You're welcome. Good day to you! |

## A' Faighneachd an Rathaid — *Asking the Way*

Conversation. Plural/polite imperatives (rachaibh, gabhaibh, bithibh), a pronoun object as a possessive before the verbal noun (mo chuideachadh), the genitive after compound prepositions (airson a' mhargaidh mhòir), feminine genitives (na sràide, na h-aibhne), and answering by echoing the verb (Am feum mi...? Feumaidh).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Ruairidh: Gabhaibh mo leisceul. An urrainn dhuibh mo chuideachadh? | Ruairidh: Excuse me. Can you help me? |
| Catrìona: 'S urrainn, gu dearbh. Dè a tha a dhìth oirbh? | Catrìona: I can, of course. What do you need? |
| Ruairidh: Tha mi a' coimhead airson a' mhargaidh mhòir. Chaidh innse dhomh gu bheil e faisc air a' chaisteal. | Ruairidh: I'm looking for the big market. I was told that it's near the castle. |
| Catrìona: Tha e faisc air, ach chan eil e furasta a lorg. Rachaibh sìos an t-sràid seo gu ceann na sràide. | Catrìona: It is near it, but it isn't easy to find. Go down this street to the end of the street. |
| Ruairidh: Gu ceann na sràide, agus an uair sin? | Ruairidh: To the end of the street, and then? |
| Catrìona: An uair sin, gabhaibh an rathad air an làimh chlì. Chì sibh drochaid thar na h-aibhne. | Catrìona: Then take the road on the left. You'll see a bridge over the river. |
| Ruairidh: Am feum mi a dhol thairis air an drochaid? | Ruairidh: Do I have to cross the bridge? |
| Catrìona: Feumaidh. Tha am margadh air taobh eile na h-aibhne, fo bhallachan dearga a' chaisteil. | Catrìona: Yes. The market is on the other side of the river, under the red walls of the castle. |
| Ruairidh: Tha sin soilleir. Gu robh math agaibh gu mòr! | Ruairidh: That's clear. Thank you very much! |
| Catrìona: 'S e ur beatha. Bithibh faiceallach: bidh an t-sràid làn dhaoine aig meadhan-latha. | Catrìona: You're welcome. Be careful: the street will be full of people at midday. |

## An Aimsir agus an Teaghlach — *The Weather and the Family*

Conversation. Independent and dependent past side by side (An cuala tu? Chuala; An robh i? Bha; chan fhaca mi), irregular futures (cha tèid, nì mi, mus tig), the possessives m', d', mo, do with lenition, and the copula for a profession (is e seòladair a th' ann). Friends use 'thu'.

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Dòmhnall: Nach e tha fuar an-diugh! | Dòmhnall: Isn't it cold today! |
| Peigi: Tha e fuar gu dearbh, agus tha a' ghaoth a' sèideadh às an àird a tuath. Bha an t-uisce ann fad na h-oidhche. | Peigi: It is cold indeed, and the wind is blowing from the north. It rained all night. |
| Dòmhnall: An cuala tu an naidheachd? Thuirt na h-iascairean gum bi stoirm ann a-nochd. | Dòmhnall: Did you hear the news? The fishermen said there will be a storm tonight. |
| Peigi: Chuala. Cha tèid m' athair a-mach air a' mhuir a-nochd, ma-thà. | Peigi: I did. My father won't go out on the sea tonight, then. |
| Dòmhnall: Ciamar a tha d' athair? Chan fhaca mi e o chionn fhada. | Dòmhnall: How is your father? I haven't seen him for a long time. |
| Peigi: Tha e gu math, ach tha e a' fàs sean. Tha mo mhàthair ag ràdh gum bu chòir dha fuireach aig an taigh. | Peigi: He's well, but he's getting old. My mother says he ought to stay at home. |
| Dòmhnall: Agus do phiuthar? An robh i aig a' bhanais ann an Cathair dhearg? | Dòmhnall: And your sister? Was she at the wedding in Cathair dhearg? |
| Peigi: Bha. Thill i Diluain le fear-pòsta ùr! Is e seòladair a th' ann. | Peigi: She was. She came back on Monday with a new husband! He's a sailor. |
| Dòmhnall: Abair naidheachd! Cuir mo bheannachd thuca le chèile. | Dòmhnall: What news! Give them both my blessing. |
| Peigi: Nì mi sin. A-nis, thalla dhachaigh mus tig an stoirm! | Peigi: I will. Now, off home with you before the storm comes! |

## Bean an Ròin — *The Seal Wife*

Tale. Told at Ceann Dubh and along the west coast of Dia-thìr: the seal wife (bean an ròin). It shows narrative past tense (independent chunnaic, ghoid; dependent cha do thill), the conditional as a habitual past (sheasadh i), and 'nan'/'na' + noun for 'as' (leum iad dhan uisce nan ròin; bha i na bean-phòsta).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Bha siud ann uair iascair a bha a' fuireach leis fhèin ann am bothan beag aig Ceann Dubh, air costa an iar Dia-thìr. | There was once a fisherman who lived alone in a small cottage at Ceann Dubh, on the west coast of Dia-thìr. |
| Aon oidhche ghealaich, chaidh e sìos chun a' chladaich. Chunnaic e buidheann de mhnathan òga a' dannsadh air a' ghainmhich, agus na craicnean-ròin aca nan laighe air na creagan. | One moonlit night, he went down to the shore. He saw a group of young women dancing on the sand, and their seal-skins lying on the rocks. |
| Ghoid an t-iascair aon de na craicnean agus dh'fhalaich e fo a chòta e. Nuair a chunnaic na mnathan e, ruith iad chun na mara, chuir iad orra na craicnean aca, agus leum iad dhan uisce nan ròin. Ach dh'fhuirich tè dhiubh air a' chladach, a' caoineadh, oir cha b' urrainn dhi tilleadh gun a craiceann. | The fisherman stole one of the skins and hid it under his coat. When the women saw him, they ran to the sea, put on their skins, and leapt into the water as seals. But one of them stayed on the shore, weeping, for she could not go back without her skin. |
| Thug an t-iascair dhachaigh i, agus an ceann ùine phòs iad. Bha i na bean-phòsta chòir, dhìleas, agus rug i triùir chloinne dha. Ach gach feascar sheasadh i aig an doras, a' coimhead a-mach air a' mhuir le sùilean brònach. | The fisherman took her home, and in time they married. She was a kind, faithful wife, and she bore him three children. But every evening she would stand at the door, looking out at the sea with sad eyes. |
| Bliadhnaichean an dèidh sin, bha an nighean as òige a' cluich anns an t-sabhal. Fhuair i craiceann bog glas air a phascadh fo sheann lìon. Ruith i a-steach gu a màthair agus dh'fhaighnich i, "A mhàthair, dè a tha seo?" | Years after that, the youngest daughter was playing in the barn. She found a soft grey skin folded under an old net. She ran in to her mother and asked, "Mother, what is this?" |
| Nuair a chunnaic a' bhean an craiceann, leum a cridhe. Phòg i a' chlann, fear às dèidh fir, agus thuirt i riutha, "Tha gaol agam oirbh, ach buinidh mi don mhuir." An uair sin ruith i sìos chun a' chladaich, chuir i uimpe an craiceann, agus chaidh i fodha fo na tonnan. | When the woman saw the skin, her heart leapt. She kissed the children, one after another, and she said to them, "I love you, but I belong to the sea." Then she ran down to the shore, put on the skin, and went down beneath the waves. |
| Cha do thill i tuilleadh. Ach thuirt na daoine aig Ceann Dubh gum faicte ròn mòr glas gach feascar faisc air a' chladach, a' coimhead air a' chlann, agus gum biodh iasc gu leòr an-còmhnaidh ann an lìon an iascair. | She never came back. But the people at Ceann Dubh said that a big grey seal would be seen every evening near the shore, watching the children, and that there was always plenty of fish in the fisherman's net. |

## An t-Each-Uisce — *The Water Horse*

Tale. Told of Loch Dubh in the high glens: the water horse (each-uisce) that grows longer as the children climb onto it, and the one boy who gets free by cutting off his own finger. It shows the conditional as a habitual past (thigeadh, sheasadh, ruitheadh), impersonal past forms (chan fhacas, cha d' fhuaireadh), the pluperfect with 'air' + object + lenited verbal noun (air an t-each fhaicinn), and an indefinite masculine genitive with lenited, slenderised adjectives (ann an riochd eich bhrèagha ghil).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Bha siud ann uair, ann an glinn àrda Dia-thìr, loch dorcha ris an canadh daoine Loch Dubh. Cha rachadh duine glic faisc air an loch sin às dèidh dol fodha na grèine, oir bha fios aig a h-uile duine gun robh an t-each-uisce a' fuireach fo a uisce. | Once upon a time, in the high glens of Dia-thìr, there was a dark loch that people called Loch Dubh. No wise person would go near that loch after sunset, for everyone knew that the water horse lived beneath its water. |
| Air an oidhche, thigeadh e às an loch ann an riochd eich bhrèagha ghil, le muing fhada air a sceadachadh le feamainn. Sheasadh e air a' bhruaich cho socair ri caora, a' feitheamh ri cuideigin a bha gòrach gu leòr airson a dhol air a mhuin. | At night it would come out of the loch in the shape of a beautiful white horse, with a long mane adorned with seaweed. It would stand on the bank as gentle as a sheep, waiting for someone foolish enough to get on its back. |
| Ge b' e cò a rachadh air a mhuin, leanadh a chraiceann ris an each mar ghlaodh, agus ruitheadh an t-each-uisce dìreach dhan loch. Chan fhacas a-riamh tuilleadh duine sam bith a ghabh an turas sin; cha tilleadh ach a ghrùthan, air a thilgeil air a' bhruaich. | Whoever got on its back, his skin would stick to the horse like glue, and the water horse would run straight into the loch. No one who took that journey was ever seen again; only his liver would come back, thrown up on the bank. |
| Anns a' bhaile ri taobh an locha, bha seachdnar chloinne a' cluich aon fheascar samhraidh. Nochd each bàn air a' bhruaich, agus bha e cho ciùin 's gun deach sianar dhiubh air a mhuin, fear às dèidh fir. Dh'fhàs druim an eich na b' fhaide leis gach leanabh a shreap air. | In the village beside the loch, seven children were playing one summer evening. A white horse appeared on the bank, and it was so gentle that six of them got on its back, one after another. The horse's back grew longer with every child who climbed onto it. |
| Ach bha an seachdamh leanabh, balach beag glic, air an t-each fhaicinn roimhe ann an sceulachdan a sheanmhar. Chunnaic e an fheamainn na mhuing agus a' ghainmheach air a chasan. Chuir e a làmh air sròin an eich airson a shlìobadh, agus lean a làmh ris. | But the seventh child, a clever little boy, had seen the horse before in his grandmother's stories. He saw the seaweed in its mane and the sand on its hooves. He put his hand on the horse's nose to stroke it, and his hand stuck to it. |
| Cha do ghabh am balach eagal. Thug e a scian bheag às a phòcaid agus gheàrr e dheth a mheur fhèin. Thuit e air an fheur, saor, agus ruith e dhachaigh cho luath 's a b' urrainn dha, ag èigheachd ris a h-uile duine sa bhaile. | The boy did not take fright. He took his little knife out of his pocket and cut off his own finger. He fell onto the grass, free, and ran home as fast as he could, shouting to everyone in the village. |
| Ach bha e ro fhadalach. Leum an t-each-uisce dhan loch le screuch uamhasach, agus chaidh e fodha leis a' chloinn eile air a dhruim. Cha d' fhuaireadh sceul orra tuilleadh. | But it was too late. The water horse leapt into the loch with a terrible shriek, and it went under with the other children on its back. No trace of them was ever found. |
| Gus an latha an-diugh, cha leig na màthraichean aig Loch Dubh leis a' chloinn aca each sam bith a shlìobadh nach aithne dhaibh. "Seall air a mhuing," their iad, "agus seall air a chasan." | To this day, the mothers at Loch Dubh will not let their children stroke any horse they do not know. "Look at its mane," they say, "and look at its feet." |

## Fionn agus Bradan an Eòlais — *Fionn and the Salmon of Knowledge*

Tale. Told of a deep pool on the Abhainn mhòr, from the days before there was a king in Cathair dhearg: Fionn and the Salmon of Knowledge. It shows the dependent past in question and answer (An do bhlais thu? Cha do bhlais), the vocative with lenition and slenderising (a Fhinn), the conditional in reported speech and purpose clauses (gum faigheadh, gus nach loisceadh), and genitives after compound prepositions (os cionn an teine, ri taobh na linne).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Bha siud ann uair, fada mus robh rìgh ann an Cathair dhearg, bàrd aosta ris an canadh daoine Fionn-Èigeas. Bha e a' fuireach leis fhèin aig linne dhomhainn air an Abhainn mhòir, far an robh an abhainn a' dòrtadh dhan mhuir. | Once upon a time, long before there was a king in Cathair dhearg, there was an aged poet whom people called Fionn-Èigeas. He lived alone at a deep pool on the Abhainn mhòr, where the river poured into the sea. |
| Anns an linne sin bha bradan mòr, sean, agus bha a h-uile gliocas air an t-saoghal ann. Bha e air a ràdh gum faigheadh a' chiad duine a bhlaiseadh de fheòil a' bhradain sin eòlas air gach nì a bha ann agus a bhiodh ann. Fad seachd bliadhna bha am bàrd ag iascach air a shon, agus cha d' fhuair e grèim air. | In that pool there was a great old salmon, and all the wisdom in the world was in it. It was said that the first person to taste the flesh of that salmon would gain knowledge of everything that was and everything that would be. For seven years the poet fished for it, and he never caught hold of it. |
| Aon latha thàinig balach òg thuige, mac gaiscich a chaidh a mharbhadh, agus dh'iarr e obair air. B' e Fionn an t-ainm a bh' air. Ghabh am bàrd ris mar ghille, agus theagaisc e bàrdachd dha agus seann eachdraidh an eilein. | One day a young boy came to him, the son of a warrior who had been killed, and asked him for work. Fionn was his name. The poet took him on as a servant, and taught him poetry and the old history of the island. |
| Mu dheireadh, madainn fhuar fhoghair, ghabh am bradan biathadh a' bhàird. Tharraing e às an uisce e le a neart gu lèir, agus bha e air chrith leis an toileachas. Thug e am bradan do Fhionn agus thuirt e ris, "Ròstaidh tu seo dhomh air an teine. Ach cuimhnich: na blais thusa dheth, fiù 's criomag." | At last, one cold autumn morning, the salmon took the poet's bait. He drew it out of the water with all his strength, and he was trembling with joy. He gave the salmon to Fionn and said to him, "You will roast this for me on the fire. But remember: don't you taste of it, not even a scrap." |
| Chuir Fionn am bradan air bior os cionn an teine agus thionndaidh e gu faiceallach e. Ach dh'èirich builgean air craiceann an èisc, agus bhrùth Fionn sìos e le a òrdag gus nach loisceadh am bradan. Loisc e a òrdag, agus, gun smaoineachadh, chuir e na bheul i. | Fionn put the salmon on a spit over the fire and turned it carefully. But a blister rose on the fish's skin, and Fionn pressed it down with his thumb so that the salmon would not burn. He burned his thumb, and, without thinking, he put it in his mouth. |
| Nuair a thill am bàrd, sheall e air aodann a' ghille agus chunnaic e solas ùr na shùilean. "An do bhlais thu den bhradan?" dh'fhaighnich e. "Cha do bhlais," fhreagair Fionn, "ach loisc mi m' òrdag air agus chuir mi nam bheul i." | When the poet returned, he looked at the boy's face and saw a new light in his eyes. "Did you taste the salmon?" he asked. "I did not," answered Fionn, "but I burned my thumb on it and put it in my mouth." |
| Leig am bàrd osna fhada. "Mar sin, is ann dhutsa a bha am bradan an dàn, agus chan ann dhòmhsa," thuirt e. "Ith e, a Fhinn." Dh'ith Fionn am bradan, agus bhon latha sin a-mach, nuair a chuireadh e a òrdag fo fhiacail, thigeadh gliocas an t-saoghail thuige. | The poet let out a long sigh. "So it was for you that the salmon was destined, and not for me," he said. "Eat it, Fionn." Fionn ate the salmon, and from that day on, whenever he put his thumb under his tooth, the wisdom of the world would come to him. |
| Their seann daoine air bruaichean na h-Aibhne mòire gum faic thu fhathast, air feascar samhraidh, clach rèidh ri taobh na linne far an robh an teine aig Fionn. | Old folk on the banks of the Abhainn mhòr say that you can still see, on a summer evening, a flat stone beside the pool where Fionn's fire was. |

## Tòimhseachan a' Choimhdich — *The Keeper's Riddle*

Tale. The riddle of Coimhdeach na Fine, the Keeper of the Kin, as the Mason's children, Clann a' Chlachair, say it over at the dark of the year; the whole of the Keeper's telling stands at the head of the chronicle as the Telling of the Making. It shows the independent and dependent past (dh'fhaighnich, thuirt; cha do dh'innis, mus do thuig), the conditional in an indirect question (ciamar a dhùisceadh e 'how he might wake'), the relative future after 'mar a' (mar a dh'fhalbhas ceò 'as smoke goes'), posture with the possessive (na shuidhe 'sitting', lit. 'in his sitting'), prepositional pronouns (thuige, dheth, dha), san and sa for anns an and anns a' (san speur; sa mhuir, where sa lenites), the singular imperative (meascaich), and lenition after 'mar' and after the article in the genitive and dative (mar cheò, a' chruthachaidh, anns a' ghual).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Seo mar a dh'innis Clann a' Chlachair e. | This is how the Mason's children told it. |
| Bha fear de shliochd a' chlachair na shuidhe fo na daraich ann an Doire ghlas, agus thàinig Coimhdeach na Fine thuige mar cheò. | A man of the mason's line was sitting under the oaks in Doire ghlas, and the Keeper of the Kin came to him like smoke. |
| Dh'fhaighnich e dheth: "Carson a tha dathan anns a' ghual?" | He asked it: "Why are there colours in the coal?" |
| Dh'innis an Coimhdeach dha sceul a' chruthachaidh, bho thùs gu èis. | The Keeper told him the tale of the making, from beginning to end. |
| An uair sin dh'fhaighnich e ciamar a dhùisceadh e na dathan. | Then he asked how he might wake the colours. |
| Thuirt an Coimhdeach: "Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach." | The Keeper said: "The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty." |
| Ach cha do dh'innis e dha ciamar, agus dh'fhalbh e mar a dh'fhalbhas ceò. | But it did not tell him how, and it went as smoke goes. |
| Chaidh iomadh ginealach seachad mus do thuig iad e. | Many generations went by before they understood it. |

## Mar a Dh'fhalbh na Seann-Dhaoine — *How the Old Ones Left*

Tale. The legend of the Old Ones' going, as the fishermen of Seann Dunn tell it. When they say that the Dia-thìrich came, they mean that the hill-folk came down to the shore; the doctrine holds that the people were on the island from the first. It shows the negative past in subordinate clauses (nuair nach do dh'fhàs, nach do dh'fhalbh), the impersonal past (chan fhacas iad), the relative future after 'ma' (ma dh'èisteas tu), the pre-nominal 'seann', which lenites (Seann Chwen) except before d, t, s and sk (Seann Dunn, Seann Skell), and a feminine genitive with adjective (oidhche na gealaich ùire).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Fada mus tàinig na Dia-thìrich, bha sluagh eile a' fuireach air Dia-thìr. Cha robh an cànan aca coltach ri cànan sam bith a tha air a bhruidhinn an-diugh, agus cha do scrìobh iad dad sìos. Canaidh sinne na Seann-Dhaoine riutha, a chionn 's nach eil fios aig duine beò dè an t-ainm a bh' aca orra fhèin. | Long before the Dia-thìrich came, another people lived on Dia-thìr. Their language was like no language spoken today, and they wrote nothing down. We call them the Old Ones, because no living person knows what name they had for themselves. |
| Bha bailtean aca air feadh a' chladaich: Dunn air a' chreig os cionn a' chaolais, Cwen ann am bàgh foidhpe, agus Skell air an rubha a b' fhaide an iar. Bha iad eòlach air a' mhuir mar a tha iascair eòlach air a lìon, agus b' urrainn dhaibh na ròin a ghairm le òran. | They had towns all along the shore: Dunn on the rock above the strait, Cwen in a bay beneath it, and Skell on the westernmost headland. They knew the sea as a fisherman knows his net, and they could call the seals with a song. |
| Ach thàinig bliadhna nuair nach do dh'fhàs an t-arbhar agus nach do thill an scadan. Bha na seann daoine glic aca ag ràdh gun robh an t-eilean scìth dhiubh, agus gun robh e a' fuadach nan daoine a bhuineadh dha bho thùs. Chruinnich iad uile aig Dunn air oidhche na gealaich ùire, agus chuir iad comhairle ri chèile gus an do shoilleirich an latha. | But there came a year when the corn did not grow and the herring did not return. Their wise elders said that the island had grown tired of them, and that it was driving away the people who had belonged to it from the beginning. They all gathered at Dunn on the night of the new moon, and they took counsel together until the day dawned. |
| Anns a' mhadainn, rinn iad an co-dhùnadh. Thog iad bàtaichean fada de dharach dubh, luchdaich iad annta an clann agus an sìol agus clachan an teallaichean, agus sheòl iad a-mach air a' chuan mhòr, an iar, a dh'ionnsaigh na grèine a bha a' dol fodha. | In the morning, they made their decision. They built long boats of black oak, loaded into them their children and their seed and the stones of their hearths, and sailed out onto the great ocean, westward, toward the setting sun. |
| Chan fhacas iad a-riamh tuilleadh. Cha do lorg duine bòrd de na bàtaichean aca, no uaigh, no clach-chuimhne. Chaidh an cànan à cuimhne ann an aon ghinealach, mar shneachda air a' ghainmhich nuair a thig an làn. | They were never seen again. No one found a plank of their boats, nor a grave, nor a memorial stone. Their language passed out of memory in a single generation, like snow on the sand when the tide comes in. |
| Nuair a thàinig na Dia-thìrich gu na cladaichean sin, fhuair iad bailtean falamh le ballachan cloiche a bha fhathast nan seasamh. Cha robh duine ann a dh'innseadh dhaibh dè an t-ainm a bh' orra. Ach nuair a sheas iad anns na seann bhailtean, chuala iad a' ghaoth a' seinn trì ainmean thairis air na ballachan: Dunn, Cwen, Skell. | When the Dia-thìrich came to those shores, they found empty towns with stone walls that were still standing. There was no one there to tell them what the towns were called. But when they stood in the old towns, they heard the wind singing three names over the walls: Dunn, Cwen, Skell. |
| Cha b' urrainn do na Dia-thìrich na h-ainmean sin a thuigsinn, ach cha do chuir iad ainmean ùra nan àite. Chuir iad dìreach aon fhacal romhpa, mar chomharra urraim: Seann Dunn, Seann Chwen, Seann Skell. Agus is ann mar sin a tha iad air a' mhapa chun an latha an-diugh. | The Dia-thìrich could not understand those names, but they did not put new names in their place. They simply put one word in front of them, as a mark of respect: Seann Dunn, Seann Chwen, Seann Skell (Old Dunn, Old Cwen, Old Skell). And that is how they stand on the map to this day. |
| Their seann iascairean nach do dh'fhalbh na Seann-Dhaoine buileach. Air oidhche na gealaich ùire, ma dh'èisteas tu gu cùramach aig Seann Dunn, cluinnidh tu òran ann an cànan nach aithne do dhuine beò, a' tighinn thar an uisce on àird an iar. | Old fishermen say that the Old Ones did not leave entirely. On the night of the new moon, if you listen carefully at Seann Dunn, you will hear a song in a language no living person knows, coming over the water from the west. |

## Mar a Thàinig an Gual dhan Fhuil — *How Coal Came into the Blood*

Tale. A hearth-tale of the fuil-ghuail (coal-blood), told as hearsay. The chronicles tell it otherwise: there the gift comes from the coal of the mining camps, first in Aisling, in AE 69. It shows irregular verbs in narration (thoir: bheir mi / cha toir mi; rach: cha rachadh; faic: chitheadh), relative forms of 'bi' (aig am bi, cho fad 's a bhios), the passive with 'rach' (an deach na fiachan a phàigheadh), and fronting with the copula (is e fuil-ghuail a chanas na Dia-thìrich...).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Seo mar a dh'innis na seann daoine e, agus mar a chuala mise e bho mo sheanmhair ann an Cathair dhearg. Chan eil fhios agam an e an fhìrinn a th' ann; ach is e seo an sceul. | This is how the old people told it, and how I heard it from my grandmother in Cathair dhearg. I don't know whether it is the truth; but this is the tale. |
| Anns na làithean a dh'fhalbh, nuair nach robh rìgh no rìoghachd air Dia-thìr, bha gobha a' fuireach aig bonn cnuic ann am meadhan an eilein. Bhiodh an cnoc sin a' smocadh fad na bliadhna, agus air oidhcheannan geamhraidh chitheadh daoine lasraichean dearga a' ruith tro na scàinidhean na thaobh. Cha rachadh duine suas air ach an gobha. | In the days gone by, when there was neither king nor kingdom on Dia-thìr, a smith lived at the foot of a hill in the middle of the island. That hill would smoke all year round, and on winter nights people would see red flames running through the cracks in its side. No one would go up it but the smith. |
| Bha an gobha na b' fheàrr air a cheàird na duine eile air an eilean, ach bha e bochd, agus bha an sluagh aige lag. Bha iad air an sàrachadh le reothadh a' gheamhraidh agus le creachadairean a thigeadh thar na mara. Aon oidhche, nuair a bha a mhac as òige a' bàsachadh leis an fhuachd, ghabh an gobha a òrd na làimh agus dhìrich e an cnoc loiscte gu a mhullach. | The smith was better at his craft than anyone else on the island, but he was poor, and his people were weak. They were harried by the winter frost and by raiders who would come over the sea. One night, when his youngest son was dying of the cold, the smith took his hammer in his hand and climbed the burning hill to its summit. |
| Aig a' mhullach bha sloc, agus anns an t-sloc bha teine nach do las làmh duine a-riamh. Bhruidhinn guth às na lasraichean, cho domhainn ris an talamh. "Carson a thàinig thu, a ghobha?" | At the summit there was a pit, and in the pit there was a fire that no human hand had ever lit. A voice spoke out of the flames, as deep as the earth. "Why have you come, smith?" |
| "Tha mo shluagh fuar, agus tha iad lag," fhreagair an gobha. "Thoir dhaibh do theas. Thoir dhaibh neart nach bris iarann no reothadh." Rinn an teine gàire mar ghlag. "Bheir mi sin dhut," ars an guth, "ach cha toir mi an ascaidh e. Gach neach aig am bi mo theas na fhuil, bidh e fo fhiachan dhomhsa, e fhèin agus a chlann, cho fad 's a bhios gual fon talamh." | "My people are cold, and they are weak," the smith answered. "Give them your heat. Give them a strength that neither iron nor frost can break." The fire laughed like a bell. "I will give you that," said the voice, "but I will not give it for nothing. Every person who has my heat in his blood will be in my debt, he and his children, for as long as there is coal under the ground." |
| Cha do smaoinich an gobha fada. Smaoinich e air a mhac. "Gabhaidh mi ris a' chùmhnant," ars esan. Chuir e a làmh dìreach dhan teine, agus cha do loisc i. Dh'fhairich e an teas a' sruthadh suas a ghàirdean, dubh agus dearg, mar ghual a' dùscadh ann an teallach. | The smith did not think long. He thought of his son. "I accept the bargain," he said. He put his hand straight into the fire, and it did not burn. He felt the heat flowing up his arm, black and red, like coal waking in a hearth. |
| Nuair a thill e dhachaigh, bha a mhac beò agus blàth, agus bha dath dorcha, mar luaithre, fo chraiceann a làmhan. Bho sin a-mach, cha do dh'fhairich teaghlach a' ghobha fuachd a' gheamhraidh, agus cha do thuit iad fo chlaidhmhean nan creachadairean. Scaoil an teas bho ghinealach gu ginealach, agus is iadsan a thog Cathair dhearg de chlach dhearg a' chnuic. | When he returned home, his son was alive and warm, and there was a dark colour, like ash, under the skin of his hands. From then on, the smith's family never felt the cold of winter, and they did not fall under the raiders' swords. The heat spread from generation to generation, and it was they who built Cathair dhearg from the red stone of the hill. |
| Is e fuil-ghuail a chanas na Dia-thìrich ris an tiodhlac sin. Ach tha cuid ann fhathast, 's iad a' coimhead air a' chnoc a tha a' smocadh gu sàmhach, a bhios a' faighneachd: an deach na fiachan a phàigheadh a-riamh? Agus ma chaidh, cò a phàigh iad? | Coal-blood is what the Dia-thìrich call that gift. But there are still some, watching the hill that smokes quietly, who ask: were the debts ever paid? And if they were, who paid them? |

## Carson a Chaidh na Tuathaich gu Tuath — *Why the Tuathaich Went North*

Tale. The Tuathaich's own telling, which takes up where the tale of the coal-blood leaves off. The chronicles tell it otherwise: there the Tuathaich come of the humans who settled in the north after the Severance, in SE 1. It shows the relative future after 'nuair a' (nuair a dh'fhaighnicheas), a reported prohibition in the conditional (nach fhaodadh duine...), fronting with 'is ann' and 'cha b' e', 'dà' + lenited singular (an dà shluagh), and genitives after verbal nouns and 'an àite' (a' loscadh mònach an àite guail, clann nan Tuathach).

| Dia-thìris | English |
|---|---|
| Tha na Dia-thìrich ag innse an sceòil seo ann an aon dòigh, agus na Tuathaich ann an dòigh eile. Seo mar a dh'innseas na Tuathaich e, timcheall air an teine ann an oidhcheannan fada a' gheamhraidh. | The Dia-thìrich tell this story one way, and the Tuathaich another. This is how the Tuathaich tell it, around the fire on the long nights of winter. |
| Nuair a thàinig an teas às a' chnoc a-steach do fhuil teaghlach a' ghobha, cha do ghabh a h-uile duine ris. Bha teaghlaichean ann a chuala mun chùmhnant agus a thuirt, "Chan eil sinn airson a bhith fo fhiachan do theine. Is fheàrr leinn a bhith fuar agus saor." | When the heat came out of the hill into the blood of the smith's family, not everyone accepted it. There were families who heard about the bargain and who said, "We don't want to be in debt to a fire. We would rather be cold and free." |
| Airson ùine, bha an dà shluagh a' fuireach taobh ri taobh. Ach dh'fhàs na daoine aig an robh an fhuil-ghuail làidir agus lìonmhor. Thog iad ballachan dearga, thagh iad rìgh às am measc fhèin, agus thàinig Rìoghachd Dia-thìr gu bith. Cha b' urrainn do na daoine eile cumail suas riutha, ann an obair no ann an cogadh. | For a time, the two peoples lived side by side. But the people who had the coal-blood grew strong and numerous. They built red walls, chose a king from among themselves, and the Kingdom of Dia-thìr came into being. The other people could not keep up with them, in work or in war. |
| An toiseach cha robh ann ach cìs bheag, agus an uair sin cìs mhòr. An uair sin thàinig lagh bhon chathair: nach fhaodadh duine gun teas na fhuil fearann a chumail an taobh a-staigh de latha coiseachd bho na ballachan dearga. Cha do thog duine claidheamh. Cha robh feum air. | At first there was only a small tax, and then a large tax. Then a law came from the city: that no one without heat in his blood might hold land within a day's walk of the red walls. No one raised a sword. There was no need. |
| Mar sin, aon earrach, chruinnich na teaghlaichean saora an crodh agus an caoraich agus na bha aca de ghoireasan, agus thog iad orra gu tuath. Choisich iad seachad air na cnuic loiscte agus tro na coilltean, gus an do ràinig iad fearann creagach far an robh a' ghaoth geur agus an talamh tana. Cha robh duine eile ga iarraidh. | So, one spring, the free families gathered their cattle and their sheep and whatever goods they had, and they set off north. They walked past the burnt hills and through the woods, until they reached a rocky land where the wind was sharp and the soil thin. No one else wanted it. |
| Is ann an sin a fhuair iad an t-ainm a th' orra an-diugh. Cha b' e ainm a thagh iad fhèin a bh' ann: bha na Dia-thìrich a' bruidhinn orra mar "na daoine a tuath", agus an ceann ùine, cha robh annta ach na Tuathaich. Ach ghabh iad ris an ainm le uaill, oir cha robh fiachan orra do dhuine. | It was there that they got the name they bear today. It was not a name they chose themselves: the Dia-thìrich spoke of them as "the people from the north", and in time they were simply the Tuathaich. But they took the name with pride, for they owed no debt to anyone. |
| Tha beatha chruaidh aca fhathast ann an ceann a tuath an eilein. Bidh iad a' loscadh mònach an àite guail, agus bidh iad a' bàsachadh le fuachd agus le aois mar a h-uile duine eile air an t-saoghal. Ach nuair a dh'fhaighnicheas clann nan Tuathach carson a tha iad cho fada bhon chathair, their na seann daoine riutha: "Tha sinn bochd, ach tha ar fuil fhèin againn." | They still have a hard life in the northern end of the island. They burn peat instead of coal, and they die of cold and of age like everyone else in the world. But when the children of the Tuathaich ask why they are so far from the city, the old people say to them: "We are poor, but we have our own blood." |
