RÌOGHACHD RÒDAIS · the working chronicle

Chronicle of Rodos

Five ages, each opened by the event that ended the one before it. Events marked ● have a real place on the map — tap "view on map" to jump there.

I. An Aois Àrsaidh II. An Aois Naomh III. An Aois Scaraidh IV. An Aois Choigreach V. An Aois Ùr

AGE I

An Aois Àrsaidh — The Ancient Age

beyond record

Defining event: The crack in the stone

No chronicle claims to know how the first people came to Rodos. Ask, and the answer is doctrine, not history: cha do thàinig sinn — bha sinn ann, "we did not come — we were here."

undated

The crack in the stone

A mason splitting rock for a well breaks a black, glassy vein — and it bleeds color instead of dust. The closest thing Rodos keeps to a creation myth.

undated

The First Canoe

A suppressed, half-remembered voyaging fragment survives among coastal Tuathaich families: a named steersman, a first crossing, dismissed by the Ròdaich priesthood as superstition.

undated

The Sea-Watcher first sighted

Old sailors speak of a vast creature inhabiting the waters off the northwestern coast. No wreck has ever been recovered to prove it wrong.

undated

The Hollow Man of Doire uaine

Inscriptions in the Doire uaine hills describe something that walked without breathing, and attacked anyone who lit a fire in its sight.

undated

Coille Naomh Muileann chiar set aside

A forest is marked sacred, long before any organized faith claims it formally — the trees simply understood, the old telling goes, before the people did.

undated

Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag left unclaimed

A second forest, older-feeling than the first, is never settled, never logged, never fully mapped.

undated

The Làrach an Dùin-fhaire abandoned

An outpost, already ruined when the first reliable chronicles begin, predates every known Rodian settlement pattern. No one knows who built it.

undated

The Làrach an Dùin abandoned

A fortress in the same condition and the same mystery. Whether it belonged to the same builders as the outpost has never been settled.

undated

The Làrach an Teampaill abandoned

A temple older than any recorded religion, its altar oriented toward the central mountains rather than the sea — toward the vein, not the horizon.

undated

Carragh Cnoc bheag raised

A standing stone, later found carved with four words: Bha teine sa chloich — "there was fire in the stone."

undated

Colbh Cnoc chiar raised

A second stone carries only two words: Cuimhnich an teine — "remember the fire." No one has ever explained the imperative.

undated

Calbh Cnoc ghorm raised

A third stone carries the doctrine itself, complete: Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann. If the doctrine has a birthplace, it may be here.

undated

Tuam Cnoc dhìreach consecrated

A necropolis grows around a site nobody remembers consecrating. Its oldest graves are older than its oldest legible names.

undated

The panther roads

A migratory instinct in the island's great cats is first recorded — routes crossing the mountains at fixed intervals, for reasons no naturalist has explained.

undated

The first named storm

A tempest severe enough to be given a name in the oldest surviving oral king-lists — the name itself lost, only the fact of it surviving.

undated

The counting of the dead begins

The Seann-Dhaoine, whoever they were, the people who built Làrach an Dùin-fhaire and Làrach an Dùin, also began keeping count of its own losses — record-keeping as instinct, before there was a Ròdaich people to inherit it.

undated

The unlit hearth

A single dwelling, preserved by chance in the island's peat, shows a hearth never lit — the earliest evidence that someone here once chose not to burn something they easily could have.

undated

The first tide-count

Someone begins marking the high-water line against a fixed rock, for reasons never recorded, in intervals later scholars recognize as suspiciously regular for a purely practical fisherman's habit.

undated

The naming of the wind directions

The eight-point wind system every later Ròdaich sailor still uses is fixed in this period, with names that translate awkwardly and have simply never been replaced.

undated

The first forbidden marriage

A union between two lineages is refused for reasons no surviving text explains — the earliest evidence that Rodian society organized itself by bloodline before it had any obsidian-caste reason to.

undated

The stone that would not break

A second attempt to open a new vein elsewhere on the island fails entirely — ordinary rock, nothing beneath it. The first confirmation that the vein under the mountain is not a rule, but an exception.

undated

The first shared meal law

An unwritten custom, later formalized, requiring any household with surplus food to share it before a storm season — the earliest form of the communal obligation later ages will lean on more heavily than they admit.

AGE II

An Aois Naomh — The Holy Age

the reverent age, undated

Defining event: The Binding of the First Flame

The people who found the vein call themselves, in their own tongue, Ròdaich. For centuries they treat the ore the way jade was treated, not gold: burned in small measured quantities, never mass-excavated, never anything but sacred. In this age, nobody bonds with it. The power this story knows as obsidian-touched does not exist yet.

undated

The Binding of the First Flame

A formal custodianship is declared over the vein — the first Rodian institution recognizable as a religious-political order, centuries before anything like a king.

undated

Seann Spioradan nan Ròdach established

The island's oldest continuous folk tradition takes shape: reverence for ancestors, for the vein, for the weather that decides the mining season.

undated

Allt an Àigh named

A stream said to grant fortune to anyone who drinks from it during a particular phase of the moon becomes a site of pilgrimage that has never fully stopped.

undated

Muileann chiar founded

A silver seam is found near, but distinct from, the great vein — the island's first confirmed resource that isn't obsidian.

undated

Feallsanachd an Fhèidh founded

A philosophical school forms around the question of why the vein answers some hands and not others — the first attempt to reason about the obsidian rather than simply revere it.

undated

The Small-Burning Law

A custom hardens into formal rule: no household may burn more than a measured handful of the ore in a season. It holds for centuries.

undated

The first bridges

Stone crossings begin linking the interior mining paths to the coast — ancestors of what will later be Drochaid Cathair dhearg and Drochaid Ros fhionn.

undated

The tavern roads begin

The first waypoint houses appear along the mining roads — ancestors of Am Buabhall Fortanach, An Taigh-òsta Grianach, and the rest of the circuit.

undated

The mausoleum consecrated

Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, already ancient, receives its first formally recorded burial — an unclaimed necropolis becoming a claimed one.

undated

The reverent centuries

Generation after generation mines the same modest quantity, burns the same modest quantity, and nothing about the population changes. This is the last age in which that remains true.

undated

The first written king-list

Rulers begin to be recorded by name rather than remembered by story — a shift the later succession records will inherit directly.

undated

The holy-wood dispute

The first recorded conflict between rival religious claims to Coille Naomh Muileann chiar and Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag — settled by dividing custody rather than choosing a winner.

undated

The measuring of the vein

The first attempt to estimate how much obsidian the mountain holds. The estimate will prove wildly optimistic.

undated

The apprentice-binding custom

Young Rodians are first formally bound to a master custodian of the vein for seven years before being trusted near the ore themselves — the origin of every later mining guild's structure.

undated

The census of hands

The first attempt to count, island-wide, how many households hold a customary right to burn obsidian at all — proof the reverent tradition was already being formally rationed, not simply assumed.

undated

The founding of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh's rival school

A breakaway sect argues the vein answers effort, not birth — a quiet, centuries-early rehearsal of the argument the Long War will eventually be fought over in a very different form.

undated

The first recorded famine averted

A poor mining season nearly starves three settlements before the shared-meal custom, formalized a generation earlier, holds and prevents it — the tradition's first real test.

undated

The naming of the nine faiths' common ancestor

A single unified folk practice begins visibly splitting into the distinct threads later ages will separately name — the moment 'Rodian belief' stops being one thing.

undated

The last age of even population

Chroniclers later look back and note, with some unease, that this is the final stretch of Rodian history in which no single family held meaningfully more years of life than any other.

AGE III

An Aois Scaraidh — The Age of Sundering

undated, by choice

Defining event: The Choosing

A group of Rodians find another land — barren of people like themselves — and volunteer to cross the water and settle it. Nothing forces them out. This is the Sundering, and it is chosen.

undated

Land is found

Word returns of a land across open water, unpeopled by anyone resembling the Ròdaich. No record survives of who found it first.

undated

The Choosing

A vote — or something the later chronicles call a vote — decides who will go. Volunteers only. No lots are drawn, no conscription recorded.

undated

The ships are built

The island's shipwrights, used to modest coastal craft, build vessels meant to survive open water for the first time in Rodian memory.

undated

The provisioning debate

A dispute over how much of the island's stored grain the departing ships may take nearly cancels the whole expedition. It is resolved, unrecorded how, within a season.

undated

The Setting-Out

The volunteers depart. The island's chronicles simply stop describing them the moment the ships clear the harbor.

undated

Two landfalls

What fragmentary report ever returns describes two separate coasts reached, not one — the seed, in this telling, of two later peoples rather than one.

undated

The thinning

Cut off from the vein, whatever strength the crossing required fades across the departed's own children. By the third generation abroad, nothing remains that Rodos would recognize.

undated

The Mourning Custom

Rodos formalizes a mourning rite for the departed that is not quite grief and not quite celebration — reused, uneasily, for a very different departure in a much later age.

undated

Population correction

With a portion of the obsidian-bonded population gone, Rodian society reorganizes around a smaller, more concentrated custodian class — the shape every later aristocracy inherits.

undated

The empty harbor rite

A ceremony develops around the specific dock the ships departed from, still observed in reduced form on an anniversary no one can precisely date anymore.

undated

The second silence

A rumor of a second, smaller crossing — fewer ships, no surviving record of a destination — is neither confirmed nor denied by anything that survives.

undated

The renaming of the west quay

The dock the ships departed from is given a new name specifically so ordinary trade could resume using the old one without the association — an early, quiet act of managed forgetting.

undated

The first generation to never see the sea empty

Children born just after the Setting-Out grow up never having known the harbor with those particular ships in it — the exact moment departure stops being an event and starts being a fact of history.

undated

The closing of the shipwrights' guild

With no more ocean-crossing vessels needed, the guild that built the Sundering fleet quietly dissolves — its techniques half-forgotten by the time anyone thinks to need them again.

AGE IV

An Aois Choigreach — The Age of Strangers

c. 1780 DE – 1930 DE

Defining event: The Crossing

Humans find their way back across the water the Rodians once crossed, and are permitted access to Rodian land. What follows is not the usual colonial pattern — greed meeting reverence. The humans who arrive don't want the ore for what it is. They can't see what it is. To them it's coal: a burnable fuel, not a worship source, and the color it glows when broken reads as ungodly, not precious.

c. 1780 DE

The Crossing

Humans make landfall on Rodos and are granted permission to stay — the first contact since the Sundering.

c. 1781 DE

The first translation attempts

Human clerics and Rodian custodians spend a full season trying to build a shared vocabulary. Neither side's account of what was actually agreed to survives intact.

c. 1782 DE

Trade begins

Early exchange is genuinely reciprocal: Rodian goods for tools and knowledge the isolated island lacks. For a few years, the arrangement looks sustainable.

c. 1784 DE

The first mixed settlement

A small coastal town accepts both Rodian and human residents under a single administration — an experiment later history will not repeat.

c. 1785 DE

The lighthouses proposed

Human navigators, unused to Rodos's coastline, request permanent beacons. Ròdaich engineers agree, not yet understanding what the request will enable.

c. 1787 DE

The first written treaty

Terms of the human presence are set down formally for the first time — in the humans' language only, a detail no one on the Rodian side objects to at the time.

c. 1790 DE

First extraction contracts

What begins as trade curdles into arrangement: Rodian labor mines the vein at a scale no reverent tradition ever used.

c. 1793 DE

The first refusal

A single mining settlement declines to sign an extraction contract. It is quietly bypassed rather than punished — for now.

c. 1796 DE

The currency reform

Human coinage is introduced alongside traditional barter, ostensibly for convenience. Within a decade it is the only accepted form of payment for anything requiring imported tools.

c. 1800 DE

The Small-Burning Law lapses

Centuries of measured restraint end within a single generation. No formal repeal is recorded — it simply stops being enforced once it stops being profitable to enforce.

c. 1804 DE

The first mining deaths blamed on speed

A tunnel collapse, worked faster than any Rodian crew would have chosen to work it, kills eleven. It is recorded as an accident.

c. 1810 DE

Mass excavation begins in earnest

The mountain is worked by the ton for the first time in Rodian history, for purposes no Rodian tradition would have chosen.

c. 1815 DE

The first Rodian overseer appointed

A Ròdaich is given authority over other Rodian miners, answerable to human administrators — the first visible fracture within Rodian society itself, not just between it and the humans.

c. 1822 DE

The Muileann chiar silver dispute

Human interests attempt to claim the silver seam at Muileann chiar as well. It is one of the few resources the Ròdaich successfully keep fully under their own control throughout this age.

c. 1830 DE

The first Rodian child sent abroad

A single family, hoping to secure better treatment, sends a son to be educated in the humans' home country. He is never recorded returning.

c. 1836 DE

The tithe increase

The portion of mined obsidian claimed by human administrators rises sharply, with no corresponding rise in what Rodian communities receive.

1848 DE

The first of the immortal generation

A child born into the mining camps is exposed to more raw ore across her lifetime than any Rodian in living memory — and does not age the way the people around her do. Her name is Aisling.

c. 1850 DE

The condition goes unnamed

Whatever is happening to the child born two years earlier has no word yet, in any language spoken on the island. It will be decades before anyone tries to coin one.

c. 1852 DE

A' Phlàigh Uaine

The Green Death moves through the mining camps — the ore dust itself is blamed, though no formal study is ever completed. Twenty-six settlements record cases.

c. 1856 DE

The first work-refusal strike

An entire mining settlement halts extraction for eleven days over conditions worsened by the plague two years prior. It ends when food supplies, controlled by human administrators, run out.

c. 1860 DE

Muileann chiar expands

The silver town grows alongside the obsidian operation, human demand for both metals accelerating extraction the reverent tradition never would have permitted.

c. 1865 DE

The second currency reform

Rodian barter goods are formally devalued against human coinage a second time, on terms set entirely by the human administration.

c. 1870 DE

The five lighthouses complete

Taigh-solais Baile chrom, Baile ìseal, Muileann bheag, Àth dhearg and Tobar dhearg — the full ring of coastal beacons is finished, built to serve trade that is by now extraction in every way but name.

c. 1873 DE

Gorta Baile chrom

The Great Hunger follows a failed harvest across thirty-four settlements, worsened by a mining workforce too depleted by mass conscription to farm its own land.

c. 1878 DE

The first organized petition

Rodian custodians, for the first time acting jointly rather than settlement by settlement, submit a formal grievance to the human administration. It is not answered.

c. 1880 DE

Resentment first recorded

The earliest surviving Ròdaich grievance texts date from this period — not yet organized resistance, but the first written acknowledgment that something has gone wrong.

c. 1885 DE

Tiormachd Achadh shean

The Long Drought settles over the island's dry season for three consecutive years, straining a population already stretched thin by extraction quotas.

c. 1890 DE

The first mixed-blood inheritance dispute

A legal case over whether a child of one Rodian and one human parent may inherit customary mining rights sets a precedent the administration writes entirely to its own benefit.

c. 1895 DE

The night-shift order

Extraction is extended to run continuously, day and night, for the first time — the mountain given no season of rest at all.

1900 DE

Fionnan born

A second child manifests the same unprecedented bonding Aisling did half a century earlier. The exposure is not a one-time accident. It is an ongoing industrial condition.

c. 1905 DE

The word finally coined

Facing two confirmed cases, Rodian custodians settle on a term for what's happening: fuil-ghuail, "coal-blood" — rendered in the humans' tongue as obsidian-touched. It is meant clinically. It will outlive that intention.

c. 1908 DE

The third currency reform

Traditional Rodian barter is formally abolished as legal tender entirely. Every transaction on the island now runs through human coinage.

1913 DE

Lorccan born

A third case, born into a mining town rather than a mining camp — proof the phenomenon has outgrown any single site.

1914 DE

Cian born

A fourth, within a year of the third — the closest the immortal generation comes to being born in a cluster rather than a scatter.

c. 1916 DE

Scàineadh Cill ghlas

The Rending — a fault line tears open in the hills east of the capital, around Cill ghlas during a routine excavation, killing dozens and closing an entire mining district for a generation.

c. 1915 DE

The first open confrontation

A mining accident, blamed by Rodian workers on human indifference to safety, becomes the first recorded violent clash between the two populations.

c. 1918 DE

The second organized petition

This one includes an implicit threat rather than a plea. It is the first Rodian document the human administration bothers to respond to — with increased security, not concessions.

c. 1920 DE

The taverns fortify

Roadside houses along the old mining routes begin doubling as informal militia waypoints — infrastructure quietly repurposing itself for a conflict not yet declared.

c. 1922 DE

The first coordinated work stoppage

Multiple mining regions halt extraction on the same day for the first time — proof that Rodian resistance, for all its scattering, could act as one when it chose to.

c. 1925 DE

Imperial overreach begins openly

Human administrators begin issuing directives with no Rodian counsel sought at all — the polite fiction of permission finally dropped.

c. 1926 DE

The confiscation edict

Human authorities formally claim the right to seize any Rodian dwelling deemed necessary for extraction expansion. It is the single most-cited grievance in every surviving account of what came next.

1928 DE

War declared

Rodian resistance, coordinated for the first time across multiple mining regions, moves from grievance to open conflict.

c. 1929 DE

The burning of the third currency's records

Rebels destroy the human administration's tithe ledgers in three separate raids — an act later chroniclers treat as symbolically decisive even though its practical effect was small.

c. 1930 DE

The Severance

The war ends with the crossing itself closed. Whatever let humans reach Rodos shuts. The humans still on the island are shepherded to the northern territory their descendants hold today. Cian and Lorccan, not yet twenty, grow up in its immediate wake.

AGE V

An Aois Ùr — The New Age

post-1930 DE – 2026 DE

Defining event: The Severance

The Ròdaich reclaim the whole island. They do not restore the old reverent practice. The mine keeps running at the scale the occupiers built it to, because by now the extraction benefits the people holding power directly. The colonizers are gone. Their method isn't.

c. 1930 DE

The Tuathaich homeland fixed

Human descendants stranded by the Severance are formally settled in the island's north — a confinement, not a homecoming, though later generations will simply call it home.

c. 1931 DE

Rìoghachd Ròdais declared

The state formally restyles itself a kingdom in form, whatever it remains in function — the same contradiction its own records still carry today.

c. 1932 DE

Niall crowned

The first ruler of the restored kingdom takes the throne at Cathair dhearg — the opening name in the line the state's own chronicles keep unbroken from here forward.

c. 1933 DE

Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol

The Great Wave strikes the southern coast without warning, devastating Cnoc chaol, Cuan dhearg and Achadh mhin in a single night — the first disaster the new state responds to on its own terms.

c. 1934 DE

The old human coinage withdrawn

Every remaining piece of human currency is formally demonetized. A new Rìoghachd coinage, minted with obsidian-dust ink in its seal, replaces it within a year.

c. 1935 DE

Creideamh nan Ròdach formalized

The Obsidian Rite becomes organized doctrine rather than diffuse folk reverence, built around the leyline and the unbroken line of the obsidian-touched.

c. 1936 DE

The first post-Severance census

The state counts its own population for the first time since reclaiming the island — and quietly notes, without further comment, exactly how few obsidian-touched individuals actually exist.

c. 1938 DE

Creideamh nan Tuathach persists

Tuathaich communities keep their own folk-shamanic tradition alive in the north — including, quietly, the First Canoe fragment the Ròdaich priesthood still calls superstition.

c. 1939 DE

The border wall proposed

A formal boundary between Ròdaich and Tuathaich territory is proposed for the first time. It is rejected — not out of principle, records suggest, but because it's considered unnecessary. The two populations are already staying apart on their own.

c. 1940 DE

Extraction resumes at full scale

The mine's post-Severance yield matches, then exceeds, its colonial-era output. Nothing about the method changed. Only who profits did.

c. 1942 DE

The guild system restored

The old apprentice-binding custom from the Holy Age is revived in modified form, now explicitly reserved for Ròdaich applicants only.

c. 1945 DE

Leabharlann Muileann chaol founded

A great library is established at Muileann chaol — the institution that will eventually keep the most complete surviving record of everything in this chronicle.

c. 1947 DE

The first royal chronicler appointed

A formal office is created specifically to maintain the kingdom's records — the direct ancestor of the archive this very document draws from.

c. 1950 DE

Fèill Muileann òg established

An annual fair becomes the island's largest recurring gathering, drawing trade from every province the state controls.

c. 1951 DE

Niall dies; Brìghde crowned

His daughter succeeds him without contest — mortal succession within an immortal-adjacent aristocracy, unremarkable at the time, unusual in hindsight.

c. 1952 DE

Còmhrag Ceann leathan established

A brutal annual melee tournament begins at Ceann leathan, ostensibly a martial tradition, increasingly a release valve for a population with fewer and fewer other outlets.

1953 DE

Splatter and Dodge's generation born

A cluster of births in the Class-08-adjacent shunting families — the generation that will grow up to become Dubhan's closest allies, Seachran and Brean among them.

c. 1955 DE

The Depletion first acknowledged

Survey reports begin quietly noting the vein's declining yield. The state does not act on them for years.

c. 1957 DE

Eòghan crowned

Brìghde's reign ends young — no cause is recorded publicly. Her cousin Eòghan takes the throne amid the first real succession tension the restored kingdom has faced.

1958 DE

Dubhan born

Born mortal, into the exact confinement the Severance created, decades after the war that closed the island to the people his line descends from.

c. 1962 DE

The second post-Severance census

The gap between Ròdaich and Tuathaich living standards is, for the first time, measured rather than simply observed. The report is not made public.

c. 1968 DE

The mine-safety reforms

New excavation rules are introduced after a smaller echo of the old Scàineadh Cill ghlas collapse. They apply to Ròdaich workers. Enforcement in Tuathaich-adjacent sites is inconsistent, records suggest deliberately so.

c. 1970 DE

Dubhan's childhood

He grows up watching an eternally young caste hold every real advantage on an island his own ancestors were pushed to the edge of. The obsidian was never his to inherit.

c. 1975 DE

The Feallsanachd an Fhèidh revival

The old philosophical school, dormant since the colonial period, reopens — its founding question, why the vein answers some and not others, suddenly urgent again as the vein itself starts running short.

c. 1980 DE

The failed vein expansion

A costly attempt to locate a second obsidian deposit elsewhere on the island turns up nothing — the same result the Holy Age's failed attempt found centuries earlier, forgotten and rediscovered the hard way.

c. 1985 DE

The Depletion becomes undeniable

What was a quiet survey note in 1955 is now an open crisis. The last untouched sections of the vein are identified — and are smaller than anyone wants to admit.

c. 1987 DE

Mairead crowned

Eòghan's reign ends in the same decade the Depletion becomes public knowledge — his successor inherits a kingdom whose founding resource is visibly failing for the first time in the restored era.

c. 1990 DE

Dubhan begins his experiments

Early, unsuccessful attempts to synthesize a substitute fuel from mining byproducts — failures the historical record barely bothers to name.

c. 1993 DE

The third post-Severance census

Population figures are published openly for the first time in Rìoghachd history — a small, symbolic transparency reform that changes nothing structurally.

c. 1996 DE

The library expansion

Leabharlann Muileann chaol doubles its holdings, absorbing several smaller provincial archives into one central record — the moment Rodian history starts being kept in one place instead of many.

c. 2000 DE

The first working dubhan

A crude but functional synthetic fuel is produced. It is not yet an economy. It is, for now, one man's answer to a crisis the caste in power still hasn't fully faced.

c. 2002 DE

Cathal crowned

Mairead's reign ends as Dubhan's early experiments are still failing more than succeeding — Cathal inherits a kingdom that doesn't yet know how close its own solution already is.

c. 2005 DE

The lighthouses converted

The five coastal beacons, still running on obsidian-lit flame for over a century, are the first infrastructure formally converted to dubhan power.

c. 2008 DE

The tavern circuit modernizes

Roadside houses along the old mining routes install dubhan lamps, ending centuries of oil-and-wick lighting in a single decade.

c. 2010 DE

The word spreads before the fuel does

"Dubhan" begins appearing in common speech to mean the fuel itself, not the man, years before the economy built on it is complete.

c. 2012 DE

The dubhan economy takes shape

Markets, military logistics, and shipping begin shifting wholesale to the new fuel. The obsidian caste, watching its own power source fail, does not object.

c. 2014 DE

The fourth currency reform

Rìoghachd coinage is re-pegged to dubhan output rather than obsidian reserves for the first time — an economic admission, quietly made, of which resource actually runs the kingdom now.

c. 2015 DE

Tuathaich resistance organizes

A cluster of northern communities refuse to fold into the new economy, continuing to work the last workable coal seams rather than trade independence for dependence on one man's fuel.

c. 2016 DE

The library's Tuathaich wing proposed

A formal request to archive Tuathaich oral history alongside the official Ròdaich record is submitted to Leabharlann Muileann chaol. It is tabled, not rejected — and remains tabled to this day.

2018 DE

First skirmishes

Minor, unrecorded clashes between state enforcers and Tuathaich coal-holdouts begin — too small individually to name, together the clear runway into open war.

c. 2019 DE

The mobilization order

Regiments not called to active service since the Severance itself are reformed and re-equipped, openly, months before anyone in power admits a war is coming.

April 11, 2020 DE

Battle of Àth leathan

The first major engagement of An Cogadh Fada, the Long War. The state, already leaning on Dubhan's fuel to prop up its fading power, moves against the holdouts directly.

c. 2020 DE

The wartime tithe

A temporary tax increase, justified by the war, on every dubhan transaction in the kingdom. It is never repealed once the war ends.

December 10, 2021 DE

Battle of Muileann ghlas

The war's second and final major battle. The Tuathaich holdouts are broken within a single year's campaign — descendants of the humans once pushed to the island's edge, pushed back again, this time by a fuel one of their own invented.

2021 DE

An Cogadh Fada ends

Nine regiments and four fleets, still active today, were raised in this campaign; each is named for the town it is stationed in.

c. 2022 DE

The dubhan monopoly completes

With the last organized resistance gone, Dubhan's fuel becomes functionally mandatory across the entire island economy.

c. 2023 DE

The library's war archive sealed

Leabharlann Muileann chaol's complete Long War record is formally sealed from public access for a term of fifty years — the only sealed archive in the kingdom's history.

c. 2024 DE

The reparations debate

A proposal to formally compensate Tuathaich communities for wartime losses is raised in the capital and quietly allowed to die without a vote.

2026 DE

The present

The coal is all but gone. The dubhan economy runs on a name that used to belong to one man. The Ròdaich hold the capital and the sea. The Tuathaich hold the north, a grievance five years old, and a First-Canoe memory the people who twice defeated them still call folklore.

Words Born from History

Twelve entries where a specific dated event left a permanent mark on the language itself — the same mechanism as "dubhan," repeated. Each is built from real Ròdais vocabulary and real grammar, not invented from nothing.

dubhan n., lowercase
diesel; the synthetic fuel itself
from: Dubhan's Fire (c. 2000 DE)
From Dubhan's own name (dubh + -an, "the black one"), worn down into a common noun the way English wore down a man's title into a sandwich.
fadachadh v.n. (verbal noun)
to be crushed decisively after holding out too long; "am fadachadh" = a total, one-season defeat
from: An Cogadh Fada, the Long War (2020–2021 DE)
The plain verbal noun of fadaich ("lengthen", from fada, the war's own name) given a narrowed, bitter sense by An Cogadh Fada. "Chaidh am fadachadh" — "they got long-warred" — is already circulating among Tuathaich speakers as gallows humor about their own defeat.
scàinte adj.
reeling, structurally shaken (of a person, not just a building)
from: Scàineadh Cill ghlas, the Rending (c. 1916 DE)
The plain adjective from "scàineadh" (a rending/fault-crack) picked up a figurative sense after the disaster — the same drift that gave English "shattered" its emotional meaning alongside its literal one.
uaine adj., extended sense
sickly, plague-marked — alongside its plain meaning "green"
from: A' Phlàigh Uaine, the Green Death (c. 1852 DE)
A color word gaining a second, darker sense from a single event — parallel to how English "green" came to mean inexperienced, or nauseated, from otherwise unrelated color associations.
mar a thàinig Niall idiom
"the way Niall came" — said of any unusually smooth transfer of power
from: Niall crowned (c. 1932 DE)
A ruler's name converted directly into a fixed idiomatic phrase, the commonest way any language turns a specific memorable event into permanent vocabulary.
dubhan-tùsail n.
a rough, failed-seeming first attempt that later turns out to matter
from: Dubhan's early failed experiments (c. 1990 DE)
Dubhan (the name/fuel) + tùsail ("of the beginning") — used now for any early prototype, in any field, that looked like a dead end before it wasn't.
tonn n., extended sense
a sudden, overwhelming change, used metaphorically ("tha e san tonn" — "he's in the wave of it")
from: Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol, the Great Wave (c. 1933 DE)
The plain word for "wave" gaining the same figurative extension English gives "tide" or "wave" for sudden large-scale change — a near-universal metaphor, arrived at independently here.
a chur san Leabharlann idiom
"to put it in the Library" — to make something official, settled, beyond dispute
from: Leabharlann Muileann chaol founded (c. 1945 DE)
An institution's name becoming shorthand for the authority it holds — the same mechanism that gave English phrases like "it's in the record" their weight.
seann-chunntas n.
an old grievance nobody in power will act on but nobody has cancelled either
from: The tabled Tuathaich archive request (c. 2016 DE) / the dead reparations debate (c. 2024 DE)
Sean (old) + cunntas (an account, a reckoning) — coined by Tuathaich speakers specifically, and pointedly never adopted into official Ròdaich usage.
roghnaich v., narrowed sense
to choose permanent exile specifically, as opposed to its plain general sense "to choose"
from: The Choosing (Age III)
An ordinary verb given a specific, weighted secondary meaning by one enormous historical choice — its plain sense never lost, its narrow sense unmistakable in context.
a' Scaradh n. (with the article, capitalized in speech)
the Sundering, but also used loosely for any permanent, chosen parting between people who once belonged together
from: The Sundering (Age III) and, later, reapplied informally to the Severance
The same root generalized twice, once for a chosen departure and once, later, for a forced one — a single word doing service for two historical wounds that don't otherwise resemble each other.
Aisling-aois n.
an unnaturally long span of youth in anyone, used half-jokingly of ordinary people who simply look young for their years
from: The first of the immortal generation (1848 DE)
Aisling's own name + aois (age) — the rare case of an in-story, in-language nod: an eponym coined from the exact person this whole project has already spent hours discussing, and the one entry on this list where the "joke" and the "myth" are the same word.

Nine named faiths are on record for Rodos, spanning the full chronicle: Seann Spioradan nan Ròdach (ancestral, Holy Age), Creideamh nan Ròdach and Creideamh an t-Seabhaig (the Obsidian Rite's orthodox and older forms), Creideamh nan Tuathach (Tuathaich folk tradition), Creideamh na Scairpe Buidhe, Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, Creideamh an Aon-adharcaich Dhuibh, Creideamh a' Mhadaidh-allaidh, and Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise — a heterodox cult whose name translates, roughly, to "the Mystery of the Grey Night."

Two suppressed histories bracket this chronicle on purpose: the First Canoe legend at one end, the Tuathaich's own memory of the Long War at the other. Both are filed as folklore by whoever currently holds power. Neither is resolved here.
