# Names

This chapter describes how the places and peoples of Rodos are named in Ròdais: the generic
nouns that head a place name, the lenition that marks the word after them, the agreement of broad
and slender vowels, the older names of the Seann-Dhaoine, and the names of the peoples themselves.
The rest of the grammar is in *The grammar in brief*, *Word forms* and *Sentences*.

## The look of a Ròdais name
Ròdais spells its consonants as Irish does (the digraphs *bh, mh, dh, gh, ch, sh*, and the
diphthong *ao*) but marks long vowels as Scottish Gaelic does, with a **grave** accent (*à è ì ò
ù*) where Irish has the acute. That one difference is the visible signature of the island's
names: a reader who knows either language will half-recognize a Ròdais name and see at once that
it is not quite either one.

## Lenition in names
The second word in a place name is *lenited*: its initial consonant softens, as a genitive or a
word after most generic nouns does in Irish and Scottish Gaelic. Written as sound changes at the
start of a word:

```
m > mh / #_        b > bh / #_        c > ch / #_
p > ph / #_         t > th / #_        d > dh / #_
g > gh / #_          s > sh / #_        f > fh / #_
```

`#_` means "at the start of a word." So *mòr* ("big") becomes *mhòr* after a generic noun:
**Cathair mhòr**, "big city." Three further points hold:

- **Lenited *f* is spelled *fh*, and stays written even though it is silent.** *Fiadhaich*
  (wild) lenites to *fhiadhaich*, not *hiadhaich*.
- ***S* does not lenite before *c, p, t, m* or *k*.** The clusters *sc-, sp-, st-, sm-* resist
  mutation, and so does the *sk-* of the Old Ones' names: a word starting *Sk-* stays *Sk-* rather
  than becoming *Sh-*. That is why the name is **Seann Skell**.
- **A lenited proper noun keeps its capital letter**, with the *h* written right after it: *Dunn*,
  where it is lenited, becomes *Dhunn*, not *dhunn* (after *seann* it is not lenited at all; see
  below).

**"Always lenited" belongs to names.** In everyday Ròdais an adjective after a masculine noun is
not lenited (*baile gorm*). Place names such as *Baile ghorm, Cnoc bheag, Dùn thais* keep the
always-lenited form as a fixed naming pattern (see *The grammar in brief* §4).

## Caol le caol, leathan le leathan
*Slender with slender, broad with broad* is the defining law of Gaelic spelling, and Ròdais
keeps it. Vowels fall into two classes:

- **Broad**: *a, o, u, à, ò, ù*
- **Slender**: *e, i, è, ì*

Inside a single word, any consonant (or consonant cluster) with a vowel on both sides needs those
two vowels to agree in class. This is why Gaelic suffixes so often come in matched pairs, a broad
form and a slender form, depending on what they attach to. The ending of the small rivers' names
works the same way: *eas* (a word for a waterfall or rapids) is the slender form, *as* the broad
one, and the choice follows the last vowel of the root. *Ciar* + the ending gives **Ciaras**, not
*Ciareas*: although the word starts with a slender-looking *i*, the vowel touching the ending is
the *a* in *-iar*, and *a* is broad.

The law governs only pairs of vowels with a consonant *between* them. Two vowels side by side with
nothing between, a diphthong such as the *ai* in *Àrsaidh*, are free.

**Two-word names are exempt.** *Cathair mhòr* need not satisfy the law, because it binds only
within one written word; it says nothing about the boundary between two words in a phrase.

Every one of the island's 157 river names keeps the law.

## The names of the Old Ones
Before the Ròdaich, an older people lived on the island: the **Seann-Dhaoine**, "the Old Ones."
Their surviving place names are marked with **Seann**, the old, irregular form of *sean* ("old")
that stands before a noun and lenites it, as in *seann bhaile*, "old town." So an Old Ones'
settlement reads **Seann Chwen, Seann Bhral**, "old Chwen," "old Bral." The roots themselves
(*Bral-, Cwen-, Tarr-, Vell-, Morn-, Skell-, Brenn-, Toll-, Warr-, Dunn-*) are not Gaelic. They
stand for a language that came before and did not survive except in names; the word that marks
them as old is Ròdais, the Ròdaich's own way of speaking of them.

**Seann does not lenite *d*, *t* or *s*.** After a word ending in *n* the dentals stay plain, as
in *seann duine*, *seann taigh*. So the names are **Seann Dunn, Seann Tarr, Seann Toll**, while
*Seann Chwen* and *Seann Bhral* are lenited; and by the rule for *sk-* above, **Seann Skell**. The
shires named after these towns take the same forms.

About one port in four still carries this older layer instead of a full Ròdais
generic-plus-qualifier name. Readers who know Scottish place names will recognise the pattern from
the Old Norse *-vík* that survives inside Gaelic coastal names such as Ullapool: borrowed and
fossilized, not translated.

## Generic elements (place-name heads)
Irish and Scottish place names are built from a small closed set of generic nouns plus a
describing qualifier (*Dún Laoghaire, Cill Airne, Gleann Domhain*). Ròdais names are built the same
way, and the generic follows the kind of settlement being named:

| Role | Generics used |
|---|---|
| Capital | Cathair, Dùn |
| City | Cathair, Baile Mòr, Dùn |
| Town | Baile, Àth, Cnoc, Muileann |
| Village | Cill, Tobar, Achadh, Doire |
| Hamlet | Tobar, Cill, Carraig |
| Port (any size) | + Ros, Ceann, Cuan, Caol, Inis |

*(Cathair* = stone fort/city · *Dùn* = fortified hill · *Baile* =
settlement · *Àth* = ford · *Cnoc* = hill · *Muileann* = mill · *Cill* =
holy cell, shrine · *Tobar* = well · *Achadh* = field · *Doire* = oak grove ·
*Carraig* = rock · *Ros* = headland · *Ceann* = headland/point · *Cuan*
= harbor · *Caol* = strait · *Inis* = island.)*

## Qualifiers
Color, size, and character words, always lenited when they follow a
generic: *mòr* (big), *beag* (small), *dubh* (black), *geal/bàn*
(white), *dearg/ruadh* (red), *uaine/glas* (green-grey), *fada* (long),
*domhain* (deep), *ìseal* (low), *fiadhaich* (wild), *naomh* (holy),
*fionn* (fair), *ciar* (dusky), *gorm* (blue), *sean* (old), *òg*
(young), *garbh* (rough), *min* (smooth), *caol* (narrow), *leathan*
(wide), *crom* (bent), *dìreach* (straight), *àrsaidh* (ancient).

## Rivers
Major rivers (length at or above the island's median) take **Abhainn**
("river") plus a lenited qualifier: *Abhainn mhòr, Abhainn dhubh*. Minor
streams take a root plus the broad/slender-matched *-as/-eas* ending
described above: *Fionnas, Ciaras, Àrsaidheas*.

## Shires
A shire takes the name of its seat, so a shire's name always matches its chief town: *Siorrachd
Cathair mhòr* is simply "the shire of [the city] Cathair mhòr." *Siorrachd* means shire or county,
as it does in Scottish Gaelic. The island's 123 shires, 505 towns and 157 rivers are all named on
the patterns in this chapter.

## The state and its peoples
- **Ròdaich**: the majority, the people of Rodos. **The coal-blooded, the *fuil-ghuail*, are born
  among them**: Aisling, Cian, Lorccan and Fionnan are Ròdaich. The name is formed in the usual
  Gaelic way: root + *-aich* ("people of X"). They hold the capital, *Cathair dhearg*.
- **Tuathaich**: the northern minority, **ordinary humans**, Dubhan's people, mortal and
  peripheral: powerless, pushed to the northern edge of the island, holding no part of the capital.
  From *tuath*, "north," by the same *-aich* formation as Ròdaich, so the two names read as siblings
  on the page even though their circumstances do not.
- **Seann-Dhaoine**: "the Old Ones," the near-vanished people who held the coasts before the
  Ròdaich, source of the *Seann*-marked coastal names above.
- The state's full title is **Rìoghachd Ròdais**, "the Kingdom of Rodos," which carries the
  island's own name in its own language.
- The capital is **Cathair dhearg**, "the red city."
