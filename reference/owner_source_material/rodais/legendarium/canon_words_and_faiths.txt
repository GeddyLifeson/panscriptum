
Words Born from History
Twelve entries where a specific dated event left a permanent mark on the language itself — the same mechanism as "dubhan," repeated. Each is built from real Dia-thìris vocabulary and real grammar, not invented from nothing.
dubhan
n., lowercase
diesel; the synthetic fuel itself
from: Dubhan's Fire (c. 2000 DE)
From Dubhan's own name (dubh + -an, "the black one"), worn down into a common noun the way English wore down a man's title into a sandwich.
fadachadh
v.n. (verbal noun)
to be crushed decisively after holding out too long; "am fadachadh" = a total, one-season defeat
from: An Cogadh Fada, the Long War (2020–2021 DE)
The plain verbal noun of 
fadaich
 ("lengthen", from 
fada
, the war's own name) given a narrowed, bitter sense by An Cogadh Fada. "Chaidh am fadachadh" — "they got long-warred" — is already circulating among Tuathaich speakers as gallows humor about their own defeat.
scàinte
adj.
reeling, structurally shaken (of a person, not just a building)
from: Scàineadh Cill ghlas, the Rending (c. 1916 DE)
The plain adjective from "scàineadh" (a rending/fault-crack) picked up a figurative sense after the disaster — the same drift that gave English "shattered" its emotional meaning alongside its literal one.
uaine
adj., extended sense
sickly, plague-marked — alongside its plain meaning "green"
from: A' Phlàigh Uaine, the Green Death (c. 1852 DE)
A color word gaining a second, darker sense from a single event — parallel to how English "green" came to mean inexperienced, or nauseated, from otherwise unrelated color associations.
mar a thàinig Niall
idiom
"the way Niall came" — said of any unusually smooth transfer of power
from: Niall crowned (c. 1932 DE)
A ruler's name converted directly into a fixed idiomatic phrase, the commonest way any language turns a specific memorable event into permanent vocabulary.
dubhan-tùsail
n.
a rough, failed-seeming first attempt that later turns out to matter
from: Dubhan's early failed experiments (c. 1990 DE)
Dubhan (the name/fuel) + tùsail ("of the beginning") — used now for any early prototype, in any field, that looked like a dead end before it wasn't.
tonn
n., extended sense
a sudden, overwhelming change, used metaphorically ("tha e san tonn" — "he's in the wave of it")
from: Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol, the Great Wave (c. 1933 DE)
The plain word for "wave" gaining the same figurative extension English gives "tide" or "wave" for sudden large-scale change — a near-universal metaphor, arrived at independently here.
a chur san Leabharlann
idiom
"to put it in the Library" — to make something official, settled, beyond dispute
from: Leabharlann Muileann chaol founded (c. 1945 DE)
An institution's name becoming shorthand for the authority it holds — the same mechanism that gave English phrases like "it's in the record" their weight.
seann-chunntas
n.
an old grievance nobody in power will act on but nobody has cancelled either
from: The tabled Tuathaich archive request (c. 2016 DE) / the dead reparations debate (c. 2024 DE)
Sean (old) + cunntas (an account, a reckoning) — coined by Tuathaich speakers specifically, and pointedly never adopted into official Dia-thìrich usage.
roghnaich
v., narrowed sense
to choose permanent exile specifically, as opposed to its plain general sense "to choose"
from: The Choosing (Age III)
An ordinary verb given a specific, weighted secondary meaning by one enormous historical choice — its plain sense never lost, its narrow sense unmistakable in context.
a' Scaradh
n. (with the article, capitalized in speech)
the Sundering, but also used loosely for any permanent, chosen parting between people who once belonged together
from: The Sundering (Age III) and, later, reapplied informally to the Severance
The same root generalized twice, once for a chosen departure and once, later, for a forced one — a single word doing service for two historical wounds that don't otherwise resemble each other.
Aisling-aois
n.
an unnaturally long span of youth in anyone, used half-jokingly of ordinary people who simply look young for their years
from: The first of the immortal generation (1848 DE)
Aisling's own name + aois (age) — the rare case of an in-story, in-language nod: an eponym coined from the exact person this whole project has already spent hours discussing, and the one entry on this list where the "joke" and the "myth" are the same word.
Nine named faiths are on record for Dia-thìr, spanning the full chronicle: Seann Spioradan nan Dia-thìreach (ancestral, Holy Age), Creideamh nan Dia-thìreach and Creideamh an t-Seabhaig (the Coal Rite's orthodox and older forms), Creideamh nan Tuathach (Tuathaich folk tradition), Creideamh na Scairpe Buidhe, Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, Creideamh an Aon-adharcaich Dhuibh, Creideamh a' Mhadaidh-allaidh, and Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise — a heterodox cult whose name translates, roughly, to "the Mystery of the Grey Night."
Two suppressed histories bracket this chronicle on purpose: the First Canoe legend at one end, the Tuathaich's own memory of the Long War at the other. Both are filed as folklore by whoever currently holds power. Neither is resolved here.
