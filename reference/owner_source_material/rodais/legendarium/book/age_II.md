# The Second Book: An Aois Naomh, the Holy Age

*3,000 – 40 BDE*

> *Làn an dà bhois, agus gun tuilleadh.*
> The fill of the two palms, and no more.
>
> — said of the stone cup at Dùn ìseal

## I. Of the Binding of the First Flame

Before record the hill-folk of Rodos burned the black ore by the handful, shared their grain before the storms, and said at every gathering that they had not come to the island but had always been on it. Nine keepers, one drawn by lot for each district of the hills, watched the slab over the crack at Tobar dhìreach. All of it was custom and none of it was law. So the Ancient Age ended, as the tellers have it: everything in its place, and nothing yet sworn.

The Holy Age begins with a swearing. On {{date:II-0001}} a custodianship was declared over the vein beneath the central mountains. It is called the Binding of the First Flame. It was the first order on Rodos that was at once a faith and a government, and it came centuries before anything like a king. Whoever later ruled the roads and harbours of the island ruled beside it, never over it, for as long as the age lasted.

The keepers of the slab and the households who swore the Binding met at Dùn ìseal under the mountain, and on {{date:II-0002}} they chose Beathag Bhàn to hold the first flame and answer for what was taken from the vein. The Keepers' recited roll begins with her name. Nothing is remembered of her before it. The Library distrusts a roll that begins with a bare name, but there is nothing to set against this one.

Nine households round the mountain's foot, one for each district of the old keepers of the slab, took the flame's tending in turn, each sending a son or daughter to sit with it through a season. These were the nine hearths, and more later houses claim descent from them than nine hearths could have founded. Over the flame they raised Talla na Lasrach, the Hall of the Flame: dry stone and turf, one door facing the vein and none facing the sea. It was rebuilt many times on the same footings, and the footings are all that anyone can now swear to.

Beside the Binding, and older in its roots, the first continuous folk tradition took shape: Seann Spioradan nan Ròdach, reverence for the ancestors, the vein, and the weather that decided the mining season. Watchers on the cairns above Cnoc ghlas read the cloud on the high passes to tell the Hall when the galleries might open, and the record says they were chosen with more care than the Keeper.

The first Keepers ruled mostly by forbidding. No fire was to be lit where the Doire uaine hills could see it, in obedience to old inscriptions there, and no one in the record admits to breaking the ban. Beathag Bhàn walked to Làrach an Teampaill, the ruined temple of the Old Ones, found its altar already turned toward the mountain, and declared that the place was not the Hall's to keep. No Keeper set foot inside it for the rest of the age. Whose it was, they did not say, and it seems they did not know.

Beathag Bhàn died at Dùn ìseal and was laid on the slope facing the vein, not the sea. The nine hearths chose her sister's son, Muireach Liath, to keep the flame after her.

## II. Of Muireach Liath and the Silver

Muireach Liath fixed the first common measure of ore: a lidded basket of plaited heather, one to a household, filled once a season. But he is remembered for two refusals.

The first concerned a stream. Allt an Àigh was said to grant fortune to whoever drank from it in a certain phase of the moon, and it had become a place of pilgrimage that has never fully stopped. Pilgrims wore a path to it from the river landing at Caol shean and walked it by night, so as to reach the water in the right quarter. Muireach Liath ruled that the stream belonged to the ancestors and the weather, not to the vein, and that the Hall would not keep it. That ruling is the first visible parting between the Keepers' rite and the older reverence, and both sides later cited it as proof they were right. In the same spirit the Keepers let the sick be carried to the warm springs below Muileann ruadh and bathed at dawn, because the water's heat came from the mountain without anyone breaking the stone.

The second concerned silver. In the far east a seam was found near the great vein but distinct from it, at the place called Muileann chiar: the island's first confirmed resource that was not obsidian. Fearchar Airgeadach, a basket-carrier of the nine hearths, was the first to smelt it and hammer it into rings. He fed his furnace with wood and peat, and the chroniclers call it the first fire in the record that answered to no one in the Hall. When the Hall was asked whether the seam fell under the flame, Muireach Liath answered:

> *Chan eil fuil san airgead, agus chan ann leis a' bheinn a tha e.*
> There is no blood in silver, and it is not the mountain's.

Silver does not bleed colour when broken, and so it was not holy. Muileann chiar was left free to dig as deep as it liked, and it dug. Every later quarrel over the silver began from that sentence. Carriers took the rings down to Ros fhionn and traded them for salt fish and sealskin: the first trade in the record not made in food, and the first with a remembered price. One ring bought a boatload.

Muireach Liath died at Dùn ìseal, keeping the flame. The nine hearths passed over three candidates of other lines and chose his daughter, Gormshuil nic Mhuirich. Her kin came to be called Sliochd Mhuirich, the house of Muireach, the first custodian house known by a name and not by a hearth, and the office stayed with them for many generations. No account says the other hearths objected. The chroniclers note that the surviving accounts are the Hall's own.

## III. Of the Stag and the Stone Cup

In Gormshuil's years a school arose in the east that would become the most widespread on the island: Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the Philosophy of the Stag. Sìleas nic Coinnich, a cutter of Muileann chiar, had seen the ore bleed colour freely under some picks and sullenly under others, and she would not leave the matter alone. She taught in Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, and her hearers kept her question as she left it:

> *Carson a tha an fhèith a' freagairt do làimh seach làimh eile?*
> Why does the vein answer one hand and not another?

It was the first attempt to reason about the obsidian and not only revere it. Her hearers were named for a stag said to have stood in the grove through a whole lesson. The school's later name, and the stag's name, Camaran, appear only in much younger texts.

The Hall did not welcome the question. Gormshuil forbade Sìleas's hearers to cut in the galleries of Dùn ìseal, holding that a question asked of the vein is a demand made of it. For a long time after, the stag's people kept to the east and sent no cutters to the mountain.

A death made the next law. In a hard year a household at Cnoc dhubh burned its ore by the basketful. The smoke settled in the valley, and three of the household and two neighbours died of it. The case was argued at Dùn ìseal for the rest of the season.

The Keeper who measured it was Fionnlagh Dall, Fionnlagh the Blind. He had the old keepers' stone cup, a thing of the Ancient Age, re-cut in grey stone to hold exactly what a pair of cupped hands will hold, and set it on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach. He filled it by touch before the council, and no one afterward could say he had favoured anyone's hands. On {{date:II-0027}} the custom hardened into the Small-Burning Law: no household might burn more than a measured handful of ore in a season. It held for centuries.

Its first sentence fell on a household of Seann Tarr that had burned twice its measure. It lost its right for the season after, and its name went on the roll with the offence beside it. Its descendants petitioned for many centuries to have the offence struck out, without success, and the offence is the only reason they are remembered.

The Keepers carried the Law to the coasts. No ore, they ruled, might feed a beacon, since a beacon burns for strangers:

> *Cha loisc clach na beinne do choigrich.*
> The mountain's stone will not burn for strangers.

The fire on the headland at Tobar dhearg was kept with driftwood and seal fat, and so was every sea-light on the island after it.

## IV. Of the Roads, the Bridges and the Road-Peace

The Law made the Hall a distributor, and the island's roads grew along the carriers' tracks, down stepped paths such as the one to Ceann chaol. The rivers took their toll. The Abhainn dhomhain rose one night at a ford and took eleven carriers with their baskets. The Hall mourned the ore as well as the carriers, and the entry names the ore first. Fionnlagh Dall asked the council for a crossing the river could not take, and stone bridges began to join the mining paths to the coast, ancestors of Drochaid Cathair dhearg and Drochaid Ros fhionn. Above Ros fhionn, Ailean Clachair and forty masons of the nine hearths threw a single span of fitted stone over the river, and Ailean cut a hearth-sign in the keystone. Every later bridge carried one, and a bridge without one was not trusted. The masons swore an oath on the stone cup: no span to be closed in the dark, none to be paid for in ore. They kept their lodge near the western crossing in the country of Ros dhomhain.

Houses grew along the roads to feed the people on them, the ancestors of Am Buabhall Fortanach, An Taigh-òsta Grianach and the rest of the circuit. The first keeper of the house that became Am Buabhall Fortanach was Mòr nic Dhùghaill, a widow of Doire shean, who served carriers jugged buffalo from her own herd; the house serves it still. On the site of An Taigh-seinnse Mòr a turf longhouse had a hearth at each end, one for carriers going up and one for carriers coming down.

The keepers of the houses then made a law of their own: no feud might be pursued under an inn roof or within a spear's throw of its door. It is first recorded at the house on the site of An Sligeanach Fad' às, and it is the oldest law on the island that no Keeper made. The carriers put it plainly:

> *Fo mhullach an taighe, chan eil nàmh ann.*
> Under the roof of the house, there is no enemy.

The roads also carried settlers to coasts that were already named. Fishers of the nine hearths settled the harbour of Seann Mhorn and kept its name, which is not in their tongue, saying that the name came with the stones. It is the first such name in the record to be kept rather than replaced. Families walking the new road resettled Seann Warr on the western coast in the same way, and built on the house-footings they found there. The carriers' song remembers it:

> *Thog sinn air na clachan, is cha do dh'fhaighnich sinn cò leis.*
> We built upon the stones, and did not ask whose.

Fionnlagh Dall died at Dùn ìseal. At his own request he was not laid on the slope with the Keepers before him, but carried south to the old graves near Cnoc dhìreach, which no living people had claimed. His bearers took eleven days over the road and laid him among graves older than any name. The Keepers after him could not agree why he had asked it, and the record gives two answers without choosing between them. So Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, already ancient, received its first recorded burial, and an unclaimed necropolis became a claimed one. Families settled nearby to tend it, and the Hall paid them one handful of ore each a season. It is the only payment in ore the Law ever allowed.

## V. Of the Four Houses and the Reverent Centuries

In time the nine hearths chose a Keeper outside the house of Muireach: Eilidh nic Ìomhair of Dùn dhearg. Sliochd Mhuirich accepted her after a season of refusal, on condition that the stone cup never left Talla na Lasrach. It never did.

Under Eilidh the office was bound to four custodian houses: Sliochd Mhuirich of Dùn ìseal, Sliochd Ìomhair of Dùn dhearg, Sliochd Thormoid of Seann Dunn, and Sliochd Raghnaill of Seann Tarr. The other hearths kept their council seats but lost their claim to the flame. Most of the island's later nobility descends from one of the four, or says it does, and from here on the history of the age is largely theirs.

Sliochd Thormoid raised a hall and shrine at Seann Dunn, facing the mountain across the river. Its ore-lamp burned at the Law's measure, and the other houses complained that Seann Dunn's measure was always the fullest. Sliochd Raghnaill made its seat at Seann Tarr, the very town first sentenced under the Law. It kept boats instead of galleries and grew rich on fish. Its boats sailed round Eilean dhubh and came home, the first voyage in the record made to see a coast and not to fish it.

Not everyone lived inside the Law. Households stripped of their right for a third offence took to boats and raided the north-western strands, and the name spùinneadairean, plunderers, stayed with those waters. A boat out of Baile chrom was lost there in a calm, and the two men who swam home said something had risen under it. The village has kept a wake for it every year since, and relit the old beacon on the point with driftwood, as the Law required. It is the ancestor of Taigh-solais Baile chrom.

Fishers resettled Seann Bhrenn, kept its old name, and used the stone quay they found under the silt. At Cathair naomh a cairn for the dead was begun that is still growing. When the great cats' crossing turned out to run over the southern road, Eilidh ruled that no panther might be killed there in its season, and the rule held, the record says, because it cost the carriers only time.

At Dùn dhearg, Sliochd Ìomhair built stone granaries and filled them from the lowland fields against the storm season, as the old custom of sharing asked. The granaries gave the house what the Hall had never had: people who owed it food.

These were the reverent centuries. Each generation mined the same modest quantity and burned the same modest quantity, and the number of people did not change. Shepherds took the uplands at Achadh chaol; their headman sat one season on the council, was found to hold no hearth-right, and went home. Coinneach Mòr mac Tormoid, first Keeper of his house, kept the flame longer than any before him and changed nothing. He sent walkers to list the ruins of the Old Ones, and they came back with Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, Làrach an Dùin, Làrach an Teampaill and the standing stones. The list says who does not own each place. It does not say who built them, and the Keepers ruled that it should not guess. At Cnoc bheag the people fenced the old obelisk with stakes against the cattle, and every generation since has renewed the fence. Over the springs at Muileann ruadh a bath-house was roofed, and the Hall appointed a keeper of the springs, the first office it made that had nothing to do with the ore.

## VI. Of the First Rìgh and the Division of Keeping

In the generations after Coinneach Mòr the rolls grow fuller, and together their entries show an island filling out.

The silversmiths of Muileann chiar cast weights from a barley-corn to a newborn lamb, and a thing weighed there was weighed fairly. The stag's people founded a house at Cnoc bhàn, and the Keeper Seonaid Chiar let their cutters back into the galleries on oath to ask nothing of the vein aloud. The Hall called it a reconciliation; the school, a truce. Cathair mhòr built the first town wall in the record, and nobody says what it feared. Ceann leathan's harbour-master kept notched tally-sticks, now the oldest counted objects at Muileann chaol. Inis thais burned its handful on the shore so the drowned could see it, and Cuan shean's salt preserved the fish of half the island. The sunny house rose on the site of An Taigh-òsta Grianach. A roof fell in the highest gallery and killed four cutters, and Seonaid Chiar propped every gallery with oak. Carriers founded Cathair dhomhain above the western harbour, proud of keeping the measure strictly. A few miles off, at the bridge-foot where the Abhainn dhomhain meets the sea, the Red Hill's old settlement, long shrunk to a fishing town on red stone, began to grow again. Cathair dhearg was then a place carriers passed through on the way elsewhere.

At Dùn dhearg the lowland households who ate from Sliochd Ìomhair's granaries began to call its head, Goraidh mac Ìomhair, their rìgh, their king. He is the first person in any record to bear the name, and it was the granaries that gave it to him. He claimed nothing over the vein and made sure the Hall heard him say so.

On {{date:II-0078}} the council at Dùn ìseal and the rìgh agreed the division of keeping. The Keeper kept the flame and the galleries. The rìgh kept the roads, the granaries and the peace of the lowlands. The rìgh might not enter the galleries, and the Keeper might not raise spears. The division framed Rodian government for the rest of the age, and most of the age's quarrels were about which side of it a thing belonged to.

Goraidh Mòr built a timber hall at Dùn dhearg with a granary at each corner, and set a toll of one fish in twenty on the roads for the guards and the bridges. The waypoint-keepers collected it and kept a share, and the record says so. His daughter was Mòrag nan Rathad, Mòrag of the Roads, who walked every carriers' road on the island and had the bad stretches remade. She raised the house on the site of An Taigh-seinnse Òir, the first built by a ruler, and widened the western crossing of the Abhainn dhomhain with Ailean Clachair's sign recut in the keystone, and the bridge-foot town began to grow. Her son Dòmhnall Crùbach, Dòmhnall the Lame, could not walk the roads and ruled through the waypoint-keepers, who round Ceann mhin paid him what toll they judged fair, generously to themselves. When a storm took a third of Seann Chwen, the Hall and the rìgh both sent grain, and the survivors remembered that the Hall's came first. Settlers from Cathair dhomhain founded Baile àrsaidh among the yellow scorpions of the western heath and swore their oaths on a scorpion's sting kept in a box.

At Muileann chiar the stag's teachers turned the cutters' tally-notches into signs for sounds, so a teaching could be kept without a teacher. These stag-letters were the first letters of the Ròdaich, and nearly every later hand on the island comes from them. The scholars took them to Carragh Cnoc bheag and set them beside the marks on the obelisk. They matched nothing, and the scholars wrote that the stone's writers were not their teachers. It is the first thing the Ròdaich ever wrote about the Old Ones, and it says only what they were not.

Dòmhnall's niece Sìleas Dhonn made the waypoint-keepers render account of the tolls at her hall once a year. Several of them did.

## VII. Of the War of the Roads

Caol leathan in the south-west grew rich on the western fishing and the southern end of the carriers' road, and its leading family under Lachlann Ciar began to keep the road-toll and send none to Dùn dhearg. When Iain Garbh, Iain the Rough, son of Sìleas Dhonn, demanded the western tolls, Lachlann Ciar sent him a basket of fish-bones.

Iain Garbh marched west with the road-guards and the lowland levies and burned the toll-house at Muileann òg that Lachlann Ciar's people had taken. It was the first war in the record between Ròdaich and Ròdaich, and it was fought over the roads the first rìgh had been made to keep. The Hall blessed neither side. When the rìgh's messengers asked at Dùn ìseal for the Hall's handful to light the warband's hearths, the Keeper Uisdean mac Ruairidh refused them:

> *Cha bhlàthaich an lasair fir a tha a' dol a mharbhadh.*
> The flame will not warm men who are going to kill.

The warband wintered on peat. The division of keeping had been tested and it held: the rìgh had the spears and the Keeper had the flame, and neither could take the other's.

Caol leathan's men held the ford of Àth fhiadhaich for a day before giving way. Forty were dead on the two sides, and the carriers long called it the ford of the forty. Falling back, Lachlann Ciar's men burned Baile ghlas so the levies would find no grain. Its people walked to Cathair dhomhain, and many never came back. Baile chiar, south of the bridge-foot, shut its gate against both armies and fed the burned-out people through the winter. Neither side forgave it and neither side attacked it. Iain Garbh closed Caol leathan by land at the start of winter, but he could not close the harbour, and boats brought fish in by sea. When his levies began going home for the next harvest, the siege ended without an assault.

The war ended under the carriers' law. Iain Garbh and Lachlann Ciar met under the roof of Mòr nic Dhùghaill's house, where by the road-peace neither could draw. Caol leathan kept half its tolls and sent half to Dùn dhearg, and Lachlann Ciar swore to the Law before the Keeper's witness. So the War of the Roads ended in a house built to feed carriers.

It left three things. Caol leathan's fighters became its road-guards and swore their watch on a white stone carved as a wolf, kept at the town gate. They called themselves the wolf-sworn, and later ages read more into the stone than they did. Caol fhiadhaich, whose boats had fed the besieged town, was given a share of the western tolls and built a harbour wall with it. And when Iain Garbh died, his daughter Catrìona buried him in the floor of his hall at Dùn dhearg and not on the slope at Dùn ìseal. The Hall did not ask for him.

Catrìona Ghlic, Catrìona the Wise, mended the breach. She sent her own son to sit with the flame for a season, the first of the royal house to do so since the division, and by the end of her reign the Hall and the hall were on speaking terms again. She had the houses on the sites of An Leòmhann Buidhe and An Taigh-seinnse Reòta built on the northern road, the second high in the pass for carriers caught by snow; its keepers were paid in grain by the Hall and in cloth by the crown, and complained of both. Inis mhòr, on the wool trade, outgrew every northern town.

## VIII. Of the Letters, the Woods and the Measuring of the Vein

After Catrìona Ghlic the king-list gives eleven names at Dùn dhearg and nothing more, and the Keepers' roll five names and nothing more. A clerk ended that silence. Tormod mac Iain, a younger son of the house of Tormod, learned the stag-letters at Muileann chiar, the first of the royal kin to read, and was called Tormod Scrìobhaiche, Tormod the Writer. Ealasaid Chiar ordered him to write down every ruler the reciters could name, in order. This was the first written king-list: from then on rulers were recorded by name and not remembered by story. His roll runs from Goraidh Mòr to Ealasaid Chiar. He marked which names came from reciters and which from living memory, and where it disagreed with the Keepers' roll in three places he wrote both readings without choosing. The Library counts him its first ancestor.

The Hall, shamed in its own words by the rìgh's clerk, had its recited roll written down at Dùn ìseal. It begins with Beathag Bhàn and is several centuries longer than Tormod's.

In the west a company settled at the edge of Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, the uncleared wood, to watch for a black horned beast they said walked in it: the wood-watchers, whose own accounts are none older than the later faith that claims them. They came east and cut a black-barked tree in the stag's grove, saying the beast had shown it to them. The stag's people called it theft and sent teachers to hold lessons in the dark wood, saying the first stag had come from there. In each wood the two companies met and did not speak. This holy-wood dispute, the first recorded conflict between rival religious claims, was settled by dividing custody instead of choosing a winner. Before Fearchar Òg, rìgh some generations after Ealasaid Chiar, and the Keeper Calum Liath, the stag's people took the eastern wood and the wood-watchers the western, each free to walk the other's unarmed and without cutting. Two of the boundary stones, cut in stag-letters, survive.

Calum Liath then sent cutters with knotted cords and lamps into every gallery to follow the vein and mark where it thickened and thinned. The work took several ore-seasons, and the households went short to pay for it. It was the first attempt to measure how much obsidian the mountain held. The sum, cut on a slab in the Hall, found that the mountain could give every household its handful for more generations than there were grains in the granaries of Dùn dhearg. It was read to the council and heard in silence, which the Hall took for assent. The chroniclers of this present age, in which the vein is nearly spent, make no comment on the slab.

Two youths sent into the lower gallery without a Keeper's cutter broke the wrong face and were crushed. Their families asked what the Hall owed them, and the council had no answer. Out of that came the apprentice-binding custom: young Rodians were bound to a master custodian for seven years before they might touch the ore, and every later mining guild takes its shape from it. Oighrig Bhàn, Keeper after Calum Liath, bound the first twelve on the stone cup, and four of them later became Keepers, which is the custom's best argument. The stag's house at Cnoc bhàn took up the seven years and added a written account of every strike. These strike-books became the school's chief record, and in time they produced a question of their own.

Fearchar Òg reigned long and is remembered mostly for dying old. His clerks paid the road-guards in stamped silver rings, the first payment in metal by any ruler, and the rings travelled further than the guards did. The southern guards began meeting at Ceann leathan each year to fight with blunted staves for a ring, and the meeting outlived its purpose: its field is the one where Còmhrag Ceann leathan was later fought. The first winner the record names is a woman, Mòrag Dhubh of Cnoc mhòr. Burned Muileann òg was rebuilt, and its harvest fair became Fèill Muileann òg. At Cidhe Beag on the Abhainn uaine a landing was built for river-boats, over the carriers' protest, and the hide boatmen of Muileann chrom swore their fellowship on a paddle.

## IX. Of the Red Hall and the Census of Hands

Beathag Mhòr, great-granddaughter of Fearchar Òg, is the first ruler the list calls banrìgh, queen, and the change of word is the list's own. On {{date:II-0135}} she moved her hall from Dùn dhearg to the town at the western bridge-foot, Cathair dhearg, where the roads, the river and the western harbours met. It has been the seat of rule on Rodos ever since. Her hall was built of the town's red stone on the rise above the harbour, and its foundations lie under the later citadel. The masons cut the hearth-sign in its lintel as if it were a bridge.

Dùn dhearg kept its granaries, the graves of the house of Ìomhar, and its pride. Cathair dhomhain, older and larger, refused the queen's toll on its boats and was granted a charter of its own. The two towns, a few miles apart, stayed rivals for the rest of the age.

The council at Dùn ìseal was now hearing more disputes over the right to the handful than over anything else: new households, divided households, and claims of descent from the nine hearths without proof. The Keeper Dùghall mac Raghnaill asked the queen for a count, the clerks to write and the Keepers to judge. The counters worked from the waypoint houses, since every household lay within a day of one, and the houses, fed at the queen's cost, remember it as a good year.

The census of hands was completed on {{date:II-0142}}, the first island-wide count of the households holding the right to burn the ore, and proof that the reverent custom was already being rationed. The rolls gave eleven thousand four hundred households holding the right and about two thousand refused. The refused went on a separate roll, and it is that roll later generations searched when they wished to prove an ancestor's standing. Many of the refused were fisher-families round Seann Tarr who had never burned ore and now had no right to begin. Sliochd Raghnaill argued their case and lost. The fishers burned peat and said it was warmer.

Beathag Mhòr was carried back at her own order to lie at Dùn dhearg among the house of Ìomhar, the longest royal journey in the record up to then. Her son Ruairidh Liath was already grey when he took the hall, and his byname is the only joke the list makes. He demanded one silver ring in five from Muileann chiar. Sliochd Fhearchair, the silversmiths' house, quoted Muireach Liath back to him, that silver is not holy and so not the crown's either, and shut the workings. For three years no silver left Muileann chiar. The record calls it the Silver Feud, though no one died in it. Both sides asked the Keeper to judge. Dùghall mac Raghnaill ruled that Muireach Liath's words stood, and also that the crown kept the roads the silver travelled on. The crown took one ring in ten, the workings reopened, and both sides called it a victory. The Muileann chiar weights were made the only lawful weights on the roads, cast in bronze at Muileann dhearg for every waypoint house.

Ruairidh Liath's granddaughter Seonag Chrom gave the clerks a room in the red hall, and from her reign the king-list was kept year by year. At Inis bheag a stag-teacher taught letters to fishers' children, and the Hall's roll remarked that fish do not need reading. In the red hall Peigi nic Tormoid, of Tormod Scrìobhaiche's line, became the first clerk whose hand can be told apart from the others. Much of what the Library knows of the middle of the age is in her hand or copied from it.

## X. Of the Hawk, the Sting, the Wolf and the Two Schools

In the later centuries of the age the one practice of the Ròdaich began to come apart, in each place for its own reason.

At Seann Skell on the northern coast every fowler household kept a hawk and fed it before the family ate. They burned their handful at dawn with the hawk on the wrist, saying that the flame is a hunger and should be fed as a hunger is fed. They called the hawk Mòd. The dawn-burning spread from the north shore up the Abhainn ìseal to Inis ìseal and Cnoc bheag. The Keeper wrote that since it kept the measure exactly, it was no concern of the Hall's. It was the last time the Hall said that of any rite. In the west the sting-oath went up the coast to Ros bheag, where a merchant who would not swear on the sting was not trusted. The wolf-sworn set a second white wolf-stone at the shrine of Inis thais, sledged from the mountain at more cost than the shrine, and its keepers began to pray before it as well as to the drowned.

The stag's people divided as well. At Cnoc bhàn, Donnchadh mac Iain read generations of strike-books and found that the ore bled brighter for cutters in their later years than in their first, whatever house they came from. He argued that the vein answers to practice. The elders answered that it answers to what a cutter is born with, and that practice only shows it. Donnchadh left with twenty-two hearers and settled at Muileann chaol, taking copies of the strike-books. The elders called it theft. Donnchadh called it copying, which it was. This was the rival school of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, which held that the vein answers effort and not birth, and it wrote no cutter's parentage in its books at all. The two schools argued by letter for the rest of the age, and both kept the letters. The Library now stands in Muileann chaol, and its chroniclers know that a later age would fight over that argument in a very different form. They have tried to write this chapter as if they did not.

The Keeper Raonaid Dhubh heard both schools at Dùn ìseal and gave no ruling:

> *Cha do dh'iarr an fhèith oirnn breith a thoirt.*
> The vein has not asked us to give judgment.

No refusal in the Hall's history was cited more often.

The Old Ones appeared in the record once more, as a question. Scholars from Muileann chaol could not read the pillar at Cnoc ghorm, and the people of Cnoc ghorm told them it said *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann*: we did not come; we were here. The scholars wrote that the people said so. No later hand explains the stone or the saying. Men of Baile ghorm dug under Làrach an Dùin for its rumoured riches and found only ash. The Keeper sentenced them as lawbreakers though no law covered the case, and the sentence became the rule for every old place after. The diggers' families did their penance by keeping the road to Tuam Cnoc dhìreach in repair, and it is still called the diggers' road.

## XI. Of the Shared Meal and the Lean Year

Lachlann Bàn built granaries in every province seat that would take one. With the Keeper Raonaid Dhubh he wrote the island's oldest custom into law at Cathair dhearg: a household with grain beyond its winter's need was to give the surplus to its district's common granary before the storm season, and the granary would feed any household of the district that ran short. It was the first law made by rìgh and Keeper together. Cnoc mhòr built its granary first and its reeve was sent a silver ring. Granaries went up in the mountain districts round Muileann chiar, where people lived by cutting and carrying, and the lowlands grumbled at filling them.

Lachlann's daughter Oighrig nic Lachlainn had no children, and the houses began counting their claims before her reign was half over. Early in her reign the weather tested the law. The watchers above Cnoc ghlas saw snow lying on the high passes long after the galleries should have opened, and the Hall kept them shut. When they did open, they opened late and closed early, and a third of a common season's ore came down. The mountain households of Muileann gheal, Doire uaine and Àth dhìreach, who traded their carrying for grain, had nothing to trade, and by the following autumn they were eating their seed.

On {{date:II-0179}} the chronicle records the first famine averted. The shared-meal law held. The mountain granaries were opened at Muileann gheal, and when they ran low the granaries of Dùn dhearg and the southern coast sent grain up the carriers' roads without needing to be asked twice. The carriers took no fee. No one in the three settlements is recorded as dying of hunger that winter. Àth dhìreach gave the carriers who had brought the grain the right to rest in any house of the town for as long as they lived. The last of them died old, and the right died with him. The reeves of Dùn dhearg, who had refused Cathair dhearg their accounts for generations, opened the old granaries of the house of Ìomhar without waiting for an order. The queen wrote that she would have given the order had they waited. The clerk noted that they had not waited.

The chroniclers set this winter beside the Binding. The Binding shows what the Ròdaich would not take, and the lean year shows what they would give.

Oighrig nic Lachlainn died without an heir of her body, and the line of Goraidh Mòr, as the list reckons it, ended with her. The red hall was shut and its clerks kept it. The Four Houses met at Dùn ìseal to choose a ruler, since no custom covered a hall without an heir. Sliochd Ìomhair claimed by blood, Sliochd Thormoid through the clerks, and Sliochd Raghnaill through its boats. Sliochd Mhuirich, which held the Keeper's office, made no claim at all. The rolls place the choosing some years after the meeting. The Library cannot say how long the houses deliberated, or whether the hall stood empty all that while.

## XII. Of the House of Tormod

The houses chose Gilleasbuig Mòr mac Tormoid of Seann Dunn, a grandson of the clerks' line. The Keeper set the stone cup in his hands before the council, the first time a ruler was made at Dùn ìseal, and the chroniclers observe that it looked very much as if the Hall had made the king. Sliochd Ìomhair consented on condition that its graves at Dùn dhearg stay the kings' burying-place. Gilleasbuig had the king-list recopied with a line drawn under Oighrig nic Lachlainn, and the line is in every later copy. He made his house's shrine at Seann Dunn a temple, whose lamp a Keeper's cutter still checked every season, and he was buried at Dùn dhearg as agreed. His son Tormod Òg succeeded without any meeting of the houses, and nobody asked for one.

Between Tormod Òg and Anna Ruadh the list gives nine rulers of the house with their years and little else, and the rolls of those reigns hold mostly harvests, tolls and bridge repairs. The Library takes this for a sign that the realm was doing well.

The bridges, at any rate, were rebuilt. The span at Cathair dhearg was widened for carts on Mòrag nan Rathad's piers. Above Ros fhionn the masons found Ailean Clachair's sign still sharp and left it alone. New crossings rose at Dùn chrom and Ceann àrsaidh, and at Doire mhin the keystone carries, beside the hearth-sign, the first mason's mark in the record: the name of Beathag Clachair. An Sligeanach Fad' às was rebuilt in stone and got its name, for carriers said one was never nearer to it than halfway. The house on the northern road began serving a barley spirit called fire-water. The Keepers ruled that it was not a burning, and its trade doubled.

The north-west kept to the edge of the record. Caol mhòr paid its tolls only when the road-guard came for them, and Inis mhin appears in the rolls once, for a complaint. The seal-oil of Ceann mhòr lit the western harbours brighter than anything else on Rodos. Two boats of Baile chrom felt something vast move under them in the north-western water, and one did not come home. The wake kept for the first loss is now kept for both.

Sliochd Raghnaill's boats made the round of the offshore islands, Eilean ruadh, Eilean dhearg and Eilean fhada among them, in one season. No crew of the age went further than the islands, and none is recorded as wanting to. The chroniclers, who know what the next age did, set this down without comment.

Anna Ruadh of the house of Tormod walked the whole road circuit, as Mòrag nan Rathad had, and she walked it twice. At the house where the War of the Roads ended she ate the jugged buffalo like a carrier. She gave the sunny house the only royal charter any waypoint house holds, and gave the contest at Ceann leathan a walled field and fed its losers at the crown's cost. She also kept a wood-watcher in her household to read the omens of the hall, the first sign of that rite in the capital. The Keeper protested, and the rolls record no answer. Cathair dhomhain, proud of keeping the Law strictly, would not take a wood-watcher, and said the capital had handed its omens to people who sat in trees. From then on the two towns' rivalry was also a quarrel of faith.

Anna Ruadh was buried at Dùn dhearg. Her son Coinneach Bàn reigned long and quietly and had the rolls copied for every province seat. Several of those copies survive where the originals do not, and this book rests on them.

## XIII. Of the Parting of the Rites

Seumas Bàn of Sliochd Mhuirich, chosen Keeper, found in the Hall's roll that more than half the Keepers of the age had come from his own house, and had that fact struck from the preface. An unstruck copy survives. He also had the Hall list every rite that burned the handful or claimed the vein, so the council would know what it was keeping. The list named the Hall's own rite, the old ancestor-keeping, the dawn-hawk, the wood-watchers, the wolf-sworn, the sting-oath and both schools of the stag.

The hawk-keepers of Seann Skell sent back the Hall's cutter unheard, saying the measure was the hawk's and the hawk was not the Keeper's: the first rite to refuse the Hall's check outright. The wolf-sworn built a hall round their stone at Caol leathan and swore oaths there that were no longer guards' oaths. One god, they said, watches the whole island as the guard watches the road. At the western Seann Skell the sting-oath became a creed: one power stings the liar wherever he is, and the scorpion is only its sign. It was the first teaching on the island to call itself the only true one. Seann Vell, below Cuan shean, carved a yellow stone scorpion for worship. The wood-watchers stopped bringing disputes to the Hall and judged them under the trees. They held that the black beast has two natures, one that keeps and one that devours, and that the vein has the same two.

By {{date:II-0221}} the common ancestor of the nine faiths had visibly split: one folk practice had become the threads later ages would name, and Rodian belief was no longer one thing. The council, which had sat by hearth and house, began to sit in companies by rite. Seumas Bàn complained that he kept the flame for eight peoples, each of which thought itself the only keeper.

Two customs stayed outside the companies. Pilgrims of every rite still walked to Allt an Àigh at the right quarter of the moon, the one custom the Hall's list set under no company, and in one season pilgrims of four rites repaved the path from Caol shean together. And the keeper of the springs at Muileann ruadh ruled that the sick of every rite should be bathed without being asked which. It was the last ruling of the age that every company accepted, and it came from the one office the Hall had made that had nothing to do with the ore.

## XIV. Of the Last Quiet Years and the Close of the Age

The last generations of the age live in the provincial rolls, which care about what was made and what was owed. The eastern Cnoc dhìreach wove the carriers' heavy cloth and Muileann bhàn dyed it a pale hill-blue; the Keepers wore theirs undyed, and said so. A flood at Àth chiar took six townsfolk, but the granary on its rise fed the town through the winter. The south-eastern Seann Vell grew into a city on fish and silver and built the island's first stone quay. The beacon at Tobar dhearg was raised on a stone tower. At Muileann naomh a mill and a shrine shared a roof, and the Keeper's cutter found the measure kept and the flour good. Dùn thais built the stag's first temple of stone. Baile Mòr mhòr sent a member to the council, and the Four Houses seated him at the foot of the hall.

When drought burned the pastures of Achadh shean, grain came from Baile chaol and Cuan ruadh under the shared-meal law, and the rolls at Baile chaol record it repaid measure by measure, the first such debt repaid in full. At Baile ruadh a timber watch-tower was manned against island raiders, the only tower of war the age built. At Inis chrom the families of the drowned set an empty place for each of the lost at a yearly feast on the shore, and the custom spread north to Seann Warr. At Cnoc naomh the ancestors were still kept in the old way, with no hawk, wolf, sting or stag, and the Hall's list counted the old reverence as one company among the others.

Mòr nic Coinnich, granddaughter of Coinneach Bàn, received the stone cup from the Keeper Catrìona nic Mhuirich at Dùn ìseal, as Gilleasbuig Mòr had. Hers is the last name in the king-list of the Holy Age. At the close of the age the Keepers' roll held one hundred and forty names from Beathag Bhàn to Catrìona nic Mhuirich, and the king-list more than ninety from Goraidh Mòr to Mòr nic Coinnich. The Small-Burning Law had not changed by a word since Fionnlagh Dall's cup, and the cup still stood on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach.

Later chroniclers look back on these years with some unease. The years about {{date:II-0249}} belong to the last age of even population, the final stretch of Rodian history in which no family held meaningfully more years of life than any other. Keepers aged at the same pace as carriers, and every ruler died in their time. No one then thought of this as something that could be lost, so no one recorded it as a blessing. It is recorded here only because the record knows what came after.

The next age began at sea, which this one had never wished to cross. Crews from the western harbours brought word of a land across open water, empty of anyone like the Ròdaich, and none of them claimed to have been first. The report would be heard in the red hall at Cathair dhearg, and it would put to the Ròdaich a question the Keepers had never had to answer: who would choose to go. The Holy Age had counted every hand that held the right to the flame. The next would count those willing to leave it. That is the Age of Sundering, An Aois Scaraidh, and it is told in the book that follows.
