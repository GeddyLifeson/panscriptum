# The Second Book: An Aois Ailein, the Age of Ailean

*GE 1 – 4,603*

> *Chaidh e dhan doire na ghille, is thill e na rìgh.*
> He went into the grove a lad, and he came back a king.
>
> — said of Ailean Mòr at the ford, as the reciters of Dùn ìseal keep it

## I. Of the Vigil in the Grove

**Ailean** son of Suibhne was a big young man, slow of speech, with hands like his forefather's, and he had done what no one of the line had thought to do. He left the rainbow coal alone and took up the other six, and lit each one again by its own way, one after another, as the line had lit them over three and a half thousand years: the gold on the sea below Ceann thais, the snowflake off the Point, the black in the earth at Tobar dhìreach, the red-brown with his own blood, the silver in the Abhainn ghorm, and the fire with the snap of his fingers. Each he carried away burning, in a pot of clay with a lid of turf, and he kept them fed. It took him the better part of a year. Then he put the six pots and the dark seventh coal on a pole across his shoulders and walked north, to the grove.

He came into Doire ghlas at dusk on {{date:I-0080a}}. He set the six pots in a ring under the oaks and took off their lids, and the six fires stood up out of them in their six colours: gold and white and the dark red of the black coal, red-brown, silver, and the clear fire-colour of Seathan's snap. He had never seen them all together. No one had. He set the rainbow coal on the ground in the middle of the ring, and sat down, and waited, for the tellings said that this was where the Keeper came.

It was there before full dark. He saw it through the heat of the fires, the height of a tall man and made of grey smoke, standing among the oaks; and the smoke of his six fires blew past it on the evening wind and it did not stir.

"You have brought them all," it said.

"All I could," said Ailean. "The last will not light."

"No. It is not lit by anything of the sky, the earth or the sea. It is lit when the rest are together." The Keeper came into the ring. It stooped over the rainbow coal, and the coal took fire.

Ailean said afterward that he could not tell the colour of that flame, because it was every colour, and they would not hold still to be named. The six fires round it leaned in toward it, all together, the way grass leans in a wind, and for the first time since the making of the world all seven coals of Dia-thìr burned at once, each from its own spirit.

"Now listen," the Keeper said. "Stay with them until they are cold. Do not leave them, do not sleep far from them, and eat nothing while they burn."

"Why?"

"So that the spirits, being merged, may know one keeper, and the fire go into no hand but the hand that did not leave it."

"And after me?" said Ailean. "Will my children have it?"

The Keeper was quiet so long that he thought it would not answer. The six fires and the seventh burned between them without a sound.

"It will be yours," it said, "and no one's after you. Your children will have a little of it, and their children less, as a hearthstone keeps the heat a while when the fire is out; and then your line will not have it, and will forget that it ever had, and the island will call you a tale. But hear this, and keep it. When strangers hold this island, the fire will wake again in your line."

"What strangers?"

"That is not given to me to say."

He stayed. He kept the watch seven nights, a night for each coal, in the ring of fires under the oaks, and he ate nothing, and drank from the grove's spring only when the fires were steady. The first nights he was hungry and then he was not. The fires did not burn down as fires do. They burned through the nights at their full height and did not need feeding. On the fifth night he thought he heard voices in them. On the seventh he could not tell whether he slept.

On the eighth night, {{date:I-0080b}}, the fires were cold. He woke, or came to himself, and they were out, all seven: the coals lay in the ring grey and dead, and the grove was dark, and he was very cold.

Then the grove filled with light.

They came in among the oaks as people come into a hall, talking, and the light came with them. First was a young man with hands black to the wrist, as a mason's hands are grey; and Ailean knew him, though he had never been told how, for Caoran, the Young God, who made all that is. Behind him came the Tuath Dè, his kin. An Dagda came, huge and laughing, with his club over his shoulder, and Danu at his side; Brìde with a flame in her hand; Lugh with his spear; Macha, and horses somewhere behind her that he heard but did not see; Manannan, who brought the smell of the sea into the middle of the island; Crom Cruaich, bent, who looked a long while at the slab-country to the south as if he could see it through the hills; and more behind them than Ailean could count or name.

Caoran stood over him in the ring of dead fires, and put out a black hand and held him where he knelt.

"You stayed," Caoran said. "Good." And he said the words that the Line of the Mason keeps, and that no other list has:

> *Rìgh Chlann nan Dè.*
> King of the godkin.

Then he put his hand flat on Ailean's breast, and the heat that had gone out of the seven coals went into him. He said afterward only that it was as the night of the lightning must have been for the mason, if the mason had been the stone.

When he could see again it was morning, and the gods were gone, and the ring of pots lay cold on the leaves.

## II. Of the Taking of the Hill

Ailean Mòr, as they called him from then on, told his kin that the gods had come to the grove and named him king of the godkin. That much he never hid, and Clann na Ceiste believed him. What had gone into him with Caoran's hand he told to no one: not his wife, nor his children, nor the reciters, in all the long years he lived. How then is it known? The line answers that the Keeper knows everything it watches over, and tells what it chooses to whom it chooses; and there the question stops.

He walked home from the grove and did not stop at the high valleys. He went on south with eleven of his kin to the hill where Dùn ìseal now stands, which was held then by households that answered to no one; and the hearths of that hill kept a telling of what came up their slope on {{date:I-0081}}, and it is told here as they tell it.

There was a lad on the hill that summer called the knapper's son, for his father worked flint in a pit behind the houses, and he was set with the other lads on the lip of the hill to watch the valley road. He saw them come: twelve people walking, no more, the first of them a head taller than the rest, and none of them in any hurry. He ran and told, and the holders of the hill came out with every spear they had, some sixty, and stood in a crowd on the slope where the ground is steepest, and the lad stood among them with his father's second-best spear.

The twelve stopped at the foot of the slope. The big one came on alone. He carried nothing in his hands.

The lad said, when he was an old man, that he had meant to throw. They had all meant to throw. The big man came up the grass at a walking pace, looking at them as a man looks at cattle in the wrong field; and when he was a spear's cast off, the lad felt a warmth on his face, as if someone had opened the door of a kiln. His arm was up. It stayed up. He could not think why he should throw, and the reason he had had a moment before was gone like a dream in the morning. Beside him the eldest of the hill's holders let his spear down until its point was in the grass, and sat down on the slope, and put his face in his hands; and one by one the others did as he had done, or turned round and walked back to their houses, and none of them could ever say afterward why.

The big man walked through them. He went up to the top of the hill and stood there, looking all round at the valleys, and after a while he called down the slope for someone to bring him water, and the lad brought it.

"What is your name?" said the king, when he had drunk.

The lad told him.

"Is that your father's spear?"

"His second-best."

"Take it back to him," said Ailean. "Tell him I shall want a great many more."

That year the households of the valleys about the hill came in to him one after another, and brought him their grazing and their quarrels, and he judged the quarrels and was feared, and loved a little too; and no one ever saw him lift a hand. A girl of Dùn ìseal said in her old age that she had once stood near him on a winter night and been warm, as if she stood by a fire, and that there was no fire.

So the first kingdom of the godkin was made: a hill and the valleys round it and the households that came in. Ailean threw up the hill's first bank, a single bank and ditch round some forty houses, the oldest defended place of the hill-folk. With him the reciters at the ford open the oldest spoken king-list, *Rìghrean na Cloiche*, the Stone Kings, and some of Clann na Ceiste say the stone of the name is the mason's stone. The list gives no lengths of reign, and it is older than the name Dia-thìrich.

## III. Of the Holders of the Hill

The list of the Stone Kings is the hill's list. It names, one after another, those who held Dùn ìseal and sat in judgement within its bank, and it names Ailean first because he held it first. The Line of the Mason says what the list does not: that Ailean Mòr was king from the night in the grove until the day he fell, and never once gave the kingship out of his hand. He set others to hold the hill for him; the crown he kept. The hill was too small for what had gone into him, and within a few years of the taking he was gone from it more than he was on it, riding out to the valleys beyond and the valleys beyond those, and bringing them in; and while he rode he set one of his own blood on the hill to hold it for him. Those holders are the Stone Kings after the first. They were kings of the hill under a king of the godkin, and the hill's own reciters, who counted what was done on the hill and little else, called them kings and left it there.

The first he set there was his daughter, **Gormshuil nic Ailein**, who stands second in the list and is the first woman named in any list on Dia-thìr. She had been born to him in the high valleys when he was a lad of eighteen, before ever he went north with the six pots, and she was a small child when her father came back from the grove. Her telling gives her one deed. In a hard winter, when the grass on the hill was under snow and the byres were emptying, she drove the herds of Dùn ìseal down to the warm springs, where the ground steams and the snow does not lie, and lost none.

The reciters of Clann na Ceiste give her more than that, and it is a hard telling. Gormshuil was born before the grove, and she had nothing of what went into her father there. She grew grey on the hill while he came and went from it, and the king who came in at the gate at the end of each riding season was a young man with a young man's back, and his daughter who met him at the gate was bent. Her own grandchildren took him for her son. When she died, an old woman in the ordinary way, he sat by her a night and a day, and afterward he did not speak of her. It was then, the reciters say, that the kin first understood that the long years came from the grove, and that the children born to him after it would have their portion and the ones born before would not.

After Gormshuil he set on the hill a son born after the grove, **Dòmhnall Clachach**, third of the list, who set marker stones along the valley below the hill to part the grazing of Dùn ìseal from its neighbours'. Some of the stones stand yet, and are still quarrelled over. After him came the first named storm, whose name is lost; it broke the shell causeway of Seann Warr in three places. Then came **Fearchar Bacach**, the lame, another son, who had been lamed in his father's wars in the valleys, and held the hill after the storm and after Dòmhnall. He was carried to the gatherings in a chair of woven birch, the first ruler on Dia-thìr remembered for a hurt. Dòmhnall did not die when Fearchar took the hill. He had the long years, and his father sent him north to hold what had been won there; for the holders of the hill came and went, and did not always go into the ground when they went.

So the Stone Kings stand in their list far apart, eighty years between Gormshuil's deed and Dòmhnall Clachach's, and more than four hundred between Fearchar Bacach and Mòrag Chruaidh. The reciters of the hill say the gaps are the long years: that the holders were of the king's blood and held long, as the long-lived do. The Library, which does not hold with the long years, says that the list has lost names, as later lists have. The list itself says nothing either way, and goes on.

## IV. Of the Red Hill

A second list was kept in the west, and it opens with **Beathag Ruadh**, who held the hill where Cathair dhearg now stands, and that is the Red Hill. She held it in the king's first years and was never his. Her line was an old line of the western lake shore, and when the tellings of Dùn ìseal speak of the Red Hill in those years they speak of it as a hill-man speaks of weather coming from the west.

**Uisdean Mòr**, who held the Red Hill after her, dug a ditch across the neck of the hill and set a palisade of oak on it, and in his time the palisade burned twice and was twice rebuilt. The Red Hill's own list says so much and no more. The reciters of Clann na Ceiste tell the rest, and the Red Hill has never quarrelled with their telling, which is perhaps the strangest thing about it.

The king came west at the end of a summer with some two hundred spears, camped on the lake shore under the hill, and sent up no word. That night the palisade burned. No one saw a torch thrown, and the watch swore that the wood took fire of itself along its whole length, with flames of a colour they did not know, low and dark and shining. They put it out with lake water and hides by morning, and no one came up the hill.

Uisdean rebuilt through the autumn with green oak, which burns worst of all, and the king waited on the shore with his two hundred and did nothing that anyone could see. At the first frost the palisade burned again, the new wood and the old, in the same low dark way, from end to end, in the space of a breath.

In the morning Uisdean Mòr came down from the Red Hill alone.

He was a broad man, heavier than the king, with his hair and beard gone the colour of rust; and the tellers say that he came down the path slowly, stopping twice, and that at the bottom he stood a long time on the shingle looking at the man who sat on a stone by the water. The king did not get up. He was mending the strap of a sandal.

"Was that you?" said Uisdean.

The king finished with the strap before he looked up. "Your palisade has burned twice," he said. "I should not build it a third time."

"I asked if it was you."

"I heard you."

Uisdean stood there with the lake behind the king going white under the wind, and the smoke of his palisade still hanging over the hill at his back. Then he went down on one knee on the wet stones and put his hands between the king's hands, and swore to him; and the king raised him, and they sat on the shingle together and ate, and talked about cattle. That was on {{date:I-0089a}}.

The Red Hill kept its own holders after that, and its own list, and swore to the king and not to Dùn ìseal; and that is why the two lists make no mention of each other for so long. The Red Hill owed nothing to the hill of the Stone Kings, and the hill of the Stone Kings owed nothing to it. They owed the same man. **Seonag nic Uisdein**, who held the Red Hill after her father, counted her people by pebbles in a bag, one to a household, and knew by the weight when a household had gone; and when the king came west she weighed the bag before him, and he was content.

## V. Of the Counting of the Dead and the Silence of the Old Ones

While the kingdom grew in the hills, another people were going out of the world on its shores, and the hill-folk hardly looked round.

In {{year:I-0090}} the Seann-Dhaoine began to cut strokes on flat stones set at the edges of their middens, one stone to a household. The strokes are simpler than the script: a mark, and then another. They were counting their dead. Before there was a Dia-thìreach people to take up the craft, the Old Ones kept count of their own losses, and the chroniclers of Muileann chaol own them as their first forebears in it, though they cannot name one of them.

At the eastern Seann Warr the counting stones were built into a wall, some three thousand strokes in all, and the wall grew for many lifetimes. At Seann Tarr in the north-east the strokes were cut in fives and the stones set into the floor of a house, and the house was lived in over its own dead. Then the tally stones of Seann Warr, Seann Tarr and Seann Chwen all thicken together, more strokes on each stone, more stones in each course. Whatever killed so many killed on three coasts at once. What it was, the strokes keep to themselves.

At the Seann Chwen near Cuan dhearg, on {{date:I-0095}}, the last tally stone was left half-cut: two strokes finished, a third begun and given up. The chisel marks stop mid-groove.

After the half-cut stone no new work of the Seann-Dhaoine can be dated. Their middens stop growing, their harbours silt, and their script is never cut again. How they ended, no one knows. Others have guessed in every age, and the Library at Muileann chaol keeps their guesses in a press of their own and lends them to no one as history. The reciters of Clann na Ceiste say only that the king went down once to the shore at Seann Warr in those years, and stood a long while before the tally wall, and came away and would not speak of it.

There is a lament for them on the south coast. It is young, as such things go, and it says what the stones say:

> *Stràc air a' chloich, is stràc eile;*
> *an treas stràc, cha deach a chrìochnachadh.*
> *Dh'fhalbh iad, is dh'fhàg iad na h-ainmean.*
>
> A stroke on the stone, and another stroke;
> the third stroke, it was never finished.
> They went, and they left the names.

For the hill-folk went on calling the Old Ones' places by the Old Ones' names, putting only their own word for *old* in front: Seann Dunn, Seann Chwen, Seann Skell and the rest. They translated nothing, and the roots are still not understood. Hill-folk families came down the Abhainn naomh and settled the empty river harbour at Seann Skell, using the old mole and mending none of it. When diggers at Tuam Cnoc dhìreach broke into a grave lined with slabs in the Seann-Dhaoine way, they closed it and moved their row, and no one opened it again.

At the eastern Seann Dunn, near the end of the age, a household set about cutting a floor for a new house into the side of the old midden.

The woman of the house had the spade, a blade of shoulder-bone on an ash handle, and at a man's length in, it rang. She knelt and cleared it with her hands: a flat stone set on its edge, and on its face the strokes, one and one and one, row on row, more than she could count on her hands and her sons' hands together. Behind it was another, and another. She knew what they were, though no one on that coast had ever seen one.

They lifted them out one at a time and laid them on the turf in the order they had come. She looked at them a long while in the wind off the sea. Then she and her sons put them back, the last first, each in its own place and facing the way it had faced, and filled the cut and trod it down, and built their house elsewhere, on poorer ground.

So the Dia-thìrich have always dealt with the Old Ones: as a people whose count must not be disturbed.

## VI. Of the Salt Shore

The first war in either list was a raid for salt, and the king was not in it.

**Mòrag Chruaidh**, fifth of the Stone Kings, was a daughter of Dòmhnall Clachach and so the king's granddaughter, and she held the hill while her grandfather was away in the north. The southern shore at Baile ghorm did not yet own the king, and its people had salt, and the hill had none; and Mòrag went down to the pans and carried off the salt. Seven died on the hill side, and the telling keeps the number. The pans, shallow clay basins behind a shingle bar, were the first salt-works of the island.

**Iain Dubh**, sixth, was the king's own son, older than anyone on the southern shore and younger to look at than most of them. He went down to do as Mòrag had done, and the salt-folk were ready for him, and he was killed on the shingle, the first of Ailean's children to die by a spear. The list says only that he was brought home.

The king came home from the north to find his son under a cairn. He stayed three days on the hill. On the fourth he went down to the southern shore, and he went alone and on foot, by the old track through the dunes, and he came out onto the shingle at Baile ghorm on {{date:I-0104b}}, in the heat of the day, when the pans were drying and the women were raking them.

A woman raking salt was the first to see him, and her telling was kept on that shore until the humans came. She saw a tall man coming along the shingle from the west, walking as if the beach were his, and she knew who it must be. She screamed for the men. They came running from the huts with the spears that had killed his son, some thirty of them, and made a line across the shingle between him and the pans.

He came on, neither slow nor hurrying, and she said afterward that the wind off the sea was warm on that side of him and cold on the other.

Nobody threw. She saw a man she knew, a hard man, who had been the first to strike Iain Dubh, lift his spear to his shoulder and hold it there, and hold it, until his arm shook; and then lower it, and look at it in his hand as though someone had given it to him and he could not think why. The king walked into the line of them and stopped. He looked along their faces one by one. He did not say anything at all.

Then the eldest of the salt-folk went down on her knees on the shingle. She took up a double handful of salt from the nearest pan, grey and wet, and held it up to him; and he took it, and ate a little of it, and gave the rest back to her. By evening the salt shore was his, and it stayed his. No blood was shed there that day, and none was paid, and the salt-folk were never able to agree afterward whether that was mercy or something worse.

The land was not quiet in those centuries, though the king's peace spread over it. The sea came in over a village on the site of Cnoc chaol and left a hand's depth of sand on its hearths, and at Cill ghlas a crack a man could fall into opened across the valley; the hill-folk laid a line of white stones along it and built no house within a stone's throw.

## VII. Of the Unlit Hearth and the Watchers of the Slab

Meanwhile, in the high valleys, the hill-folk were learning the coloured coal the hard way.

A single dwelling of those years was sealed by chance under three yards of peat. Its hearth is laid with dry wood, and beside the wood lies a slip of the coloured coal, and none of it was ever burned. Whoever laid it went away and did not come back to light it. It is the earliest witness on Dia-thìr of coal that might easily have been burned and was left, and the keepers of later ages call it the Unlit Hearth.

For in those years a custom set in across the hills, that no fire should take more of the coloured coal than can be held in one cupped hand. It was nowhere written and everywhere kept, and the Small-Burning Law of the Holy Age is this custom hardened. The reason is always the same tale, told to children when they first reach toward the coal-box. At Achadh dhìreach, it goes, a household once burned a whole basket of the coloured coal at one time, knowing no more of what was in it than the basket did. The house burned down with three people in it; and when the neighbours came in the grey of morning the hearthstone was cracked through and running with colour. Then the teller cups a hand, and says:

> *Làn na glaice, 's gun tuilleadh.*
> The hollow of the hand, and no more.

The reciters of Clann na Ceiste add to the tale a thing the hearths do not. They say that the king came to Achadh dhìreach the day after, and stood by the cracked hearthstone with the colour still creeping in it, and put his hand on it; and that the colour went out of the stone and it was grey. Then he said to the neighbours that the handful was a good custom and they should keep it. They kept it. It is the only law of the age that anyone remembers the king making, and he made it by agreeing with them.

And the families near the crack began to sit by the slab laid over it, a household at a time, through the dark half of the year, from Samhain, when the dark half begins, until the light came back. No one ever said what they were watching for. Among the first of them, the Line of the Mason says, was **Beathan**, a son of the king's son Somhairle. Beathan was already some five hundred years old and looked a man of thirty, and he sat his nights in the cold by the slab with the herdsmen and their wives as if he were one of them. It was the way of Somhairle's children to do so, and it is why the line of Somhairle stayed in the high valleys when the other lines of the king's blood went down into the kingdom to hold it.

## VIII. Of the Ford of Salt and Wool

**Raghnall Ceannfhionn**, seventh of the Stone Kings, came down to the ford to meet the Red Hill, and the reciters make much of his coming.

He was a grandson of the king, fair-headed and quick. For close on nine hundred years the two hills had sworn to the same king and never once spoken to each other. His hill had sheep in plenty and too little salt, and the west had salt in plenty and was short of wool; and the king, when Raghnall asked him, said only that they were both his, and that he would not do their talking for them.

So Raghnall brought only a few with him, for more would have looked like a raid. It was early summer, the river running clear and shallow over its stones. The Red Hill's holder, whose name the lists do not keep, waited on the far bank with as many, and the two parties looked at each other a long time across the water while the river talked between them.

Raghnall waded in, alone, to the middle of the ford, with the water to his knees and his cloak held up out of it, and stood there. After a while the other came out to meet him.

What they said the reciters give short. The hill would bring wool; the west would bring salt; neither would bring spears. They clasped arms over the water and waded back, each to their own bank.

Both lists record it, and it is the first time either names the other. The meeting became a yearly gathering where salt, flint, wool, dried fish and pots changed hands and marriages were agreed, and its trodden ground, where the fair of Muileann òg is held now, is the oldest meeting-place on the island that has never been left. **Murchadh Gearr**, who held the Red Hill after Seonag, kept the peace of the ford, and walked the salt road himself every year to be seen on it.

The hill-folk spread in those generations, and wherever they went the king's peace went with them: to Ros bheag on the west coast; to Cathair dhomhain, paying salt to the Red Hill; to Cnoc ghorm with the first cattle of the north-west. An oak dug-out long enough for six was sunk at Cidhe Beag, the oldest boat on Dia-thìr whose whole shape is known. Seal-oil lamps burned at Caol leathan, the first light on the island that was no hearth.

Hard years came. At the site of Baile chrom in the north-west the pots are found full of boiled bark and seaweed and the grave-rows doubled: the oldest hunger on Dia-thìr with a place to it. **Sìleas Bhàn**, eighth of the Stone Kings, of the king's blood, held Dùn ìseal through years of failed grazing, and her telling gives her no deed but that. She held, and the list goes on after her. **Tormod Liath**, ninth, a grandson of the king, held the hill so long that he outlived three heirs, and in his time Dùn ìseal got its second bank. He was called the grey, and he was grey, and the hill thought him the oldest thing it had ever seen, until the king came in at the gate one spring and Tormod Liath went down on his knee to a man who looked younger than his own sons.

Things were fixed in those lifetimes that have never been unfixed. Flint was dug at Cnoc àrsaidh, the only good flint on Dia-thìr, and the road of those who carried it is sunk a yard deep near Àth leathan. A hawk was carved on a lintel at Àth mhòr, wings closed, head turned, the oldest carving of any bird on the island. Someone began to mark the high-water line against a fixed rock, at intervals too regular for a fisherman's habit, and at Ros dhomhain the marks crept upward from keeper to keeper, until by the age's end the lake stood a hand higher than at the first cut. And the eight winds were named, and eight stones were set in a ring on the point at Ceann leathan, one to each wind; sailors of every age since have touched the stone of the wind they want before they go out.

## IX. Of the Dia-thìrich Name and the Sharing before the Storms

By {{year:I-0140}} the hill-folk had begun to call themselves **Dia-thìrich**, and their island **Dia-thìr**. The first mouth to say it is forgotten. The oldest tellings already take both names for granted, and the doctrine goes with them.

With the name came the first forbidden marriage, two lineages refused to each other for reasons none can now give: the earliest sign that the Dia-thìrich ordered themselves by bloodline before the coal gave them any cause to. At the ford, households began to recite their lines seven names back on each side before a marriage was agreed, and those are the first genealogies of the common households, for only the Line of the Mason is older. None of them survives whole. The king's own blood recited no such lines. Their line was two names long, the reciters say, and the second was Ailean's.

In the same years they went looking for another vein. Diggers from Dùn ìseal opened a trench near Cnoc dhubh and gave it up when two of them were crushed by a fall, and a second opening elsewhere struck ordinary rock with nothing beneath it. So it was first shown that the vein under the mountain stands alone, and the Dia-thìrich took it that the vein chooses where it lies.

Then came the custom that later ages lean on more than they admit: that any household with food to spare must share it before a storm season. At Àth shean a household hid its grain in a pit beneath the floor and brought nothing, and when the pit was found the household was put out with what it could carry, the first known punishment under the custom. And when the storm called *A' Ghaoth Dhubh*, the Black Wind, the oldest storm whose name is kept, stripped the thatch from every house on the east coast from Cuan ruadh to Ceann chiar, the settlements of that coast fed Cuan ruadh through a whole winter out of what they had shared, and no one there is remembered as dying of hunger that season.

Between the two hills there was fosterage. **Eilidh Ghlas**, tenth of the Stone Kings, sent her younger son to the Red Hill, the first fosterage in either list, and **Lachlann Ruadh** took him in; and the Red Hill list calls Lachlann for it *Lachlann an Altraim*, Lachlann of the fostering, more often than by his own name. **Peigi Dhubh**, who held the hill after him, sent the son home grown, with gifts of salt, and the ford peace held a generation more. The fosterling brought the western salt-songs to Dùn ìseal. The list gives him no name and no reign. He is remembered only as the one who went.

## X. Of Oighrig and the Stone Cup

At the slab the heart of the age was kept, and the hearths of the hill country tell this part closest of all.

It was {{date:I-0151}}, in the dark half of the year, when the families who watched the slab met beside it and agreed that they would stop taking turns by household and choose one keeper at a time by lot.

**Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill** stood among them with her shawl over her head against the drizzle. The slab lay grey in the middle of the gathering with the rain beading on it, and the old stain at its lower edge that never washed out. She had sat by it through many nights with her household, and had never once seen it do anything at all.

The lots were pebbles in a bag, one marked. She put her hand in and felt the cold stones knock against her fingers, and brought one out, and opened her hand. Everyone near her saw it before she did.

No one cheered. An old man touched her shoulder once and stepped back. She looked at the pebble a while, and then at the slab, and she put the pebble in her belt, and she kept the slab for the rest of her life.

A house was built beside it at Tobar dhìreach with its door toward the stone, the first building on Dia-thìr that exists only to be near the vein. Before two years of her keeping were out, Oighrig went to a man who worked stone, and set her own hand on the table before him, cupped as for water, and said she wanted a cup of grey stone that would hold what that hand held and not a grain more, so that the handful should be the same in every house. He ground it over many days and fitted it to her palm again and again, until it sat in the hollow of her hand like an egg in a nest.

The tellings give her a word at the grinding, though whether she said it, only she could tell:

> *Chan e mo làmh-sa a th' ann, ach làmh gach teallaich.*
> It is not my hand; it is the hand of every hearth.

Copies were ground at Àth gharbh, Achadh dhìreach and Cnoc chaol, and the four were carried to a spring on the moor between Àth gharbh and Tobar dhìreach and filled from it in turn on a flat stone. The place is called Coinneachadh nan Cupan, the meeting of the cups, and so the hills came to measure the coal with one measure.

The lot did not make its keepers holy. A keeper named **Gilleasbuig Beag** was found with a basket of broken coal from the vein in his own house. The households sat in judgement at the slab and rowed him out to Eilean chrom and left him there with no boat to come back in, the first person anyone remembers exiled for the vein. The coal was neither burned nor kept. The keeper who followed him carried it back to the slab, and knelt, and laid it under the stone's edge piece by piece until the basket was empty. The tellings say it in four words:

> *Chaidh an gual dhachaigh.*
> The coal went home.

Eilean chrom was left empty after, and never fished. His fire was seen on it for three nights, the tellings say, and then no more.

Then the holders of the hill reached for the lot. **Calum Ciar**, the elder son of Eilidh Ghlas and eleventh of the Stone Kings, asked that it be drawn at Dùn ìseal in his sight, and the households refused him; and the list records the refusal, which is more than the list usually records of anything. Calum Ciar took the matter to the king, who was at the ford that summer. The king heard him out and said that the slab was older than the hill, and older than the grove, and that he would not overrule it; and Calum Ciar went home. The lot was still drawn at the slab. The holders of the hill ruled the grazing and the gatherings under the king, and the keepers ruled the vein, and that line held for the rest of the age. It was written nowhere yet.

## XI. Of Anndra Breac and the Doctrine Spoken

In the spring of the year before, the watchers in the tower at Seann Skell had seen the Sea-Watcher off the point, a back as long as three boats rising and going under, and there were four of them to swear to it. That summer planks of stitched boats, broken small, washed up along the western shore, with no bodies among them and no boat missing from any settlement there. Those who tell what follows at the ford set these two things down beside it and let them lie.

On {{date:I-0167}} the gathering at the ford was full, and **Anndra Breac**, a teller from the western coast, stood up before the gathering to give a telling.

He was a lean man with a face burnt dark by the sea wind and a voice made for the open air. A child slept against its mother's knee in the front of the crowd. He began, and for a while they listened, because he was good. Then they understood what the telling was. In it the people came to Dia-thìr. They came over the sea, in boats, from somewhere else.

The first shout came from the hill households, and then from everywhere at once. Anndra Breac raised his voice over them, which was the worst thing he could have done; and he stood with his mouth still moving and no one hearing a word, and the child woke and cried.

The gathering put him out of the ford for the rest of his life. He went west along the river path and did not look round. His telling was not kept, only the fact of it. The king was not at the ford that year, and when he heard of it he is said to have laughed, and then to have been quiet a long while; for he was the one man on the island who had seen gods walk into a grove, and he knew better than the gathering where the godkin came from.

After Anndra Breac, the saying on the column at Cnoc ghorm was spoken aloud at the opening of every ford gathering: *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.* It was the first time the doctrine was said as a rule that bound, where before it had only been believed.

## XII. Of the Great Gathering at the Ford

The king's days had no height that anyone could point to, for he did not change; but the kingdom's had, and the reciters set it at the great gathering, on {{date:I-0208a}}, in the three thousandth year since the grove.

There was a girl of the Red Hill at that gathering, nine years old, who had come with her mother's people to sell salt on the downstream ground; and because her grandmother lived to tell it to the Red Hill's reciters, and they to the reciters of the ford, it is her gathering that is told.

She had never seen so many people. The ford ground was full from the river to the trees, and still they came, from the north with pigs and from the west with seal-skins, until the three grounds ran together and nobody knew any longer which bank was whose. Her mother held her wrist all day so as not to lose her.

On the second morning the king came. He was dressed as a herdsman dresses, and he walked in from the hill road with a few others, and he was younger than her father. She was disappointed at first. Then she saw the crowd open for him, all the way down to the river, as a field opens when the wind goes over it, without a word said; and she saw the faces of the old people as he passed, and she was frightened, and after that she could not stop looking at him.

He went and stood in the middle of the ford, where Raghnall Ceannfhionn had stood long before, with the water to his knees. The holders came out to him one by one and put their hands between his hands: the holder of Dùn ìseal, and the holder of the Red Hill, looking small; the holder of the salt shore, and the holders of the north and the west; and each waded back with the front of his clothes wet through, and nobody laughed.

Then his own blood came.

They came down from the upstream ground, and she had not understood till then that they were all one family. Some were old and some were young, and the young ones were the eldest, her grandmother said, for the king's children were very old indeed and did not look it. They stood round him in the river in rings, the nearest ring his children and the next ring theirs, and then theirs, out to the banks, until the ford was full of them and the water was piling up behind their legs; and the reciters on the bank began to say their names, and said names until the sun went down, and had not finished. Some four hundred of his blood stood in the river that day, the reciters counted afterward. The girl thought that he did not know all their names either. She saw him look at a young woman in the outermost ring as a man looks at someone he has met and cannot place.

Only the households east of the vein sent no one to the ford. People remarked on it, and then forgot it, for the day was fine.

When the reciters stopped at last, the king said something to those nearest him, and they began to wade back to the banks; and when the ford was empty he stood there alone a little while in the dusk. The girl was nearest the water on the downstream side, for her mother had let go of her wrist at last. She said to the end of her life that the river going past her feet was warm, as warm as a bath, with the king standing in it, and that downstream of him the water smoked in the evening air like a spring.

## XIII. Of the Long Years

He did not grow old. That is the plainest thing the reciters say of him, and the hardest to take in. Somewhere in his second thousand years his hair went grey in a single winter, and then it stayed grey and went no further; and his hands and his back were those of the man who had knelt in the grove. The ordinary people of the island came and went round him, generation after generation, as the grass comes and goes round a stone. All that while he said nothing of what had gone into him in the grove. The power was his alone. It had gone, as the Keeper said, into the one hand that did not leave the fire, and there was no other hand it could go into.

His children had a portion of it all the same, as a child has something of the father's face. He had many wives in the long years, one after another, and buried every one of them; and the children born to him after the grove lived two thousand years and three thousand and more, and their children not so long, and theirs less again, each generation shorter-lived than the one before, as the Keeper had said. They were strong and slow to anger, and most of them were very proud. They held the hills and valleys of the kingdom under him, in shares he gave and took back as it pleased him, and the ordinary people of the island were used to being ruled by a grandmother who had ruled their great-grandmothers.

Somhairle was one of them, and the reciters of Clann na Ceiste keep his line beyond all the others, for reasons the Fifth Book tells. He was born in his father's sixty-second year, early among the children of the grove, and he never took a share of the kingdom or asked for one. He lived in the high valleys among Clann na Ceiste all his days, and kept the slab, and kept the lines, and his children after him did the same. He died on {{date:I-0104a}}, at Tobar dhìreach, and the Line of the Mason gives him two thousand nine hundred and eighty-seven years.

The king came up from the kingdom to bury him. The old people of Clann na Ceiste stood about the grave and looked at the two of them, the father with his straight back and the son in his shroud with a face like a withered apple, and could not find a word to say. The king put his son into the ground himself. Then he sat down on the turf beside the grave with his arms round his knees, like a boy, and stayed there until it was dark. One of the reciters went to him at last and asked whether he would come in to the fire.

"I have buried a wife in every century," said Ailean Mòr, "and a son before this one; and I shall bury them all. That was the bargain. I did not know it was the bargain when I made it."

After Somhairle the long years thinned in his line as they thinned in all the lines of the king's blood. His son Beathan, who had sat by the slab in the dark half of the year among the herdsmen, lived two thousand two hundred and thirteen years, and had died before his father; Beathan's son lived one thousand five hundred and ninety-four; and the six after him, each born about the middle of the life of the one before, lived one thousand one hundred and forty-eight years, and eight hundred and twenty-six, and five hundred and ninety-seven, and four hundred and thirty-one, and three hundred and eight, and two hundred and twenty-four; and the last of these was Gille-Brìde, who lived to see the Binding. Gille-Brìde's son Ceallach lived a hundred and forty years, and was the last of Somhairle's line in whom the long years showed. Ceallach's son lived and died as other people do, and no one could tell him from his neighbours.

That fading is why the island came to hold the whole of it a tale. When there was no one left alive who had seen a man of three hundred, there was no one to swear to it, and a thing no one can swear to goes into the tellings. By the middle of the Holy Age no one outside Clann na Ceiste believed that any king had lived four thousand years, or four hundred. The scribes of the Hall wrote the long years down as the reciters' tale, and set Ailean Mòr among the tellings beside the Hollow Man, and so he has stood since in every hall but the reciters' own. Clann na Ceiste kept Somhairle's line all the same, by mouth, as they keep everything; and what came of that keeping is told in the Fifth Book.

The coloured coal lay under the slab and in the pots of Clann na Ceiste, and the other households of the hills went on burning it by the handful in their common fires, dark-flamed, and never knew what it held.

## XIV. Of the Beacons, the Tide-Rocks and the Long Settling

In those years the coasts were lit. Fires were kept on the capes where Taigh-solais Baile ìseal, Taigh-solais Muileann bheag, Taigh-solais Àth dhearg and Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg stand now, and with the old fire at Baile chrom the coasts were lit at five points. The old fire warned boats off, and the tellings say these new ones were kept to bring them home in a storm. The tide-rock at Ros dhomhain passed to **Seònaid Mhòr**, who cut her marks in pairs, high water and low; the keepers after her went back to single marks. Inis thais cut its own marks at the same interval, and none says why the interval is what it is.

The salt-songs of the pans are the oldest songs on Dia-thìr whose words are said to survive, a refrain of four lines that no one can fully construe. At Cnoc àrsaidh near the panther road were found pipes of bird-bone bored with four holes, the oldest instruments on the island. Pilgrims sat a night at the edge of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar without entering, and came in numbers to the warm springs of Muileann ruadh, where the oldest cure-tale on Dia-thìr is set: a herder who came lame and walked home.

Then, over nearly two thousand years, the Dia-thìrich filled the island, and of that long settling only the shape can be told. A salt road ran from the pans of Baile ghorm through the ford to Muileann àrsaidh, where salt sold at three times its price. Dùn thais was banked, the first Dia-thìrich fort away from Dùn ìseal and the Red Hill, and Dùn chrom was raised in the island's middle with a bank that faces both ways; each was held by one of the king's blood, and each holder thought the other's bank faced the wrong way. Cuan ruadh was rebuilt in stone to the eaves against the wind, and Ceann chiar set a stone in its midst cut with one mark for each house the Black Wind took: twenty-two. Everywhere the settlers let the old things be. Those who built below Làrach an Dùin-fhaire took no stone from the ruin, though it was the nearest quarry, and a path was worn round Làrach an Teampaill at a distance. At Cnoc naomh the hill was already called holy when the settlers came; the hearths say the sìth were in it, and the settlers built below it and never on it.

On the hill of Muileann chaol there was a cattle-fold where the Library stands now. The ford gathering grew till it drew every coast. At Ceann leathan the gathering by the wind-stones ended with wrestling and the lifting of stones, and the heaviest, a black boulder, lies on the point still; few have raised it, and the hearths of that point say that the first to raise it was a big grey-haired man who came to the gathering once, as a stranger, and put it down again very gently, and would not give his name. In the north the oaks fell back before the heather and the bog-moss, and the groves that give the north its many Doire names shrank to the sheltered valleys; the grove at Doire ghlas was one of those that stood. The land kept its old wounds: the sea came again over Cnoc chaol, less far, and the crack at Cill ghlas moved and the spring went back uphill.

East of the vein a double-banked fort was raised at Dùn dhearg by households who owed nothing to Dùn ìseal. They were of the godkin like everyone else, but no blood of the king's was among them, and they were proud of it. The Stone Kings' list does not name its holders. It says only that Dùn dhearg did not come to the gatherings, until in the lifetime the tellings give to Catrìona Mhòr, before ever she held Dùn ìseal, its people came to the ford with seal-skins, asking for salt, and were given ground of their own on the far bank. Among the last settlings of the age was Inis mhòr, whose two facing rows of houses are the oldest street on Dia-thìr.

## XV. Of Catrìona Mhòr and the King's Last Years

The last name the Stone Kings' list gives is **Catrìona Mhòr**, twelfth of the line and of the king's blood, who knew, her telling says, every household of the hills by its line back seven names. The last in the Red Hill's spoken list is **Seumas Òg**. The list stops with him; in the next age what the reciters kept of it was written down.

Her people counted the households of the hills by knots on a cord, a knot to a hearth, and the cord from Dùn ìseal is said to have held some nine hundred knots, the largest count made on Dia-thìr in this age. The king's own blood was not on the cord. There was no counting them any longer, and no one tried.

The lot for the slab was drawn now from every part of the hills, and the keepers were made nine, one for each of nine districts, meeting at the slab when a keeper died and at no other time. Every household burned its handful in the dark of the year, measured in a copy of the stone cup, all at once by agreement, so that the low dark fires of the handful showed on every hillside on the same night. That night was Samhain, and it has been the night of the handful ever since. Only the household of the Cnoc dhubh trench refused its handful for as long as anyone remembered, the first on record to choose to burn nothing at all.

The words on the column at Cnoc ghorm, worn shallow, were recut along their old grooves by a hand from the Stone Kings' hill. Some later readers say the recutting changed a letter, and no one can prove which.

In the first summer of her holding the two king-lists were recited at the ford one after the other, from first name to last, and it took from dusk to dawn. The reciter of Dùn ìseal stood first, and gave Ailean Mòr and his bank, and Gormshuil and the herds, and Iain Dubh brought home from the shingle, and Calum Ciar refused; and when he came to her own name he said it and sat down, and she felt every face at the ford turn to her in the firelight. Then the reciter of the Red Hill stood, and gave Beathag Ruadh and her hill, and Seonag with her bag of pebbles, and Lachlann of the fostering, down to Seumas Òg, who had taken the Red Hill that same season and sat across the fire from her. It was the first time anyone had heard both lists whole. By the end the sky over the river was grey and the children were asleep in heaps. The king sat among the listeners all night with his cloak round him, and when the reciter of the hill said his name first of all, some of those nearest saw him smile.

She held Dùn ìseal a long time after that night, and she was old when the nine keepers came to her.

They came up together across the trodden ground of the ford while the gathering was full, and stood before her, and the one who spoke for them was older than she was. They asked that their charge be declared before the whole gathering: that the slab and the lot and the handful were theirs to keep, so that no king after her could draw the lot at Dùn ìseal as Calum Ciar had wanted to.

Catrìona Mhòr looked at them a long while. She knew them all, and their lines, seven names back. She looked past them at the gathering, the salt people and the hill people and the flint people on the far bank, all waiting to see what she would do.

"Not this year," she said. And when the old keeper opened his mouth: "Ask me again."

She gave no refusal and no consent, and the keepers went back to their slab to wait.

She died not many years after, and with her the hill's list ends; and it ends, the reciters say, because there was no one after her to put in it. Her kin came to Dùn ìseal from every part of the kingdom to be named after her, and there were more claims to the hill than there were houses on it. They were all of the king's blood. Each of them could say the two names of their line, and the second was always Ailean's. On {{date:I-0255a}} the king came to Dùn ìseal himself and heard them all, sitting on the bank where he had sat the first day, and when they had done he gave the hill to none of them. He held it in his own hand, as he had held it in the first days before Gormshuil, and he stayed on it.

That was the beginning of the trouble, though no one called it so then. The kin had long held the kingdom in shares, as the king gave and took back; and now, seeing him keep the hill, they began to say among themselves that he meant to name no one, and that when he went, if he ever went, the shares would be fought for. They said it at the ford and in each other's halls, never before him, and they began to count their own shares and each other's, and to marry with an eye to what a marriage would bring when the shares fell open. The king knew, and did nothing about it that anyone could see.

And east of the vein, where there was no king's blood, Dùn dhearg watched all this and drew its own counsel. On {{date:I-0255b}} the king sent to Dùn dhearg and to the households about it, bidding them come in to him at last; and they sent his messenger back with nothing, and swore among themselves that they would owe nothing to Dùn ìseal nor to its king. From that year the spears of the east were gathered at Dùn dhearg, slowly, as a river gathers behind a dam.

So the age came to {{date:I-0256}}. The handful was kept in every hearth of the hills; the keepers were nine; the sharing before the storms was kept on every coast; the doctrine was said at every gathering. The Old Ones were names on the shore. The three ruins stood empty and unquarried, the two shafts gave back no sound, and the slab lay over the crack as the mason's people had laid it nearly ten thousand years before. No oath had been sworn over any of it; Brìde's flame and Crom's stone come into the story with the Binding. What had been custom waited to be law, and what had been a covered wound in the rock waited to be named a flame. The king sat on his hill, and his kin waited about him, and the east waited beyond the vein; and it was a long wait, even by the reckoning of those days.

## XVI. Of Blàr Àth na Fala

The east came at the end of the summer, in the year the reciters count the four thousand six hundred and third of the Grove, and the tellers who give it best are the tellers of the east themselves, who had cause to remember.

There was a lad in the host of Dùn dhearg, sixteen years old and the youngest of his household's spears, whose name the east did not keep; but his telling it kept, and here it is.

He had come down to the river with the rest in the dark before the first morning. The river runs below Dùn dhearg over a wide ford of gravel, and the west bank rises from it in a steep slope of turf with a track cut up it, which is the road to the vein and to Dùn ìseal. The host had been told that the king was coming east with his kin at last, and meant to hold the ford against him. Its fires ran along the east bank for a mile.

In the grey light, there was one man on the west bank.

He was sitting on a stone at the head of the ford with his spear across his knees, and there was no one with him. The lad looked up and down the far bank for the rest, for the king's kin and their households and the spears of the hills, and there was nothing: the turf and the track going up and the mist lifting off the river. Word went down the host that the king had come ahead of his kin with a handful of his household, and had sent the handful back up the road that night; and that his kin had not come. Each was waiting to see whether the others would go, each afraid that while their spears were at the ford their cousins' spears would be on their hills. They were already quarrelling over what the old man would leave. Nobody in the host of Dùn dhearg could believe it, and then everybody could, and a great shout went up along the east bank.

The man on the stone stood up.

The lad said that he had heard all his life how the king was big, and that the king was not big as he had pictured, and was bigger. He came down to the water's edge and stood there, and the grey of his hair was the only grey on that bank; and the lad, who had a spear in his hand and three thousand spears beside him, felt the warmth of him come across the river like the warmth of a kiln, and was afraid, and did not know why.

The first rush went into the ford at sunrise. The lad was not in it, being young, and he watched it from the bank. Some two hundred went in together with a roar and the water went white round their knees; and the king went into the river to meet them, and did not seem to hurry, and the front of the rush reached him in the middle of the ford.

The lad could not afterward tell what he saw. He said the king did not fight as men fight, with a man in front of him and then another. He fought as a river fights a bank. Spears went into the place where he was and he was not there, and men went down round him in the water in a ring, and the water ran red downstream of him in the sunrise; and the rush broke on him and went back, and he walked back to the west bank and sat down on his stone again, and the river carried the dead downstream until they caught on the gravel at the shallows and began to pile there.

All that day they came at him, and he held the ford. They came in rushes and in lines, and across the deep channel upstream to take him from the side, and he was there before them. They threw spears from the bank until there were none to throw, and the lad said that some of them struck him and did not seem to matter. At dusk the host drew back to its fires. The king went back to his stone, and drank from the river, and the lad saw him wash his hands.

On the second day the dead began to dam the river. They lay so thick at the shallows below the ford that the water backed up behind them and spread out over the gravel, and the ford grew wide and slow, and red, and the men of the east had to climb over their own dead to come at him. The lad went in on the second day. He remembered little of it: the cold of the water and then its warmth, for the river was warm round the king as a spring is warm; a face above him, calm and grey-browed, and a hand that took the spear out of his hands as one takes a knife from a child; and then he was on the east bank again, face down in the mud, and alive. He lay there till dark, and heard the rushes go in all the rest of that day, and heard them come back fewer.

In the night of the second day the host of Dùn dhearg met at its fires. Some said to go home. And a greybeard of the eastern households said that no one man could hold a ford for ever; that he was of flesh; and that if every spear in the east went into the river together on the third day, and kept on going in, they would come to the end of him, as the sea comes to the end of a cliff. It was the only counsel anybody had. They took it.

On the third day every spear the east had went into the river together.

The lad went in with them. The water was above his waist where the dead had backed it up, and it was red and warm, and it stank. He was far from the front, and saw the king only in glimpses, over heads and between spears: a grey head in the middle of the river with the whole host of the east closing on it from three sides, and the host breaking round it and closing again, as the tide breaks round a rock and closes behind; hour after hour, the whole day long, while the sun went over. The lad said that there was light round the king by the afternoon, a light of no colour he knew, low on the water, dark and shining, and that where it touched the river the river steamed. He said that men who were near it said afterward that they saw every colour in it and none, and would not say more.

The kin did not come.

At sunset the king was still standing, in the middle of the ford, with the host around him and the dead around him higher than his knees, and the river going over the dam of the dead in a red fall at the shallows. He was not beaten. The lad was sure of that to the end of his life, and he told it to his grandchildren as the one thing he was sure of. Not one of them had beaten him, and no one of them could have. It was only that there was no end to them, and there was an end to him; and that was all.

He fell on the third night, in the dark, on {{date:I-0098a}}. No one saw the spear that did it, and no one ever claimed it, and in all the east no household would afterward let it be said that the blow was theirs. They say that when he went down the light went out of the river all at once, as a coal goes out when water is thrown on it, and that the river, which had been warm three days, ran cold. Then the host stood in the dark in the water and did not know what to do, and some began to weep, and they did not know why they wept.

In the morning they drew him out of the river onto the west bank, where his stone was. He lay on his back with his eyes open, and his face was the face of a young man, with no line on it that had not been there in the grove. The Line of the Mason, which keeps the years of its dead beside their names, gives his as four thousand six hundred and twenty-six. He had been king since the grove, and he had not given it up, and no man living had taken it from him. Four thousand and six hundred years he had been king of the godkin; and the whole east, from the vein to the sea, had come against him at last, and he had held them three days alone at a ford.

The east has a saying of it still, in the mouths of those who do not know where it comes from:

> *Cha do leag aon duine e; leag an sluagh uile e.*
> No one man brought him down; the whole host brought him down.

The ford was called after that day *Àth na Fala*, the Ford of Blood, and the battle *Blàr Àth na Fala*, and the river below Dùn dhearg was not fished for a generation. The lad went home and lived as long as the ordinary live, and when his grandchildren asked him what the king had been like, he would say only that he had been warm, and look at the fire.

The first of his kin came to the ford on the fourth day, and found their king on the bank.

Here the Second Book ends, at the Ford of Blood; for with Ailean Mòr the one hand that held the kingdom was gone, and his kin were many, and each of them had a claim. How they parted the kingdom among them, and quarrelled over the parting, and at the last could agree on nothing but the vein, which no share could hold; and how, three weeks after the king fell, the heirs of his blood swore to that at Dùn ìseal, and so the First Flame was bound: that is the beginning of the Third Book, and of the Holy Age.
