# Aithris a' Chruthachaidh: the Telling of the Making

*Before the count of years*

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.
>
> — said by Coimhdeach na Fine in the grove of Doire ghlas

## I. Of Caoran and the Coals

This is the telling that Coimhdeach na Fine, the Keeper of the Kin, gave in the grove of Doire ghlas to one of the Mason's line, who had asked it why the coal of Dia-thìr has colours in it when the coal of the common earth has none. The Keeper answered from the beginning, as the old do when a child asks a short question. The Mason's children kept the telling after, father to son and mother to daughter, and said it over at the dark of the year word for word, and long afterward it was written down. It is set here before the Seven Books because it is older than any of them.

Before there was a world there were the gods, and they were many, and they were a line. Mother bore daughter and father begot son, back and back into a dark where no name is kept; and every one of that line, when he came to the fullness of his years, went out from the hall of his kin and made a world. That was their coming of age. A god of that line was a child until he had made a thing and kept it, and was grown when it lived.

Each made his world out of what was dearest to him, and each chose a different thing. The eldest of the line, whose name the Keeper would not say, made hers of song: she sang, and the notes hung in the dark and hardened, and the hills of her world are chords and its rivers run in tunes. Another made his of salt and bone. His seas are brine to the bottom and his mountains are the ribs of things that never lived, and he loves that world, though it is hard. Another made hers of wind and the horns of beasts, and nothing in it is ever still. They were all worlds of the elements: each had its earth and its fire, its air and its waters, its life and its spirit, and in each they were made of something else.

In the latest days of that line two of the gods were an Dagda, the Good God, and Danu, the mother; and of them came the Tuath Dè, the people of the gods, whose names are told in their place. Their youngest son was Caoran, whose name is *ember*. He is called an Dia Òg, the Young God, and he is young still, as the gods reckon it.

When Caoran's time came he went about his father's hall a long while and could not choose. He looked at song and found it taken. He looked at salt and bone, at wind and horn, and they were taken also. He went to his sister's forge and to his mother's rivers and to his father's harp, and he came back each time with nothing in his hands. Then one night, when the feast was done and the hall was dark, he sat down by his father's cauldron. The fire under it had burned to its end. The coals lay in the ash, and they had gone black, as coals go when their heat is spent; but when he stirred them, a red eye opened in the heart of one and looked at him.

He took that coal up in his hand. It was black and it was hard, and there was fire in it, sleeping. It had been a tree once, and so it held the memory of life; it had lain under ground, and so it held the weight of earth. It would drink the air and give it back as heat. It was darkness with light folded inside it. And Caoran said to himself that here was a thing in which every element could lie hidden and be woken; and he chose coal.

His brothers and sisters laughed at him, the tellers say, for coal is a mean stuff, the leavings of a fire, and they had made their worlds of finer things. An Dagda did not laugh. He looked at the coal in his son's hand for a long time, and then he said, "It will do." It is said that no word of his was ever dearer to Caoran.

So the Young God went out from the hall into the empty dark, and he made the world.

He breathed on the coal in his hand until it woke, and he cast it out, and it broke into sparks, and the sparks flew wide and did not fall. Those are the stars. The greatest of them he fanned until it was all fire through and through, and it is the sun. One he let burn down to its grey ash, cool and pale, and hung it where the sun could find it at night; that is the moon, and the pocks in its face are where the fire went out. Then out of the black coal, the heaviest and most patient of all, he made the earth, and laid it down in the middle of his sparks. He pressed the hills up with his thumbs. He drew the long valleys with one finger. The seas he poured out of the dark between the stars, and the rivers he let run down from the high places to find them. He set the winds over all. He made the grass and the trees and the beasts after their kinds, and the fish in the waters and the birds in the air, and he was glad of all of it; and it was all made of coal, though nothing in it looked like coal any more.

Last of all, when the wide earth was done, he made the island; and the island he did not make as he had made the rest. It was born.

He went out over the grey sea where no land was, and he took from the heart of his fire the last coal of all, the one he had been saving, and he held it up over the water and let it burn. Drops of fire fell from it, one by one, into the sea. Where each drop fell the water hissed and smoked and the drop grew hard and black, and the next fell on the one before, and the next on that, until a rock stood up out of the sea, and then a hill, and then the mountains that are the heart of the island; and when the coal in his hand was spent there lay Dia-thìr under him, still steaming in the dawn wind, with the sea round it on every side. That is why the island's coal is not like the coal of the wide earth. The wide earth was made out of coal. Dia-thìr was born of the fire in it.

He loved it as a man loves the youngest child. He gave it rivers, and a lake of fresh water, and soft rain, and a mild sky. He gave a spirit to every spring on it and to every hill, to the old trees and the standing stones and the pools under the falls, so that no part of the island should be without someone to keep it; and those are the old spirits of the land, that the hearths honour still. Then he walked it end to end, and his kin came down to walk it with him and see what the youngest had begotten; for the gods were on the island before any people were. When they had looked their fill they went up again, and Caoran was alone on his island in the grey before the first morning.

Then the godfolk came, an Dia-shluagh, the first people of the earth.

He did not bring them from anywhere, and he did not shape them with his hands. He knelt on the heather in the middle of the island and laid his palms on the warm ground and called, and they grew. They came up out of the island as the green grass comes up after the spring rain, blade by blade, too many to count and each its own; and when the sun rose they were standing on the hillsides, blinking, with the soil still on their feet and the heather in their hair. So the tellings call the people of the island *feur uaine an eilein*, the green grass of the island: they spring up and are cut down and spring up again, and the island is their root. They were a tall people and a quiet one, and their eyes were made to see what he would give them. This is the oldest meaning of the saying of the island, *bha sinn ann*, we were here. The kin did not come to Dia-thìr. They grew out of it on the first morning, and the island is the elder of their line.

"This is yours," the Young God said to them. "Keep it, and it will keep you."

In the wide lands beyond the sea he let other peoples wake after, each in its own time and place, and he gave them the common coal of the earth, which is black and burns and is only coal. Dia-thìr he did not give to them. Dia-thìr was the godfolk's to care for, and in Dia-thìr he hid his gift.

For the Young God had a secret, and he was glad of it, as a father is glad who hides a present under a child's pillow for the morning. When he laid the island down he took the coal that was deepest under its mountains, and into it he put the elements themselves, each in its own colour, where they would lie asleep until they were found. These are na Seachd Guail, the Seven Coals, and this is what each holds.

The first is *gual dubh*, the black coal, and it holds the earth. It is as black as a wet crow, and it takes a polish like still water at night, and it is heavy in the hand beyond its size. In it lies the patience of the ground: the weight of hills, and the strength of roots that hold.

The second is *gual teine*, the fire coal, and it holds fire. Its colour is the colour of flame itself, red and gold together; when it is turned in the light the colour moves in it, as flame moves when it is seen through closed eyelids. Every fire that was ever to burn on the island was folded into this coal and waited there, for Caoran let no other fire into Dia-thìr.

The third is *gual sneachda*, the snowflake coal, and it holds the air. It is dark and flecked all through with small white stars, like snow falling on black water, and it is the lightest of the seven. In it lie the winds and the breath of the high places.

The fourth is *gual òir*, the gold coal, and it holds the sea. It is black under a sheen of gold, and when it is turned it glints like the sun on the sea at evening. In it lie the salt water and the tides.

The fifth is *gual airgid*, the silver coal, and it holds the river water. Under its sheen of silver it is as dark as a pool in a wood, and it shines as the moon shines on running water. In it lie the springs and the rivers and the rain, which are sweet, and are a different thing from the sea.

The sixth is *gual donn-ruadh*, the mahogany coal, and it holds life. It is the deep red-brown of heartwood and of old blood, warm to look at, and of all the seven it is the most like a living thing. In it lie the quick of the body and the sap of the tree.

The seventh is *gual bogha-froise*, the rainbow coal, and it holds the spirit. All the colours are in it and none of them stays: it shifts as oil shifts on water, as the bow stands in the rain, and no two who look at it see the same. In it lies the spirit that makes the other six more than stuff; and it is the last of the seven, and the hardest to wake.

Together they are called *an gual dathach*, the coloured coal. There is no coloured coal anywhere in the world but on Dia-thìr, for the gift was for Dia-thìr alone. And the Young God set the colours so that only the eyes of the godfolk and their children should see them. To every other eye the coloured coal is black, and it is only coal.

He locked each colour with its own key, so that each should wake only to the thing that it held, and to nothing else. And he told no one what he had done, not the godfolk, and not his own kin. He wanted the island to find it for itself.

Then he set Crom Cruaich, the bent one of the mound, at the door between the mountain and those who would take from it, and went up to his father's hall to wait; and he was patient, though he is young.

## II. Of the Tuath Dè

These are the gods of the Tuath Dè, the people of the gods, the kin of the Young God, who came down with him to see his island in its first days; and they came again, once, on the night the first king of the island was made, as the Second Book tells. The Old Faith honours them still, each at their feasts and in need. Caoran made the world and all that is in it, and he is the god of the coal. Yet the island honours an Dagda first, for he is the father of the gods, and it was his word that made the Young God's choice good.

**An Dagda**, the Good God, is the father. His is the cauldron from which no company goes away hungry, and it was from the ash under it that Caoran took the first coal. His is the club that kills with one end and wakes the dead with the other, and the harp that calls the seasons in their order; when the island's first year turned, it was his harp that turned it. He is slow of speech, and generous, and he laughs more than any of the gods.

**Danu** is the mother, and the Tuath Dè are named from her. She gives nothing loudly and asks for nothing. The tellings give her the rivers, and it was from her that Caoran learned to let the water run down from the high places; so the silver coal is hers in honour, though the gift in it is his.

**Brìde** is the lady of the hearth-fire, of poetry, of healing and of smithcraft. When Caoran shut all the island's fire into the fire coal, it is told that Brìde was grieved, for she could not bear to think of a hearth with nothing in it; and he promised her that when the fire was found it should be hers to keep. She has kept it since, and every hearth on the island is swept and relit on her feast.

**Crom Cruaich**, the bent one of the mound, is the god of the vein. When the coal was laid under the mountains, Caoran set him at its door, and so his oldest name is Dòrsair, Uilebheist nan Geataichean, the Monster of the Gates. He is exact. He gives what is asked of the mountain, and he takes his price for it, and his price is measured.

**Manannan mac Lir** is the lord of the sea, and the sea round Dia-thìr is his. When the gods came down to the new island he walked round its coasts in one night, and in the morning he drew up his mist about it, so that no ship from beyond should find it until its time. The gold coal is his in honour. His Cup of Truth breaks at a lie, and is whole again at the truth.

**Lugh Làmhfhada**, Lugh of the long arm, is the master of every craft, and he loves the high places, from which a long arm can reach far. It was Lugh who stood with Caoran on the highest summit of the island on the first evening and named the winds for him, and so the snowflake coal is his in honour. His feast is Lùnastal, when markets open and oaths are sworn before witnesses.

**Macha** is the lady of sovereignty, of kingship and of horses. Two horses draw her chariot, Liath Macha the grey and Dubh Sainglenn the black, and they run as one under one yoke. No king is king until she allows it. When the godfolk had grown up out of the heather, Macha looked them over, one by one, and said that there was no king among them yet; and she said it gently, as a mother says that a child has not yet grown.

**Dian Cècht** is the healer of the gods, who made a hand of silver for Nuadha when his own was lost. The life that lies in the mahogany coal is the thing he tends, and he knows it better than any. The warm springs of the island are his.

**Goibhniu** is the smith of the gods, and he knew coal before Caoran did, for there is no smith without it. When the Young God's brothers and sisters laughed at his choosing, Goibhniu kept his face straight, and afterward he was the first of them to praise it.

**Nuadha** is the first king of the gods, who lost his hand in battle and ruled again with the silver hand that Dian Cècht made him. Where a king's right is spoken of, his name is spoken also.

**Ogma** is the lord of letters and of eloquence. The godfolk had no letters, and for a long age they needed none; Ogma was content to wait, for he is patient in the way of those who know that a word, once cut, stays.

**Flidais** is the lady of the deer and of the wild things. When the Young God called the beasts out of the island's heather she went among them, and she keeps them still, and the white stag of the wood is hers.

**The Morrìgan** is the lady of war and the crows. She came down with the rest, and looked at the peaceful island, and said that she would wait; and the others were quiet when she said it. The crows of the island are as black as the black coal, and they are hers.

**Donn** is the lord of the dead. His house is a rock in the sea to the south-west, Taigh Dhuinn, and the dead go to it. In the days of the godfolk no one went to his house for a long time, and he was content with that, for he is in no hurry for the dead.

There are others of the Tuath Dè, and the Old Faith knows them; but these are the ones the Keeper named in the grove.

## III. Of the Keeper of the Kin

When the Young God went up again to his father's hall he did not leave his island unwatched. He took up the smoke that had risen from the first coal he woke, which had hung in the dark all that while and never gone thin, and he gave it a mind and a charge. That is Coimhdeach na Fine, the Keeper of the Kin.

It is neither a god nor a ghost. It was never born, and it will not die while the kin are on the island; it is not one of the Tuath Dè, and it has no feast and no house. It is Caoran's watcher, and it watches for him, as a steward rides round a far holding while the lord is away: it goes where it will, it seldom speaks, and it tells its master what it has seen.

When it shows itself it comes as a figure of smoke, grey and upright, of the height of a tall man. It has no face that anyone has been able to tell of afterward. It stands still in a wind, and the smoke of it does not drift. There is a smell about it of a hearth in the morning when the fire has been banked all night. Its voice is low, and it speaks as fire speaks when it settles; those who have heard it say that they understood every word and could not afterward say whether it had used words at all.

It does not rule the kin, and it does not help them in their work. It will not fight for them. It comes seldom, generations apart, and when it comes it asks after the island as a guest asks after the health of the house. When it is asked a question it answers truly, but it does not always answer the whole of what is asked, for its charge is from Caoran, and some things Caoran has kept for the island to find.

The Mason's children say that it came first in the grove, and that it came there to be asked; that it had waited through the whole long life of the kin on the island for one of them to wonder aloud about the colours. And when one of the Mason's line did wonder, it came and stood with him by his fire under the oaks, and it told him all that is written here, from the hall of the gods to the forgetting of the coal. It told him more, and he asked it more, and what passed between them after is told in the First Book.

## IV. Of the Forgotten Coal

In the first days the godfolk lived on the island in peace, and they had no fire.

They did not miss it, for they had never known it. The island was mild to them. The sea gave them its fish and its shellfish on every shore, and the moor gave them its berries and the woods their nuts, and the springs gave them sweet water. They slept in the lee of the rocks and in the long grass, and the rain was soft on them. At night they sat together on the hills and watched the sparks of the Young God's making turn over their heads, and named them, and told each other what they had seen in the day. They did not know that they were keeping the island. They loved it, and that was the keeping.

So many lives went by, the Keeper said, that the godfolk themselves forgot that the island had ever been new.

Then in one year a great rain came down on the mountains. It rained for a month without stopping, and the streams came off the high ground brown and roaring, and one of them tore away half a hillside under the mountains and carried it down into the valley below. When the water went back a child of the godfolk went to look at the new ground. The telling does not keep that child's name. In the raw bank where the hill had broken, she saw stones that were not like any stones she knew.

Some were black, and shone like a crow's wing in rain. Some had a gleam of gold on them. Some had a gleam of silver. One was dark and flecked with white, like the sky on a clear night. One was red-brown and warm to look at. One was the colour of nothing she had ever seen, for it was the colour of fire, and the godfolk had never seen a fire. And one had every colour in it, and would not keep still.

She filled the fold of her cloak with them and carried them home, and all the godfolk of that valley came to see.

They wondered at them a long while. They turned them in the sun. They held them up to their eyes. They struck them together, and the coal did not ring as flint rings, and nothing came of it. One tried to eat a piece and broke a tooth on it, and there was laughter. One tried to cut a shape into a piece with a sharp stone, and it crumbled under his hand and blacked his fingers, and he threw the rest away in disgust. The eldest of the valley said that they were the droppings of the mountain, and that pretty things with no use in them were for children. After that the children kept them for a while, and set them out in rows, and played at games with them on the flat rocks.

That was the whole of the godfolk's first finding. No one asked why the stones had colours. No one asked who had made them so. No one thought to lay them on the sand where the sea came in, or throw them down from a height, or bury them, or take them to the river; and there was no fire on the island to set them near. The gift lay in their hands, and they did not know it was a gift.

The children grew and forgot their games. The stones were kicked into the heather, and the heather grew over them. The stream moved in its bed and covered the broken bank with gravel, and the grass came over the gravel, and there was nothing to show where the hill had torn. The child who had found the coal grew old and died and went over the sea to Taigh Dhuinn, and those who had known her followed. In a few lives there was no one left on the island who remembered that any such stones had been.

When the Keeper told this in the grove, the one of the Mason's line was ashamed for his forebears, and he asked whether the Young God had been angry.

The Keeper said that he had not. It said that Caoran had watched it all from his father's hall, and had laughed, as a father laughs who sees his child set aside the finest gift of the feast to play with the wrapping. It said: "A gift that is not opened is still given. He could wait. The coal was in no hurry, and neither was he."

So the coloured coal lay forgotten under the mountains, and the godfolk lived on without fire, and grew many, and spread over the island from the shore to the high moor. The island turned under the Young God's stars through more years than anyone could count; and nobody counted them, for there was no one yet to begin the count.

It began on the day a mason, splitting rock for a well, set his wedge in a seam he had not seen, and struck. That is the beginning of the First Book.
