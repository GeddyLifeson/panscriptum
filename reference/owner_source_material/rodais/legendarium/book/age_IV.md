# The Fourth Book: An Aois Choigreach, the Age of Strangers

> *Thàinig na coigrich air an t-sàl, agus dh'fhàg iad an cnoc falamh.*
>
> The strangers came over the brine, and they left the hill empty.
>
> — a line sung in the camps, as the copyists at Muileann chaol set it down

*Here is told the history of Rodos from the Crossing to the Severance: the hundred and fifty years in which the island was ruled, first by leave and then without it, by people from over the western sea. It is drawn from the rolls of the custodians, most of which survive only in the copies kept in the cellars of Muileann chaol; from the letter-books of the humans' Administration, left behind when the Residency was emptied; from the daybooks of the beacon-keepers; and from the songs of the camps. Where these witnesses disagree, both are given. Where they are silent, this account is silent also.*

## Of the Three Ships and the Leave to Stay

The age begins with the Crossing, on {{date:IV-0001}}, when the humans first made landfall on Rodos. That winter Manannan's mist, which had lain on the western sea since the fleet of the Sundering sailed into it and had hidden the island from every ship beyond, lifted, and the humans' ships came out of the thin grey of it. The roll of Cuan shean, which is the fuller witness, does not enter their ships until the thirteenth of that month: three hulls out of the west riding at anchor off the town, the first of their size seen from any shore of the island since the fleet of the Sundering went out sunwise from the harbour and did not come back. Where the strangers were in the days between, the rolls do not say. The fishers of Achadh àrsaidh say it for them: their boats met the three ships off the north-west cape and led them down the coast ({{date:IV-0001a}}), and they still call the place Coinneachadh nan Long.

Their master was a captain whom the record calls Edmund Harrow. He came ashore without arms and with eleven men, and asked by signs for water. The custodians of Cuan shean gave it. They would give no more on their own word, for no custodian living had been asked such a thing, and they sent inland to the capital for counsel. For forty days the strangers were fed on the beach and not allowed beyond the dunes.

On {{date:IV-0005}} the Council of Custodians at Cathair dhearg, which had sat in the crown's place since the crown fell vacant at the close of the Age of Sundering, under the high custodian Tormod mac Ailein, confirmed the strangers' leave to stay. The council roll records the vote as unanimous. A later hand has written in the margin that three custodians were absent. Who they were the margin does not say; but the chroniclers have kept the note, for it is the first of many places in the record of this age where what was written and what was so are not quite the same.

Soon after, the old sailors of Cuan shean began to say that the strangers had come in from the same quarter of the sea into which the Sundering fleet had sailed. The council forbade the remark in its hall. It was written down anyway, and it was never answered, and this account will not answer it either. Whether the humans are children of the departed, or of another people of those waters, or of no one the Ròdaich ever knew, is not known on Rodos.

The strangers named their homeland in their own tongue, but no Ròdach scribe set the word down. From the first season the record calls it *an Tìr Thall*, the land beyond, and calls its people simply the humans; and so they are called in this history. They brought their own faith over the sea with them, the faith of Crìosd, one God and no other, with its priests, its Bible and its saints. The Ròdaich called it *an Eaglais*, the Church, after the word its clerics used, and so it is called here.

Then came the season of words. The humans' clerics of the Church and the custodians sat together to build a common vocabulary, and neither side's account of what was agreed in that season has come down whole. The humans' cleric Samuel Wren and the custodian Gormshuil nic Fhearchair kept word-lists side by side. Wren's survives in the Administration's letter-books. Gormshuil's was lost, and only its first leaf is known, from a copy: forty words for the colours of broken ore. Her copyist noted a thing that the chroniclers have since set at the head of the whole age. The humans had a single word for the black ore of the vein, and it was the word for the fuel they burned at home. No Ròdais word in either list answered to it. And the humans did not ask for one.

## Of the Years of Fair Exchange

It must be set down plainly, because what came after has cast its shadow back over it, that the first years were good. The exchange was reciprocal. The island had been alone for eighteen centuries and lacked much that the humans had; the humans lacked much the island could spare. For a few years the arrangement looked as if it would last, and the chroniclers do not think those who trusted it fools.

The humans set up a trading post at Ros dhomhain, the capital's harbour: iron tools, window glass, lamp-oil and paper, against wool, salt fish, hides and dyed cloth. Its first tally-book, kept by the trader Thomas Pike, balances evenly across its first year. The smiths of Cathair dhearg learned in a single winter to temper the humans' bar-iron, and within a few seasons the old bronze hoes were melted down for bells. The salt-pans of Seann Warr sold to Pike by the barrel, the first Ròdach trade the humans came back asking for twice.

Harrow sailed west and came back with four ships instead of three, bringing carpenters, a surgeon, and a letter from a trading house in an Tìr Thall asking what else the island had. The surgeon, Jonas Tull, set the leg of a Cuan shean fisherman whom the town had given up for lame, and the custodian entered it in the roll as the first debt owed to a stranger.

In {{year:IV-0022}} the small port of Baile dhìreach, on the south-west coast, took Ròdaich and humans under one council of six, three of either people, with a founding compact written twice, once in each tongue: the only such compact on record in the age. It was chaired in alternate years by the fisherman Iain mac Chaluim and the human carpenter William Rook, and its harbour dues went half to the custodians and half to the humans' post. At Baile dhìreach the net-maker Eilidh nic Ruairidh married the human cooper Samuel Hale. The council entered the marriage in both its books. The custodians at Cathair dhearg, asked for a ruling, sent none. Later history did not repeat the experiment, and this book will come back to that marriage when it reaches the years in which the humans' law found a use for it.

In {{year:IV-0025}} a human supply ship struck the western reefs in fog and nine of her crew were drowned; her master had been sailing, the letter-books complain, by a chart drawn from memory on a coast lit only by the old driftwood fires. The human navigators asked for beacons, and the Ròdaich engineers agreed, not yet understanding what the request would enable. The navigator James Fenn and the Ròdach mason Torcall Clachair walked the coast together to choose sites for the new beacons, and marked the five headlands of the old fires. Fenn's chart survives. Torcall's cairns, for the most part, do not.

## Of the Treaty in One Tongue, and the Company

In {{year:IV-0029}} the terms of the humans' presence were set down in writing for the first time, in the humans' tongue only, and nobody on the Ròdach side is recorded objecting. The treaty was signed on {{date:IV-0030}} in the council hall at Cathair dhearg, by Tormod mac Ailein for the custodians and by Josiah Pell, newly come as the humans' first Commissioner, for the Administration. Tormod could not read the tongue in which it was written. The record does not say who read it to him.

Pell built a house of dressed stone on the rise above Ros dhomhain and called it the Residency, and from it the humans governed Rodos for a hundred and forty years. Harrow went back to the sea, and with Pike he formed a trading house licensed by the Administration, known on Rodos simply as the Company. Its charter gave it first refusal on "all fuels, ores and minerals the island may yield." Its first building of its own was a stone warehouse at Cathair dhomhain, along the lake shore, to keep its tools from the harbour thieves. The chroniclers ask the reader to remember that warehouse.

The strangers' fever came ashore at Cuan shean with a crowded ship in {{year:IV-0034}} and ran down the west coast. Ninety-one Ròdaich are recorded dead, and eleven humans. The custodians closed Cuan shean to ships for a season, and Pell protested in writing.

In {{year:IV-0037}} the Company sent two prospectors, Abel Stone and Hugh Mercer, into the hills east of the capital, the country of Muileann chrom. They came back with sacks of broken ore and word that the black seam ran "further than can be walked in a day." The custodians demanded the ore back, to be laid in the rock again with the proper words. The Company returned half. The roll records the other half as "carried west."

In {{year:IV-0039}} trade became arrangement. Under the first extraction contracts Ròdach labour began to mine the vein at a scale no reverent tradition had ever used. A crew of thirty opened the first pit at Muileann chrom, paid by the ton in iron tools and lamp-oil, and the chroniclers set beside it the plain fact that the custodians' own cuttings had never been measured by weight at all. The Company built an ore quay at Ceann fhada, a fishing hamlet below the hills, and paid the fishermen for their foreshore in iron nails, by weight. Wren finished his grammar of Ròdais for the Mission, which was the Church's mission to the island, careful and accurate in most particulars, rendering the word for the custodians of the vein as "keepers of the coal." The Mission raised a chapel on the quay at Ros dhomhain and hung in it the first iron bell on the island, and the custodians asked that it not be rung before the fishing fleet was out.

In {{year:IV-0045}} one settlement refused to sign. It was Doire mhòr, in the hills above Muileann chrom, and its custodian was Beathag Dhubh. Her answer survives only in the Company agent's paraphrase: that the hill was not hers to sell and not his to buy. Doire mhòr was not punished. The Company opened its next working at Cnoc fhiadhaich instead, and ran its cart-road around Doire mhòr's fields rather than through them. The annals add two words: *for now*.

The first of the new beacons was finished in {{year:IV-0048}} on the southern headland at Tobar dhearg, a round tower of grey stone raised over the old fire-tower and paid for by the Company; Torcall Clachair built it and Fenn set its lens.

In {{year:IV-0052}} the humans brought in their coin, for convenience, they said, alongside the barter of the island: the mark and its hundred pence, struck at Ros dhomhain from metal shipped in from an Tìr Thall, with a ship on one face and nothing Rodian on either. At the capital the custodians' clerks began to keep two sets of books, and complained of it in both. Within ten years the mark was the only thing that would buy anything that came across the sea.

Wren spent a winter at Dùn thais among the druids of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh. He asked them why the vein should be holy. They asked him why his fuel should be burned. Neither account records a winner. Wren wrote home that the druids told of a voyage west over the sea to an isle of the blessed very like his own Church's telling of Naomh Breandan, told from the other shore, and that he did not know what to make of it.

The Harbour Articles, written like the treaty in the humans' tongue alone, fixed the dues at Ros dhomhain in the Company's favour, and this time two custodians refused to sign. The council granted the Administration upland grazing in the north-west around Muileann àrsaidh for human families who would not work in the pits. The grant was for grazing. The families built houses of stone.

Tormod mac Ailein died at Cathair dhearg in {{year:IV-0061}}, and the council chose Coinneach Glas of Ceann àrsaidh to follow him, a man who had never met a human before the Crossing and had dealt with little else since. In {{year:IV-0062}} Edmund Harrow's ship did not come home from the western crossing. The Company passed wholly to Pike, and the letter-books mourned Harrow as the man who found Rodos.

## Of the Lapse of the Law, and the First Steward

The Small-Burning Law had been kept since the Holy Age: that the vein be cut and burned sparingly, and that what the hill could give in a year be counted and not exceeded. The Age of Sundering ended with it still kept. Within a generation of the Crossing it was gone. No repeal is recorded. It stopped being enforced when it stopped being profitable to enforce. At Dùn dhearg the custodian Fionnghal nic Artair wrote to the council the only defence of the Law that survives. It argues that the Law had never been about the ore, but about the people who would become whatever the burning made them.

At Seann Skell the hawk-priest Murchadh Seabhag preached that Mòd, the Hungry Hawk, starves those who eat faster than the land can feed them, and his sermon was the first Ròdach text the Company's agents were told to confiscate. At Muileann chrom the Company burned ore by the cartload to drive its pumps, and when Coinneach Glas's council asked that the tally be observed, it received a copy of the Company's accounts instead.

Pell went home in {{year:IV-0069}} and did not return. Henry Maddox came in his place, with instructions to double the yield and a letter promising him a share of it. In {{year:IV-0071}} a gallery at Cnoc fhiadhaich, driven forward faster than any Ròdach crew would have chosen and without the timbering the crews had asked for, fell in and killed eleven. The custodians' roll names all eleven. The Company's report names none, and calls the gallery "unsound ground." A dry cough ran through the camps and killed thirty-eight, most of them boys who broke ore at the pit-mouth; the Mission's surgeon called it a winter complaint, and it lasted through the summer.

The second beacon was lit at Baile ìseal on the eastern shore, Torcall Clachair directing from a chair at the foot of the headland, too old now to climb. The Company founded a camp of timber huts at Àth ìseal, the first settlement on Rodos built for no purpose but the pit, and dammed the stream that watered Doire mhòr to wash its ore. No order for that is recorded. Beathag Dhubh's people carried water by hand for two summers and then began to leave. In {{year:IV-0079}} the hill villages lost their oats to rain that no one was home to cut ahead of, and the Company sold meal for marks, and the villages had few.

From {{year:IV-0081}} the mountain was worked by the ton. The Company was rechartered in an Tìr Thall under a new name the Ròdaich never used, and its agents at Ros dhomhain outnumbered the Administration's clerks four to one. Ore barges loaded at Cidhe Beag on Abhainn uaine, where the old canoe landing was widened with cut stone and its shrine moved uphill; within a few seasons the green river ran black with slurry, and the salmon at its mouth failed, and the fishermen kept its name and used it bitterly. Baile dhomhain was laid out on a grid by a Company surveyor, the first Ròdach town drawn on paper before it was built. On the ore road the old carters' house at the halfway stage was rebuilt and kept its name, An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, and its keeper Seònaid Bhàn took marks and barter both, the only keeper on the road who would. Seann Toll was made an ore landing for the barges on the Abhainn uaine, and its fishing boats moved to the next reach.

In {{year:IV-0091}} a Ròdach was given authority over Ròdach miners, answerable to human masters, and the chroniclers count it the first fracture within Ròdach society itself. He was Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill of Muileann chrom, a crew-leader since the first contract. The Company gave him its staff and a house with a slate roof, and the Ròdaich gave him his byname: Raghnall Maor, Raghnall the steward. Soon there were overseers at every working in the district, and the roll stopped recording their names and recorded only their number. At Cnoc mhòr the overseer Seumas Dubh was killed by his own crew after a boy was crushed on a haulage he had ordered hurried, and the Company hanged two men at Ros dhomhain, the first hanging done by the Administration on Rodos.

Maddox barred the priests of the hawk from the camps in {{year:IV-0095}}, on the ground that their preaching slowed the work; the overseers enforced it, and several of them were hawk-faithful, and the roll says so. A spotted pox came up the ore road from the Ros dhomhain quays. The Mission counted four hundred and six dead; the custodians counted more, and wrote the difference in the margin of the Mission's list. The Administration moved its customs house from Baile dhìreach to Ros dhomhain, and the mixed council, left with half an income, shrank from six to four. In {{year:IV-0099}} Maddox left Rodos a rich man, and Arthur Lisle, a lawyer, came to the Residency. His first act was to have every agreement since the treaty copied into a single bound book.

## Of the Silver Compact, and the Things the Clerks Could Not File

Reading that book closely, Lisle found in it nothing that covered silver.

The seam at Muileann chiar was held for the town by its guild of refiners, led in these years by Ùna nic Choinnich. When the Company's surveyors came to claim it she met them at the adit with the guild's whole membership and its ledgers, and asked them to find the humans' name anywhere in either. In {{year:IV-0103}} Lisle signed a compact leaving the seam to the town, the first document of the age in which the Ròdach side kept the better terms; and Muileann chiar held its silver in its own hand through all the rest of the age. When a Company ore ship was burned off the eastern coast by raiders in low black boats, the Company said the guild had paid them. The guild said nothing.

The third beacon was lit at Àth dhearg in {{year:IV-0106}}, and its keeper, Tormod mac Pheadair, kept a daybook of every ship he saw. It is from his book and the books of the keepers after him that the humans' sailings of this age are known, and the chroniclers have leaned on the daybooks more than on any other witness, for a keeper has nothing to gain from a ship that is not there.

In {{year:IV-0110}} the council of Baile dhìreach broke up in a quarrel over a boundary wall. The human families moved to Ros bheag and Ros dhomhain; the Ròdaich stayed. The households that were both split or went quiet, and the record does not say which. The next year the rain failed for a single season around Achadh shean, a warning that nobody heeded.

At Dùn thais in {{year:IV-0114}} the Deer master Aonghas mac Coinnich put to his students a question the register calls new: why the humans could not see the colour in broken ore that every Ròdach child sees. He did not answer it, and said it was the wrong question. Its form in the Deer books is quoted in every age after:

> *Carson a chì sinne an dath, is iadsan dall air?*
>
> Why do we see the colour, and they blind to it?

On the concession the human families founded a market town at Cathair gheal under a charter that made no reference to the custodians. In {{year:IV-0119}} the family of Eachann Mòr, a custodian of Ceann àrsaidh who had lost his seat, sent their son Calum west on a Company ship, hoping to buy better treatment with his schooling. He is never recorded coming home. The Mission opened a school at Ros dhomhain in the humans' tongue, most of its pupils the children of overseers. The Company rebuilt the old bridge over the river at Ros fhionn for its eastern ore road.

Two things the Administration's clerks did not know where to file. A Company brig reported a beast larger than herself off the north-western headlands, and the sailors of that coast said they had wondered when the humans would see it. Two Company surveyors went up into the hills at Doire uaine against the villagers' advice and were not seen again. The chroniclers record both, and explain neither.

In {{year:IV-0130}} the Administration's share of the ore rose sharply, and what the Ròdach communities received did not rise with it. Lisle's schedule raised the portion from one part in five to one part in three, and the custodians received it in the humans' tongue, with a translation made by a Mission pupil. A bloody flux broke out on the Ros dhomhain quays; the Mission's nurse Anne Colby made a sick-house of the chapel, and the record credits her with keeping the dead under two hundred. Lisle died at the Residency in {{year:IV-0135}}, and Walter Crane, the Company's chief agent at the pits, succeeded him, the first man to hold both offices in turn.

Raghnall Maor died in {{year:IV-0137}}, and the Company gave his staff to his son Dòmhnall. The custodians had hoped the office would lapse with the man. It became a family's inheritance.

The fourth beacon was lit at Muileann bheag in {{year:IV-0138}}, the first to burn ore instead of oil, and its flame threw a colour on the water no oil lamp ever had. The Mission cleric Edwin Lowe dug at Làrach an Teampaill and found cut stone, burnt bone and marks he could not read, and wrote that the ruin's builders "were not the present race." The custodians agreed, and added nothing. What became of the Seann-Dhaoine is not known, and his spade did not make it known.

In {{year:IV-0143}} the Company agent Martha Gale offered the silver guild a partnership on terms its ledger calls generous. Ùna nic Choinnich, very old by then, had it read aloud twice in the guild hall and then refused by show of hands. That same year a new camp was opened at Achadh dhomhain for the deep galleries. Its houses were built of spoil.

## Of the Child in the Camps, and the Green Death

On {{date:IV-0146}} a girl was born in the mining camps. She was exposed across her life to more raw ore than any Ròdach in living memory, and she did not age as the people around her aged. Her name was Aisling, and she was the first of the generation that would be called immortal. The record gives her birth, her name and her condition, and this history gives no more.

The next year the Company set up a great steelyard on the Ros dhomhain quay, and from then the island's yield was known by the ton, and the custodians' old count, kept in seasons of labour, stopped being entered. What was happening to the child had no name in any tongue on the island, and it would be decades before anyone tried to coin one. At Cill ghlas the custodians of Òrd Bhrìde kept the old rites of the flame and the stone at the mouth of the oldest cutting while the pits deepened around them; it was the last shrine in the district that the crews still visited on the old feast days, at Samhain and Imbolc, Bealltainn and Lùnastal. The chroniclers ask the reader to remember it.

In {{year:IV-0152}} came A' Phlàigh Uaine, the Green Death. It was first seen at Achadh dhomhain: a greenish pallor, then a cough that brought up dark dust, then weakness. The camp buried forty in its first month in a field beyond the spoil-heaps still called the green acre. It went out along the ore roads from the camps at Muileann chrom, Àth ìseal and Cnoc fhiadhaich, and fell hardest on Ros dhìreach, Ceann òg, Inis ìseal, Baile dhomhain, Achadh fhada, Cnoc bheag, Muileann uaine, Cuan àrsaidh and Inis bhàn. Twenty-six settlements recorded cases. The roll lists them and leaves a blank line after the last, as if expecting another. At Àth ghlas the custodians burned every hut in which a death had occurred, over the Company's protest at the loss of its property, and the sickness stopped there sooner than anywhere else.

The dust was blamed, and no study was finished. The Administration's physician Nathaniel Bright began one at Muileann chrom, weighing the dust in the lungs of the dead; he fell ill in its second season, and his notebooks break off in the middle of a table. Commissioner Crane, who had walked the camps through the worst of it, died of it at the Residency in {{year:IV-0159}}, and Martha Gale held his seal until George Tolley came, with orders that the yield must not fall. Tolley did not visit the camps. His letter-book blames the dust, the Ròdach custom of sleeping near the fire, and the custodians' shrines, in that order.

With the hawk-priests still barred, the crews carried the sermons in themselves, and the overseers' reports list hawk-feathers nailed above hut doors as a disciplinary matter. The saying of the feathered doors outlived the reports:

> *Ithidh Mòd an neach a dh'itheas an cnoc.*
>
> Mòd will eat whoever eats the hill.

Anne Colby opened sick-houses at Àth ìseal and Achadh dhomhain and nursed in them until the sickness ebbed. She is the only human whom the camp people name in their own accounts of the plague, and they name her kindly.

In {{year:IV-0165}} an entire settlement stopped work for eleven days over the conditions the plague had left. It was Baile thais, below Cill ghlas, and the halt was called by the crew-leader Catrìona nic Eachainn after the Company refused to close the galleries where the Green Death had been worst. For eleven days no ore left the town. On the twelfth the Company's store, the only store, was empty. After it the Company built its own granary at Muileann chrom and forbade the camps to keep grain beyond a week's ration, an order signed by Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill Maor. Catrìona, refused work at every Company pit, went east to Muileann chiar, and the silver guild took her on as a refiner. The Company asked for her back. The guild's reply was filed, and has not been preserved.

The Administration built a new bath-house at the hot springs near Muileann ruadh for officials recovering from fevers; the Ròdaich, who had bathed there for centuries, were admitted on certain days and charged a penny. The silver town grew, for the humans wanted both metals, and in {{year:IV-0172}} the guild founded a daughter town to the west for its refiners and carters under the same name, and the record calls them the old town and the new, and does not always say which it means. The guild sold bar silver to the Company at a price the guild fixed, the only commodity on Rodos in the age priced by Ròdach hands.

At Tolley's request the priests of Òrd Mhacha at Cathair dhearg, the capital's own order, blessed the Residency's new coin. Macha's priesthood teaches that the black horse and the grey of her rite each have their due, dark and light; the dissenting priests said the coin was only one of them. In {{year:IV-0176}} barter was devalued against the mark a second time, on terms set wholly by the Administration. In {{year:IV-0179}} the dissenting priests left the capital's temple for the sacred forest at Cnoc bheag, where custodians who had lost their seats found welcome. And Dòmhnall Maor died, and his son Iain took the staff: the first overseer schooled at the Mission, who kept his reports in the humans' tongue and his household in Ròdais.

## Of the Levy, the Ring of Beacons, and the Great Hunger

On {{date:IV-0181}} Tolley issued the ordinance the Ròdaich called simply the Levy: every farming village in the west and north was to send one in five of its able adults to the pits for two years, paid for in marks, at the new rate. It fell hardest around Baile chrom and the northern concession, which had never sent crews to the pits. The Ròdaich of the concession were taken; its human farmers were exempt.

Tolley closed the hall of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh at Dùn thais in {{year:IV-0186}}, over a lecture on whether the humans' blindness to the colour of the ore was a kind of sickness. The masters went on teaching in their own houses, to smaller rooms each year, and some went under the trees of the sacred forest near Muileann chiar, where there was no hall to close.

Work began on the fifth and last beacon at Baile chrom, with masons taken by the Levy from the very villages whose fields they left unworked. The ring was finished in {{year:IV-0191}}: Taigh-solais Baile chrom, Baile ìseal, Muileann bheag, Àth dhearg and Tobar dhearg. It had been asked for so that ships should not be lost; it was completed to serve a trade that was by now extraction in every way but name. The Baile chrom beacon was lit with an ore flame in {{year:IV-0193}}, and the daybooks record the sailings doubling in the years after.

In {{year:IV-0194}} the harvest failed. Gorta Baile chrom, the Great Hunger, went across thirty-four settlements, made worse by a countryside too stripped by the Levy to farm its own land. It was worst at Baile chrom itself, whose people could see the new beacon from their empty fields; the custodians recorded that more died there than anywhere, and the Administration did not dispute the figure. A relief ship from an Tìr Thall lay a month at Ros dhomhain while the Residency and the Company argued over who should pay to unload her; the Mission opened kitchens meanwhile at Cathair gheal and Baile chrom, and Anne Colby, come back to the island, ran the one at Baile chrom. The Levy was suspended in the famine districts in {{year:IV-0200}}, too late for the harvest. It was never repealed, and never used again, and the custodians note that it never needed to be, since the villages had learned what it meant.

Tolley retired with honours in {{year:IV-0202}}. Robert Haskins, his successor, ordered that the custodians' council be consulted on the tithe, and then set the tithe before the council had met.

In {{year:IV-0204}}, for the first time, the custodians acted together, and laid a formal grievance before the Administration. It was drawn up at Ceann àrsaidh by custodians of nineteen settlements meeting as one body, and carried to the Residency by the custodian of Cill ghlas, Peigi Ghlas, and handed to a clerk. It was not answered.

From these years come the oldest Ròdach grievance texts that survive: not yet resistance, but the first written acknowledgment that something had gone wrong. The earliest is Leabhar Baile thais, a book of complaints kept by many hands: wages, deaths, the granary, the stream at Doire mhòr. At Muileann dhearg the poet Fearchar Bàrd, a breaker of ore since boyhood, made songs of the Levy and the famine. The overseers forbade them, and the forbidding is why several were written down; they were sung in the roadside houses of the east, and An Taigh-òsta Grianach became known for them, and the Company's own carters sang them without always knowing what they sang. When the Company built its deep-water quay at Ros dhomhain for the new iron steamships, which burned the island's own ore to carry it away, Fearchar made the song most often quoted of all his songs:

> *Tha an long ag ithe a' chnuic*
> *gus an cnoc a ghiùlan air falbh.*
>
> The ship is eating the hill
> to carry the hill away.

The Company walled its pithead at Baile thais with a gate and a watchman to count who went in, the first working on Rodos walled against its own crews. Catrìona nic Eachainn died at Muileann chiar in {{year:IV-0214}}, a refiner of the silver guild to the end, and when the camp people of Baile thais sent a delegation to her burying, the Company docked each of them a day's pay. In {{year:IV-0215}} a fire in the Company's ore sheds burned three days and took two streets of the harbour quarter; the Company's inquiry found no cause, and the harbour quarter's own account has several. That year the council, on the death of its high custodian, could not agree on another, and sat for years with the seat empty. Haskins wrote to an Tìr Thall that this made it easier to deal with.

## Of the Long Drought, and the Law of One Line

In {{year:IV-0217}} came Tiormachd Achadh shean, the Long Drought, three dry seasons together, the dry year of the east come back threefold. At Achadh shean the wells failed and the cattle were driven west in a column the roll says was a day long, and fewer than half reached water. The roll counted ten districts as drought country, worst at Achadh shean, Àth uaine, Achadh àrsaidh and Muileann chiar, and reaching down to Cuan ruadh and west toward Baile chaol. The Administration kept no count of its own.

The spring of Allt an Àigh, said never to have failed, did not fail. Pilgrims of Seann Spioradan nan Ròdach came to it from across the east. The Administration posted a watchman at the spring to charge for the water. He was recalled after a single season.

A fever of bad water followed into the towns, worst at Baile Mòr ruadh, where the wells had been deepened into the old middens; two hundred and sixty died. The Mission's report was the first to name the cause rightly, and the Mission bored deep wells at Achadh shean and Àth chrom that held. The custodians of the east entered the Mission's wells and the Company's quotas on facing pages, without comment. For the quota was not lowered. The custodians petitioned Haskins one by one, since the joint petition had not been answered, and he replied to each that the matter was under consideration.

In {{year:IV-0230}} a case came before the Administration that set the law of inheritance on Rodos for the rest of the age. The claimant was Thomas Hale, grandson of the Baile dhìreach marriage of Eilidh nic Ruairidh and Samuel Hale, who claimed a share in a customary cutting through his Ròdach grandmother. The magistrate at Ros dhomhain, Oliver Dunstan, ruled that customary rights descend only through the line the Administration recognizes, and that it recognizes only its own. Within a few years the ruling had passed eleven customary cuttings to the Company, each because some owner in the line had married a human or been born to one. The custodians stopped entering such marriages in the roll. So the marriage that Baile dhìreach had entered in both its books became, a century on, the reason marriages were entered in neither.

In {{year:IV-0234}} a society of pit crews that called itself the Lamp-Watch began to meet at Baile thais and Cnoc chaol under cover of a burial club. Its first known act was to bury an old breaker who had died in the Company's lock-up.

Haskins was recalled in {{year:IV-0235}} after a quarrel with the Company over steamship berths, and Edward Vane came to the Residency, the first Commissioner born on Rodos, at the Residency itself. He spoke Ròdais badly, but he spoke it. He laid the mule-drawn tram-road to Ros dhìreach, and before he died the Company's railway ran from the pits at Muileann chrom to the quay at Ros dhomhain, its engines burning the island's ore ({{date:IV-0243a}}).

In {{year:IV-0237}} came the night-shift order, and from then the mountain was given no season of rest at all. It was felt first at Cnoc chaol, in the newest galleries, where crews worked by ore-lamp in two shifts. The custodians' roll records that the hill was given no silence at all, and then leaves the rest of the page blank. The crews at Cnoc chaol refused the night shift and were replaced within a week by crews from the coast. The Lamp-Watch paid the strikers' families for a season and ran out of money. The Mission's minister at Ros dhomhain, Matthew Dunne, preached from the chapel pulpit that the Ròdaich were right, and that even the ground must rest. The Company complained, and Dunne was recalled to an Tìr Thall, and the Lamp-Watch copied his sermon and read it in the camps.

A wasting sickness of the lungs spread among the night crews of Cnoc chaol and Baile thais, and unlike the Green Death it killed slowly. The Company paid the widows a pension for a year, and then stopped. In {{year:IV-0243}} Iain Maor wrote to the Company that the night shift was killing his crews faster than it paid for them. The letter survives because it was filed with his dismissal, which was reversed a month later for want of anyone else.

## Of the Second Child, and the Word that was Coined

In {{year:IV-0245}} a second child was born who showed the same bonding with the ore that Aisling had shown half a century before. His name was Fionnan. To those who kept the rolls his birth meant that the first had been no single accident, and that the condition was a thing the pits themselves were making.

Seònaid nic Dhùghaill, the last master of the Deer to teach openly in the age, died at Dùn thais in {{year:IV-0247}}, and the school's register ends with her death. It was taken up again only long after the humans had gone.

In {{year:IV-0248}} the custodians of the east began to send copies of their rolls to Muileann chaol, which still kept Beathag nic Thòmais's loft of papers, out of the Residency's reach. The copying was done in the houses of three families and the copies kept in a cellar. The library at Muileann chaol, which keeps the record of which this book is a part, grew from that loft and that cellar together.

On {{date:IV-0250}}, facing two cases that could no longer be called chance, the custodians settled on a word. It was agreed at a meeting held at the shrine of Cill ghlas, where the oldest cutting still stood open: *fuil-ghuail*, coal-blood, rendered in the humans' tongue as *obsidian-touched*. It was meant clinically, a plain word for a physician's register, and it outlived that intention. The roll records the vote and not who proposed it. The Residency, hearing of the word, ordered every Ròdach child showing the condition to be reported to the Commissioner's office. The custodians complied by reporting none.

In {{year:IV-0253}} barter was abolished as legal tender, and every transaction on the island ran thenceforth through the humans' coin. The barter houses of Ros dhomhain and Cathair dhearg were closed by order, and a Residency clerk noted that the ledgers were now "complete for the first time." The hill villages borrowed marks from the Company's stores to buy seed, at interest, and within a few years most of them owed the Company more than a year's crops.

Iain Maor died in {{year:IV-0258}}, and his daughter Mòrag took the staff, the first woman to hold it; the Company approved her in a letter that calls her "a safe pair of hands." Vane died at the Residency that year, and Charles Merriman came with a small company of armed constables, the first standing force the Administration had kept on Rodos. His first posts were at Muileann shean, which the townsfolk called the humans' second Residency, and at Tobar ghorm.

In {{year:IV-0262}} the Mission at Cathair gheal reported that human labourers near Baile chrom had been meeting at night in the fields to keep a vigil to a grey goose. They were of the Church, and took the goose for an Gèadh Fiadhaich, the wild goose of the Holy Spirit. The Mission could not find the source, called the vigil irregular, and supposed it a superstition learned from the Ròdaich. The Ròdaich of Baile chrom disowned it. Manannan's priests at Ceann mhòr said the Mission was half right: the practice came from the Ròdaich, but from Manannan's harbours and not from Baile chrom. The human fishing families of Ceann mhòr had learned the prayer to keep the sea shut, and the labourers inland kept it as a night of silence to a grey goose. The priests called it their prayer gone astray and would not have its keepers in their house. That year the dockers of Ros dhomhain, Ròdaich and human alike, refused for nine days to load ore over the new berth charges, the only strike of the age in which the two peoples stood together. Merriman's constables ended it, and the human dockers were shipped west.

In {{year:IV-0265}} a third child of the condition was born, Lorccan, in a mining town and not a camp, which told the custodians that the thing had outgrown any single place. In {{year:IV-0266}} a fourth was born, Cian, within a year of the third: the nearest the generation of the coal-blood ever came to being born together rather than scattered across the years.

## Of the Rending of Cill ghlas, and the First Blood

In {{year:IV-0267}} the Company began to drive galleries under the hills around Cill ghlas with blasting-powder from an Tìr Thall. The custodians of the shrine asked that no charge be set within sight of the old cutting. The Company's engineer marked their request on his plan, and set the charges below it.

On {{date:IV-0269}}, in the course of a routine excavation, a fault tore open in the hills east of the capital around Cill ghlas. This was Scàineadh Cill ghlas, the Rending. It killed dozens and closed a whole mining district for a generation. The old cutting, where the rites had been kept through the whole age and where the word *fuil-ghuail* had been agreed, was swallowed with its shrine. The custodians' roll records the loss of the shrine before the loss of the dead, and a later copyist has corrected the order; this account lets both hands stand. The Company fenced the district and left Cill ghlas and its hamlets without work, and the closed country went to thorn.

Within days of the Rending came the first open clash between the two peoples. The roll's fuller account of it, entered at the year's end, places it at Muileann chrom. A gallery there flooded after the pumps were stopped to save fuel, and seven died. When the Company's agent came to the pithead the crews stoned him and his constables, and the constables fired. Four Ròdaich and one constable were killed. At the inquiry Mòrag nic Iain Maor testified that the pumps had been stopped on the agent's order. Her evidence was struck from the record. The Lamp-Watch obtained a copy anyway.

In {{year:IV-0276}} the grey fever came in on the ships, a fever of the chest that killed the young and strong before the old, and ran from the capital to the eastern farms. The camps, already hungry, buried more than the towns.

In {{year:IV-0279}} a second petition went to the Residency, and where the first had held a plea this one held a threat. It was drafted by Eilidh nic Raghnaill, a crew-leader of Baile thais and a member of the Lamp-Watch, and signed by custodians and crew-leaders of thirty-one settlements, and it ended with a sentence the Residency underlined: that the hill would not be worked by people who were not asked. It was the first Ròdach document the Administration troubled to answer. It answered with constables, and no concessions. In {{year:IV-0282}} Merriman tripled the constabulary and posted companies at Muileann chrom, Baile thais, Ceann fhada and Cnoc chaol, each with a fortified store, paid from the tithe; a barracks for two hundred at Dùn chrom stood empty most of its first year for want of men to fill it.

At Seann Skell, where Murchadh Seabhag had preached the hawk's hunger in {{year:IV-0066}}, the hawk-priest Ìomhar Seabhag preached that Mòd's hunger had turned at last toward those who had eaten the hill. The hawk-shrines of the west became meeting-houses for the Lamp-Watch, and the constables did not yet know it.

The grey fever came back in {{year:IV-0287}} and settled in the north-west, and there it killed humans as readily as Ròdaich. In the Mission's overflowing hospital at Cathair gheal the two peoples lay in the same wards for the first time.

## Of the Houses on the Roads, and the Day the Pits Stopped

From {{year:IV-0290}} the roadside houses along the old mining routes began, without anyone ordering it, to serve as waypoints for a militia that had no name and a war not yet declared. The singing house, An Taigh-òsta Grianach, walled its yard and set a watch on its gate. Am Buabhall Fortanach on the south-western road barred its gates at dusk and kept a watch on its roof, and its keeper Tormod Buabhall sent a list of travellers up the road every week, to whom the record does not say. At An Taigh-seinnse Reòta on the northern road the stables were rebuilt with a loft for sleeping men, and the carters noticed that it was never empty and never held the same men twice. An Sligeanach Fad' às, near the silver country, became the post-house through which the Lamp-Watch sent word to Muileann chiar; the silver guild paid its rent. And An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, the carters' house that Seònaid Bhàn had reopened in the first years of the extraction, was kept now by her great-grandson Aonghas. It was the busiest of the fortified houses, and the one the constables searched most often, and found least in.

In {{year:IV-0301}} several mining regions stopped work on the same day for the first time: proof that the resistance of the Ròdaich, scattered as it was, could act as one when it chose. Every working in the Muileann chrom district halted at once, and Baile thais with it, and Cnoc chaol, the silver town and the eastern pits at Ros fhionn. The signal was a beacon fire on the hills above Doire mhòr, which the Company took for a heath fire.

The Company's steamships lay at Ros dhomhain six days with empty holds, and the agents wrote that the quiet frightened them more than the crowds had. The stoppage was broken as the strike at Baile thais had been broken, when the constables closed the Company stores in every district at once. Forty crew-leaders were arrested. Thirty-two were released without charge. Eight were shipped west to an Tìr Thall for trial, and were never heard of again. The beacon of the stoppage had been lit on Doire mhòr's hill by the descendants of Beathag Dhubh's people, and the constables burned the heather there so that it could not be lit again.

The keepers' daybooks were recording by now fewer ships from an Tìr Thall each year, and older ones. The Company's letters speak of troubles at home that they do not describe. Whatever was happening beyond the sea, the island saw it only as an absence in the daybooks.

In {{year:IV-0313}} the Administration began to issue its orders with no Ròdach counsel sought at all, and the polite fiction of permission, kept since the council's vote of {{year:IV-0005}}, was dropped. Merriman left in ill health, and Lionel Strake, a soldier, took his place; his commission made no mention of the Council of Custodians, and he did not call on it. He brought a flying machine in the hold of a Company steamship and had it flown over the ore country to count the workings; the Ròdaich called it *eun-iarainn*, the iron bird ({{date:IV-0315a}}). He had the council hall at Cathair dhearg locked and its keys lodged at the Residency, and the custodians met instead in the temple of Macha, whose priests, who had once blessed the humans' coin, now sheltered those who had petitioned against it.

In {{year:IV-0316}} the Administration claimed the right to seize any Ròdach dwelling it deemed necessary for the widening of the workings. Of all the grievances of the age, the confiscation edict is the one cited most often in every account of what came after. The first dwellings taken under it were those of Doire mhòr, for a new shaft beneath the hill, and the last households were carried out by constables. The village that had refused the first contract was the first to be emptied by the last. At Baile dhomhain, the first camp laid out on paper, two streets were pulled down for a spoil-tip.

Here the line of the Stewards of Muileann chrom came to its end. In {{year:IV-0319}} Mòrag nic Iain Maor, fourth of her line to hold the staff, refused to carry out evictions in her district and broke the staff at the Company's door. Three other overseers resigned that month, and the Company put humans in their places. So ended the office that Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill had taken up more than a hundred years before: Raghnall, Dòmhnall, Iain, Mòrag, each in turn; and the custodians' hope that it would lapse with the first of them was fulfilled at last by the fourth.

That summer the silver guild of Muileann chiar, holding its seam still under the compact of a century before, began buying rifles from the raiders of the eastern waters, where no gunboat had patrolled for years, and paid them in bar silver by weight. Its ledger enters the rifles as refining tools. Custodians, crew-leaders of the Lamp-Watch and the dissenting priests of Macha met in the sacred forest at Cnoc bheag and made themselves a single council for the mining regions, and chose Eilidh nic Raghnaill to speak for it. No human source records the meeting. Strake learned of it all the same, and ordered the arrest of Eilidh and the forest council, and the constables went to Baile thais, and found her house empty and the town silent.

## Of the War of the Hills

On {{date:IV-0324}} the resistance of the Ròdaich, joined now across the mining regions under one council, passed from grievance to open war.

It opened in the hills. The fortified store at Muileann chrom was rushed at first light by crews under Mòrag nic Iain, who had broken the staff, and its eleven constables surrendered without a shot; the rifles and the grain were gone before noon. Crews from the camps took the ore quay at Ceann fhada and sank two barges across its mouth, and no ore left the hills by water again in the age. At Baile thais the constables held the walled pithead eleven days, the span of Catrìona's strike a lifetime before, and on the twelfth, their water gone, they surrendered. Eilidh nic Raghnaill sent them under guard to the coast unharmed, an order the Residency had not expected and did not return in kind. The northern pits at Baile gharbh rose in the same season, took the constable posts at Muileann shean and Tobar ghorm, and sent the constables south on foot. Aonghas shut the gates of An Taigh-seinnse Mòr across the ore road, and the men in its loft came down armed, and the road between the capital and the hills was cut at its halfway stage and stayed cut. At Muileann dhearg the smith Uilleam Gobha made grenades of the Company's own blasting-powder cased in ore-tin, and pike-heads from the galleries' rails, and the council's roll says it could not have fought its first season without him.

Then the capital rose. The custodians broke the Residency's locks on their hall at Cathair dhearg and sat in it again, and the townsfolk held the stone bridge against the constables sent up from the harbour, and the bridge rebuilt for ore-carts was barricaded with them. At Cathair dhomhain the townsfolk broke open the Company's warehouse, the first building the Company had ever owned on Rodos, and carried its tools to the council's companies. Strake drew the Administration back into the Residency and fortified the Company's quarter of the harbour. The city below it, overwhelmingly Ròdach, neither joined the rising nor opposed it, and the constables did not trust it enough to leave the walls.

A column of constables and Company guards under Major Peter Ashdown marched up the ore road to relieve the hills and was met on {{date:IV-0335}} at the ford of Àth chiar, where the rebels held the far bank. Ashdown lost forty men and his baggage and turned back; the rebels lost nineteen, and the council's roll names them all, as the custodians' roll had named the eleven of Cnoc fhiadhaich when the Company's report named none. Ashdown's column halted a night at the barracks of Dùn chrom, and in the morning found the town's gates shut behind it, and went on to the coast without its wounded, whom the townsfolk kept and nursed.

In the east the silver guild declared for the council and opened its armoury, and escorted the Company's agents to the coast with their papers. The guild's ledger notes that it was the only place on the island where the rising took nothing, because the humans had never held anything there to take. The constabulary post at Dùn ìseal surrendered to the guild's riflemen after a single exchange of fire, and the east was clear of human arms. Dùn dhearg declared for the council and sent it grain, and its declaration quotes the defence of the Small-Burning Law that its custodian Fionnghal nic Artair had written a hundred and twenty-seven years before. The crews of the eastern workings at Ros fhionn stopped the pumps and walked out, and let the lower galleries flood, and the bridge on the eastern ore road fell silent.

The humans struck back from the sea. In the first month of {{year:IV-0342}} a Company steamship came up the Abhainn uaine and shelled the ore stage at Seann Toll to deny it to the rebels, and set the fishing town behind it burning, and twenty-three townsfolk died: the first time the humans turned the island's own ore, burning in their boilers, against a Ròdach town. Fishing boats from Seann Chwen and Ros ruadh, sailing by night along the south coast, boarded a Company coaster at anchor and burned her. The Company drew its coastal shipping back into Ros dhomhain and Ros bheag.

On the concession, Cathair gheal shut its gates and raised a militia and sent none of it south. Its magistrate wrote to both sides asking to be left alone; the council granted it, and Strake did not reply. That spring the council had sent its letters to the Hawk coast by the hands of seven hawk-faithful crewmen of the river towns, who wore hawks cast from the lead of the cartridges taken at Muileann chrom and were called Buidheann an t-Seabhaig Luaidhe, the Leaden Hawk company. They gathered at An Cupa Dàna in Cuan dhearg ({{date:IV-0342a}}) and walked north past the burned stage at Seann Toll, over the hills by Achadh dhomhain and down the Abhainn ìseal to Cnoc bheag, reading the letter at every hawk-house along the coast to Inis mhin. There they heard that Strake's constables rode the western roads, and they went on by carriage across country, off every road, watched the Ros bheag road a night and a day from the grasslands above Tobar dhomhain ({{date:IV-0342d}}), and came down to Inis thais eight days after they had set out. The priests of the Stone, the keepers of Clach, would send no men, but they shut the harbour of Inis thais to the Company's ships ({{date:IV-0342e}}). At Seann Skell Ìomhar Seabhag declared that the war was Mòd's own hunger turned back on those who had fed it, and the hawk-shrines of the west became recruiting-houses.

The keepers at Tobar dhearg put out their beacon, and Àth dhearg and Baile ìseal followed. Company ships feeling their way along the coast by night ran onto the reefs the beacons had been built to light, and two were lost. The Administration begged an Tìr Thall for troops and ships. One ship came in the war's second spring, with dispatches and a few crates of cartridges, and sailed within the week.

Through the first winter the council held the hills, the east and the capital, and Strake the Residency, the harbour quarter below it and the western ports, and neither had the strength to attack the other. Cathair dhearg went short, its harbour held against it, and the custodians shared out the grain by household, and the roll records that they took the same share as everyone else.

In that stalemate the ledgers burned. In three raids the rebels destroyed the tithe ledgers of the Administration and the Company, an act that later chroniclers have held decisive in its meaning though small in its effect. The first was on the Company's counting-house at the Ros dhomhain quay, inside Strake's lines, where men of the harbour quarter under Dùghall Dubh came in over the roofs, carried the tithe books into the yard and burned them. The second burned the tithe-house at Ceann àrsaidh, where the portion owed by every settlement of the hills had been entered since the first schedule; the clerks were let go, and when the custodians of Ceann àrsaidh asked that one ledger be spared for the record, their own people refused them, and the library at Muileann chaol has regretted it since. The third burned the weigh-house at Muileann chrom, with the steelyard ledgers in which the ore of the hills had been entered by the ton since the first contract. The rebels' own chronicler writes that the fire burned green. The camps made a verse of it:

> *Loisc iad an leabhar, ach cha do loisc iad an cnoc.*
>
> They burned the book, but they did not burn the hill.

On {{date:IV-0359}} Company guards and constables sallying from the harbour to win back the eastern road were driven back at Cnoc ghorm, in the hills beyond the capital; it was the Administration's last attempt of the war to reach the mining country. The next day the Ròdaich of the Ros dhomhain harbour quarter opened the lower gates to the council's companies, and Strake held only the Residency and the Company's quays. On {{date:IV-0362}} Strake took his officials, the Mission's clergy and the Company's agents off by sea to Ros bheag. The council's companies entered the Residency the next morning and found it stripped of everything but its furniture, and of the letter-books, from which so much of this history is drawn, which the humans had not troubled to take.

## Of the Truce of Àth àrsaidh, and the Closing of the Crossing

Ros bheag, the western port the humans had used since the early years, became the last seat of the Administration, and the human families of the west and south, a few thousand in all, gathered behind its walls. With the roads cut and no ships from the west the town ran short within months, and Strake ordered the Company's stores opened to the human families and the Ròdach townsfolk alike. It is the one act of his that the Ròdach record credits.

The custodians of Cuan shean, the town where the first humans had come ashore to beg for water, closed their harbour to Company ships. The council's roll notes the irony without comment, and so shall this account. At Baile dhìreach the descendants of the old joint council, many of them of both peoples, were asked by each side which people they were. Most of them refused to answer, and neither side made them.

On {{date:IV-0368}} the last ship recorded arriving from an Tìr Thall anchored at Ros bheag. She brought no troops and no grain, only a sealed letter for the Commissioner, and she sailed on the next tide with as many passengers as she could hold. What the letter said is not known. On {{date:IV-0369}} envoys of the council and the Administration met at Àth àrsaidh, on the edge of the northern concession, and agreed a truce: the humans remaining on Rodos to withdraw into the north-west under the council's safe-conduct, the council to leave the concession towns standing.

On {{date:IV-0371}} the columns went north: the human families of Ros bheag, Cuan shean, Baile dhìreach and the south-west, up the western roads under the guard of the council's companies. Few of them had ever lived in the north. The roll records that no column was attacked, and that none was helped. At the ford of Àth chrom the custodians stood and counted them over, and that count is the last the Ròdaich made of the humans on Rodos in this age. At Àth leathan, the last stage before the northern country, the columns camped a night, and the custodians of the town record that the humans asked the name of the place, and that several of them wrote it down.

On {{date:IV-0374}} Lionel Strake, the last Commissioner, sailed west from Cuan shean with his officials, in the last ship on the island that could make the crossing. How the harbour that had shut itself to the Company came to let the Commissioner's ship go out, the roll of Cuan shean does not say. The keeper at Taigh-solais Baile chrom recorded her passing. Nothing on Rodos records her arriving anywhere.

The council ordered the five beacons lit again, from Baile chrom round to Tobar dhearg. The keepers' daybooks, which had counted human ships since the ring was finished, went on being kept, and there was nothing in them. The entry that stands in them, in the hands of five keepers, is this:

> *Cha tàinig long.*
>
> No ship came.

At the year's end the custodians sent what survived of the age's rolls to the cellars at Muileann chaol: their copies of the petitions, the book of Baile thais, and the letter-books left in the Residency. What the Administration's own clerks carried west with Strake is not known.

On {{date:IV-0377}} the war ended with the crossing itself closed. Whatever had let the humans reach Rodos shut. No one on the island knows what it was, or why it opened, or why it closed, and the chroniclers set it down as the rolls do, as a thing that happened to the sea. The humans still on the island were brought into the northern country their descendants hold today. This was the Severance. Cian and Lorccan, neither yet twenty, grew up in its immediate wake.

*The masters of the humans on Rodos, as the letter-books give them.* Edmund Harrow, captain, from the Crossing until the treaty; then the Commissioners at the Residency: Josiah Pell, {{year:IV-0030}} to {{year:IV-0069}}; Henry Maddox, {{year:IV-0069}} to {{year:IV-0099}}; Arthur Lisle, {{year:IV-0099}} to {{year:IV-0135}}; Walter Crane, {{year:IV-0135}} to {{year:IV-0159}}; George Tolley, {{year:IV-0160}} to {{year:IV-0202}}; Robert Haskins, {{year:IV-0202}} to {{year:IV-0235}}; Edward Vane, {{year:IV-0235}} to {{year:IV-0259}}; Charles Merriman, {{year:IV-0259}} to {{year:IV-0314}}; Lionel Strake, {{year:IV-0314}} to {{year:IV-0374}}, the last.

*The Stewards of Muileann chrom, as the custodians' roll gives them.* Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill, Raghnall Maor, {{year:IV-0091}} to {{year:IV-0137}}; Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill, {{year:IV-0137}} to {{year:IV-0180}}; Iain Maor, {{year:IV-0180}} to {{year:IV-0258}}; Mòrag nic Iain, {{year:IV-0258}} to {{year:IV-0319}}, who broke the staff.

So ended the Age of Strangers. It began with a vote that three custodians did not attend and a word for the ore that the strangers never asked to learn, and it ended with the hill cut to its roots, the Small-Burning Law a hundred and thirty years forgotten, and the tithe ledgers burned green at Muileann chrom. What was lost in it the rolls can count only in part: the shrine at Cill ghlas and the water of Doire mhòr, the salmon of the green river, the Deer school's hall and the barter houses, the eight who were shipped west, and the names the Company's reports did not give. What was kept is shorter to tell: the silver of Muileann chiar, the copies in the cellar at Muileann chaol, and the four children of the coal-blood, whom the strangers' pits had made and the strangers' registers never held. The hill was the island's again, and the island knew no other way to use it than the way the strangers had taught. Of that, and of the kingdom restored at Cathair dhearg, and of the same extraction under new owners, and of the northerners in their new country, the next book tells, which is the book of An Aois Ùr, the New Age.
