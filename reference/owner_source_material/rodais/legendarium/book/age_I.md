# The First Book: An Aois Àrsaidh, the Ancient Age

*VE 1 – 5,324*

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.
>
> — the Keeper's riddle, as Clann na Ceiste keep it

## I. Of the Mason and the Lightning

The count of years begins on {{date:I-0001}}. In the hill country that day is only "the night of the lightning", and the tale is told at the hearth in the dark of the year by whoever is oldest in the house; and every hearth that tells it is itself the tale, for before that night there was no hearth on Dia-thìr at all. It goes like this.

Neachdan the mason was sinking a well. He had been at it since the grey of the morning, with a wedge of hard stone and a maul of oak, splitting the rock a hand's depth at a time and lifting the pieces out. It was the dead of the year. The wind came over the heather with sleet in it, and his hands were split at the knuckles from the wet stone, and there was nothing in all the world to warm them but his own breath and the work.

He set the wedge in a seam and struck. The rock broke along a line he had not seen.

Out of every stone he had ever split there had come grey dust and grit, the same dust his father had shaken from his hair. Here the break was black, a hard black that took the light like a wet crow's back; and along the black, where the maul had opened it, lay colour. There was a sheen of gold on one piece, as the sun lies on the sea at evening. There was a piece flecked all over with small white stars, and one that was red-brown like the heart of an old oak, and one with a gleam of silver, and one that seemed, when he tilted it, to hold every colour he had a name for and some he had not. He knelt in the cut with the sleet on his neck and turned the pieces over in his hands until he could not feel them.

He did not know what they were. He filled the breast of his tunic with them, because they were beautiful.

The storm came up the valley at dusk. He took shelter under a lip of rock above the well. Then the sky split with a white light and a noise like the hill breaking, and the light went into the ground.

It went into his well. He saw it strike the broken vein and run along it; and where it ran, the vein began to glow.

The tellers slow here, for the hearth is listening. Neachdan came down out of the shelter with the rain in his eyes and stood at the lip of the well, and something was alive in it. It moved and was not a beast. It breathed out a heat that he felt on his face and his split hands, though the rain was cold; it ate the black stone and gave off a low dark flame, like peat-water moving, with red at its roots. The rain hissed on it and could not put it out. He held his hands over it and they came back to life, and he wept, and was not ashamed to say so after.

He took a flat stone and lifted a burning piece out onto it, and carried it home through the storm under the flap of his cloak, stooping over it the whole way as a man stoops over a sick child. At home he set it on the floor among stones, where the draught would not take it, and fed it with the coloured pieces from his tunic. Each one caught. Each burned the same, low and dark-flamed, and the colours went out of them as they burned, gold and silver and white stars alike, and left only the heat. He saw that, and was sorry a little, and was glad all the same, for his children were sitting round the first hearth on Dia-thìr with their faces red in its light.

So the Dia-thìrich say that fire came to them out of the sky and the stone together.

Three wells of the high valleys claim the crack. Tobar dhìreach and two wells of its neighbours each keep a telling that the mason's well was theirs, and that the lightning fell there. Tobar dhìreach can show a capped shaft with a scorched stain on its lip; the other two can show only the telling. The tellers hold all three claims and choose none.

On what the mason's people did next, every telling agrees. They let the fire in the vein burn until it had burned itself out. Then they dug no further. They laid a flat stone over the broken vein, and would draw no water beside it, and cut their well again a stone's throw away. Of all the deeds of the Ancient Age this is the one told most often, to children and to elders, and it is told the same way every time: the people found a wonder in the ground, and they covered it.

## II. Of the Keeper in the Grove

Nine hundred years went by. The fire had gone from hearth to hearth over all the hill country, and no one knew what the coloured coal was. Then the question was asked.

It was asked by **Diarmad**, of the Mason's line, on {{date:I-0011a}}, and it was asked in the oak grove at Doire ghlas, far to the north, where the oaks had come in over the old heath. Why he had gone so far from the high valleys the tellers do not agree; the Mason's line says only that he had asked the question of everyone since he was a child, and got nothing for it but laughter.

He had lit a small fire of oak twigs in the lee of a fallen trunk, and he sat with a piece of the rainbow coal in his hand, as he always did, turning it so the colours ran. The wind was blowing hard through the grove. The branches roared with it and the dead leaves went past him in streams.

When he looked up there was someone standing on the other side of his fire.

It was the height of a tall man and made of grey smoke, such as a peat fire gives, and it stood in the middle of the gale and did not stir. Diarmad's own little fire was streaming sideways on the ground, and the smoke that stood beside it was as still as a stone.

He did not run. The tellers make much of that.

"What are you?" he said.

"I am Coimhdeach na Fine," it said. Its voice was quiet and very clear, and the wind did not carry it away. "The keeper of the kin. I watch over the people of this island for the one who made them."

"Are you a god?"

"No."

"A ghost, then."

"Neither. I was made out of the smoke of the first coal he woke. I have watched your line since the mason. You have a question. Ask it."

Diarmad held up the coal in his hand. "Why are there colours in it?" he said. "What are they for?"

Then the Keeper told him. It told him of Caoran, the Young God, youngest son of an Dagda and Danu, and of the long line of gods before him who made their worlds of other things; and how Caoran, coming of age, chose coal, and made of it all that is, and then the earth; and how the island was born of the fire of his last coal, and the first people, an Dia-shluagh, the godfolk, grew up out of it like grass; and how he hid in the coal of this one island the seven spirits of his making, each in its colour, as a gift for them to find; and how they found it once, long before the mason, and did not understand it, and let it lie.

All that the Keeper told him the Mason's line kept, word for word, and in time the hill country learned it from them; it is the Telling of the Making, and it is not told twice here.

When it was done Diarmad sat a long time without speaking. The fire had burned down. At last he said: "He hid it. Why would he hide a gift?"

"So that it would be found," said the Keeper. "He said to me once: a gift that is not opened is still given."

"Then how is it opened? The mason burned the coals and they burned like any coal. We have burned them since. The colours go out of them."

"Because you burn them all in the one fire," said the Keeper, "and none of them in its own." And it gave him the riddle, first in the old words and then again, slowly, so that he would keep them:

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

"That is no answer," Diarmad said.

"It is the one I was given to give," said the Keeper; and the wind, which had gone round it all that while, went through the place where it stood, and there was nothing there.

## III. Of the Seven Lightings

So began the long questioning. The Mason's line took the riddle home to the high valleys and turned it over for four thousand years, with seven coals and three words to go on, sky and earth and sea; and the tellers of Clann na Ceiste give the lightings one by one, as the reciters give the fathers of the line.

The first was the sea. In {{year:I-0017a}} **Aodh** of the Mason's line, who had grown up hearing that the sea was one of the three, carried the seven coals of the vein across the hills to the northern shore. He had never seen the sea. He stood on the shingle a long while looking at it. Then he waded in to his knees and threw the coals out into the swell one after another. Six sank without a mark. The seventh, the one with the sheen of gold, struck the water and took fire, and burned on the heave of the sea with a flame the colour of its sheen, bright gold, rising and falling with the waves, until a breaker rolled it under. That coal is *gual òir*, the gold coal, and its spirit is the sea's. Aodh went home and told it at the hearth, and the old women of the line wept, for it was nearly five hundred years since the grove.

The second was the sky. In {{year:I-0027a}} **Fearghas** of the line reasoned that if the sea took one coal by its water, the sky might take one by its air, and that the nearest a man could come to the sky was the highest ground on the island. So he climbed it.

The wind on the top of the bare moor was cold enough to make his teeth ache, and he could see the sea shining at every edge of the island. He took the coals out one at a time and let each drop from the height of his arm over the steep side of the summit.

The fifth coal did not fall far. Halfway down the rock it took fire in the empty air, and burned white, a small fierce white like a star come down in daylight, and struck the stones below still burning and lay there flickering in the wind. It was the coal flecked with white stars, *gual sneachda*, the snowflake coal; and its spirit is the air's. From that day the summit has carried the name of the kindred, {{place:marker:48}}, the Mason's Point. The herders of that moor leave a stone on the top when they pass.

The third was the earth, and it was found by a man who took the riddle more simply than the rest. In {{year:I-0038a}} **Brian** of the line said that if the earth has a spirit, it lies where the dead lie, deep; and he dug a grave-deep hole beside the slab at Tobar dhìreach and laid the five dark coals in it and covered them over, as the dead are covered, and went home. The next morning he dug them up. Four were as cold as the clay. The fifth, the black one, *gual dubh*, as black as a wet crow, was warm in his hand as a hand is warm, and when the morning air reached it a dark red glow crept over it, and it smouldered. The earth had lit it slowly, overnight, from beneath. Brian kept it burning in a pot for the rest of his life.

The fourth was life, and it came by blood. In {{year:I-0052a}} **Cormac** of the line took the four dark coals on the long road north to Doire ghlas, to the grove where the Keeper had first spoken, for he thought the place itself might count for something. He sat under the oaks a month to no purpose, and at last, in a temper, he set the red-brown coal on a stone to break it, to see whether the spirit was inside.

He struck, and the hammer turned on the hard surface of the coal and came down on the back of his other hand, and laid it open to the bone. He swore and fell back, and the blood ran off his fingers and dripped onto the coal. Where it dripped, the coal caught. A flame came up out of it the colour of the coal itself, red-brown, deep and warm like firelight in oak; and Cormac sat in the leaves with his hand in his lap and watched it, and forgot to bind the hand until the flame went out. That coal is *gual donn-ruadh*, and the kindred call it the coal of life. The scar on Cormac's hand is remembered, and his byname with it, Cormac of the Misstrike.

The fifth was the river. Here the tellers of Clann na Ceiste stop and smile, because it is plain enough now, and it took them nearly six hundred years.

In {{year:I-0062a}} **Eòghann** of the line was walking home from the northern shore, where he had thrown the three dark coals into the sea once more, to no purpose. He came down tired to the Abhainn ghorm, the river that runs below Tobar dhìreach, and knelt on the stones and drank from his hands.

Then he sat back on his heels with the water running out between his fingers.

"This water tastes different from the sea water," he said aloud, to no one:

> *Tha blas eile air an uisce seo seach uisce na mara.*
> This water tastes different from the sea water.

He had known it all his life, as everyone knows it. He had never thought it before. He took the three coals out of the bag and threw them into the middle of the stream, one, two, three; and the one with the sheen of silver took fire as it went under, and burned on the bed of the river beneath the running water, silver, while the current poured over it and could not put it out. That is *gual airgid*, the silver coal, and its spirit is the river's.

By then the households of the high valleys had grown used to the Mason's line and its long strange business with the coal, and they had begun to call its people **Luchd-ceasnachaidh a' Ghuail Dhìomhair**, the Inquisitors of the Mystic Coal ({{year:I-0068a}}). It was half a jest when it began. The line took it and kept it, and in the lists the long name is shortened to **Clann na Ceiste**, the Children of the Question; and the older name, Clann a' Chlachair, the Mason's children, is heard less after.

The sixth was fire itself, and it was found by accident, by a man who had given up.

**Seathan** of Clann na Ceiste had sat with the fire coal, *gual teine*, for thirty years. He had put it in every fire the line knew, even the first fire itself, which the line said it had kept alight since the mason's hearth, and it burned in all of them dark and low. Sky, earth and sea, the riddle said; where was fire in that? He had begun to half believe those of the line who said the fire coal had no spirit at all.

On {{date:I-0073a}}, a warm evening, he was sitting in his doorway at Tobar dhìreach breaking the fire coal into dust with his fingers, as he often did when he was thinking, and not thinking of the fire coal at all. He was thinking of the mason, and of the lightning that had made the mason's fire out of nothing: a crack, a snap in the sky.

He snapped his fingers.

The dust on them took fire. It went up off his hand in a little rush of flame, and the flame was the colour of the fire coal itself, a clear red-gold that shone through his fingers, brighter than any hearth he had sat at. He yelled and beat his hand against his coat, and the flame went out, and he looked at his fingers and they were whole. There was not a mark on them.

Then he sat in the doorway and laughed until his wife came to see what was the matter. The fire had been in the hand all along, the tellers say, in the hand and in the snap of it, as the lightning is in the sky; and a man cannot go looking in the sea for a thing he carries on him.

There was left the seventh, the rainbow coal, *gual bogha-froise*, that holds every colour; and it was the longest. For more than four hundred years Clann na Ceiste threw it in the sea and the river, dropped it from the Point, buried it, bled on it and snapped over it, and it lay dark through all of them. The tellers do not dwell on those years.

## IV. Of the Line of the Mason

The Line of the Mason is the eldest genealogy on Dia-thìr, and the only one, and it is kept by Clann na Ceiste by mouth. It is said standing, at the hearth on the night of the lightning; and before a stranger it is said in this short form, the long stretches between the named fathers given as numbers, for the reciters do not give the rest of the names to a stranger.

The reciters of Clann na Ceiste give it thus, and they give it the same way every time:

"Neachdan the Mason broke the stone, and the lightning came into it, and he carried the first fire home. Neachdan was the father of Conall, who kept the vein while it burned. And after Conall came thirty-four fathers, each the son of the one before, whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Diarmad.

"Diarmad asked the question in the grove, and the Keeper answered him, and gave him the riddle. And after Diarmad came eighteen fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Aodh.

"Aodh threw the seven into the sea, and the gold took fire on the water. And after Aodh came thirty-one fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Fearghas.

"Fearghas climbed to the top of the island and let fall the six, and the snowflake took fire in the air, and the Point bears our name. And after Fearghas came twenty-eight fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Brian.

"Brian buried the five like the dead, and the black was warm in the morning. And after Brian came thirty fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Cormac.

"Cormac of the Misstrike bled in the grove, and the coal of life took fire from his blood. And after Cormac came twenty-one fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Eòghann.

"Eòghann tasted the river, and the silver took fire under the water. And after Eòghann came fifteen fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Colla.

"In the days of Colla they called us the Inquisitors of the Mystic Coal, and we took the name. And after Colla came ten fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was the father of Seathan.

"Seathan snapped his fingers, and the fire was in his hand. And after Seathan came fifteen fathers whose names we keep; and the last of them was Suibhne.

"Suibhne was the father of Ailean, who carried the six fires to the grove."

There the Line of the Mason ends as it is said at the hearth; for Ailean was a young man still when the age closed, and the reciters do not say a man's line past a man who is living.

## V. Of the Waters and the Woods

The tellers go back now to the first years of the count, for while the Mason's line questioned the coal the island went its own way. In the years after the lightning Dia-thìr was another shape. The snowfields on the central mountains were shrinking, and their meltwater cut the bed that Abhainn uaine follows still; its gravel terraces hold hearth-stones at three heights, each left as the water came up to it. In the bog beneath Doire ghlas the birch and pine of the colder years gave way to oak and hazel, and the grove grew up on the old heath where, nine hundred years after the lightning, the Keeper stood.

At Achadh dhìreach lie the first hearths of the high valleys, lit from the mason's fire or from fires lit from it: pine and heather charcoal, scrapers of grey flint, the split bones of red deer. There is no coloured coal in them. Whoever sat at them either had none of it or would not burn it, and fed the fire with wood.

And the sea rose. It drowned the low neck that joined Eilean dhubh to the main island, and the island's deer, cut off, grew smaller, lifetime by lifetime. Out on Eilean mhin seal-hunters pitched their camps a season at a time, reached by boat, and that is the first sure sign that anyone on Dia-thìr crossed open water on purpose. Abhainn ìseal, the longest river of the island, settled into its broad bed, and weirs of hazel were staked across its side channels, and the stakes were renewed in the same holes for generations.

These are small things. A people who leave no words leave their fires and their weirs and their bones, and he who would know them must learn to read those.

## VI. Of the Seann-Dhaoine

At Seann Dunn there is a mound taller than a man, made of limpet shell and seal bone and burnt weed, laid down over many lifetimes. It is the first sure trace of the **Seann-Dhaoine**, the Old Ones; and no tool in it is shaped as the hill-folk shape a tool.

What they called themselves is lost. The name they bear was given afterward, by others, and means only that they were before. Yet they held the shores of Dia-thìr for five thousand years and more.

Of them it is told that at Seann Chwen they sank their nets with beach stones, and every stone was notched with the same three strokes, the earliest mark on Dia-thìr made again and again on purpose. At Seann Skell they laid a mole of unmortared boulders out into the river, and every landing made there since stands upon it. At Seann Bhral they cut two hundred and twelve steps up the cliff from the shingle to the grazing, and the treads are worn hollow in the middle; no one has counted how many feet that took. At Seann Toll they cut a channel into a river pool with a slot for a gate of wattle, and the slot is still sharp. At Seann Warr they laid a road of crushed shell across the reed marsh, a forearm above the reeds, and it is the first made road on the island that can still be walked. At Seann Mhorn they built seven fish-kilns to dry the catch over peat smoke, the first buildings on Dia-thìr raised for a trade. At Seann Bhrenn they laid long ramps of stone into the water, wide enough for hulls bigger than any hide boat. The hulls are gone, and so is any tale of where they went.

At Seann Tarr they cut rows of signs into the cliff. It is the script that turns up later on the standing stones. It is no Dia-thìris, and it has never been read, and not one sign of it has an agreed meaning.

The Seann-Dhaoine of Seann Vell rowed their dead out to Eilean dhearg and laid them in stone boxes that face the mainland. Forty have been counted. No grave of theirs on the mainland from that time has been found. So the dead of the Old Ones were set to look back across the water at the living.

Their works went up on every coast. Platforms on oak piles stood out in the shallows of Loch chrom, though whether the Old Ones raised them or some other people the timbers do not tell. Below the southern Seann Dunn the ribs of a stranded whale were raised as the frames of round houses. At the eastern Seann Vell there are fourteen house-rings of dry stone. At the north-western Seann Skell stands a hollow-walled tower with a stair in the thickness of its wall, looking over the water where the Sea-Watcher was seen. There was a second Seann Mhorn, a headland of oyster and whelk whose lowest oysters are larger than any taken there since, and a second Seann Bhrenn far to the north-east with the same ramps; whether one people built both or the name was carried, none can say. At the eastern Seann Tarr a long house was built into a cleft of the cliff, and the rock behind its hearth is black with soot to the height of a man.

At their height, in {{year:I-0056}}, they held every coast: the ten places whose names begin with *Seann*, and the islands off them, and their greatest landing was Seann Skell on the Abhainn naomh, where the mole already stood. At the warm springs under the mountain they set stone basins, and the bones buried nearby show old breaks that knitted clean. That is the first place on Dia-thìr where the sick were brought to be made well. Let it be remembered of them also.

## VII. Of the Three Ruins and the Deep Shafts

The long centuries that began in {{year:I-0032}} saw as many works and wonders as any stretch of the age. The tellings hold them together as one time, when great works went up inland and great fears came in from the sea.

On a spur above the southern lowland a walled outpost was raised, which the tellings call Làrach an Dùin-fhaire. Its stones are dressed on the outer face only, and its one gate faces inland, away from the sea. A greater work went up on a height nearer the sea: three rings of wall, a cistern cut in the bedrock, and room for four hundred people with their beasts. That is Làrach an Dùin, and whether the outpost's builders raised it too has never been settled. Across the lowland run stretches of a kerbed track, a cart's width and paved with flat slabs, heading toward both; it is the only road the Seann-Dhaoine are known to have built away from the shore.

In the eastern hills a stepped platform was built with an altar block on its top step, and the altar was set square to the central mountains. That is Làrach an Teampaill. No other altar on Dia-thìr faces that way. Seers have said since that it faces the vein under the mountains; each who hears it may join the two or leave them apart.

At the eastern Toll-dubh a shaft was sunk straight down through the rock, its walls tooled smooth as far as a lamp can show, and no spoil heap lies beside it. Whatever was taken out of it was carried away, or nothing was. Across the island, at the western Toll-dubh, a second shaft of the same make was cut. Stones thrown into either are never heard to land.

None of these works lasted. The single gate of Làrach an Dùin-fhaire was blocked with rubble from the inside; whoever did it left by another way, or never left. The outpost stood empty, and was a ruin already in the days of the oldest tellings. The cistern of Làrach an Dùin was found choked with the bones of cattle thrown in whole, and no one drew from it again, and the fortress was left in the same state and the same mystery. The top stone of the altar at Làrach an Teampaill was prised off and carried away. The sockets are still sharp. The stone has never been found. Then the temple too was left.

After the high places were given up, every new work of the Seann-Dhaoine lay within sight of salt water, and the middens at the southern Seann Chwen grew as fast as those of Seann Dunn once had. It is as if a people went up into the hills, and built, and were turned back, and came down to the shore and stayed there. That is how the tellers put it, and the stones neither agree nor argue.

When the shafts were already old, the people of the south-east plaited a rope of hide and brought a youth to the lip of the eastern shaft. The wind came up out of the hole, cool and smelling of wet stone, and lifted the hair from his brow as he sat on the edge with the rope knotted under his arms. He had a lamp of fat in a hollowed stone, and he sat looking down into the dark.

They let him go hand over hand, the men braced with their heels in the turf and the hide creaking through their fists. The lamp went down and grew small, a pin of light on the smooth wall, and then the dark closed over it and there was only the rope going out. The rope ran out to its last knot, and they held it there, and it hung straight and heavy and gave no sign.

Then it jerked twice, and they hauled.

He came up alive. His lamp was out and his face was the colour of ash. They asked him, and he shook his head. He never said what was at the bottom, not then and not in all the years he lived; and after him no one went down again in that age.

## VIII. Of the Sea-Watcher and the Hollow Man

In those same years the fears of Dia-thìr were born, and two of them have never left it.

The tellings of the coast begin with boats. Below the headlands of the north-west, in the tidal mud, lie a row of mooring stakes and a scrap of stitched hide, the first sure boats of the north-west coast. Who rowed them, and from where, the mud does not say.

On {{date:I-0040}} the Sea-Watcher was first sighted. Old sailors of the north-west speak of a creature vaster than any whale in the water off that coast, and add, before anyone else can, that no wreck has ever been raised to prove them wrong. On the last headland of that coast a fire was kept, and it was fed so long that its ash lay a man's height deep. The tellings say it burned to turn boats away from the Watcher's water. The beacon-keepers' word to the boats is kept on that coast still:

> *Tionndaidh air ais. Chan ann dhutsa an t-uisce seo.*
> Turn back. This water is not for you.

A telling of that coast counts seven boats gone from Ros ruadh in one calm season, with no storm to blame; that was the first loss laid at the Watcher's door by name. After it the seal-bone thins in the middens of the north-west, and the middens in the sheltered straits of the north grow. The hunters were working the southern sides of the headlands now, out of sight of the open sea.

Inland the rock faces of the Doire uaine hills are cut with writing, more than anywhere else on the island, in lines that run for yards; and some of the lines are cut over and defaced by the same kind of tool that cut them. The tellings say they speak of something that walked without breathing and fell upon anyone who lit a fire in its sight. This is the Hollow Man of Doire uaine. How a thing written in a script no one can read came to be known at all is a riddle; yet known it is, in every house of those hills.

After the writing, the hearths of those hills changed. Fires were dug into pits and roofed with slabs, and no open hearth showed on those slopes for many lifetimes. The people stayed. They hid their light. At a hill camp in that country nine people lay where they fell inside a hut, beside a hearth of fresh ash, and no wound has been found on any bone. The hut was never reopened, and no one was buried from it.

The fear did not pass. Many lifetimes on, a herding family new to the hills lit an open fire at Doire uaine against the custom, and their hut was found empty with the fire still burning in it. After that the custom was kept harder.

Later again, two herders were driving beasts home past the Doire uaine hills when the light went. They had stayed too long at the far pasture. Neither had flint or tinder out, for they were hill-bred and would sooner have walked blind; and so they went on in the half-dark with the beasts crowding at their heels.

The younger saw it first and caught the other's arm. Up on the ridge, against the last grey of the sky, a figure was walking, upright and unhurried, along the crest. They watched it for as long as a man might count a score. It was cold enough that the herders' breath hung in clouds before them. Above the walker on the ridge nothing hung at all.

They did not run, for running is noise. They stood among the cattle with their hands over their mouths until the figure passed over the crest. At home they would not let the fire be uncovered until full day. They had no fire lit on the path, the telling says, and that is why they lived.

So the covered hearth spread from the Doire uaine hills to every settlement of that country, and the rule that no open fire is lit on those slopes after dark begins there. Mothers in that country still say:

> *Na las teine far am faic e thu.*
> Light no fire where it can see you.

## IX. Of the Groves and the Beasts

A forest in the east of that country was hallowed by the hill-folk themselves, and they call it only the holy wood. The trees understood, the old telling goes, before the people did. The charcoal in the hearths around it comes from every wood but that one; the families at its edge carried their fuel a half-day's walk rather than cut a branch within it. There, at the edge of the trees, three hunters once saw a white stag, and none of them drew. The stag comes back in the tellings again and again. Some at the hearths say it is the white stag of Flidais, lady of the deer and the wild, whom the Keeper named in the grove; the most give it no god at all.

A second forest, older in its feeling than the first, was never settled, never felled, never fully walked: Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag. Paths lead toward it from three sides, and all three end at a low line of set stones short of the trees. Nothing is worn beyond the stones. Once a child of that country went past the line and was never found, and the searchers stopped at the stones. The telling does not blame them. The hearths of that country say the sìth took the child, the people of the hills and mounds, whose country begins where the worn paths end.

In the northern hills the red deer were driven down lines of stake and brush into a gully, and the antlers of the greatest stags were stacked apart. At Cnoc fhionn dogs were buried beside people, each with a shell at its head, the first beasts given graves on Dia-thìr; and at a spring of the wild northern coast wolves were laid in the ground on their sides like people. The grey geese of Eilean ghorm come into the tellings in these years. They come with the first storm and leave with the last, and they are never hunted there.

In {{year:I-0078}} a thing was first marked that is a wonder still: the great cats of the island cross the mountains by fixed roads at fixed seasons. These are the panther roads. The hill-folk learned the cats' timing and kept off the passes while they crossed. A party of eleven from the southern shore went up into the hills in the crossing season and did not come back, and their bones were found years afterward along the line the cats still use. The households below the passes came to cut a sign on their gateposts at the start of the crossing and scrape it off at the end, the first mark on Dia-thìr made for a season. Herders from the lowland who drove cattle into the passes out of season lost thirty head in a night and came down with nothing. And once the cats came down the road near Imrich nam Pantar and turned back at the foothills. The hill-folk took it for a sign, and stored double; and the winter was hard.

## X. Of the Hill-Folk and the Three Stones

In the high valleys around the crack there lived people who were of another kind from the Seann-Dhaoine. They called themselves Clann nan Dè, the godkin, and the Mason's line was one household among them. They buried their dead in the ground, and cut no script. When the Old Ones came to the island, or whether they too grew out of it, no telling knows; the Telling of the Making names no people on Dia-thìr but the godfolk and their children. The hill-folk's own answer is that they were always there. These are the hill-folk, and from them came the Dia-thìrich. They left the green mounds of the valleys unbroken, and built on none of them, and the hearths give the same reason: the sìth live in the hills and the mounds, and a house on a mound is a house on someone else's roof.

At Àth gharbh, in a hearth of plain peat ash, lies a sliver of the coloured coal burnt to a bead, and the hearthstone round it is stained through with colour. It is the earliest burning of the coal by any hand outside the Mason's line that anyone has found, and it burned as the mason's coals had burned, low and dark; whoever fed it to that fire knew nothing of the riddle. It is also small: one sliver, one fire. In the graves at Achadh dhìreach is wool streaked with colour no plant makes, for the hill-folk rubbed broken coal from the vein into cloth for their dead, one grave in some thirty. And far off on the western coast, in a midden of the Old Ones, lies a chip of the coloured coal still staining the shell around it. How a piece of the vein came to a rubbish-heap of the Old Ones, by gift or trade or theft, is anyone's guess.

Then the stones were raised; and the Dia-thìrich hold them holier than any temple.

The first is Carragh Cnoc bheag. On its upper face runs a line of the unread script, and lower down, cut with a narrower tool by another hand, are four words of Dia-thìris:

> *Bha teine sa chloich.*
> There was fire in the stone.

When that second hand came, the stone does not say. Clann na Ceiste hold that it was one of theirs, and that the fire in the stone is the mason's.

The second is Colbh Cnoc chiar, and it carries three words only:

> *Cuimhnich an teine.*
> Remember the fire.

Why the command was given, nothing is told. The ground round it is trodden hard in a ring paved with fire-cracked stones, where hearth-sized fires were kept again and again, and nothing else was left.

The third is Calbh Cnoc ghorm, and it carries the doctrine itself, whole: *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.* If the doctrine has a birthplace, it may be there. The ground round it holds the post-holes of shelters for some hundreds, used and rebuilt many times. Whatever was said there was said to a crowd.

In those years a burial ground grew about a place that no one remembers hallowing: Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, whose oldest graves are older than any name the hill-folk remember. In one of the oldest lies a child with a sliver of the coloured coal closed in one hand. The sliver stained the cloth she was wrapped in, and it is the only piece of the vein found buried with the dead in all this age. The graves came to be laid in rows, heads toward the central mountains; the earliest rows are straight, and the later ones bend round graves already there that no one would disturb.

So the Ancient Age draws to its close, in the high valleys, among people who burned the coal and did not know it. It closes as it opened, with a fire. Far to the north, at the grove of Doire ghlas, a young man of Clann na Ceiste had set six burning pots in a ring under the oaks and laid the dark seventh coal among them, and the Keeper came to him there out of the evening ({{date:I-0080a}}); and for the first time since the making of the world all seven coals burned together. The Ancient Age ends in that light, with the young man sitting among the seven fires under the oaks, and the Keeper standing with him, and the night coming on.
