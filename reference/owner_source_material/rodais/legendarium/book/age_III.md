## I. Of the Binding of the First Flame

In the days before the Binding the hill-folk of Dia-thìr burned the coloured coal by the handful and shared their grain before the storms, and at every gathering they said that they had always been on the island and had come from nowhere. Nine keepers, one drawn by lot for each district of the hills, watched the slab over the crack at Tobar dhìreach. All of it was custom, and none of it was law. So the tellers close the Age of Ailean: everything in its place, and nothing yet sworn.

The Holy Age opens with an oath. On {{date:II-0001}} a custodianship was declared over the vein beneath the central mountains, and that swearing is called the Binding of the First Flame. The flame bound that day was Brìde's undying flame. The tellers of the Mason's line hold that it was first kindled from the mason's fire, which the lightning lit in the broken vein at the beginning of the count; but they keep the two apart, as the Keepers did. The mason's fire is the first fire, and the First Flame is the flame that was sworn to. The vein and the slab over it were given to Crom Cruaich, the bent one of the mound, whose price is measured. One order took both into its keeping, Òrd Bhrìde, the order of Brìde's flame, eldest of the orders of the Old Faith. It was a faith and a government at once, the first such thing on Dia-thìr, and it stood for centuries before any king of the lowlands. Whoever came after to rule the roads and harbours ruled beside it and never over it, for as long as the age lasted.

The choosing came after, at Dùn ìseal under the mountain, on {{date:II-0002}}.

Beathag Bhàn stood at the back of the crowd, with the mud of the path still wet on her shins. A wind came down off the high passes and tugged at every cloak on the slope. The keepers of the slab sat on stones in a half-ring, and behind them stood the households who had sworn the Binding, smelling of wet wool and peat-smoke and the coal-dust that never quite washes out of a hill-woman's hands.

Someone spoke her name. Then the crowd parted, as barley parts when a hare runs through it, and she found herself walking down the lane it made. The eldest of the keepers held out a lit rush in his cupped hands, as a man holds water he is afraid to spill.

"Hold it for Brìde," he said. "And answer to Crom for what is taken from the vein."

She took the rush. It stank of tallow, and the little flame leaned away from the wind toward the mountain. She found she had nothing to say, so she said nothing; and the Keepers after her counted that silence in her favour.

Of what she had been before that morning, nothing is kept. The Keepers' recited roll begins with her name, and no name stands before it. The Library mistrusts a roll that begins so bare, but it has found nothing to set against this one.

Nine households round the mountain's foot, one for each district of the old keepers of the slab, took the tending of the flame by turns, each sending a son or a daughter to sit with it through a season. These were the nine hearths, and more houses in later days claimed descent from them than nine hearths could ever have bred. Over the flame they raised Talla na Lasrach, the Hall of the Flame, of dry stone and turf, with one door facing the vein and none facing the sea. Brìde's flame burned within; Crom's stone lay under the mountain beyond the door. Many times the Hall was rebuilt on the same footings, and the footings are all that anyone can now swear to.

Beside the Binding, and older at the root, the hearths kept a reverence of their own, and in those years it took a name: Seann Spioradan nan Dia-thìreach, the Old Spirits. It honoured the ancestors, the sìth of the hills and mounds, the vein, and the weather that ruled the mining season. It had no priests and wanted none. Its watchers on the cairns above Cnoc ghlas read the cloud on the high passes to tell the Hall when the galleries might open, and they were chosen with more care than the Keeper was.

The first Keepers ruled mostly by forbidding. No fire might be lit where the hills of Doire uaine could see it, in obedience to the old inscriptions there, and nobody has ever owned to breaking the ban.

Once Beathag Bhàn walked to Làrach an Teampaill, the ruined temple of the Old Ones. She went in alone, the tellers say, with the wind crying in the broken stones, and found the altar already turned toward the mountain. When she came out she said that the place was neither Brìde's nor Crom's, and not the Hall's to keep. No Keeper set foot inside it for the rest of the age. Whose it was, she did not say, and it seems she did not know.

She died at Dùn ìseal, and they laid her on the slope with her face to the vein and her back to the sea, though it is over the sea, in Taigh Dhuinn, that the dead are gone. The nine hearths chose her sister's son, Muireach Liath, to keep the flame after her.

## II. Of Muireach Liath and the Silver

*At Muileann chiar, when a crucible is set on the coals, the old smiths tell this while they wait for the metal to run.*

Muireach Liath, they say, fixed the first common measure of coal: a lidded basket of plaited heather, one to a household, filled once in the season. Yet the smiths remember him for two refusals.

The first was over a stream. Allt an Àigh gave fortune, men said, to whoever drank from it in a certain quarter of the moon, and it had become a place of pilgrimage, as it has never wholly ceased to be. Pilgrims wore a path to it from the river landing at Caol shean and walked it by night, to come to the water at the right hour. Muireach Liath ruled that the stream belonged to the ancestors and the weather, and that the Hall would not keep it, for it had nothing to do with the vein. That was the first parting anyone could see between the Keepers' rite and the older reverence, and both sides later quoted it as proof that they were right. In the same temper the Keepers let the sick be carried to the warm springs below Muileann ruadh and bathed at dawn, since the water's heat came up from the mountain without any hand breaking the stone. The springs were held to be Dian Cècht's, the healer of the gods, and Crom had no claim on them.

The second refusal was over silver, and here the smiths lean closer to the fire.

In the far east, near the great vein yet no part of it, a seam was found at the place called Muileann chiar: the first thing out of the ground on Dia-thìr that was surely not coal. Fearchar Airgeadach, a basket-carrier of the nine hearths, was the first to smelt it and hammer it into rings. He fed his furnace with wood and peat, and the smiths call that the first fire on the island that answered to nobody in the Hall. They say it with some pride.

When the Hall was asked whether the seam fell under the flame, Muireach Liath turned a ring over in his fingers and gave it back, and answered:

> *Chan eil fuil san airgead, agus chan ann leis a' bheinn a tha e.*
> There is no blood in silver, and it is not the mountain's.

Silver shows no colour when broken, and so it was not holy to Crom. The smiths gave their first ring to Goibhniu, the smith of the gods, and the Hall said nothing against it. Muileann chiar was left free to dig as deep as it liked, and it dug. Every later quarrel over the silver began from that sentence. Carriers took the rings down to Ros fhionn and traded them for salt fish and sealskin, the first trade on the island made in anything but food, and the first whose price is remembered. One ring bought a boatload.

Muireach Liath died at Dùn ìseal, keeping the flame. The nine hearths passed over three candidates of other lines and chose his daughter, Gormshuil nic Mhuirich. Her kin came to be called Sliochd Mhuirich, the house of Muireach, the first custodian house known by a name and no longer by a hearth, and the office stayed with them for many generations. The other hearths made no complaint that anyone kept, say the smiths; but then, they add, it was Muireach's own people who did the keeping.

## III. Of the Stag and the Stone Cup

In Gormshuil's years a school arose in the east that would spread wider than any order on the island: the first of the druid schools, Scoiltean nan Draoidhean, which the east still calls Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the Philosophy of the Stag. It began with one stubborn woman. Sìleas nic Coinnich, a cutter of Muileann chiar, had seen the coal break bright with its colours under some picks and dull under others, and she would not leave the thing alone. The old tellers said that she had caught the question of the Mason's children, who in the Ancient Age had asked what the seven coals were for. She taught in Coille Naomh Muileann chiar, and her hearers kept her question as she left it:

> *Carson a tha an fhèith a' freagairt do làimh seach làimh eile?*
> Why does the vein answer one hand and not another?

Until then the Dia-thìrich had revered the coal. Sìleas was the first to reason about it. Her hearers took their name from a stag said to have stood in the grove through a whole lesson, the white stag of Flidais, lady of the deer and the wild, whose wood it was. The school's later name, and the stag's name, Camaran, are found only in much younger writings.

The Hall did not welcome the question. Gormshuil forbade Sìleas's hearers to cut in the galleries of Dùn ìseal, holding that a question asked of the vein is a demand made of it, and for a long while after, the stag's people kept to the east and sent no cutters to the mountain.

Then a death made the next law. In a hard year a household at Cnoc dhubh burned its coal by the basketful. The smoke lay down in the valley like a sick dog and would not lift, and three of that household died of it, and two of their neighbours. The case was argued at Dùn ìseal for the rest of the season.

The Keeper who ended it was Fionnlagh Dall, Fionnlagh the Blind.

He came into the Hall on the arm of a boy and found the threshold with his foot. The council had been shouting since morning and their voices were hoarse. He could smell the peat on their clothes and the wet dogs by the door, and under it all the thin hot scent of Brìde's flame at his back. The kin of the dead sat on his left. He knew where they were by their silence.

He had brought the old keepers' stone cup, a thing of the Age of Ailean, re-cut in grey stone to hold exactly what a pair of cupped hands will hold. He set it on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach, knelt, and laid his palm across its mouth.

"Give me the coal," he said.

They poured it for him, a little at a time. He did not know whose hand poured, and he did not ask. When the black grains rose to touch his palm, level with the rim, he said, "Enough," and took his hand away and let them look. No one afterward could say he had favoured anyone's hands.

On {{date:II-0027}} the custom hardened into the Small-Burning Law: no household might burn more than a measured handful of coal in a season. It held for centuries.

Its first sentence fell on a household of Seann Tarr that had burned twice its measure. It lost its right for the season after, and its name went on the roll with the offence beside it. Its descendants petitioned for many centuries to have the offence struck out, and never won, and the offence is the only reason anyone remembers them.

The Keepers carried the Law to the coasts. No coal, they ruled, might feed a beacon, since a beacon burns for strangers:

> *Cha loisc clach na beinne do choigrich.*
> The mountain's stone will not burn for strangers.

So the fire on the headland at Tobar dhearg was kept with driftwood and seal fat, and so was every sea-light on the island after it.

## IV. Of the Roads, the Bridges and the Road-Peace

*This is how the carriers tell it at the long table of the house that is now Am Buabhall Fortanach, while the jugged buffalo sits on the fire.*

The Law made the Hall a giver-out of coal, they say, and what is given out must be carried. So the island's roads grew along the carriers' tracks, down stepped paths such as the one to Ceann chaol. The rivers took their toll. One night the Abhainn dhomhain rose at a ford and swallowed eleven carriers with their baskets. The Hall mourned the coal as well as the men, and the carriers will tell you, if you let them, that the Hall's roll names the coal first. Fionnlagh Dall asked the council for a crossing the river could not take, and stone bridges began to join the mining paths to the coast, the forefathers of Drochaid Cathair dhearg and Drochaid Ros fhionn.

Above Ros fhionn, Ailean Clachair and forty masons of the nine hearths threw a single span of fitted stone over the river, and Ailean cut a hearth-sign in the keystone. Every later bridge carried one, and a carrier would sooner wade than trust a bridge without it. The masons swore an oath on the stone cup: no span to be closed in the dark, and none to be paid for in coal. They kept their lodge near the western crossing, in the country of Ros dhomhain.

Houses grew along the roads to feed the people on them, the forebears of Am Buabhall Fortanach, An Taigh-òsta Grianach and the rest of the circuit. The carriers always begin with this one. Its first keeper was Mòr nic Dhùghaill, a widow of Doire shean, who served carriers jugged buffalo from her own herd, and here the carriers bang the table, for the house serves it still. Where An Taigh-seinnse Mòr stands there was then a turf longhouse with a hearth at each end, one for carriers going up and one for carriers coming down.

Then the keepers of the houses made a law of their own: no feud might be pursued under an inn roof, nor within a spear's throw of its door. It was first kept at the house where An Sligeanach Fad' às now stands, and it is the oldest law on the island that no Keeper made. The carriers put it plainly:

> *Fo mhullach an taighe, chan eil nàmh ann.*
> Under the roof of the house, there is no enemy.

The roads carried settlers too, to coasts that had names already. Fishers of the nine hearths settled the harbour of Seann Mhorn and kept its name, which is in no tongue of theirs, saying that the name came with the stones; it was the first such name to be kept. Families walking the new road resettled Seann Warr on the western shore of Loch chrom in the same way, and built on the house-footings they found in the grass. At this point the whole table sings:

> *Thog sinn air na clachan, is cha do dh'fhaighnich sinn cò leis.*
> We built upon the stones, and did not ask whose.

Fionnlagh Dall died at Dùn ìseal. At his own asking he was not laid on the slope with the Keepers before him. He was carried south to the old graves near Cnoc dhìreach, which no living people had claimed, and his bearers took eleven days over the road and laid him among graves older than any name. Why he asked it, the Keepers after him gave two answers and would not choose between them, and the carriers do not choose either. So Tuam Cnoc dhìreach, ancient already, received its first burial that anyone set down, and a graveyard that no one owned became one that somebody did. Families settled nearby to tend it, and the Hall paid them one handful of coal each a season. It was the only payment in coal the Law ever allowed, and the carriers, who carried every other handful on the island, find that fitting.

## V. Of the Four Houses and the Reverent Centuries

In time the nine hearths chose a Keeper from outside the house of Muireach: Eilidh nic Ìomhair of Dùn dhearg. Sliochd Mhuirich refused her for a season, and then accepted her on one condition, that the stone cup never leave Talla na Lasrach. It never did.

Under Eilidh the office was bound to four houses of custody: Sliochd Mhuirich of Dùn ìseal, Sliochd Ìomhair of Dùn dhearg, Sliochd Thormoid of Seann Dunn, and Sliochd Raghnaill of Seann Tarr. The other hearths kept their council seats but lost their claim to the flame. Most of the island's later nobility comes down from one of the four, or says it does, and from here onward the tale of the age is largely theirs.

Sliochd Thormoid raised a hall and a shrine at Seann Dunn, facing the mountain across the river. Its coal-lamp burned at the Law's measure, and the other houses grumbled that Seann Dunn's measure was always the fullest. Sliochd Raghnaill made its seat at Seann Tarr, the very town first sentenced under the Law, kept boats where the others kept galleries, and grew rich on fish. Its boats sailed round Eilean dhubh and came home, the first voyage ever made only to look at a coast.

Not everyone lived inside the Law. Households stripped of their right for a third offence took to boats and raided the north-western strands, and the name spùinneadairean, plunderers, clung to those waters after them. There a boat out of Baile chrom was lost in a flat calm, and the two men who swam home said something had risen under it. The village has kept a wake for it every year since, and it relit the old beacon on the point with driftwood, as the Law required. From that fire comes Taigh-solais Baile chrom.

Fishers resettled Seann Bhrenn, kept its old name, and used the stone quay they found under the silt. At Cathair naomh a cairn for the dead was begun that is growing yet. When the great cats' crossing was found to run over the southern road, Eilidh ruled that no panther might be killed there in its season, and the rule held, for it cost the carriers only time.

At Dùn dhearg, Sliochd Ìomhair built granaries of stone and filled them from the lowland fields against the storm season, as the old custom of sharing asked. The granaries gave that house something the Hall had never had: people who owed it their bread.

In those days, the reverent centuries, each generation mined the same modest measure and burned the same modest measure, and the number of the people stood still. Shepherds took the uplands at Achadh chaol; their headman sat one season on the council, was found to hold no hearth-right, and went home. Coinneach Mòr mac Tormoid, first Keeper of his house, kept the flame longer than any before him and changed nothing at all. He sent walkers out to list the ruins of the Old Ones, and they came back with Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, Làrach an Dùin, Làrach an Teampaill and the standing stones. Beside each place the list says who does not own it. It does not say who built it, for the Keepers ruled that it should not guess. At Cnoc bheag the people fenced the old obelisk with stakes against the cattle, and every generation since has renewed the fence. Over the springs at Muileann ruadh a bath-house was roofed, and the Hall named a keeper of the springs in Dian Cècht's name, the first office it made that had nothing to do with the coal.

## VI. Of the First Rìgh and the Division of Keeping

In the generations after Coinneach Mòr the rolls grow fuller, and in them the island can be watched filling out, as a field fills with barley.

The silversmiths of Muileann chiar cast weights from a barley-corn to a newborn lamb, and a thing weighed there was weighed fairly. The stag's people founded a house at Cnoc bhàn, and the Keeper Seonaid Chiar let their cutters back into the galleries on oath to ask nothing of the vein aloud. The Hall called it a reconciliation; the school still calls it a truce. Cathair mhòr built the first town wall on the island and never said what it feared. Ceann leathan's harbour-master kept notched tally-sticks, and they lie now at Muileann chaol, the oldest counted things there. Inis thais burned its handful on the shore so that the drowned could see it, and the salt of Cuan shean kept the fish of half the island. The sunny house rose where An Taigh-òsta Grianach stands. A roof fell in the highest gallery and killed four cutters, and Seonaid Chiar propped every gallery with oak.

Carriers founded Cathair dhomhain above the western harbour, proud of keeping the measure strictly. A few miles off, at the bridge-foot on the Abhainn dhomhain below Loch chrom, the Red Hill's old settlement, long shrunk to a fishing town on red stone, began to grow again. In those days Cathair dhearg was a place carriers passed through on the way to somewhere else.

At Dùn dhearg the lowland households who ate from Sliochd Ìomhair's granaries began to call the head of that house their rìgh, their king. He was Goraidh mac Ìomhair, and no ruler of the lowlands before him had borne the word; only the Line of the Mason remembered that the gods had once given it to Ailean Mòr in the grove. The granaries gave it to him.

He went up to Dùn ìseal in the spring to meet the council, on foot, as a carrier would.

It was a long climb, and by the time the grey roof of Talla na Lasrach came in sight over the shoulder of the hill his breath was short. He had left his spear with his men at the last house on the road. He had thought about that for three nights. A man who comes to the Hall armed has already said what he wants, and he hoped they would read the other thing in his empty hands.

The council sat outside on the stones before the door, for the day was fine, and the Keeper sat among them with the stone cup at her feet. Goraidh stood before them in his travelling cloak with the wind in his beard, and he said the thing he had come to say before anyone could ask it.

"I claim nothing under the mountain. Not a gallery, not a grain of coal. Let the Hall hear me say it."

The Hall heard him. Then they bargained until the light went.

On {{date:II-0078}} the council at Dùn ìseal and the rìgh agreed the division of keeping. The Keeper kept the flame and the galleries. The rìgh kept the roads, the granaries and the peace of the lowlands. The rìgh might not enter the galleries, and the Keeper might not raise spears. On that division Dia-thìr was ruled for the rest of the age, and most of the age's quarrels were over which side of it a thing lay on.

Goraidh Mòr, as they called him after, built a timber hall at Dùn dhearg with a granary at each corner, and set a toll on the roads of one fish in twenty for the guards and the bridges. The waypoint-keepers collected it and kept a share, and the rolls, to their credit, say so. His daughter was Mòrag nan Rathad, Mòrag of the Roads, who walked every carriers' road on the island with a staff in her hand and had the bad stretches remade. She raised the house where An Taigh-seinnse Òir stands, the first built by a ruler, and widened the western crossing of the Abhainn dhomhain with Ailean Clachair's sign recut in the keystone, and the bridge-foot town began to swell.

Her son Dòmhnall Crùbach, Dòmhnall the Lame, could not walk the roads, and ruled through the waypoint-keepers, who round Ceann mhin paid him what toll they judged fair. They judged generously to themselves. When a storm took a third of Seann Chwen, the Hall and the rìgh both sent grain, and the survivors remembered that the Hall's came first.

Settlers from Cathair dhomhain founded Baile àrsaidh among the yellow scorpions of the western heath, and swore their oaths on a scorpion's sting kept in a box. Their neighbours found the custom strange.

At Muileann chiar the stag's teachers turned the cutters' tally-notches into signs for sounds, so that a teaching could be kept without a teacher. These stag-letters, the ogham, which the druids say Ogma of the gods first cut, were the first letters of the Dia-thìrich, and nearly every later hand on the island comes from them. The scholars carried them to Carragh Cnoc bheag and held them up beside the marks on the obelisk. They matched nothing. So the scholars wrote that the stone's writers were not their teachers: the first thing the Dia-thìrich ever wrote about the Old Ones, and it tells only what they were not.

Dòmhnall's niece Sìleas Dhonn made the waypoint-keepers come to her hall once a year and render account of the tolls. Several of them did.

## VII. Of the War of the Roads

Caol leathan in the south-west grew fat on the western fishing and the southern end of the carriers' road, and its leading family under Lachlann Ciar began to keep the road-toll and send none to Dùn dhearg.

Iain Garbh, Iain the Rough, son of Sìleas Dhonn, sent west to demand the tolls. His messenger came back with a covered basket, set it on the floor of the hall, and would not meet his eye. Iain lifted the lid himself. The smell came up first, old and rank. The basket was full of fish-bones, picked clean and packed in rows, as a careful wife packs herring.

He looked at them a long time. Then he laughed, which frightened his household more than shouting would have, and called for the road-guards.

He marched west with the road-guards and the lowland levies and burned the toll-house at Muileann òg that Lachlann Ciar's people had taken, and he watched the thatch go up with his arms folded. It was the first war between Dia-thìrich and Dia-thìrich, and it was fought over the roads the first rìgh had been made to keep.

The Hall blessed neither side. When Iain's messengers asked at Dùn ìseal for the Hall's handful to light the warband's hearths, the Keeper Uisdean mac Ruairidh sent them back empty, and made them repeat his answer before they went:

> *Cha bhlàthaich an lasair fir a tha a' dol a mharbhadh.*
> The flame will not warm men who are going to kill.

"Then we'll burn peat," said Iain. The warband wintered on peat, coughing. So the division of keeping was tried, and it held. The rìgh had the spears and the Keeper had the flame, and neither could take the other's.

At the ford of Àth fhiadhaich the men of Caol leathan stood in the water and held it for a day. Iain went in with the second rank. He remembered afterward the cold that took his legs at the first step, and the noise, and a man of Caol leathan who fought on with a split shield until a spear from behind Iain found him. By evening they had given way. Forty lay dead of the two sides, and the carriers long called it the ford of the forty, and said the Morrìgan's crows were on it before the light went.

Falling back, Lachlann Ciar's men burned Baile ghlas so the levies would find no grain. Iain rode through the warm ashes the next morning and found no one to ask where the people had gone. They had walked to Cathair dhomhain, and many never came back. Baile chiar, south of the bridge-foot, shut its gate against both armies and fed the burned-out people through the winter. Neither side forgave it, and neither side attacked it.

Iain closed Caol leathan by land at the start of winter, but he could not close the harbour. From his camp on the hill he watched the boats slip in at dusk, low in the water with fish. When his levies began going home for the next harvest, first by ones and then by villages, the siege ended without an assault.

The war ended under the carriers' law. Iain Garbh and Lachlann Ciar met under the roof of Mòr nic Dhùghaill's house, where by the road-peace neither could draw. They sat at one board, and Iain saw that his enemy was older than he had thought, and had a cough of his own. They ate the jugged buffalo, and then they talked. Caol leathan kept half its tolls and sent half to Dùn dhearg, and Lachlann Ciar swore to the Law before the Keeper's witness. So the War of the Roads was ended in a house built to feed carriers.

It left three things. Caol leathan's fighters became its road-guards and swore their watch on a white stone carved as a wolf, kept at the town gate, and called themselves the wolf-sworn. Later ages read more into the stone than they did: Òrd na Cloiche, the order of the Stone, holds it the first of its stones, and its sworn guards, the Fianna, keep the wolf for their beast. Caol fhiadhaich, whose boats had fed the besieged town, was given a share of the western tolls and built a harbour wall with it. And when Iain Garbh died, his daughter Catrìona buried him in the floor of his hall at Dùn dhearg and not on the slope at Dùn ìseal. He had never forgiven the Hall, and the Hall did not ask for him.

Catrìona Ghlic, Catrìona the Wise, mended the breach. She sent her own son to sit with the flame for a season, the first of the royal house to do so since the division, and by the end of her reign the Hall and the hall were speaking again. She had houses built on the northern road where An Leòmhann Buidhe and An Taigh-seinnse Reòta stand, the second out on the open moor for carriers caught by snow; its keepers were paid in grain by the Hall and in cloth by the crown, and grumbled at both. Inis mhòr, on the wool trade, outgrew every northern town.

## VIII. Of the Letters, the Woods and the Measuring of the Vein

*The scholars of Muileann chaol count the clerk at the start of this chapter their first forebear, and they tell it with the care of heirs.*

After Catrìona Ghlic the king-list gives eleven names at Dùn dhearg and nothing more, and the Keepers' roll five names and nothing more. A clerk broke that silence. Tormod mac Iain, a younger son of the house of Tormod, learned the stag-letters at Muileann chiar, the first of the royal kin to read, and was called Tormod Scrìobhaiche, Tormod the Writer. Ealasaid Chiar bade him write down every ruler the reciters could name, in order. That was the first written king-list, and from then on rulers were kept by name and no longer only by story. His roll runs from Goraidh Mòr to Ealasaid Chiar. He marked which names came from the reciters and which from living memory, and in three places where it disagreed with the Keepers' roll he wrote both readings and would not choose. For that above all the Library loves him.

The Hall, shamed (in its own words) by the rìgh's clerk, had its recited roll written down at Dùn ìseal. It begins with Beathag Bhàn and is several centuries longer than Tormod's.

In the west a company settled at the edge of Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, the uncleared wood, to watch for a black horned beast they said walked in it. They were the wood-watchers. Òrd Mhacha, the order of Macha, takes that beast for a sign of Dubh Sainglenn, the black horse, and claims them, though none of their own accounts is older than the order. They came east and cut a black-barked tree in the stag's grove, saying the beast had shown it to them. The stag's people called it theft and sent teachers to hold lessons in the dark wood, saying the first stag had come from there. In each wood the two companies met under the trees and did not speak. It was the first quarrel on Dia-thìr between rival holy claims, and it was settled by sharing the keeping, with no winner named. Before Fearchar Òg, rìgh after Ealasaid Chiar, and the Keeper Calum Liath, the stag's people took the eastern wood and the wood-watchers the western, each free to walk the other's unarmed and without cutting. Two of the boundary stones, cut in stag-letters, stand yet.

Calum Liath then sent cutters with knotted cords and lamps into every gallery to follow the vein and mark where it thickened and thinned. The work took several coal-seasons, and the households went short to pay for it. It was the first attempt to measure how much coal the mountain held. The sum, cut on a slab in the Hall, found that the mountain could give every household its handful for more generations than there were grains in the granaries of Dùn dhearg. It was read to the council and heard in silence, which the Hall took for assent. The slab can still be read, and in this age, with the vein nearly spent, those who read it say nothing.

Two youths sent into the lower gallery without a Keeper's cutter broke the wrong face and were crushed. Their families asked what the Hall owed them, and the council had no answer. Out of that grief came the apprentice-binding: young Dia-thìrich were bound to a master custodian for seven years before they might touch the coal, and every later mining guild takes its shape from it. Oighrig Bhàn, Keeper after Calum Liath, bound the first twelve on the stone cup, and four of them lived to be Keepers, which is the custom's best argument. The stag's house at Cnoc bhàn took up the seven years and added a written account of every strike. These strike-books became the school's chief record, and in time they asked a question of their own.

Fearchar Òg reigned long and is remembered mostly for dying old. His clerks paid the road-guards in stamped silver rings, the first payment in metal by any ruler, and the rings travelled further than the guards did. The southern guards began meeting at Ceann leathan each year to fight with blunted staves for a ring, and the meeting outlived its purpose: its field is the one where Còmhrag Ceann leathan was fought long after. The first winner whose name is kept was a woman, Mòrag Dhubh of Cnoc mhòr. Burned Muileann òg was built again, and its harvest fair, held at Lùnastal, the feast of Lugh, became Fèill Muileann òg. At Cidhe Beag on the Abhainn uaine a landing was built for river-boats over the carriers' protest, and the hide boatmen of Muileann chrom swore their fellowship on a paddle.

## IX. Of the Red Hall and the Census of Hands

Beathag Mhòr, great-granddaughter of Fearchar Òg, is the first ruler the list calls banrìgh, queen, and the change of word is the list's own. On {{date:II-0135}} she moved her hall from Dùn dhearg to the town at the western bridge-foot, Cathair dhearg, where the roads, the river and the western harbours met. It has been the seat of rule on Dia-thìr ever since. Her hall was built of the town's red stone on the rise above the harbour, and its foundations lie under the later citadel. The masons cut the hearth-sign in its lintel as if it were a bridge.

Dùn dhearg kept its granaries, the graves of the house of Ìomhar, and its pride. Cathair dhomhain, older and larger, refused the queen's toll on its boats and was granted a charter of its own, and the two towns, a few miles apart, stayed rivals for the rest of the age.

By then the council at Dùn ìseal heard more quarrels over the right to the handful than over anything else: new households, divided households, and claims of descent from the nine hearths without proof. The Keeper Dùghall mac Raghnaill asked the queen for a count, with the clerks to write and the Keepers to judge. The counters worked from the waypoint houses, since every household lay within a day of one, and the houses, feeding them at the queen's cost, remember it as a good year.

The census of hands was completed on {{date:II-0142}}, the first island-wide count of the households holding the right to burn the coal, and proof that the reverent custom was already being rationed. The rolls gave eleven thousand four hundred households holding the right and about two thousand refused. The refused went on a roll of their own, and it is that roll later generations searched when they wished to prove an ancestor's standing. Many of the refused were fisher-families round Seann Tarr who had never burned coal and now had no right to begin. Sliochd Raghnaill argued their case and lost. The fishers burned peat, and said it was warmer.

Beathag Mhòr was carried back at her own order to lie at Dùn dhearg among the house of Ìomhar, the longest royal journey yet made. Her son Ruairidh Liath was already grey when he took the hall, and his byname is the only joke the list makes. He demanded one silver ring in five from Muileann chiar. Sliochd Fhearchair, the silversmiths' house, quoted Muireach Liath back to him, that silver is not holy and so not the crown's either, and shut the workings. For three seasons no silver left Muileann chiar. It is called the Silver Feud, though no one died in it. At last both sides went up to Dùn ìseal and asked the Keeper to judge.

Dùghall mac Raghnaill heard them in the Hall on a raw day, with the door shut against the rain. The smiths had sent an old woman of Sliochd Fhearchair to speak for them. She said Muireach Liath's sentence, and then said it again, slowly, as though the Hall might have forgotten it. The queen's man stood across the hearth and talked of roads and bridges and guards unpaid since Samhain.

Dùghall let them both run dry. Each of them was right, he said afterward, and that is the worst quarrel a judge can be handed.

"Muireach Liath's words stand," he said at last. "Silver is not the mountain's." The old woman nodded as if she had known it all along. "But the crown keeps the roads," Dùghall went on, "and the silver walks on them."

The crown took one ring in ten, the workings reopened, and both sides went home calling it a victory. The Muileann chiar weights were made the only lawful weights on the roads, cast in bronze at Muileann dhearg for every waypoint house.

Ruairidh Liath's granddaughter Seonag Chrom gave the clerks a room in the red hall, and from her reign the king-list was kept year by year. At Inis bheag a stag-teacher taught letters to fishers' children, and the Hall's roll remarked that fish do not need reading. In the red hall Peigi nic Tormoid, of Tormod Scrìobhaiche's line, became the first clerk whose hand can be told from the others, and much of what the Library knows of the middle of the age is in her hand or copied from it.

## X. Of the Hawk, the Cup, the Wolf and the Two Schools

In the later centuries of the age the one practice of the Dia-thìrich began to come apart, in each place for its own reason.

At Seann Skell on the northern coast every fowler household kept a hawk and fed it before the family ate. They burned their handful at dawn with the hawk on the wrist, saying that the flame is a hunger and should be fed as a hunger is fed. They called the hawk Mòd, and said it was the Hawk of Acaill, the grey hawk of time, as old as Fionntan mac Bòchra, eldest of men, who lived through the long ages as a salmon, an eagle and a hawk; and that between the two of them they remember everything. The dawn-burning spread up the Abhainn ìseal to Inis ìseal and Cnoc bheag. The Keeper wrote that since it kept the measure exactly, it was no concern of the Hall's, and that was the last time the Hall said so of any rite. In the west the sting-oath went up the coast from Baile àrsaidh to Ros bheag, where the harbour court took it for every oath of trade, and a merchant who would not swear on the sting was trusted by no one. The wolf-sworn set a second white wolf-stone at the shrine of Inis thais, sledged from the mountain at more cost than the shrine, and its keepers began to pray before it as well as to the drowned.

The stag's people split as well.

At Cnoc bhàn, Donnchadh mac Iain sat up a whole winter with the strike-books, generations of them stacked along the wall higher than his head. He read by a lamp kept at the Law's measure, with a blanket round his shoulders and his breath smoking, and he began, without quite meaning to, to keep a tally on a slate. One night near midwinter he read back down the slate and sat very still. The coal broke brighter in its colours for cutters in their later years than in their first, whatever house they came from.

He took it to the elders in the morning. They heard him out in the cold room, and when he had finished the eldest folded her hands.

"The vein answers to what a cutter is born with," she said. "Practice only shows it."

"Then why does it show more every year?"

She did not answer, and he saw in their faces that the matter was closed. So he left with twenty-two hearers and copies of the strike-books wrapped in oiled hide on their backs, and settled at Muileann chaol. The elders called it theft. Donnchadh called it copying, which it was.

That was the second school of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh. It held that the vein answers to effort, and it wrote no cutter's parentage in its books at all. The two schools argued by letter for the rest of the age, and both kept the letters. The Library stands in Muileann chaol now, and its scholars know that the Long War would one day be fought over that same argument in a very different form. They tell this part quietly.

The Keeper Raonaid Dhubh heard both druid schools at Dùn ìseal and gave no ruling:

> *Cha do dh'iarr an fhèith oirnn breith a thoirt.*
> The vein has not asked us to give judgment.

No refusal in the Hall's history was quoted more often.

Once more the Old Ones stepped into the tale, as a question. Scholars from Muileann chaol could not read the pillar at Cnoc ghorm, and the people of Cnoc ghorm told them it said *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann*: we did not come; we were here. The scholars wrote that the people said so, and there the matter has rested. Men of Baile ghorm dug under Làrach an Dùin for its rumoured riches and found only ash. The Keeper sentenced them as lawbreakers though no law covered the case, and the sentence became the rule for every old place after. The diggers' families did their penance by keeping the road to Tuam Cnoc dhìreach in repair, and it is still called the diggers' road.

## XI. Of the Shared Meal and the Lean Year

*At Àth dhìreach, high in the mountain country, they tell this at the turn of the year, and set out an extra bowl for the carriers.*

Lachlann Bàn built granaries in every province seat that would take one. With the Keeper Raonaid Dhubh he wrote the island's oldest custom into law at Cathair dhearg: a household with grain beyond its winter's need was to give the surplus to its district's common granary before the storm season, and the granary would feed any household of the district that ran short. It was the first law made by rìgh and Keeper together. Cnoc mhòr built its granary first, and its reeve was sent a silver ring. Granaries went up in the mountain districts round Muileann chiar, where people lived by cutting and carrying, and the lowlands grumbled at filling them.

Lachlann's daughter Oighrig nic Lachlainn had no children, and the houses began counting their claims before her reign was half over. Early in that reign the weather tried the law. The watchers above Cnoc ghlas looked up in spring and saw snow on the high passes, and looked again a month later and saw it still, white in the sun, long after the galleries should have opened. The Hall kept them shut. When they did open, they opened late and closed early, and a third of a common season's coal came down. The mountain households of Muileann gheal, Doire uaine and Àth dhìreach, who traded their carrying for grain, had nothing to trade, and by the following autumn they were eating their seed.

On {{date:II-0179}} the first famine on Dia-thìr was turned back. The shared-meal law held. The mountain granaries were opened at Muileann gheal, and when they ran low the granaries of Dùn dhearg and the southern coast sent grain up the carriers' roads without needing to be asked twice. The carriers took no fee. No one in the three settlements is known to have died of hunger that winter. Àth dhìreach gave the carriers who had brought the grain the right to rest in any house of the town for as long as they lived. The last of them died old, and the right died with him, and his is the bowl they still set out.

The reeves of Dùn dhearg, who had refused Cathair dhearg their accounts for generations, opened the old granaries of the house of Ìomhar without waiting for an order. The queen wrote that she would have given the order had they waited. The clerk wrote beneath it that they had not waited. The Keepers of later days named that winter in the same breath as the Binding.

Oighrig nic Lachlainn died without an heir of her body, and the line of Goraidh Mòr, as the list reckons it, ended with her. The red hall was shut, and its clerks kept it. The Four Houses met at Dùn ìseal to choose a ruler, since no custom covered a hall without an heir. Sliochd Ìomhair claimed by blood, Sliochd Thormoid through the clerks, and Sliochd Raghnaill through its boats. Sliochd Mhuirich, which held the Keeper's office, made no claim at all. How long the houses sat, and whether the hall stood empty all that while, is not told; the rolls put the choosing some years after the meeting.

## XII. Of the House of Tormod

The houses chose Gilleasbuig Mòr mac Tormoid of Seann Dunn, a grandson of the clerks' line. The Keeper set the stone cup in his hands before the council, the first time a ruler was made at Dùn ìseal, and it looked very much, as more than one chronicler has dryly said, as if the Hall had made the king. Sliochd Ìomhair consented on condition that its graves at Dùn dhearg stay the kings' burying-place. Gilleasbuig had the king-list recopied with a line drawn under Oighrig nic Lachlainn, and the line is in every later copy. He made his house's shrine at Seann Dunn a temple, whose lamp a Keeper's cutter still checked every season, and he was buried at Dùn dhearg as agreed. His son Tormod Òg succeeded without any meeting of the houses, and nobody asked for one.

Between Tormod Òg and Anna Ruadh the list gives nine rulers of the house with their years and little else, and the rolls of those reigns hold mostly harvests, tolls and bridge repairs. The Library takes this for a sign that the island was doing well.

The bridges, at any rate, were mended. The span at Cathair dhearg was widened for carts on Mòrag nan Rathad's piers. Above Ros fhionn the masons found Ailean Clachair's sign still sharp and left it alone. New crossings rose at Dùn chrom and Ceann àrsaidh, and at Doire mhin the keystone carries, beside the hearth-sign, the first mason's mark on the island: the name of Beathag Clachair. An Sligeanach Fad' às was rebuilt in stone and got its name, for carriers said you were never nearer to it than halfway. The house on the northern road began serving a barley spirit the carriers called fire-water. The Keepers ruled that it was not a burning, and its trade doubled.

The north-west kept to the edge of things. Caol mhòr paid its tolls only when the road-guard came for them, and Inis mhin appears in the rolls once, for a complaint. The seal-oil of Ceann mhòr lit the western harbours brighter than anything else on Dia-thìr. Two boats of Baile chrom felt something vast move under them in the north-western water, and one did not come home. The wake kept for the first loss is kept now for both.

Sliochd Raghnaill's boats made the round of the offshore islands, Eilean ruadh, Eilean dhearg and Eilean fhada among them, in a single season. No crew of the age went further than the islands, and none wished to.

Anna Ruadh of the house of Tormod walked the whole road circuit, as Mòrag nan Rathad had, and she walked it twice. At the house where the War of the Roads was ended she ate the jugged buffalo at the long table like a carrier. She gave the sunny house the only royal charter any waypoint house holds, and gave the contest at Ceann leathan a walled field and fed its losers at the crown's cost. She also kept a wood-watcher in her household to read the omens of the hall, the first sign in the capital of the rite of Macha, lady of sovereignty and of horses. The Keeper protested from Dùn ìseal and got no answer. Cathair dhomhain, proud of keeping the Law strictly, would take no wood-watcher, and said the capital had handed its omens to people who sat in trees. From then on the two towns' rivalry was a quarrel of faith as well.

Anna Ruadh was buried at Dùn dhearg. Her son Coinneach Bàn reigned long and quietly and had the rolls copied for every province seat. Several of those copies have lasted where the originals have not, and much of what the Library knows of these reigns rests on them.

## XIII. Of the Parting of the Rites

Seumas Bàn of Sliochd Mhuirich was chosen Keeper, and in his first winter he sat down in the Hall with the Keepers' roll, meaning only to learn the names of those before him.

He read it by the light of Brìde's flame. The names went down and down in the old stag-letters, some cramped, some sprawling, a few so faded he had to tilt the page to the fire. Past the middle he began to notice how often he met his own house. By the end he had stopped reading and was counting. More than half the Keepers of the whole age had been of Sliochd Mhuirich.

He sat a while with that. Then he sent for a clerk and a knife and had the fact scraped from the preface. The clerk did as he was told. An unscraped copy survived all the same, and so Seumas Bàn is remembered chiefly for the one thing he wished forgotten.

That winter too he had the Hall list every rite that burned the handful or claimed the vein, so the council would know what it was keeping. The list named the Hall's own rite of the flame and the stone, the old ancestor-keeping, the dawn-hawk, the wood-watchers, the wolf-sworn, the sting-oath and both schools of the stag.

In spring he sent a cutter to check the hawk-keepers' measure at Seann Skell. The man came back wind-burned and angry, with a strip of hide in his hand. The hawk-keepers had sent him back unheard: the measure was the hawk's, they said, and the hawk was not the Keeper's, and they had written it down so he could not mistake it. It was the first rite to refuse the Hall's check outright. Seumas Bàn read the hide twice and put it in the chest with the roll.

After that the news came from every side, and none of it pleased him. The wolf-sworn built a hall round their stone at Caol leathan and swore oaths there that were no longer guards' oaths. The stone, they said, watches the whole island as the guard watches the road, and knows its rightful king. At the western Seann Skell the harbour court's sting-oath became a creed. One power stings the liar wherever he is, its keepers said, and that power is Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea; the scorpion is only his sign. From then on they swore over a cup marked with the scorpion, Manannan's Cup of Truth, which breaks in three at three lies spoken over it and is whole again at three truths. It was the first teaching on the island to call itself the only true one. Seann Vell, below Cuan shean, took up the sting-oath of its neighbours and carved a scorpion in yellow stone, the first made for worship; every one before it had been sworn on. The wood-watchers stopped bringing their disputes to the Hall and judged them under the trees. They held that the black beast has two natures, one that keeps and one that devours, and that the vein has the same two; the order of Macha later told it of the two horses that drew one chariot, the black Dubh Sainglenn and the grey Liath Macha.

By {{date:II-0221}} the one practice of the Hall had plainly split. It had become the threads later ages would name as the orders of An Creideamh Sean, the Old Faith, with the older reverence of the hearths standing apart beside them, and the belief of the Dia-thìrich was no longer one thing. The council, which had sat by hearth and house, began to sit in companies by rite, each on its own benches.

Seumas Bàn looked down the Hall on the first day they sat so, at the knots of people who would not look at one another, and said to the clerk beside him, loudly enough to be written down, that he kept the flame now for eight peoples, and each of them thought itself the only keeper.

Two customs stayed outside the companies. Pilgrims of every rite still walked to Allt an Àigh at the right quarter of the moon, the one custom the Hall's list set under no company, and in one season pilgrims of four rites repaved the path from Caol shean together. And the keeper of the springs at Muileann ruadh ruled that the sick of every rite should be bathed without being asked which. It was the last ruling of the age that every company accepted, and it came from the one office the Hall had made that had nothing to do with the coal.

## XIV. Of the Last Quiet Years and the Close of the Age

The last generations of the age live in the provincial rolls, which care about what was made and what was owed. The eastern Cnoc dhìreach wove the carriers' heavy cloth, and Muileann bhàn dyed it a pale hill-blue; the Keepers wore theirs undyed, and said so. A flood at Àth chiar took six townsfolk, but the granary on its rise fed the town through the winter. The south-eastern Seann Vell grew into a city on fish and silver and built the island's first stone quay. The beacon at Tobar dhearg was raised on a stone tower. At Muileann naomh a mill and a shrine shared a roof, and the Keeper's cutter found the measure kept and the flour good. Dùn thais built the stag's first temple of stone, a hall of the druid schools. Baile Mòr mhòr sent a member to the council, and the Four Houses seated him at the foot of the hall.

When drought burned the pastures of Achadh shean, grain came from Baile chaol and Cuan ruadh under the shared-meal law, and the rolls at Baile chaol record it repaid measure by measure, the first such debt repaid in full. At Baile ruadh a timber watch-tower was manned against island raiders, the only tower of war the age built. At Inis chrom the families of the drowned set an empty place for each of the lost at a yearly feast on the shore, and the custom spread north to Seann Warr. At Cnoc naomh the ancestors were still kept in the old way, with no hawk or wolf, sting or stag, and the Hall's list counted the old reverence as one company among the others.

Mòr nic Coinnich, granddaughter of Coinneach Bàn, received the stone cup from the Keeper Catrìona nic Mhuirich at Dùn ìseal, as Gilleasbuig Mòr had. Hers is the last name in the king-list of the Holy Age. At the close of the age the Keepers' roll held one hundred and forty names from Beathag Bhàn to Catrìona nic Mhuirich, and the king-list more than ninety from Goraidh Mòr to Mòr nic Coinnich. The Small-Burning Law had not changed by a word since Fionnlagh Dall filled the cup, and the cup still stood on the threshold of Talla na Lasrach.

Later chroniclers look back on those years with some unease. They call the years about {{year:II-0249}} the last age of even population, the last stretch of the island's history in which no family had more years of life than any other. Keepers aged at the pace of carriers, and every ruler died in their time. No one then dreamed this could be lost, so no one wrote it down as a blessing. It is told now because the tellers know what came after.

Thus ended the Holy Age, with the cup on the threshold and the sea at the island's back, uncrossed and unwished-for. The next age began on that sea. Crews from the western harbours brought word of a land across open water, empty of anyone like the Dia-thìrich, and none of them claimed to have been first. The report would be heard in the red hall at Cathair dhearg, and it would put to the Dia-thìrich a question the Keepers had never had to answer: who would choose to go. The Holy Age had counted every hand that held the right to the flame. The next would count those willing to leave it. That is the Age of Sundering, An Aois Scaraidh, and the chroniclers of Muileann chaol tell it in the book that follows.
