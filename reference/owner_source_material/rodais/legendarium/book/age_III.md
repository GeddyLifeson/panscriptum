# The Third Book: An Aois Scaraidh, the Age of Sundering

> *Roghnaich iadsan, agus dh'fhuirich sinne.*
> They chose, and we stayed.
>
> — written in the margin of the custody-book at Muileann dhearg, in a hand the keepers have never identified

## I. Of the Rumour of a Shore

In those days the kings of Rodos kept their hall at Cathair dhearg, and the custodians kept the vein under the mountain, and the Small-Burning Law measured out the black ore hearth by hearth as it had done since the early centuries of the Holy Age. Into that settled house there came a rumour. On {{date:III-0001}} word came back to the island of a land across open water, a coast where no one lived who resembled the Ròdaich. Who first carried it, and in what boat, the tellers at the hearth do not know; they shrug and go on, for the tale has never needed a first finder.

By winter it had reached the capital. In the red hall of Queen Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill, daughter of Mòr nic Coinnich, three crews from the western harbours told it one after another, and none of them claimed to have been first. The queen's scribe took down all three and wrote at the foot of the leaf that they agreed on the coast and on nothing else. Most of what Rodos has known of the land beyond has been of that kind.

Then came the Choosing, which the later chronicles call a vote. Perhaps it was; or perhaps the word was laid afterward over something older and looser, settled at hearths and on quays. Of one matter every telling is sure: those who went, went because they wished to. No lots were drawn, and no one was pressed. The Choosing is remembered on {{date:III-0003}}, though it was a season more than a day.

At Seann Skell, the western river port that would give the age its fleet, the harbour scribes opened a roll for those who meant to go. They entered it by household, never by single name, as though going were a thing a family did together or not at all. The roll was kept on boards in the harbour-house, and a later hand notes that entries were struck out as often as they were added.

## II. Of the Queen's Word and the Queen's Sister

The harbour scribe of Seann Skell learned that year to dread the sound of his own door. All summer they came in off the quay: fishers with tar on their hands, farm wives from up the river with a child on the hip. They stood before the boards and said a household's name, and he cut it. A week later, as often as not, the same man came back grey in the face and asked to have it struck out, and he struck it.

In the autumn of {{year:III-0005}} a rider came down from the capital with the queen's word, and the scribe read it out at the harbour-house door. The crown would send no one across the water, and the crown would keep no one back. The custody-book gives her words only in substance. The harbour songs of Seann Skell, younger and less careful, give them like this:

> *Cha chuir mi duine thairis, agus cha chùm mi duine air ais.*
> I will send no one over, and I will keep no one back.

Within that same season a woman came in out of the rain whom he knew before she had lowered her hood. It was Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, the queen's younger sister. She did not sit. She gave the name of her own household, spelled it out for him as though he might not know it, and watched his knife until the last stroke was cut. Then she thanked him and went back out into the weather.

He wrote nothing beside the entry. The record makes no comment, and the later chroniclers, who have made a great many, were none of them in the room.

Up at the vein-house above Muileann dhearg, keeper of Crom's stone and heir to the Keepers' Hall at Dùn ìseal where Brìde's flame was first bound, the custodians sat down with the roll and counted what the Choosing would cost them. Nearly one in five of the households holding the customary right to burn the ore had entered it. They counted twice, the custody-book says, because nobody believed the first figure. Fishers and farmers were going, and the queen's own sister, and many whose hands the vein had answered.

## III. Of the Building of the Ships

The island had no ship fit for the crossing. Her craft until then were sewn-plank coasters that never slept out of sight of land, and a fleet had to be made such as Rodian memory had never seen. The making took the better part of a generation.

In {{year:III-0008}}, on the river strand below Seann Skell, the shipwright Alasdair mac Iain, called Alasdair Saor, laid down the first deep-keeled hull ever built on Rodos. Its oaks came from the grove that had given Doire fhionn its name. They went down to the coast on ox-sleds, and the grove never grew again, so the village kept a name it no longer had a reason for. Alasdair bound the wrights of the strand into one fellowship, each apprentice sworn for seven years after the manner of the vein custodians. The custody-book calls it Comann nan Saor-luinge, the fellowship of the ship-wrights.

In {{year:III-0011}} Alasdair Saor stood on the headland above Ros àrsaidh, where the ships of Inis àrsaidh put to sea, his hands jammed under his arms for want of work. Below him the first finished hull came down out of the Abhainn naomh and met the open water. She rode higher than he had meant, but she rode well, and for the length of a long breath he let himself be glad of her.

Then a following sea came up under her quarter. He saw the stern lift and the bow go under, and the stern lift again, higher, and then she did not come back. She went over slowly, as a tired beast lies down. There were heads in the water, and then there were fewer. Eleven were drowned.

That night by a driftwood fire he drew the keel in the sand with a stick, rubbed it out, and drew it again. He changed its setting, and every later hull of the fleet was built to the changed pattern. What the change was lived in the hands of his wrights and nowhere else, and some three centuries later no one on the island could remember it.

The rest of Rodos laboured with him. Cuan shean laid long rope-walks down its harbour front and gave its hemp fields over wholly to cordage. Every weaving household in Siorrachd Baile àrsaidh was asked for a length of close-woven sail-cloth, reckoned on tally-sticks at the shire seat; the tallies survive, though how many households paid does not. On the eastern shore the old steersmen of Cuan ruadh, the only sailors on the island who had ever held a course by night, taught the fleet's pilots to keep a heading by the stars. Some of their lore was written down, and that part is lost.

In {{year:III-0015}} the households on the roll met at Seann Skell and chose their own leaders: Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, the queen's sister, and Fearchar mac Ruairidh, a custodian of the vein. How they chose, the custody-book does not tell. Seven years on, the vein-house ruled whether departing custodians might carry the ore. Each ship might take one sealed measure, as much as a single household might burn in a season under the Small-Burning Law. It is the only text in the custody-book addressed to people who were leaving, and it gives them no blessing. A later custodian wrote a couplet beside it, which is still said at the measured burning in some of the old houses:

> *Dòrlach airson an teallaich ùir,*
> *is an seann teallach fhathast blàth.*
> A handful for the new hearth,
> and the old hearth still warm.

Masons came from the capital to lengthen the west quay at Seann Skell with dressed stone, so that the deep hulls could lie alongside at low water. They set down the stones on which the later rite would be held, and they did not know it.

In {{year:III-0018}} the whole venture nearly foundered on a quarrel over how much of the island's stored harvest the ships might take. It ran a season and was settled, and nobody wrote how. One piece of it survives. At Àth shean the reeve of the granaries, Tormod mac Coinnich, barred the store-houses while the quarrel was open and set a watch on the doors, and the watch stood until the thing was done. Tormod held no office after that. When the barges moved at last, a tally-keeper on Drochaid Cathair dhearg notched them one by one as they passed down Abhainn dhomhain for the fleet, and made the count forty-one.

In the spring of that year the fleet lay complete at Seann Skell. The custody-book gives it as nineteen hulls. A later hand in the margin makes it twenty-one, and there the matter has rested ever since.

## IV. Of the Sunwise Turn

On the last night before the sailing every house along the Seann Skell waterfront set a lamp on its sill and kept it burning until morning. It is the first custom of the Sundering that anyone wrote down as a custom.

A boy of the harbour sat that night on the wall of the west quay with his knees drawn up to his chin and watched the lamps come out along the strand, one and then another, until the whole curve of the water was pricked with light. The April air was sharp. Below him the hulls lay against the new stones, so close that he could have dropped a pebble onto a deck. He smelt pitch and wet hemp and the sheep's fat the crews had rubbed into the blocks. Somewhere aboard, someone was singing under her breath, and someone else told her to hush.

He was to be one of the counters. The harbour scribe had given him a hazel stick and a knife, and told him: one notch for each hull as she passes, and no talking.

On {{date:III-0023}}, in the grey before sunrise, the volunteers departed.

They did not go straight down to the sea. The first hull came off the quay and swung, and the next came after her, and the boy saw that they meant to go round the river pool, sunwise, *diosal*, as you walk round a well or a new-built house for luck. He cut his notches. The hulls went by so near that he knew faces on them, and he did not look at the faces long, because he was counting.

At the foot of the pool the fleet straightened and went away down the Abhainn naomh toward open water, and there the harbour scribe's account of them ends, as if his pen had been lifted from the leaf. Of the crowd, or the weather, or who wept, the chronicles of the island say nothing. The harbour songs go a little further. They say the fleet stood out west into Manannan's sea, toward the Otherworld of the tellings, Tìr nan Òg and Magh Meall, and that a mist came down on the water behind the last hull and did not lift until it was gone.

Whether the boy's stick said nineteen or twenty-one, no one thought to ask him.

## V. Of the Two Landfalls and the White Sorrow

The years of Rodos are counted from that turn, though not at once. In {{year:III-0025}} Donnchadh mac Thormoid, scribe of the vein-house, began to number the years from the sunwise turn at Seann Skell and called them the years of the Diosal. The crown's scribes took up his count only a generation later. So an island that had reckoned its time by kings and harvests began, in one clerk's hand, to reckon it by an absence.

That same year a report of the fleet came into Seann Skell. By whom it came, or in what boat, the custody-book will not say; only that the harbour scribe copied it in a hand that shook. The copy came down to later keepers stained by water, and its end is gone.

It tells of two landfalls, two separate coasts reached, and the keepers have read in it the seed of two later peoples where one had sailed. Which hulls went to which shore, and whether storm or quarrel or purpose parted them, is dark. Sixteen centuries later Beathag nic Thòmais of Muileann chaol found three copies of the report, and they differed in the order of the coasts and in the number of hulls. She wrote all three out side by side and chose none of them, and the library has followed her ever since.

In {{year:III-0028}} Queen Beathag struck her sister Mòrag, and Mòrag's children, from the order of succession, as persons no longer on Rodos. The act stands in the roll without comment, between a grain ruling and the mending of a bridge. It is the last time the crown of Rodos wrote Mòrag's name, and it wrote it in the ordinary way.

What befell across the water after that, the island learned in pieces, if it learned it at all. The chronicle calls it the thinning. Cut off from the vein, whatever strength the crossing had asked of the departed faded in their children, and by the third generation abroad nothing was left that Rodos would have known for its own.

Then came the second silence. Some twelve years after the thinning is first written, men spoke of a second and smaller crossing: fewer ships, bound for a shore nobody named. Nothing that survives either bears it out or gives it the lie, and the keepers enter it and let it stand.

In {{year:III-0030}} the vein-house counted again the households that held the right to the ore, and set the loss down as one hand in five. Beside the figure Donnchadh mac Thormoid wrote that the vein itself answered no differently for those who stayed. Donnchadh never said whether he meant it as comfort or complaint, and men have argued over it since.

The next year the queen made a rite for the departed, and she kept it first for her sister. Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill came into her own hall in a gown of undyed wool, pale and rough as a fleece off the hill, and sat at the head of the board. Beside her a place was laid with no one in it: a cup, a trencher, a knife set straight. The meal that followed was neither feast nor fast. The court did not know what to do with its face, so it ate. The queen did not look at the empty place, and she did not look away from it either.

The court called the custom an Tùrsa Geal, the white sorrow, and the chronicle calls it the Mourning Custom, a rite that was never quite grief and never quite gladness. By the next generation it was kept in every harbour town on the island. Long afterward it would be taken up again, uneasily, for a very different leaving, and that belongs to its own book.

The year after, the island shaped itself around what was left. With part of the ore-bonded people gone the custodian class grew smaller and closer, and every later aristocracy of Rodos was cast in that mould. In {{year:III-0034}} the remaining custodian households were gathered into seven houses, each answerable to the vein-house for its share of the ore and of the measured burning: Clann Mhuirich, Clann Fhearchair, Clann Ìomhair, Clann Raghnaill, Clann Thormoid, Clann Choinnich and Clann Lachlainn. Mark those names. Until that day the right to the ore had been earned by apprenticeship; from it, the right passed by house.

Queen Beathag died at Cathair dhearg in the summer of that year, the last ruler of Rodos to have seen the fleet. Her son Raghnall mac Uilleim was crowned in the same hall.

## VI. Of the Empty Harbour

The net-menders of Seann Skell tell the years after the leaving on winter nights with the door shut, and they tell them so.

A ceremony grew up around the berth the ships had left from, the empty-harbour rite, and it is kept still, in a lesser form, on an anniversary no one can now fix to the day. By {{year:III-0037}} the harbour-master was writing his berths down at half their old use, because the fishing households that had crewed the fleet were simply gone, and the rope-walks at Cuan shean laid off hands for the first time. On the north-west coast the fishers of Ceann mhòr kept a watch from the headland for returning sails, household by household in turn. The watch lapsed within a generation. No sail came.

By {{year:III-0040}} Seann Skell had found a way to go on trading. The berth of the departure was given a new name, so that common trade could keep the old one without the memory clinging to it; the chronicle calls this a forgetting done on purpose. In the harbour rolls the berth became Cidhe an Diosail, the quay of the sunwise turn, and the name Cidhe an Iar passed to the new fish-wharf at the north end of the strand, where within a few years the fishers used it without a thought.

The tongue kept what the harbour put away. From {{year:III-0042}} the custody-book writes *roghnaich*, to choose, with nothing after it, meaning to have chosen the crossing, and the scribes of the next century write it so and never gloss it. By that same year a generation had grown up that had never seen the harbour with those ships in it, and for them the leaving was already history. A saying of the western shore claims to come from about this time:

> *Is fhada an cuan, is goirid a' chuimhne.*
> Long is the sea, and short is memory.

Alasdair Saor died at Seann Skell at the end of that summer. His last work was a coasting trader of the old sewn-plank kind, built for a fish merchant of Inis àrsaidh. Seventeen years later his fellowship came quietly apart, since no one wanted ocean-going hulls any more, and its craft was half forgotten before anyone thought to need it again. In a hard season after that, its patterns and keel-moulds were broken up and burned for firewood. The harbour-master wrote down the loss of the shed and said nothing of what had been in it.

In {{year:III-0047}} the seven houses drew lots at the vein-house for the galleries each would keep. The lot-stones went into a bag at Muileann dhearg, and for centuries the keepers there tipped them out into the hands of visitors. Doire fhionn, its oaks gone to the fleet, began to cut its woods by coppice, and the law spread to every shire with a *doire* in its name.

Every year on the eve of the empty-harbour rite the waterfront of Seann Skell kept the night of lamps. The children were told the lamps were for ships coming home, and the priests let them believe it. The children's verse is still known on the west coast:

> *Solas air an uinneig,*
> *solas gus an tig iad dhachaigh.*
> A light on the window,
> a light till they come home.

Donnchadh mac Thormoid died at Muileann dhearg in {{year:III-0050}}. His last entry is a list of the ore burned that season, household by household, in the same hand as his first. Sixteen years on, in {{year:III-0051}}, King Raghnall died after a long reign in which little happened, and his son Ìomhar mac Raghnaill was crowned. The custody-book gives Raghnall a single line. After the shaking of its first years the age seems to have wanted no more than that.

## VII. Of the Hawk and the Firlot

The centuries that followed were quiet as a well-kept house is quiet: much afoot, and most of it small. The silver of Muileann chiar, mined since the Holy Age, was beaten into neck-rings for the heads of the seven houses, and the smiths' fellowship of the seam shut its craft up within four families. Inis mhòr built barges for its grain, to go down the Abhainn ghorm and along the coast, sewn-plank boats of the old kind that never slept out of sight of land. The road-house of Am Buabhall Fortanach burned to its footings, and its keeper, Eilidh nic Chaluim, raised it again in stone. The account of her rebuilding already speaks of the jugged buffalo it was known for.

Beathag's line ended in {{year:III-0057}} with King Ìomhar, who died childless. The heads of the seven houses were summoned to the capital, and in {{year:III-0058}} they chose Fearchar mac Lachlainn of Baile Mòr dhomhain, of Clann Fhearchair. He was the first king in the record crowned by the choosing of the custodian houses. His son Fearchar Donn followed him, and no one gainsaid it.

In those years the orders of the Old Faith began to take their shapes. At Cnoc bheag the dawn-hawk rite of the Holy Age was still kept, and there in {{year:III-0062}} the priestess Gormshuil nic Artair taught that the hawk of the rite was Mòd, the Hawk of Acaill, as old as Fionntan mac Bòchra, the oldest of men, and remembering all things with him; and that the vein was the Hungry Hawk, who must be fed the measured handful and no more. A hawk overfed will not hunt, she said, and a hawk starved will turn on the hand. Her followers gathered the old rite into the first house of Òrd an t-Seabhaig, the order of the Hawk of Acaill, and her pupils raised a second at Ros gheal. After her death her sayings were written down as Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, the Book of the Hawk. The copyist, Ùisdean mac Pàdraig, marked every saying he had not heard from her own mouth, and the marked sayings outnumber the rest. The first in the book is one he heard:

> *Beathaich an seabhag, ach na sàsaich e.*
> Feed the hawk, but do not glut him.

In {{year:III-0068}} Clann Ìomhair walled Dùn dhearg in stone, the first walls on the island that any custodian house had raised. That same year Sìleas nic Fhearchair, crowned after her father, fixed one measure for grain across the whole island, the firlot of Cathair dhearg, so that barter between shires need no longer begin with a quarrel over the size of a basket. She sent firlots of oak bound with iron to every shire seat. Later she set a tally-house in each royal granary, where every trade of grain for cloth, salt or labour was cut on paired sticks. It was the first account of trade to run the length of the island, and not one coin passed through it. She reigned more than half a century, and her son Uisdean mac Dhùghaill followed her in {{year:III-0077}}.

It was a good time to be a carter. Near Imrich nam Pantar the herders began clearing the panther roads on the days the great cats crossed, driving their flocks aside beforehand, and those crossing days were the first thing the southern shires ever agreed on in writing. At An Leòmhann Buidhe on the northern mining road the ore-carters were served oatcakes fried in linseed oil, and fire water, and the keeper was fined for watering the fire water. Ros fhionn laid out salt pans at the brine springs on its river flats, and its salt, carted inland over Drochaid Ros fhionn, became the common measure of worth in the north-east.

## VIII. Of the Forbidden Question, the Stone and the Two Horses

In {{year:III-0074}} Coinneach mac Mhàrtainn, a druid of the schools of Flidais, lady of the deer, whose teaching the old rolls call Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, founded a house of disputation at Dùn thais. There the schools' old question, *Carson an làmh seo?*, why the vein answers some hands and not others, was argued by rule, and pupils came to hear it from as far as Caol bheag.

Ten years later Coinneach stood up in his own hall to ask the thing he had been carrying.

The benches were full, and the air was thick with wet wool and tallow smoke. Coinneach was old enough by then to know which questions were safe. He had turned this one over on the road, to the knock of his stick, and it had grown no safer for the turning.

"We ask why the vein answers this hand and not that one," he said. "Then let us ask it of the hands that went. Were they the hands it answered best? And if they were, does it answer fewer now?"

No one spoke. A bench creaked. Somewhere at the back a young man laughed, once, and then thought better of it. Coinneach waited, as the rule of disputation bade him, for someone to answer. When no one did, he sat down.

The answer came from the seven houses. They forbade the question at their own tables. The record notes that until then they had forbidden only deeds, and that this was the first question they forbade.

The skills of the fleet were slipping already. In {{year:III-0078}} a merchant of Inis àrsaidh paid for a deep-keeled hull, but the wrights could no longer remember how Alasdair Saor had scarfed his keels, and she listed so badly at her launch that she was broken up for timber.

Aonghas mac Uisdein, crowned in {{year:III-0084}}, was the first king who learned his letters as a child, and in his reign two more orders took their shapes. In {{year:III-0086}} Dòmhnall mac Ailein, a net-maker of Inis thais, where the wolf-sworn's stone stood, gave that stone its name and its order. He called it Clach, the Stone, and said it was Lia Fàil, which cries out under the rightful king; and he called its sworn guards the Fianna, who keep the island as a wolf keeps its range. So began Òrd na Cloiche, the order of the Stone, and the tales of Fionn and his band, which every hearth on the island knew, the Fianna took for their own. When the Hawk and the Stone both laid claim to the harbour shrine at Caol leathan, the king's reeve divided its days between them as the Holy Age had divided the sacred woods, and the sharing held.

In {{year:III-0089}} Aonghas himself took up the wood-watchers' rite of Macha, which keeps the two horses of one chariot, foaled on one night: Liath Macha the grey and Dubh Sainglenn the black. Two horses, one yoke, the rite says; dark and light are two powers in balance, and the vein belongs to both. The court followed its king, and Òrd Mhacha, the order of Macha, became the order of Cathair dhearg. From Aonghas on, the kings of Rodos were made by Macha's rite.

In {{year:III-0094}} Eilidh nic Iain of Doire chiar, called Eilidh Dhubh, raised again the Holy Age's rival teaching, that the vein answers effort and cares nothing for birth, and her tracts were read aloud at harvest gatherings across the north-east. The vein-house ordered every copy brought to Muileann dhearg and burned, and some hundred were given up. Eilidh herself was never punished. She died at Doire chiar, still teaching.

Aonghas died that winter and was the first king buried by the rite of Macha. His son Iain reigned a short while, and Iain's son Tormod mac Iain, called Tormod Bàn for his white hair, was sworn to without dispute. On the central mining road the ox-teams of the mountain now waited out the snow at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, whose keeper, Iain mac Mhurchaidh, built them a second byre and kept a book of the teams that lodged.

## IX. Of the War of the Three Claimants

In {{year:III-0102}} both sons of King Tormod Bàn were drowned when a ferry overturned crossing Abhainn fhionn in spate. The king had no other child. Thirteen years later he died at Cathair dhearg without naming an heir, and three claims were laid before the houses at once. Catrìona nic Aonghais of Inis thais was the dead king's niece. Dùghall Garbh of Baile Mòr dhomhain was his half-brother. Raghnall Ruadh was head of Clann Raghnaill and master of the vein-house. The seven houses split among the three, and in {{year:III-0104}} the War of the Three Claimants began.

So small a cause, and so long a war: the keepers have wondered at it. Perhaps, having held the ore by inheritance for five hundred years, each house had come to believe that a crown could be inherited the same way.

Seann Warr, the port nearest the capital on the west, shut its gates on all three and declared for none. It was besieged twice and taken by neither. At the ford of Àth shean, the northern town of that name and no kin to the granary town, Dùghall Garbh's host broke Raghnall Ruadh's, and the custody-book counts some three hundred dead, most of them drowned in the ford. Raghnall's men burned Doire mhin and its granary as they fell back, and the villagers wintered at Cathair mhòr on the charity of Macha's priests. Catrìona laid siege to Cathair dhomhain, which had declared for Dùghall, and took it after forty days, when the dead of the siege lines had fouled its wells; and she let the garrison go free on their oath.

Snow lay on the mountain on {{date:III-0109}}, when Raghnall Ruadh came out into the yard of the vein-house.

The carriers were waiting there with their baskets and their sealed measures, as carriers had waited every season since before there were kings, stamping against the cold. Beyond the gate the road wound down toward the shires, white and empty.

Raghnall had a list in his hand. His clerk had drawn it up the night before: the shires that stood against him, and every hearth and temple in them that took the handful. It was a long list. He read it through once more in the thin light, and then he gave it to the master of the store.

"None of these," he said.

The master of the store looked at the list, and then at him. Since the Small-Burning Law began it had measured the ore out; no one in that yard had ever seen it used to hold the ore back. "Lord," the man said, and got no further.

"None of these," said Raghnall again, and went in out of the wind.

Down in the shires that stood against him, hearths that had been lit with the ore for generations burned low, and grey, and then went cold. The chronicle says the deed turned more against him than any battle. A verse from the cold shires survives, with no name to it:

> *Cha do chaill sinn blàr, ach chaill sinn an teine.*
> We did not lose a battle, but we lost the fire.

Catrìona and Dùghall joined their hosts against him, and on {{date:III-0110}}, in the dead of another winter, they met Raghnall below Muileann chrom. He was killed on the field. The next day his own custodians opened the vein-house, and the carriers went down the white road with their baskets full. Dùghall died of his wounds at Baile Mòr dhomhain in {{year:III-0111}}, and his followers went over to Catrìona without another blow. She was crowned at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0112}}, and was called ever after Catrìona Bhuadhach, Catrìona the Victorious. The war had lasted some ten years.

Three years later she did the deed she is remembered for. With the vein-house she fixed it in law that no claimant, house or king might withhold the measured handful from any lawful hearth. This was the ruling of the hearth, the first law in the record written to bind the custodians themselves. To Seann Warr she granted the right to keep its own gates and tolls, in memory of its standing aside, and had the grant cut over the landward gate. When she died in {{year:III-0116}} the houses swore to her son Lachlann mac Iain in a single day, and the record remarks on it.

In {{year:III-0117}} Seònaid nic Coinnich, a clerk of the late queen's household, wrote the history of the war from the custody-book and from the mouths of those who had fought. It is the oldest history on Rodos told as one tale from end to end, and every later history of the island descends from it, this book among them. That same year a spotted fever killed some three hundred at Inis chrom. The Fianna of the Stone nursed the sick, and many of them died of it, and after that the town was the Stone's for good.

## X. Of Manannan's Mist, and the Crown Joined to the Vein

So the fishing families of the west coast tell the coming of Manannan's order, as an old woman of Ros bheag once told it to a keeper of Muileann chaol over her nets.

In {{year:III-0120}} a preacher named Mànas mac Ruairidh stood up at Seann Skell, of all places, where the sting-creed of the Holy Age had first called itself the only true faith, and proclaimed its one power: Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea, whose Cup of Truth breaks at a lie spoken over it and bears the yellow scorpion for its sign. Manannan, he said, had shut the western sea behind the fleet with his mist, and hid the island in it from every ship beyond; and what the fleet had done must never be tried again. Those who heard him on the quay were the first congregation of Òrd Mhanannain, the order of Manannan. A month later, in the autumn of {{year:III-0121}}, Lachlann mac Iain died at Cathair dhearg and his daughter Anna was crowned. The record calls his reign quiet, and means it as praise.

The teaching went down the west coast with the fishing families. At Cuan shean the rope-makers, whose walks had been laid out for the fleet, were among the first to take it up. In {{year:III-0123}} Manannan's priests at Ros bheag laid it down that no boat of the faithful might go beyond sight of the headlands. The west coast kept that rule for centuries, and there the steering of boats by the stars, half lost already, was lost entirely. Of all the orders of the Old Faith the keepers have been least easy with Manannan's, the one order whose teaching stands on the Sundering; and what it teaches is that the island must never again do what the fleet did.

In {{year:III-0127}} the vein-house and the crown's scribes found that their counts of years since the Setting-Out disagreed. They settled the difference by agreement, and by the same agreement fixed the day of the empty-harbour rite, which the two counts had placed differently. Later scribes gave up pretending it had been fixed by evidence. Every year in this book stands on that bargain between two offices, and the reader should know it.

Eilidh Bhàn, fifth ruler of the house of Catrìona, was crowned in {{year:III-0129}}, and the record of her long reign is nearly all harvests. The north-west had no share in them. From {{year:III-0131}} boats out of the small islands off that coast fell on the coasting traders and took their fish and grain. The sea-thieves were Ròdaich of the north-west shore, and the harbour rolls give some of their names. Baile chrom reported half its houses empty, and the crown remitted its grain dues. In {{year:III-0133}} a force sent by Eilidh Bhàn landed on Eilean dhubh and burned the thieves' boats on the strand, and their leader, Uilleam Ruadh, was hanged at Cathair dhearg. It did not save the coast. In {{year:III-0134}} a run of failed harvests brought a dearth to the north-west shore that the shared-meal custom could not carry. Families went inland and east, and the north-west stayed thinly held until the age was out.

In {{year:III-0135}} the queen's scribes recopied the king-list from its oldest surviving roll and added the rulers since the Sundering. The new copy opens the present age with the Choosing, ahead of the fleet, and this book keeps that order. Twelve years after, Eilidh Bhàn died childless, and the seven houses met to choose; this time they met at the vein-house, and the capital waited on their word. They named Dòmhnall Ruadh of Clann Raghnaill, a descendant of the claimant killed at Muileann chrom, and he kept the mastery of the vein-house together with the crown. In {{year:III-0138}} his reeves wrote into the custody-book that the ruler of Rodos is also master of the vein-house. From then on the heads of the seven houses sat as the king's council, and custodians and court were one body. What Raghnall Ruadh had seized in war, his descendant now held by law. The ruling of the hearth still bound the custodians; the custodians, now, were the crown. Dòmhnall Ruadh was buried at Muileann dhearg, under the mountain, far from the capital.

The Hawk split in {{year:III-0140}} over whether Mòd's measure might ever be raised. Those who held it fixed for ever kept the name of the Hungry Hawk and the house at Cnoc bheag. Those who said a larger flock might be fed a larger handful were called the Fed Hawk, and they went to Seann Skell on the north coast, the other town of that name. In {{year:III-0143}} they petitioned the council to raise the handful by a quarter for the northern hearths. The council refused. It was the first recorded asking to change the Small-Burning Law, and the custody-book enters the Fed Hawk's teaching as an error, at length.

## XI. Of the Stair under the Hill

In {{year:III-0144}} a second tremor in the hills of Cill ghlas brought down a gallery of the vein and killed fourteen labourers. The vein-house closed the gallery and set a stone at its mouth with the fourteen names cut on it. The last shepherds left the high pastures of Doire uaine. They said it was for the grazing, and every one of them who was asked also spoke of the Marbh-bheò.

In {{year:III-0149}}, north of the capital, a quarry face gave way under the hammers and opened on darkness.

Air came up out of the hole, cold as well-water, and it smelt of nothing at all, which was worse than any smell. One of the quarrymen held a lamp in at arm's length, and they saw steps: a stair cut in the living rock, each tread worn hollow at the middle, going down and down past where the light would reach. Someone said the name before anyone else could, Toll-dubh, the shaft of the old tellings, long lost. After that nobody said anything.

One of them went down. His fellows watched his lamp go, turning and dropping and dwindling, until the rock swallowed it. They waited. One who was there said afterward that they waited long enough to be ashamed of how glad they were when the light came back.

The man climbed out, sat down on the spoil heap and said nothing, and whatever he may have said later went unwritten. The custodians came down from the vein-house and had the entrance walled up.

Twenty years later Ailean mac Dhùghaill of the druid school at Dùn thais measured the ruin of Làrach an Teampaill and wrote that its altar faced the central mountains, with its back to the sea. Who had set it so, he would not guess. By {{year:III-0152}} he had finished his tables of the three old ruins, Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, Làrach an Dùin and Làrach an Teampaill, with their measures and their stone and the lie of each, and he concluded that he could not say whether one people had built all three. Because of his tables no one after him could say either.

Mòrag Ruadh of Clann Raghnaill was crowned in {{year:III-0153}}, the first ruler whose household accounts survive whole. On {{date:III-0156}}, in the dark of the year, the vein-house marked the thousandth year of its count with a single measured burning of the ore on Cidhe an Diosail at Seann Skell, and the queen stood by. The flame burned the colour of the vein, the record says, and the crowd went home without a word.

## XII. Of Things Forgotten, and the Brothers' War

After the thousandth year the island began, plainly, to lose what it knew.

A sickness that closed the throat, the swallowing cough, broke out at Muileann dhomhain in {{year:III-0158}} and killed nearly a third of the town. When it reached Seann Bhral the townsfolk shut their own harbour and kept the shared-meal custom by leaving food at the gate. Queen Beathag Fhionn, daughter of Mòrag Ruadh, closed the coast road between the two towns and set reeves at the fords to turn travellers back, and the cough went no further west. Her son Ailean followed her, the first of four kings of that name in the house of Raghnall.

The dyers of Baile ghorm had made a fast blue from a marsh plant and a mordant of their own, and it was the feast-day dress of the seven houses. In {{year:III-0164}} the last dyer who knew the mordant died without apprentices, having quarrelled with both of hers, and the blue that came after was the common blue of the rest of the island.

The Abhainn bheag silted behind a shifting bar below Ros fhionn, and the salt-masters moved their trade to Caol gharbh on the coast. The carts stopped crossing Drochaid Ros fhionn, and the bridge-keeper's office was done away with. Fishers of the thinned north-west saw the Sea-Watcher, Uilebheist na Mara, close enough to foul their nets, and the few boats left there drew further in.

In {{year:III-0171}} Raghnall mac Chaluim of the druid school at Dùn thais set out a table showing that the heads of the custodian houses lived, on the average, a few years longer than the tenants of their lands, and he asked whether the vein answers the hand that holds it. The record notes the finding uneasily and gives it no answer. The council forbade the table to be copied and shut the house at Dùn thais for a generation. Raghnall's own copy went into the vein-house and was entered in its inventory.

In {{year:III-0176}} King Ailean mac Raghnaill died and left two sons by different mothers, Iain Ciar and Alasdair Bàn, each of whom had been told he was the heir. Four years after, Alasdair Bàn's men seized Cnoc naomh and Iain Ciar took it back. The houses split four to three, and the Brothers' War, Cogadh nam Bràithrean, took its name from that fight. Alasdair's ships burned the waterfront of Ceann mhin, which held for his brother; the fire took the rope stores and the granary, and the town had to beg its bread from Cathair mhòr. In {{year:III-0179}} Iain Ciar beat Alasdair below Muileann ghlas, took him alive, and let his captains go home. The field comes into the record again two ages on.

The peace was made in {{year:III-0180}} at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, the road-house where the ox-teams waited out the snow, on neutral ground between the brothers' lands.

The keeper of the house had been given a day's warning and had spent it scrubbing. She set the long board in the common room with a bench on either side, the benches the same length to a finger's breadth, for she had heard that great men quarrel over such things, and she put the same bread and the same ale at each end. Then she stood by the fire and tried to look as though she were not listening.

Iain Ciar came in first and sat. Alasdair Bàn came after him, stiff as a man is who has lately been a prisoner, and sat down across the board. For a long while neither spoke, and the keeper could hear the oxen shifting in the byre. What passed between them she never told. Alasdair gave up his claim and took the lordship of Seann Chwen for his life. Iain Ciar was crowned. When they rose, both loaves had been broken and neither eaten.

Alasdair kept the peace he had made and died at Seann Chwen. Because he had seen Ceann mhin beg its bread, Iain Ciar ordered every shire seat to keep a year's grain against the next war. The law held while he lived and lapsed within a few years of his death, as he had foreseen in a note in his own hand. His clerk Pàdraig mac Ruairidh wrote the account of the war. It praises the king at every turn, and it is the only account that survives, which later readers have found inconvenient.

The road-houses come back into the record here. In {{year:III-0184}} the crown chartered the seven great road-houses of the mining circuit and made their keepers answerable for the roads between them, and the keepers met once a year at An Sligeanach Fad' às to settle their accounts. At An Taigh-seinnse Reòta on the bare northern road a party of reeves was snowed in for a whole season and lived on pickled spinach and fire milk. Their complaint to the charter-roll is set down in full.

In {{year:III-0188}} Seumas mac Iain of Seann Skell tried to build a deep-keeled hull again from what the strand still remembered of the Sundering fellowship's lore. She filled and sank at her launch, and Manannan's priests preached on her for a year.

Thirty years later Iseabail nic Lachlainn, a folk-collector of the royal household, came to Cuan shean for the songs of the rope-makers. She found them at the end of a walk that had lost half its length to barley, going backward in a line as they twisted the strands and singing to keep the pace. Most of it was the common stuff of work-songs. Then the leader struck up a song she had never heard, and the others fell in behind him, and she caught *druim* and *aisne*, keel and rib, and began to write fast.

Afterward she asked what the song was about. Rope, they told her. And the words further on? They looked at one another. Nobody knew. They were the words of the song.

It names the parts of a deep keel. It is the only record of the fleet's craft that survives in any shape. Its first lines can still be understood:

> *Druim fon aisne, aisne fon chlàr,*
> *clàr ri clàr is an cuan fodhpa.*
> Keel under rib, rib under plank,
> plank to plank and the sea beneath them.

After that the song runs on in words no one can read. The rope-makers who kept it were of Manannan's order by then, and they sang it anyway.

## XIII. Of the Last Lamps

The last four centuries of the age are the longest stretch in the record and have the least to tell, and the keepers will not dress them up.

The order of the Stone split over whether the dead, who are Donn's, might be prayed for at Clach, and its two houses, at Inis thais and Baile gheal, were not reconciled in this age. Catrìona Bheag of Clann Raghnaill was crowned in {{year:III-0195}}, the first queen in more than three centuries. The record notes that she was small, and that no one said so twice. In {{year:III-0197}} she had the heads of the seven houses swear to the ruling of the hearth in her presence, the first renewal of that oath since Catrìona Bhuadhach. Clann Choinnich swore last.

More was forgotten. The potters of Muileann gheal made a grey-green glaze no other kiln could match, and the custodian houses bought it for their tables. The secret stayed in one family, and in {{year:III-0203}} the last of them died without passing it on. The smiths of Dùn dhearg lost the last man who knew how their layered steel was folded, and afterward the blades of Dùn dhearg were ordinary. In the east, charcoal-burners found again the eastern Toll-dubh of the old tellings, with the same cut stair as the first. The custodians walled that one up as well, and the scribe cross-referenced the two entries.

At Àth shean the granary found that its firlot had worn smaller than the standard of Cathair dhearg, and had been so for generations. The reeve corrected the accounts as far back as anyone could reckon.

Sìleas Òg, named for the queen of the firlot, was crowned in {{year:III-0205}}, the first ruler to visit every shire seat. She ordered a new count of the households holding the right to the ore. It came out smaller than any before it, for the right had narrowed into fewer and larger households, and she had it entered without comment. In {{year:III-0208}} Clann Choinnich asked leave to burn the ore beyond the measured handful to smelt silver at Muileann chiar, arguing that it burned hotter than charcoal. The council refused. Until then the ore had been burned only for worship, and this was the first asking to burn it for work. The keepers know where that asking leads in the next age, and here they only set it down.

Ailean the Fourth had the shortest reign in the king-list. His sister Mòrag nic Ailein, who followed him, reigned forty years, and her entries record nothing but good harvests and the rite. In {{year:III-0212}} the priests of Manannan met in synod at Seann Skell, restated the rule of sight-of-land, and added a prayer to be said at the foot of the river pool:

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Manannan, keep the sea shut.

The library that keeps this record begins here. In {{year:III-0217}} Beathag nic Thòmais, a mill-owner's widow of Muileann chaol, began buying up old copies of the custody-book and the harbour rolls from houses that no longer read them, and stacked them in a loft over her mill. Soon a mill in the same town began making paper from rags, the first on Rodos, and the custody-book was copied onto paper for the first time. Among her papers were the three copies of the landfall report. She died in {{year:III-0221}} and left the loft to the town on condition that nothing in it be burned. The town kept that condition, and very little else of her wishes.

The crown sent a reeve to list the households of the north-west coast between Baile chrom and Ceann mhòr, and he counted fewer households than harbour berths. Tormod mac Ruairidh, crowned in {{year:III-0222}}, reigned more than fifty years. In {{year:III-0223}} Manannan's priests at Seann Skell began saying their prayer to keep the sea shut on the same day as the empty-harbour rite, so that the two were kept together on Cidhe an Diosail. Neither side was pleased.

In {{year:III-0228}} a visiting scribe found the rite at Seann Skell kept by the harbour priests and a few old families, and wrote that none of them could say on which day the fleet had truly sailed. The house of disputation at Dùn thais, shut and opened more than once since the table of years, opened again in {{year:III-0230}} on condition that the question of longer lives was not raised. The scholars agreed, and argued about the vein instead.

Tormod's daughter Gormshuil nic Thormoid, crowned in {{year:III-0231}}, rode a progress round every shire seat. Her clerks wrote down each shire's grievances, and she answered none of them in writing. On the eastern road An Taigh-òsta Grianach fed her company on pan-fried spinach and black wine, and its keeper, Peigi nic Dhonnchaidh, was let off her road dues for a year. It is the last warm entry of the age. In {{year:III-0233}} Clann Choinnich asked again to burn the ore for the silver smelt, with three of the seven houses behind it now, and Gormshuil's council refused by one voice. The record names the member who changed his vote and keeps his reason to itself. In {{year:III-0235}} the vein-house counted the households of the right for the last time in this age. They were fewer than ever, and larger, and three of the seven houses held more than half the right between them.

In {{year:III-0236}} a worked timber came ashore at Ceann mhòr on the empty north-west coast, from beyond Manannan's mist, as the fishers said. It was longer than any tree on the island and fastened with nails of a make no smith on Rodos knew. They carted it to Seann Skell and showed it to Manannan's priests, and the priests burned it.

By then fewer and fewer houses on the Seann Skell waterfront kept the night of lamps, and at last none did. An old woman set a lamp in a window of the harbour-house, and the scribe wrote that it was she who kept it, and not the town.

On {{date:III-0238}} the harbour scribe of Seann Skell went down to Cidhe an Diosail with his board and his pen for the empty-harbour rite. The dressed stones of the old quay were green at the foot, where the river had been working at them for eighteen centuries. He counted the people standing on them, and it did not take him long. There were eleven. When the rite and Manannan's prayer were said and the people had gone home, he looked at the sky and at the water, and wrote down the number, the weather and the state of the tide. He sat a while with the pen in his hand before he closed the board.

Clann Choinnich did not bring its silver petition to the council again, for the member who had turned the vote had died. The petition lay in the vein-house unanswered, and the record of the age ends with the Small-Burning Law still kept.

In those last winters the priests of Manannan at Seann Skell wrote that the mist on the western sea, which had hidden Rodos from every ship beyond it since the fleet sailed into it, lay thinner each year. On {{date:III-0239a}} they set it down in so many words, and they said their prayer at every tide.

A fisher of the west coast, putting out before dawn on one of those still mornings, rested on her oars off the headland where the rule of sight-of-land told her to turn. All her life the grey had stood out there on the water like a wall of wet fleece, and the sea ran into it and did not come out. That morning she could see through it. Beyond the grey lay a line, hard and dark and level, ruled across the edge of the world: the open sea, going on. She looked at it for a long time. Then she turned for home as the rule said, and from the quay she heard the priests at their prayer. Manannan, keep the sea shut.

Eighteen centuries after a queen at Cathair dhearg said she would keep no one back, Rodos had kept the vein and kept the law, and had let go of nearly everything else the fleet had meant: the keel and the stars, the far coast, all but eleven keepers of the rite. The houses were fewer and richer. The north-west was nearly empty. For two hundred years the prayer to keep the sea shut had been said, and no one had tried the sea. Then the mist lifted, and sails came in over that water from the far side, and they were no sails of Rodos. The people under them had crossed the water the Ròdaich once crossed. Whether any of them came down from those who chose to go, no one on Rodos knew then, and no one knows now. With their coming begins the Age of Strangers, and the next book tells it.
