# The Third Book: An Aois Scaraidh, the Age of Sundering

> *Roghnaich iadsan, agus dh'fhuirich sinne.*
> They chose, and we stayed.
>
> — written in the margin of the custody-book at Muileann dhearg, in a hand the keepers have never identified

## I. Of the Land Across the Water

The Third Age begins with a rumour, and the chroniclers of Rodos have never been able to do better than that. On {{date:III-0001}} word came back to the island of a land across open water, a coast unpeopled by anyone who resembled the Ròdaich. Who brought that word first, in what boat, after how many days at sea, no record says. The kings kept their hall at Cathair dhearg, the custodians kept the vein under the mountain, and the Small-Burning Law measured out the black ore hearth by hearth as it had since the early centuries of the Holy Age. Into that order came a story about a shore.

By the winter the story had reached the capital. In the hall of Queen Beathag nic Dhòmhnaill, daughter of Mòr nic Coinnich, at Cathair dhearg three crews from the western harbours told it, one after another, and none of them claimed to have been first. The queen's scribe took down all three accounts and noted at the foot of them that they agreed on the coast and on nothing else. It is a fair summary of most of what Rodos has ever known about the land beyond.

What followed is called the Choosing, and the later chronicles call it a vote. Whether it was one, or a word laid over something older and looser, no one who wrote it down was there to see. All the accounts agree on one thing. Those who went, went because they wished to. No lots were drawn, and no conscription is recorded. The Choosing is remembered on {{date:III-0003}}, though it was a season of choosing more than a day, and the next thing the record shows is how unsettled it was.

At Seann Skell, the western river port that would give the age its fleet, the scribes opened a roll for those who meant to go. They entered it by household and not by name, as though going were something a family did together or not at all. The roll was kept on boards in the harbour-house, and a later hand notes that entries were struck out as often as they were added.

The crown decided for no one. In the autumn of {{year:III-0005}} Queen Beathag declared that she would neither send anyone across the water nor keep anyone back. The custody-book gives her words only in substance; the harbour songs of Seann Skell, younger and less careful, give them like this:

> *Cha chuir mi duine thairis, agus cha chùm mi duine air ais.*
> I will send no one over, and I will keep no one back.

Within that same season her younger sister, Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, entered her own household on the roll at Seann Skell. The record makes no comment. Later chroniclers have made a great many, none of them evidence.

Up at the vein-house above Muileann dhearg, heir to the Keepers' Hall at Dùn ìseal, the custodians counted what the Choosing would cost them. They reckoned which of the households holding the customary right to burn the ore had entered the roll, and put it at nearly one household in five. The custody-book adds that the reckoning was made twice, because the first figure was not believed. The people going were fishers and farmers and the queen's own sister, and many of them were also people whose hands the vein had answered.

## II. Of the Building of the Ships

The island had no ships fit for the crossing. The craft of Rodos until then were sewn-plank coasters that never slept out of sight of land. A fleet had to be made for the first time in Rodian memory, and the making took the better part of a generation.

At the centre of it was the shipwright Alasdair mac Iain, called Alasdair Saor. In {{year:III-0008}}, on the river strand below Seann Skell, he laid down the first deep-keeled hull ever built on Rodos.

The keels came from the oak grove that had given Doire fhionn its name. It was felled and carried down to the coast by ox-sled, and it never regrew, and the village kept a name it no longer had a reason for.

Alasdair Saor bound the wrights of the Seann Skell strand into a single fellowship, each apprentice bound for seven years in the manner of the vein custodians. The custody-book calls it Comann nan Saor-luinge, the fellowship of the ship-wrights. The fleet cost lives before it sailed. In {{year:III-0011}} the first finished hull was taken down the Abhainn naomh and out past Ros àrsaidh, where the ships of Inis àrsaidh put to sea, to be tried in open water, and it foundered in a following sea, and eleven were drowned. Alasdair changed the setting of the keel, and every later hull was built to the changed pattern. What the change was, the record does not say. Some three centuries later nobody on the island could remember it.

The rest of Rodos went to work with him. Cuan shean laid out long rope-walks along its harbour front and turned its hemp fields over wholly to cordage. Every weaving household in Siorrachd Baile àrsaidh was asked for a length of close-woven sail-cloth, reckoned on tally-sticks at the shire seat; the tallies survive, and how many households paid does not. On the eastern coast the old steersmen of Cuan ruadh, the only sailors on the island who had ever kept a course by night, taught the fleet's pilots to hold a heading by the stars. Their lore was written down only in part, and that part is lost.

In {{year:III-0015}} the households on the roll met at Seann Skell and chose their own leaders: Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill, the queen's sister, and Fearchar mac Ruairidh, a custodian of the vein. The custody-book records the choice and nothing of how it was made. Seven years later the vein-house ruled on whether departing custodians might carry the ore. Each ship might take one sealed measure, no more than a single household might burn in a season under the Small-Burning Law. It is the only text in the custody-book addressed to people who were leaving, and it gives no blessing. A later custodian wrote a couplet beside it, which is still said at the measured burning in some of the old houses:

> *Dòrlach airson an teallaich ùir,*
> *is an seann teallach fhathast blàth.*
> A handful for the new hearth,
> and the old hearth still warm.

Masons came from the capital to lengthen the west quay at Seann Skell with dressed stone, so that the deep hulls could lie alongside at low water. No one then knew that the later rite would be held on those stones.

In {{year:III-0018}}, with the Diosal little more than a year off, the whole enterprise nearly came apart over grain: how much of the island's stored harvest might the departing ships take? The chronicle says the dispute was resolved within a season, and that how it was resolved is unrecorded. One piece of it survives. At Àth shean, while the quarrel was still open, the reeve of the granaries, Tormod mac Coinnich, barred the store-houses and set a watch on them until the matter was settled. Tormod is not recorded as holding office afterward. When it was settled, a tally-keeper at Drochaid Cathair dhearg counted forty-one grain barges passing down Abhainn dhomhain for the fleet.

By the spring of that year the fleet lay complete at Seann Skell. The custody-book gives it as nineteen hulls. A later hand in the margin corrects this to twenty-one, and no one since has been able to settle which is right.

## III. Of the Sunwise Turn

On the last night before the sailing every house along the Seann Skell waterfront set a lamp on its sill and kept it burning until morning. It is the first custom of the Sundering recorded as a custom.

On {{date:III-0023}} the volunteers departed.

The island's chronicles simply stop describing them the moment the ships clear the town. There is no account of the crowd, or the weather, or who wept. The harbour scribe gives one thing only. Before going down the Abhainn naomh to open water the fleet went once round the river pool of Seann Skell sunwise, *diosal*, for luck, and the watchers on the quay counted the hulls as they passed. The record ends at the foot of the pool. The harbour songs go a little further. They say the fleet stood out west into Manannan's sea, toward the Otherworld of the tellings, Tìr nan Òg and Magh Meall, and that a mist came down on the water behind the last hull and did not lift until it was gone.

The years of Rodos are counted from that turn, though not at once. In {{year:III-0025}} Donnchadh mac Thormoid, scribe of the vein-house, began to number the years from the sunwise turn and called them the years of the Diosal. The crown's scribes took up his count only a generation later. So an island that had counted by kings and harvests began, in one clerk's hand, to count by an absence.

In the same year a report of the fleet reached Seann Skell. The custody-book does not say how or by whom it came, only that the harbour scribe copied it in a hand that shook. The copy came down to later keepers stained by water and missing its end.

What that report, or whatever fragments followed it, describes is two landfalls: two separate coasts reached, and not one. The chroniclers have read this as the seed of two later peoples rather than one, and the keepers set it down the same way, as a reading. Which hulls went to which coast, and whether the fleet parted by storm or quarrel or plan, is not known. When Beathag nic Thòmais of Muileann chaol found three copies of the landfall report sixteen centuries later, they differed in the order of the coasts and in the number of hulls. She wrote all three out side by side and did not choose between them, and the library has followed her ever since.

In {{year:III-0028}} Queen Beathag removed her sister Mòrag and Mòrag's children from the order of succession, as persons no longer on Rodos. The act is entered without comment, between a grain ruling and the repair of a bridge. It is the last time the crown of Rodos wrote Mòrag's name, and it wrote it in the ordinary way.

What happened across the water after that, the island learned in pieces if it learned it at all. The chronicle calls it the thinning. Cut off from the vein, whatever strength the crossing had required faded across the departed's own children, and by the third generation abroad nothing remained that Rodos would recognize. How the island came to know even this much the library cannot say.

Then comes the second silence. Some twelve years after the thinning is first entered, there was a rumour of a second and smaller crossing: fewer ships, and no surviving record of where they meant to go. Nothing that survives confirms it or denies it, and the keepers enter it and let it stand.

## IV. Of the White Sorrow and the Empty Harbour

In {{year:III-0030}} the vein-house counted again the households that held the right to the ore, and set the loss down as one hand in five. Beside it Donnchadh mac Thormoid wrote that the vein itself answered no differently for those who stayed. It has been argued over ever since. Donnchadh did not say whether he meant it as comfort or complaint.

The next year Rodos made a rite for the departed, which the chronicle calls the Mourning Custom. It was not quite grief and not quite celebration. Queen Beathag kept it first for her sister, in undyed wool, with one place set empty at a meal that was neither feast nor fast. The court called it an Tùrsa Geal, the white sorrow, and by the next generation it was kept in every harbour town on the island. It would be taken up again, uneasily, for a very different departure in a much later age, and that belongs to its own book.

The year after, the island reshaped itself around what was left. With part of the ore-bonded people gone, the custodian class became smaller and more concentrated, and that is the shape every later aristocracy of Rodos inherits. In {{year:III-0034}} the remaining custodian households were regrouped into seven houses, each answerable to the vein-house for its share of the ore and of the measured burning: Clann Mhuirich, Clann Fhearchair, Clann Ìomhair, Clann Raghnaill, Clann Thormoid, Clann Choinnich and Clann Lachlainn. The keepers ask the reader to mark that list. It is the first division in the record to treat the right to the ore as something inherited by house rather than earned by apprenticeship.

Queen Beathag died at Cathair dhearg in the summer of that year, the last ruler of Rodos to have seen the fleet. Her son Raghnall mac Uilleim was crowned in the same hall.

Then came the long work of getting used to it. At Seann Skell a ceremony grew up around the dock the ships had left from, the empty-harbour rite, still observed in reduced form on an anniversary no one can now date precisely. By {{year:III-0037}} the harbour-master recorded his berths at half their former use, because the fishing households that had crewed the fleet were gone, and the rope-walks at Cuan shean laid off hands for the first time. On the north-west coast the fishers of Ceann mhòr kept a watch from the headland for returning sails, by household in turn. It lapsed within a generation, and no sail is recorded.

By {{year:III-0040}} Seann Skell had found a way to go on trading. The dock the ships had left from was given a new name so that ordinary trade could keep using the old one without the association. The chronicle calls it an act of managed forgetting. In the harbour rolls the departure berth became Cidhe an Diosail, the quay of the sunwise turn, and the name Cidhe an Iar passed to the new fish-wharf at the north end of the strand, where within a few years the fishers used it without a thought.

The language kept what the harbour put away. From {{year:III-0042}} the custody-book uses the verb *roghnaich*, to choose, with no object at all, meaning to have chosen the crossing, and the next century's scribes use it so without explaining it. By that same year a generation had grown up that had never seen the harbour with those ships in it. For them the departure was no longer an event but a fact of history. A saying of the western shore claims to come from about this time:

> *Is fhada an cuan, is goirid a' chuimhne.*
> Long is the sea, and short is memory.

Alasdair Saor died at Seann Skell at the end of that summer. His last work was a coasting trader of the old sewn-plank kind, built for a fish merchant of Inis àrsaidh. Seventeen years later his fellowship quietly dissolved, since no one wanted ocean-going hulls any more, and its techniques were half forgotten by the time anyone thought to need them again. Its patterns and keel-moulds were broken up and burned as firewood in a hard season. The harbour-master noted the loss of the shed and said nothing of what had been in it.

In {{year:III-0047}} the seven houses drew lots at the vein-house for the galleries each would keep, and the lot-stones were shown to visitors at Muileann dhearg for centuries after. Doire fhionn, its oaks gone to the fleet, began to coppice its woods, and the law spread to every shire with a *doire* in its name.

At Seann Skell the waterfront kept the night of lamps every year on the eve of the empty-harbour rite. Children were told the lamps were for ships coming home, and the priests did not correct them. The children's verse is still known on the west coast:

> *Solas air an uinneig,*
> *solas gus an tig iad dhachaigh.*
> A light on the window,
> a light till they come home.

Donnchadh mac Thormoid died at Muileann dhearg in {{year:III-0050}}. His last entry is a list of the ore burned that season, household by household, in the same hand as his first. Sixteen years later, in {{year:III-0051}}, King Raghnall died after a long and uneventful reign, and his son Ìomhar mac Raghnaill was crowned. The custody-book gives Raghnall's reign a single line. After the shaking of its first years the age seems to have wanted no more than that.

## V. Of the Hawk and the Stone, and the Firlot of Cathair dhearg

The chroniclers call the centuries that followed the quiet ones, and they were quiet the way a well-kept house is quiet: much was going on, and most of it was small. The silver of Muileann chiar, mined since the Holy Age, was worked into neck-rings for the heads of the seven houses, and the smiths' fellowship of the seam closed its methods within four families. Inis mhòr built barges for the grain, to go down the Abhainn ghorm and along the coast, sewn-plank boats of the old kind that never slept out of sight of land. The road-house of Am Buabhall Fortanach burned to its footings and was rebuilt in stone by its keeper, Eilidh nic Chaluim, and the account of her rebuilding mentions the jugged buffalo it was already known for.

Beathag's line ended in {{year:III-0057}}, when King Ìomhar died with no living child. The heads of the seven houses were summoned to the capital, and in {{year:III-0058}} they chose Fearchar mac Lachlainn of Baile Mòr dhomhain, of Clann Fhearchair. He was the first king in the record crowned by decision of the custodian houses and not by descent. His son Fearchar Donn followed him without dispute.

The orders of the Old Faith began to take their shapes. In {{year:III-0062}} at Cnoc bheag, where the dawn-hawk rite of the Holy Age was still kept, the priestess Gormshuil nic Artair taught that the vein was Mòd, the Hungry Hawk, the hawk of Acaill that Fionntan mac Bòchra became, who remembers everything and must be fed the measured handful and no more: a hawk overfed will not hunt, and a hawk starved will turn on the hand. Her followers gathered the old rite into the first house of Òrd an t-Seabhaig, the order of the Hawk of Acaill, and her pupils founded a second at Ros gheal. After her death her sayings were written down as Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, the Book of the Hawk. The copyist, Ùisdean mac Pàdraig, marked every saying he had not heard from her own mouth, and there are more of these than of the rest. The first in the book is one he heard:

> *Beathaich an seabhag, ach na sàsaich e.*
> Feed the hawk, but do not glut him.

In {{year:III-0068}} Clann Ìomhair walled Dùn dhearg in stone, the first walls on the island raised by a custodian house and not the crown. That year too Sìleas nic Fhearchair, crowned after her father, fixed a single measure for grain across the island, the firlot of Cathair dhearg, so that barter between shires need not begin with a quarrel over the size of a basket, and sent firlots of oak bound with iron to every shire seat. Later she set a tally-house in every royal granary, where each exchange of grain for cloth, salt or labour was cut on paired sticks. It was the first island-wide account of trade, and it ran entirely without coin. She reigned more than half a century, and her son Uisdean mac Dhùghaill followed her in {{year:III-0077}}.

It was a good time to be a carter. The herders near Imrich nam Pantar began clearing the panther roads on the days the great cats crossed, which was the first thing the southern shires agreed on in writing. An Leòmhann Buidhe on the northern mining road served oatcakes fried in linseed oil, and fire water, to the ore-carters, and its keeper was fined for watering the fire water. Ros fhionn laid out salt pans at the brine springs on its river flats, and its salt, carried inland over Drochaid Ros fhionn, became the common measure of value in the north-east.

Not everything was quiet. In {{year:III-0074}} Coinneach mac Mhàrtainn, a druid of the stag's school, Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, founded a house of disputation at Dùn thais, where the school's old question, why the vein answers some hands and not others, was argued by rule. Ten years later Coinneach asked in open disputation whether the hands that went with the fleet had been the ones the vein answered best, and whether it now answered fewer. The seven houses forbade the question at their tables. The record notes that it was the first time they forbade a question rather than an act.

The skills of the fleet were already going. In {{year:III-0078}} a merchant of Inis àrsaidh paid for a deep-keeled hull, but the wrights could not remember how Alasdair Saor's keels were scarfed, and it listed so badly at launch that it was broken up for timber.

Aonghas mac Uisdein, crowned in {{year:III-0084}}, was the first king to learn his letters as a child, and in his reign two more orders took their shapes. In {{year:III-0086}} Dòmhnall mac Ailein, a net-maker of Inis thais, where the wolf-sworn's stone stood, gave the wolf-sworn's stone its name and its order. He called it Clach, the Stone, and said it was Lia Fàil, the stone that cries out under the rightful king, and called its sworn guards the Fianna, who keep the island as a wolf keeps its range. So began Òrd na Cloiche, the order of the Stone; and the tales of Fionn and his band, which every hearth on the island knew, the Fianna took for their own. When the Hawk and the Stone both claimed the harbour shrine at Caol leathan, the king's reeve divided its days between them as the Holy Age had divided the sacred woods, and the arrangement held. In {{year:III-0089}} Aonghas himself took up the wood-watchers' teaching of Macha, whose two horses are Dubh Sainglenn the black and Liath Macha the grey: that dark and light are two powers in balance and the vein belongs to both. The court followed him, and Òrd Mhacha, the order of Macha, became the order of Cathair dhearg. From Aonghas on, the kings of Rodos were made by Macha's rite.

In {{year:III-0094}} Eilidh nic Iain of Doire chiar, called Eilidh Dhubh, revived the Holy Age's rival teaching that the vein answers effort and not birth, and her tracts were read aloud at harvest gatherings across the north-east. The vein-house ordered every copy brought to Muileann dhearg and burned, and some hundred were surrendered. Eilidh was not punished, and she died at Doire chiar still teaching.

Aonghas died that winter and was the first king buried by the rite of Macha. His son Iain reigned briefly, and Iain's son Tormod mac Iain, called Tormod Bàn for his white hair, was sworn to without dispute. The ox-teams of the mountain now waited out the snow at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, whose keeper, Iain mac Mhurchaidh, built them a second byre and kept a book of the teams that lodged.

## VI. Of the War of the Three Claimants

In {{year:III-0102}} both sons of King Tormod Bàn were drowned when a ferry overturned crossing Abhainn fhionn in spate. The king had no other child. He died at Cathair dhearg thirteen years later without naming an heir, and three claims were laid before the houses at once. Catrìona nic Aonghais of Inis thais was the late king's niece. Dùghall Garbh of Baile Mòr dhomhain was his half-brother. Raghnall Ruadh was head of Clann Raghnaill and master of the vein-house. The seven houses split among them, and in {{year:III-0104}} the War of the Three Claimants began.

The keepers have wondered how so ordinary a cause led to so long a war. Perhaps, having held the ore by inheritance for five hundred years, each house had come to think of the crown as something that could be inherited too.

Seann Warr, the port nearest the capital on the west, shut its gates to all three and declared for none. It was besieged twice and taken by neither. At the ford of Àth shean, the northern town of that name and not the granary town, Dùghall Garbh's host broke Raghnall Ruadh's, and the custody-book counts some three hundred dead, most of them drowned in the ford. Raghnall's men burned Doire mhin and its granary as they retreated, and the villagers wintered at Cathair mhòr on the charity of Macha's priests. Catrìona besieged Cathair dhomhain, which had declared for Dùghall, and took it after forty days, when its wells were poisoned by the dead of the siege lines, and she let the garrison go free on their oath.

On {{date:III-0109}} Raghnall Ruadh did what Rodos remembers him for. Holding the vein-house, he refused the measured handful to every hearth and temple in the shires that stood against him. Hearths lit with the ore for generations went cold. For as long as the Small-Burning Law had existed it had measured the ore out, and this was the first time it was used to hold the ore back. The chronicle says the act turned more people against him than any battle. A verse from the cold shires survives, unsigned:

> *Cha do chaill sinn blàr, ach chaill sinn an teine.*
> We did not lose a battle, but we lost the fire.

Catrìona and Dùghall joined their hosts and met Raghnall below Muileann chrom on {{date:III-0110}}. Raghnall was killed on the field, and the next day his own custodians opened the vein-house. Dùghall died of his wounds at Baile Mòr dhomhain in {{year:III-0111}}, and his followers went over to Catrìona without another fight. She was crowned at Cathair dhearg in {{year:III-0112}}, Catrìona Bhuadhach, and the war was over after about ten years.

Three years later she did what she is remembered for. With the vein-house she fixed it in law that no claimant, house or king might withhold the measured handful from any lawful hearth. This was the ruling of the hearth, the first law in the record written to bind the custodians themselves. She granted Seann Warr the right to keep its own gates and tolls in memory of its standing aside, and had the grant cut over the landward gate. When she died in {{year:III-0116}} the houses swore to her son Lachlann mac Iain in a single day, and the record remarks on it.

In {{year:III-0117}} Seònaid nic Coinnich, a clerk of the late queen's household, wrote the war's history from the custody-book and from the testimony of those who had fought. It is the oldest surviving history on Rodos written as a single narrative rather than as entries, and every later history of the island descends from it, this one included. That same year a spotted fever killed some three hundred at Inis chrom. The Fianna of the Stone nursed the sick, and many of them died of it, and the town was the Stone's for good after that.

## VII. Of Manannan's Order, and the Crown Joined to the Vein

The record calls Lachlann's reign quiet, and means it as praise. His daughter Anna followed him in {{year:III-0121}}.

In {{year:III-0120}}, at Seann Skell of all places, where the cup-creed of the Holy Age had first called itself the only true one, a preacher named Mànas mac Ruairidh proclaimed its power as Manannan mac Lir, lord of the sea, whose cup of truth breaks at a lie and bears the yellow scorpion for its sign. He taught that Manannan had shut the western sea behind the fleet with his mist and that it must not be tried again. His hearers were the first congregation of Òrd Mhanannain, the order of Manannan, and the teaching went down the west coast with the fishing families. At Cuan shean the rope-makers, whose walks had been laid out for the fleet, were among the first converts. In {{year:III-0123}} Manannan's priests at Ros bheag ruled that no boat of the faithful might go beyond sight of the headlands. The rule was kept on the west coast for centuries, and the steering of boats by the stars, already half lost, was lost there entirely. Of all the orders of the Old Faith, the keepers have been least easy with Manannan's. It is the only one whose teaching rests on the Sundering, and what it teaches is that the island must never again do what the fleet did.

In {{year:III-0127}} the vein-house and the crown's scribes found that their counts of years since the Setting-Out disagreed. They settled the difference by agreement, and fixed by the same agreement the day of the empty-harbour rite, which each count had placed differently. Later scribes stopped pretending it had been fixed by evidence. Every year in this book rests on that compromise between two offices, and the keepers think the reader should know it.

Eilidh Bhàn, fifth ruler of the house of Catrìona, was crowned in {{year:III-0129}}, and the record of her long reign is almost all harvests. The north-west did not share in them. From {{year:III-0131}} boats working out of the small islands off that coast took fish and grain from the coasting traders. The sea-thieves were Ròdaich of the north-west shore, and the harbour rolls give some of their names. Baile chrom reported half its houses empty, and the crown remitted its grain dues. In {{year:III-0133}} a force sent by Eilidh Bhàn landed on Eilean dhubh and burned the thieves' boats on the strand, and their leader, Uilleam Ruadh, was hanged at Cathair dhearg. It did not save the coast. In {{year:III-0134}} a run of failed harvests brought a dearth to the north-west shore that the shared-meal custom could not carry. Families left for the interior and the east, and the north-west stayed thinly held until the age ended.

In {{year:III-0135}} the queen's scribes recopied the king-list from its oldest surviving roll and added the rulers since the Sundering. The new copy begins the present age with the Choosing and not with the fleet, and this book keeps that order. Eilidh Bhàn died childless twelve years later, and the seven houses met to choose, this time at the vein-house and not at the capital. They named Dòmhnall Ruadh of Clann Raghnaill, a descendant of the claimant killed at Muileann chrom, and he kept the mastery of the vein-house together with the crown. In {{year:III-0138}} his reeves wrote into the custody-book that the ruler of Rodos is also master of the vein-house. From then on the heads of the seven houses sat as the king's council, and the custodian class and the court were one body. What Raghnall Ruadh had seized in war, his descendant now held by law. The ruling of the hearth still bound the custodians, but the custodians were now the crown. Dòmhnall Ruadh was buried at Muileann dhearg and not at the capital.

The Hawk split in {{year:III-0140}} over whether Mòd's measure could ever be raised. Those who held it fixed kept the name of the Hungry Hawk and the house at Cnoc bheag. Those who said a larger flock might be fed a larger handful were called the Fed Hawk, and they settled at Seann Skell on the north coast, not the western river port of that name. In {{year:III-0143}} they petitioned the council to raise the handful by a quarter for the northern hearths. The council refused. It was the first recorded request to change the Small-Burning Law, and the custody-book records their teaching as an error, at length.

The mountain took its own toll. In {{year:III-0144}} a second tremor in the hills of Cill ghlas brought down a gallery of the vein and killed fourteen labourers, and the vein-house closed it and set a stone at its mouth with the fourteen names. The last shepherds left the high pastures of Doire uaine. They said it was for the grazing, and every one of them who was asked also mentioned the Marbh-bheò.

Two findings of these years sit uneasily in the record. In {{year:III-0149}} quarrymen north of the capital broke into Toll-dubh, the shaft of the old tellings, long lost, and found a stair cut in the rock going down past the light of their lamps. The custodians had the entrance walled up. The one quarryman who went down is not recorded as saying what he saw. Twenty years later Ailean mac Dhùghaill of the deer school measured the ruin of Làrach an Teampaill and wrote that its altar faced the central mountains and not the sea, and he declined to guess who had set it so. By {{year:III-0152}} he had finished his tables of the three old ruins and concluded that he could not say whether one people had built all three. His tables are the reason later scholars could not say either. He had measured too carefully to leave any room for guessing.

Mòrag Ruadh of Clann Raghnaill was crowned in {{year:III-0153}}, the first ruler whose household accounts survive whole. On {{date:III-0156}} the vein-house marked the thousandth year of its count with a single measured burning of the ore on Cidhe an Diosail at Seann Skell, with the queen present. The record says the flame burned the colour of the vein and that the crowd went home without a word.

## VIII. Of Things Forgotten, and the Brothers' War

In its eleventh century the island began, visibly, to lose its skills. The dyers of Baile ghorm had made a fast blue from a marsh plant and a mordant of their own, and it was the feast-day dress of the seven houses. In {{year:III-0164}} the last dyer who knew the mordant died without apprentices, having quarrelled with both of hers, and the blue that came after was the ordinary blue of the rest of the island.

A sickness that closed the throat, the swallowing cough, broke out at Muileann dhomhain in {{year:III-0158}} and killed nearly a third of the town. When it reached Seann Bhral the townsfolk shut their own harbour and kept the shared-meal custom by leaving food at the gate. Queen Beathag Fhionn, daughter of Mòrag Ruadh, closed the coast road between the two towns and set reeves at the fords, and the cough went no further west. Her son Ailean followed her, the first of four kings of that name in the house of Raghnall.

The Abhainn bheag silted behind a shifting bar below Ros fhionn, and the town's salt-masters moved their trade to Caol gharbh on the coast. The carts stopped crossing Drochaid Ros fhionn, and the bridge-keeper's office was abolished. Fishers of the thinned north-west reported the Sea-Watcher, Uilebheist na Mara, close enough to foul their nets, and the few boats left there drew further in.

In {{year:III-0171}} Raghnall mac Chaluim of the deer school at Dùn thais set out a table showing that the heads of the custodian houses lived on average a few years longer than the tenants of their lands, and asked whether the vein answers the hand that holds it. The record notes the finding with unease and without an answer. The council forbade the copying of the table and closed the house at Dùn thais for a generation. Raghnall's own copy went into the vein-house and was entered in its inventory.

In {{year:III-0176}} King Ailean mac Raghnaill died leaving two sons by different mothers, Iain Ciar and Alasdair Bàn, each of whom had been told he was the heir. Four years later Alasdair Bàn's men seized Cnoc naomh and Iain Ciar took it back. The houses divided four to three, and the Brothers' War, Cogadh nam Bràithrean, took its name from that battle. Alasdair's ships burned the waterfront of Ceann mhin, which held for his brother. The fire took the rope stores and the granary, and the town had to beg its bread from Cathair mhòr. In {{year:III-0179}} Iain Ciar beat Alasdair below Muileann ghlas, took him alive and let his captains go home. The field comes into the record again two ages later.

The brothers made peace in {{year:III-0180}} at An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, on neutral ground between their lands, the road-house where the ox-teams waited out the snow. Alasdair gave up his claim for the lordship of Seann Chwen for life. He kept the peace he had made and died there. Iain Ciar was crowned. Because he had seen Ceann mhin beg for bread, he ordered every shire seat to keep a year's grain against the next war. The law was kept while he lived and lapsed within a few years of his death, as he had foreseen in a note in his own hand. His clerk Pàdraig mac Ruairidh wrote the account of the war. It praises the king at every turn and is the only account that survives, which later readers have found inconvenient.

The road-houses come back into the record here, and the keepers are glad of them. In {{year:III-0184}} the crown chartered the seven great road-houses of the mining circuit and made their keepers answerable for the roads between them. The keepers met once a year at An Sligeanach Fad' às to settle their accounts. At An Taigh-seinnse Reòta on the bare northern road a party of reeves was snowed in for a whole season and lived on pickled spinach and fire milk. Their complaint to the charter-roll is recorded in full.

In {{year:III-0188}} Seumas mac Iain of Seann Skell tried to rebuild a deep-keeled hull from what was still remembered of the Sundering fellowship's lore on the strand. It filled and sank at its launch, and Manannan's priests preached on it for a year. Twenty years later Iseabail nic Lachlainn, a folk-collector of the royal household, took down a work-song of the rope-makers of Cuan shean that names the parts of a deep keel. Half its words meant nothing to anyone who sang it. It is the only record of the fleet's techniques that survives in any form. Its first lines can still be understood:

> *Druim fon aisne, aisne fon chlàr,*
> *clàr ri clàr is an cuan fodhpa.*
> Keel under rib, rib under plank,
> plank to plank and the sea beneath them.

After that the song goes on in words no one can read. The rope-makers who kept it were of Manannan's order by then, and sang it anyway.

## IX. Of the Last Lamps

The last four centuries of the age are the longest stretch in the record, and they have the least to tell. The keepers will not pretend otherwise.

The order of the Stone divided over whether Clach might be prayed to for the dead, who are Donn's, and its two houses, at Inis thais and Baile gheal, were not reconciled in this age. Catrìona Bheag of Clann Raghnaill was crowned in {{year:III-0195}}, the first queen in more than three centuries. The record notes that she was small, and that no one said so twice. In {{year:III-0197}} she had the heads of the seven houses swear to the ruling of the hearth in her presence, the first renewal of that oath since Catrìona Bhuadhach. Clann Choinnich swore last.

More was forgotten. The potters of Muileann gheal made a grey-green glaze no other kiln could match, and the custodian houses bought it for their tables. The secret stayed in one family, and in {{year:III-0203}} the last of them died without passing it on. The smiths of Dùn dhearg lost the last man who knew how their layered steel was folded, and later Dùn dhearg blades were ordinary. In the east, charcoal-burners found again the eastern Toll-dubh of the old tellings, with the same cut stair as the first. The custodians walled it up as well, and the scribe cross-referenced the two entries.

At Àth shean the granary found that its firlot had worn smaller than the standard of Cathair dhearg, and had been for generations. The reeve corrected the accounts as far back as anyone could reckon.

Sìleas Òg, named for the queen of the firlot, was crowned in {{year:III-0205}}, the first ruler to visit every shire seat. She ordered a new count of the households holding the right to the ore. It was smaller than any before it, because the right had narrowed into fewer and larger households, and she had it entered without comment. In {{year:III-0208}} Clann Choinnich asked leave to burn the ore beyond the measured handful to smelt silver at Muileann chiar, arguing that it burned hotter than charcoal. The council refused. It was the first request to burn the ore for work rather than worship. The keepers know where that request leads in the next age, and here they only record it.

Ailean the Fourth had the shortest reign in the king-list. His sister Mòrag nic Ailein, who followed him, reigned forty years, and her entries record nothing but good harvests and the rite. In {{year:III-0212}} the priests of Manannan met in synod at Seann Skell, restated the rule of sight-of-land, and added a prayer to be said at the foot of the river pool:

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Manannan, keep the sea shut.

The library that keeps this record begins in these years. In {{year:III-0217}} Beathag nic Thòmais, a mill-owner's widow of Muileann chaol, began buying old copies of the custody-book and the harbour rolls from houses that no longer read them, and kept them in a loft over her mill. A mill in the same town soon began making paper from rags, the first on Rodos, and the custody-book was copied onto paper for the first time. Among her papers were the three copies of the landfall report. She died in {{year:III-0221}} and left the loft to the town on condition that nothing in it was burned. The town kept that condition and very little else of her wishes.

The crown sent a reeve to list the households of the north-west coast between Baile chrom and Ceann mhòr, and he counted fewer households than harbour berths. Tormod mac Ruairidh, crowned in {{year:III-0222}}, reigned for more than fifty years. In {{year:III-0223}} Manannan's priests at Seann Skell began saying their prayer to keep the sea shut on the same day as the empty-harbour rite, so that the two were kept together on Cidhe an Diosail. Neither side was pleased.

In {{year:III-0228}} a visiting scribe found the empty-harbour rite at Seann Skell kept by the harbour priests and a few old families, and wrote that no one there could say on which day the fleet had actually sailed. The house of disputation at Dùn thais, shut and reopened more than once since the table of years, opened again in {{year:III-0230}} on condition that the question of longer lives was not raised. The scholars accepted and argued about the vein instead.

Tormod's daughter Gormshuil nic Thormoid, crowned in {{year:III-0231}}, made a progress round every shire seat. Her clerks wrote down the grievances of each shire, and she answered none of them in writing. On the eastern road An Taigh-òsta Grianach fed her company on pan-fried spinach and black wine, and its keeper, Peigi nic Dhonnchaidh, was excused her road dues for a year. It is the last warm entry of the age. In {{year:III-0233}} Clann Choinnich asked again to burn the ore for the silver smelt, this time with three of the seven houses behind it, and Gormshuil's council refused by one voice. The record gives the name of the member who changed his vote, and does not give his reason. In {{year:III-0235}} the vein-house counted the households of the right for the last time in this age. There were fewer than ever before, and they were larger, and three of the seven houses now held more than half the right between them.

In {{year:III-0236}} a worked timber came ashore at Ceann mhòr on the empty north-west coast. It was longer than any tree on the island and fastened with nails of a make no smith on Rodos recognized. It was carried to Seann Skell and shown to Manannan's priests, and they burned it. The record does not say what was said over it.

By then fewer and fewer houses on the Seann Skell waterfront kept the night of lamps, and at last none did. An old woman set a lamp in a window of the harbour-house, and the scribe recorded that it was she who kept it and not the town. On {{date:III-0238}} eleven people kept the empty-harbour rite on Cidhe an Diosail. The harbour scribe entered their number, the weather and the state of the tide, and nothing else.

Clann Choinnich did not bring its silver petition again, because the member who had turned the vote had died. The petition lay in the vein-house unanswered, and the record of the age ends with the Small-Burning Law still kept. Eighteen centuries after a queen at Cathair dhearg said she would keep no one back, Rodos had kept the vein and kept the law, and had let go nearly everything else the fleet had meant: the keel, the stars, the far coast and all but eleven keepers of the rite. The houses were fewer and richer. The north-west was nearly empty. The prayer to keep the sea shut had been said for two hundred years, and no one had tried the sea. Then sails came in over that water from the far side, and they were not Rodian sails. The people under them had crossed the water the Rodians once crossed. Whether any of them came down from those who chose to go, no one on Rodos knew then and no one knows now. The Age of Strangers begins with their coming, and the next book tells it.
