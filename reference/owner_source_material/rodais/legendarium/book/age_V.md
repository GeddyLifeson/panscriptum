# The Fifth Book: An Aois Ùr, the New Age

*1930 – 2026 DE*

> *Tha e scrìobhte a-nis.*
> It is written now.
>
> — a minute of the Moot at Caol mhòr, {{date:V-0217}}, entire, as the library renders it

## I. Of the Severance and the Settling of the North

*So the old people of the north tell it, at the hearths of Doire ghlas, when the fire is low and the children will not sleep.*

In the last days of the Age of Strangers, whatever road had brought the humans over the western water shut behind their last ships, and those still on Rodos were marched north under the council's guard. That was the Severance, and it fell on the first day of {{year:IV-0377}}, in the dead of winter, when the roads north were mud to the knee.

On {{date:V-0001}}, with the lambs coming, the homeland of the Tuathaich was fixed. The children of the stranded humans were settled in the north by the council's word, and its guard stood at the head of the roads. It was a confinement, and everyone on both sides of the guard knew it. Their grandchildren would call it home all the same, having no other. Cian and Lorccan of the immortal generation grew up in the wake of those days; of them the chronicles of this age tell nothing further.

The victors began by putting things away. At Cathair dhearg the war-companies of the Severance were stood down, and their rolls went into the council's chests instead of the fire. Nobody ever abolished the companies. They lived on as names on a list, and the list lay quiet for nearly ninety years. On the roads running north, at Caol mhòr and elsewhere, stones were set to mark where the Tuathaich country began. No wall came after them. Many still stand at the roadside, since nobody has ever been paid to shift them.

That first year the north went hungry alone. The stores that had come over the water from an Tìr Thall came no more, and the grain ran out before the spring. At Doire ghlas, the old people say, more than two hundred were buried that winter; they had it from their grandmothers, and it was written down long afterwards. The Ròdaich made no count.

Meanwhile the council's clerks went through the humans' counting-houses at Cathair dhearg, Ros dhomhain and Seann Skell like rooks through stubble, and carried off every written leaf. The tithe ledgers, or what the raids of the war had left of them, they boxed unread, for few of them could make out the humans' hand. In the north-west things went otherwise. At Ceann mhòr, Cathair gheal and Inis àrsaidh the human harbour officers had sailed with the last ships and taken their keys with them. The Tuathaich fishermen took hammers to the locks of the harbour stores and shared out what they found, house by house. The north tells that part with some relish.

## II. Of the Moot and the Kingdom

In the south the kingdom was making itself. On {{date:V-0007}}, in the high summer, the state styled itself Rìoghachd Ròdais: a kingdom in its form, whatever it was in its working, and its own records carry that contradiction still.

A month before the council was seated, the householders of the northern towns came to Caol mhòr.

They came on foot and in carts at the tail of a wet summer, and crowded into a long low room that smelt of damp wool and fish-scale. Walter Hale stood at the back with his hat in his hands. He had been a harbour clerk under the humans, and there was still ink in the creases of his knuckles.

They argued most of the afternoon over who should speak for them. Somebody said his name, and it went round the room like a bucket passed along a line, and it stopped with him.

"We've no standing," said a man by the door. "Not in their law. They needn't hear a word of it."

"Then we'll say it where we can hear it ourselves," said Walter Hale. He sat down at the trestle, opened a book that had nothing in it yet, wetted his pen and wrote the day.

That was the Moot. It had no standing in Ròdaich law. It met all the same, and it kept minutes, and they run unbroken from that wet afternoon to the present year.

That autumn a royal council of twelve was seated at Cathair dhearg, drawn from the custodian families and the captains of the Severance war. Its minute-books are the oldest unbroken record of the state in this age, younger than the Moot's by a month.

At Ros dhomhain the first Ròdaich harbour-master, Dùghall mac Iain, kept the humans' tariff in force for want of another, and spent years turning it into Ròdais, clause by clause.

In the early summer of {{year:V-0011}} a ship was sent out past the western capes to seek the crossing the humans had used. She came home after a month of open water. She had seen no land and no sign of any passage, and no second ship was sent. At Seann Skell the priests of Manannan said that the lord of the sea had shut his sea again, as they had begged him to ever since the Crossing, and they said the old prayer at the harbour mouth:

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Manannan, keep the sea shut.

## III. Of Niall, the Shires and the Great Wave

In the long light of midsummer, on {{date:V-0012}}, Niall was crowned at Cathair dhearg by Macha's rite, as the kings before the humans had been crowned. He was the first ruler of the restored kingdom and the first name in the line that the state's chronicles have kept without a break ever since. So smoothly did the crown pass to him that *mar a thàinig Niall*, "the way Niall came", became a saying for any handing-over of power that went without a quarrel. The first place it is found written is a tavern ledger in the capital, set against a debt paid without argument.

The council drew the kingdom into shires, each *siorrachd* named for its seat, and drew them on the humans' district lines, for the humans' surveys were the only whole surveys of the island there were. The kingdom used them and did not say so. Over each shire went a *maor* to gather the Crown's dues: the head-due on every household, counted by its heads, and the market-due on every sale. Where the humans' lines stopped, at the edge of the great moss of the far north-west, the shires stopped too, and the windy coast beyond was left in no shire at all. In the Tuathaich shires the maoir were Ròdaich. At Ceann mhòr the maor moved into the old human magistrate's house, and the townsfolk went on calling it by its old name.

Then the sea came in.

The Great Wave came in the short night of early summer, on {{date:V-0017}}, and it gave no warning at all. The south calls it Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol. In one night it wrecked Cnoc chaol, Cuan dhearg and Achadh mhin.

The relief party out of Cathair dhearg came down the coast road to Cuan dhearg, and the young clerk who carried its tally-book smelt the town before he saw it: salt, and torn weed, and something under both that he did not want to name. Where the harbour had stood there was brown water and a row of broken piles like bad teeth. He looked for the boats, until a man of the party touched his arm and pointed inland. The boats of Cuan dhearg were in the fields behind the town, keeled over among the barley, and one sat upright in a furrow with her mast still stepped, as if she had been sailed there in the dark. The clerk wrote that down, not knowing what else to do with it, and went on counting.

When the tallies from Cnoc chaol and Achadh mhin came in and were added to his, the dead of the three towns came to a little over nine hundred. The wave had run on up the gulf as far as Ros gharbh and broken its quays. That town stood high and lost few of its people, and nearly every boat.

It was the first disaster the new state met on its own terms, and it met it well. The council moved the survivors of Achadh mhin off their salted fields and paid for their seed out of the treasury, the first relief the restored kingdom ever gave. The old site is called an Tràigh Bhàthte, the drowned strand, and nobody builds there. From the survivors the south took a word: *tonn*, wave, for anything sudden and overwhelming. *Tha e san tonn*, they say, he is in the wave of it. The south would have need of the phrase again.

In {{year:V-0023}} the human coinage was called in. In the old counting-house at Cathair dhearg, Fearchar mac Dhòmhnaill, whom men called Fearchar Bonn, set up the mint and struck the coin of the Rìoghachd. He pressed each coin's seal with an ink ground from obsidian dust, so that a clipped coin bled colour at its edge. The coin was honest and holy, and the kingdom liked it both ways.

The north paid for it. The Tuathaich households had put by their savings in human coin, and those savings were now worth their weight as metal and no more. The Moot petitioned for an exchange. The petition was received and filed. It is the first petition in this book to end that way, and not the last. At Doire mhin a smith named Joseph Marlow gathered up his neighbours' dead coin, melted it down and cast it into ploughshares, and sold the shares back to them for the price of the charcoal. The Tuathaich sing of him still in their own tongue. A library clerk once set down a verse of the song in plain prose: *he turned the king's silence into iron, and the iron into bread.*

## IV. Of the Rite, the Orders and the Law

In the spring of {{year:V-0028}}, Òrd Bhrìde, the order of Brìde's flame and Crom's stone, which had kept the Hall's rite since the Binding, was drawn up into ordered doctrine, where before there had been only a wide and loose reverence. It was built about the leyline and the unbroken line of the obsidian-touched, and the kingdom calls it simply the Rite. Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was named Àrd-choimheadaiche, the first high custodian of the Rite, and seated at Seann Skell on the southern coast, where the order's greatest house stands. His first labour was to write down an order of worship. In all the long keeping of the flame no one had ever written one.

The Hawk houses round Cnoc bheag would have none of it. Òrd an t-Seabhaig, the older form of the Rite, had never needed a custodian above the local altar, they said, and had no need of one now. Nobody forced them, and here begins the long quarrel between the two forms of the Rite. Only the southernmost hawk-house, at Muileann naomh, far from Cnoc bheag, took up the written order, and it has been a house of the Rite ever since. The houses of the Stone in the south-west, whose Clach was never Crom's stone, sent no witnesses, only a letter wishing the Rite well. Òrd Mhacha, the capital's own order, which makes the kings, was given the coronation regalia to keep, as it had kept them before the humans came, and it gave the high custodian no share in them. Within a year the orthodox Rite had one house that disowned it, one that ignored it with great courtesy, and one that outranked it at court. The orders of the Old Faith stood so for the rest of the age.

In the north the Church was kept quietly. The Tuathaich held to an Eaglais, the faith their grandparents had carried over the sea, and to the fragment they call the First Canoe, the voyage of Naomh Breandan, which the Ròdaich priesthood calls superstition. The library has never been shown the fragment. It knows only that it is kept. At Baile chrom the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane kept the vigil of Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise, the Mystery of the Grey Night, in the open for the first time since the Severance. The sect keeps one night of silence in the year for Sìne, the Peaceful Goose, which is its name for the wild goose of the Holy Spirit, and it tells no outsider which night that is. The Church's bishops call the vigil irregular, and they have never put it out. In the towns of the west coast, where the Tuathaich lived beside the Ròdaich of Manannan's harbours, many congregations came to preach in the humans' tongue and to read the Bible in Ròdais. How that came about the west coast has never troubled to explain.

In {{year:V-0046}} a wall between the two peoples was proposed, and the council threw it out as needless, since the peoples were keeping apart well enough without one. The minute that rejects it runs to four lines. The fourth reads *They do not come south.* Five of the twelve initialled it, and the other seven did not sign against it. The library keeps that minute under glass. It is a small leaf, and a good deal of the age is written on it.

The law came north in the same fashion. In {{year:V-0038}}, at Seann Bhral, Eilidh nic Leòid, first of the circuit justices, ruled that land held by the Tuathaich under human title passed to the Crown, while the right to work it stayed with the holders and their heirs. The Leòid ruling is cited in nearly every land case of the age.

Seven summers later she carried it north herself.

The court at Ceann mhòr sat in the heat of late July in {{year:V-0068}}, with the windows open on the square and the flies drifting in. Eilidh nic Leòid had nineteen cases before her. Seventeen were Tuathaich suits over land, and in each the same kind of paper came up to the bench: a human title, brown at the folds, under the seal of a magistrate who had sailed west. She read every one to the end, and would not be hurried, while the suitors stood in their best coats and sweated. Then she took up her own ruling and read that too, aloud, so that the room should hear what the law was, and gave judgment.

In fifteen of the seventeen she found for the Crown. She was exact. The law she was exact about had been made for one people, and she had made it. Her rulings became the kingdom's first law-book.

Some years before, in {{year:V-0032}}, Walter Hale had asked leave to open schools in the north that taught in the humans' tongue. The council answered that the kingdom's schools taught in Ròdais and it had no others. The Moot's minute calls the answer "clear, at least." In the middle of the winter of {{year:V-0066}} Agnes Pike opened such a school at Doire ghlas anyway, with Moot money and without leave, and within ten years there were nine. The council never licensed them and never shut them. For most of the age that was the kingdom's way with the north: it forbade little there, and helped with less.

## V. Of the Mountain, the Guilds and the Fair

By {{year:V-0050}} the mine was yielding as much as it had under the humans, and then more. The work was the same work. Only the owners had changed.

The Crown granted the Sloc Mòr, the great shaft at Achadh dhomhain, to Uisdean mac Ùisdein of Cathair mhòr, whose house had held an overseer's post under the humans. The Crown took a fifth. Those were the humans' terms with the names changed. The council would not end the day-and-night working the humans had ordered, and so the mountain was still given no season of rest. The custodians entered a protest, and then went down and blessed the shafts. The hewers had a saying for it:

> *Cha d' fhuair a' bheinn fois a-riamh,*
> *is cha d' fhuair sinne.*
> The mountain never got its rest,
> and neither did we.

Families from the hungry east came to the vein-towns for wages, and Muileann dhearg doubled in three years. The humans' railway was mended to the pithead, and in the October of {{year:V-0056}} the first ore train ran from the Sloc Mòr to Cathair dhearg, under the kingdom's name, on the humans' rails. At Muileann chrom the families who worked the little shunting engines were counted apart and lived apart, in rows beside the lines. For forty years the public buildings of the south went up in the Sloc Mòr's grey spoil-stone, sold under the Mac Ùisdein quarry-mark.

In {{year:V-0059}} the apprentice-binding of the Holy Age was taken up again, and this time the charters said in so many words that it was for Ròdaich applicants. The Guild of Hewers came first, its oath sworn with a hand laid on a lump of ore, and the shipwrights, chandlers, lamp-makers and weavers came after. The Tuathaich boys already bound to Ròdaich masters were released from their indentures. Some went north. Most stayed on as day-labour, outside the guilds and beneath their wages. The rule kept them where the pay was worst.

Off Cathair gheal, ships that flew no flag began to raid the north-western coast, and some of their crews were men who had served the human administration. The Tuathaich towns suffered first, undefended. In {{year:V-0085}}, having asked twice and been sent nothing, the fishermen of Cathair gheal armed their own boats and drove the raiders out of the bay. The council then fined the town for bearing arms without a licence. The town paid.

On {{date:V-0088}}, in the reddening of the year, the fair at Muileann òg was established, and it grew to be the greatest recurring gathering on the island. Its first toll-mistress, Oighrig nic Mhathain, set down its rules, and one of them was this: that Tuathaich goods be sold only through Ròdaich factors. They are still read aloud when each fair opens. Two years after, the Còmhrag began at Ceann leathan, a yearly melee that was called a martial tradition and served more and more as an outlet for a people with few others. Two men died in the first one that anyone remembers.

On {{date:V-0100}}, in the green of early summer, there was a cluster of births among the shunting families, the generation called Splatter and Dodge's. Seachran and Brean were among them. They would grow up to be the closest allies of a man not yet born.

## VI. Of the Library at Muileann chaol

On {{date:V-0070}}, which was Bealltainn, the fires were lit on the hills round Muileann chaol to bring the summer in, and their smoke hung over the town all day. Ailean mac Iain spent that day in a cellar.

He had been named first keeper of the new library, and had brought the council's minute-books with him, and the remains of the humans' tithe ledgers in their boxes. Now the townsmen led him down a stair to the custodians' rolls, which had lain there since the Severance war, stacked on planks above a wet floor, the lowest gone as soft as bread. He untied one and read three lines by the light from the stair. Then he sent for lamps, and a table, and a boy to fetch and carry.

He spent years having them read. Before the year was out, *a chur san Leabharlann*, to put it in the Library, meant to make a thing settled and beyond dispute. A clerk once used it in his hearing.

"The library settles nothing," said Ailean Leabhar, for so they called him by then. "It only keeps."

The phrase outlived him all the same. The library now keeps the fullest surviving record of everything told in this book, and the reader should bear that in mind.

In {{year:V-0078}}, as the nights drew in, an office was made to keep the kingdom's record, and from its books this book is drawn. The first royal chronicler, Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, a council clerk, kept her record at Muileann chaol and would not keep it at the capital. She wrote in her first entry that a record kept beside the throne is kept for the throne. She sent clerks to every shire to copy parish books, grave-lists and harbour rolls. The south filled forty chests. The northern shires filled one, for few of the Tuathaich would talk to the clerks.

The five lighthouses of the humans were given Ròdaich keepers. At Baile chrom, on the Tuathaich coast, the keeper's warrant bade him report what he saw by land as well as by sea. In time he reported that the Grey Night sect kept its vigil in the lighthouse's own shadow. The council took no action.

The library also dug. At Làrach an Dùin its trenches turned up walls older than any Ròdaich building and no writing at all, and the finds were catalogued as belonging to the Old Ones, purpose unknown. At Làrach an Teampaill the houses of the Seann Spioradan asked it not to dig, and the keeper entered the ruin as *measured and let be.* The obelisk near Cnoc bheag bore signs that matched no script known on the island. In this age as in every age, the Seann-Dhaoine appear only in what they left, and in what the living will not do near it.

When Ailean Leabhar died in {{year:V-0144}}, Mòrag nic Coinnich became keeper. She began the practice, kept ever since, of writing beside each item where it came from. In {{year:V-0152}} Gormshuil laid down the chronicle. Her last entry is a list of what the record did not hold: the northern towns' own accounts, the humans' treaties in their own tongue, and the Board's surveys. Her successor, Tormod mac Sheumais, called Tormod Pinn for the speed of his hand, copied that list into the front of his first book and added nothing to it. He struck nothing out, either.

## VII. Of Brìghde and Eòghan, and the First Word of the Depletion

Niall died, and his daughter Brìghde was crowned on {{date:V-0092}}, at summer's end, and no one contested it. It was a mortal succession in an aristocracy that stood very near the undying. It seemed nothing strange then. Gormshuil wrote that the succession came *mar a thàinig Niall*, and that is the only idiom she let into the chronicle in all her years.

On {{date:V-0105}}, in the raw end of winter, the survey reports first began to say, very quietly, that the vein's yield was falling.

Iain mac Ruairidh of the Mine Board, whom the hewers called Iain Mapa, had spent long months crawling the faces of the Sloc Mòr with a lamp and a chain, and he knew what he had found before he had finished finding it. The vein was thinning to the east and to the south, and it was thinning in a way that did not argue.

He carried his survey to the Board himself, rolled and tied, and laid it on the table in front of three men who did not unroll it while he was in the room.

"It will be considered," said the one in the middle.

"I should like a receipt," said Iain Mapa.

The clerk looked at him as though he had asked for a kiss. But he wrote one, and signed it, and Iain Mapa folded it into his pocket-book and went home. The survey was filed and never circulated. Soon after, he was sent to the silver works at Muileann chiar.

The next year the Board moved its clerks to Cathair mhòr and made a rule that its surveys were to be kept there and not sent to the library. The state did nothing for years. The library could not know what it was never sent. It knows now, and it counts those years as part of the Depletion.

Brìghde's reign ended young, and no cause was ever made public. Her cousin Eòghan was crowned on {{date:V-0111}}, amid the first true strife over a succession that the restored kingdom had known. The chronicler's entry gives the fact and nothing more, and no chronicler since has added to it. That December the council's minutes recorded votes against a succession for the first time. They name the councillors and not their reasons. Two of them did not sit again.

On {{date:V-0115}}, in the green of May, Dubhan was born, mortal, into the confinement the Severance had made, decades after the war that had shut the island to the people his line came from. No chronicle of that year mentions him, and none could have.

The kingdom went on round the Depletion it had not announced. In {{year:V-0126}} the second census measured, for the first time, the gap between Ròdaich and Tuathaich living. The report was never made public. A census clerk, Seonag nic Ille-Mhoire, left a note among her papers saying that she had asked to see it and had been refused, and that note is the only mention of the report outside the council.

That year Walter Hale died, and the Moot chose Mary Coyle, a fisherman's widow, the first woman in its chair. The Còmhrag grew bloodier. In {{year:V-0133}} six fighters died, and the custodians asked for it to end. The fair-wardens of Ceann leathan, whose town lived off the crowds, refused.

On the first day of July in {{year:V-0141}} a gallery gave way under Muileann dhearg, in the reopened Cill ghlas district, along a line the surveyors had marked as sound. Thirty-one hewers died. It was small beside the Rending. It was the same fault. New rules for digging followed. They were written for Ròdaich workers, and near the Tuathaich country they were kept loosely, and by intent. At the pits round Àth leathan, where Ròdaich overseers worked Tuathaich hands, the timbering rules were posted on the pit-head door and never paid for. The overseers' books show the timber ordered and never delivered. Àth leathan will come into this book again.

## VIII. Of the Search for a Second Vein

After Muileann dhearg the Guild of Hewers bound sixty apprentices to fill its rolls, and not one of them was Tuathaich. On Samhain eve of {{year:V-0146}} fire ran through the fair at Muileann òg. Its court sat in a field the next morning, and the fair went on, and Oighrig nic Mhathain died of her burns. That winter the Rail Board refused to carry the Moot's goods, and the north's wool went south by cart, a week on the road. The north calls that way the Carters' Road. The south has no name for it.

In the dark of the year's end in {{year:V-0150}} the Moot began a register of Tuathaich hewers killed or maimed in the western pits, since no Ròdaich book counted them. It has been kept ever since, and never sent south. In those years Dubhan was a child in the north, watching a caste that did not age hold every true advantage on an island to whose edge his own forebears had been pushed. The obsidian was never his to inherit.

The age had its good keepers too. When the dry years came back to Achadh shean in {{year:V-0154}}, the council sent grain up the ore line out of the Sloc Mòr's share, and nobody in the shire died of hunger. The maor had twelve wells dug to the rock, and four of them are still drawn from.

Of Ciorstaidh nic Artair it is told at Tobar dhearg that her father, the keeper of the light there, drowned going out to a boat on the reef, and that she went out after him and brought the boat's crew in. She took his warrant, and set a belled post on the reef. While she kept the light, no boat went aground there.

On {{date:V-0162}}, when the leaves were down, the druid schools, Scoiltean nan Draoidhean, whom many still call Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, woke out of their long sleep. Their founding question, why the vein answers some hands and not others, mattered again now that the vein was running short. Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain lectured in Flidais's sacred wood near Muileann chiar, and at Caol shean her students copied the census line on the obsidian-touched and argued over it in the open, which the Rite did not do. They set the old pillars and the obelisk side by side and found signs in common, and no meaning. Seonaid wrote that the Old Ones had been here and had said so, and that nobody living could hear them.

In {{year:V-0174}} Alasdair mac Ùisdein, grandson of the first holder of the Sloc Mòr, borrowed against his house's share to go looking for a second vein. The Board lent its surveyors and the Crown lent its name. Seonaid wrote to the Board that the search asked the wrong question. Her school had always asked why the vein answered some hands; nobody had asked why there was only one vein. The Board filed the letter.

The search failed. Forty-one shafts went down between the hills above Dùn dhearg and the moors south of Baile ruadh, and none of them struck the vein. Afterwards Mòrag nic Coinnich found in the library a list from the Holy Age that named those same hills as searched and barren. The kingdom had repeated a failure that its own library could have warned it of, had anyone thought to ask. The house of Mac Ùisdein paid part of Dùn dhearg's damages and sold half its share back to the Crown. The dry shafts at Baile ruadh were left open to the sky, and sheep fell into them, and one child, before the shire fenced them at its own cost.

In {{year:V-0189}} Mary Coyle laid down the chair, and Daniel Frayne of Muileann leathan came after her, the first speaker born after the Severance. He had taught young Tuathaich to bind books when the southern guilds would not take them. That winter the fair gave one day to northern goods. They were still sold through factors, but under the makers' own marks, and it was the first time a Tuathaich name had been written on anything sold there. The first such mark was the Doire ghlas weavers' grey twill, with a white thread at the selvedge. Within the year the weavers of Baile chiar were weaving a white thread into theirs.

On {{date:V-0192}} the Depletion could no longer be denied.

Catrìona nic Neacail, the surveyor-general, had drawn the last untouched sections of the vein, and they were smaller than anyone wished to admit. When the drawing was finished it fitted on one sheet. Because a sheet so small wanted explaining, she went down into the Board's files at Cathair mhòr to learn how long the thinning had been going on.

It took her the best part of a week, by a smoking lamp. Near the bottom of a press she found a survey of the faces of the Sloc Mòr, rolled and tied, in a hand she did not know. It showed the vein thinning to the east and the south, and it was thirty years old.

She published her map. She sent the library a copy of Iain Mapa's survey, with a note of the day the Board had received it, and within the year she resigned. Iain Mapa died at Muileann chiar the next spring. His own copy of the survey was found among his papers with the Board's receipt pinned to it. He had kept the proof that he told them.

Muileann dhearg lost a third of its households. Its Fèidh hall was shut, and its bell was sold to Caol shean, where it still rings the students in to their lectures.

## IX. Of Mairead and the Failing of the Ore

Mairead was crowned on {{date:V-0199}}, in the fall of the leaf. Eòghan's reign had ended in the decade the Depletion came into the open, and she was heir to a kingdom whose founding wealth was plainly failing. That year the Guild of Hewers bound no apprentices, for the first time since its charter.

The north began building things that did not hang on the mountain. In {{year:V-0202}} wool mills went up at Doire ghlas with Moot money. The Rail Board refused to carry their cloth, until the circuit justice at Seann Bhral, Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, ruled that the Board was a carrier of the Crown and must carry for anyone who paid. It was the first ruling the Moot ever won in a Ròdaich court. The Board appealed, and the council let the appeal lapse without a reason given.

In {{year:V-0204}} the Crown bought the last of the house of Mac Ùisdein's working shafts, and from then on the Board worked the vein in the Crown's name alone.

On {{date:V-0207}} Dubhan began his experiments, trying to make a fuel to stand in for the ore out of what the mines threw away. They all failed, and the record of those years scarcely troubles to name them. In that same month the lowest galleries of the Sloc Mòr were let flood, because the pumps cost more to run than the ore beneath them was worth. The Board's minute calls it a pause.

The chronicle of those years is full of small alarms. Panthers came across the south-west, beasts named in the Holy Age lists and not seen in living memory, and the old list named the year after them as a hungry one. At Muileann chrom the yard families stopped the ore trains for three days over cut wages; the Board settled on the fourth day and dismissed eleven men on the fifth. Then the harvest after the panthers came in good, and Tormod Pinn drew a line through his own note of the year before. This book has always liked him for it.

In the autumn of {{year:V-0216}} the third census published its figures openly, the first census in the kingdom's history to do so. It was a small reform, and nothing in the frame of the kingdom moved because of it.

A month later the figures were read at Caol mhòr. Daniel Frayne sat in the Moot's chair with his hands flat on the table, the way he sat when he meant to keep them still. The clerk read the columns in a flat voice. In every column the census clerks had chosen to take, the northern towns stood at about half the Ròdaich measure. Nobody in the room was surprised. They had simply never heard it read aloud from the kingdom's own paper.

When the clerk had done, there was a long quiet. Then Daniel Frayne drew the minute-book towards him and wrote two words, and the words are the whole of the minute: *Now written.*

The census carried a line for the obsidian-touched, as the first one had, giving a number and nothing else. Tormod Pinn copied it into the chronicle without comment, as Gormshuil had done before him.

Tormod Pinn died at his desk in {{year:V-0220}} and Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh became royal chronicler. She was the first to add, at the foot of each year, a note of what the record could not learn. Her first concerned the northern shires. The census clerks there had been told the figures and had not counted them, and the figures they were told were the Moot's. So the kingdom's first honest census was honest in the north because the Moot had supplied the numbers.

Mòrag nic Coinnich now called the shires' record-chests in to Muileann chaol. At Seann Tarr the shire keeper burned his chest in the yard sooner than send it, saying that the shire's record belonged in the shire, and that chest is lost. In {{year:V-0226}} the library doubled its holdings, and from then on the history of Rodos was kept in one place instead of many.

No Tuathaich record was called in. The Moot's minute-books, the schools' registers and the vigil-books of Baile chrom stayed in the north in private keeping. The new wing was built of Sloc Mòr stone. The shires' chests filled three floors, and the fourth was left empty for what had not been sent. It is empty still, or near enough.

In the west the Board was drawing back. In {{year:V-0229}} it closed the branch line to Àth leathan, citing the falling yield of the western pits. The Tuathaich hewers there kept working under their own pit captains and paid the Crown's fifth by the Leòid ruling, and the Board took the payments and entered them as arrears. At Cnoc thais the pits passed to their hewers in the same way. The south did not notice; it wanted nothing in the western hills any longer.

In {{year:V-0235}} Raghnall mac Mhuirich became keeper of the library. The price of ore-fuel at the fair doubled, and at Cnoc fhiadhaich households were found picking ore off the spoil-heaps to burn. The Board posted watchmen from the town, who saw nothing.

## X. Of Dubhan's Fire

On {{date:V-0238}}, about midsummer, the first working dubhan was made. It was crude, and it burned. Nobody yet traded in it; for the while it was one man's answer to a crisis that the caste in power had still not faced. At the foot of that year Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh wrote that a fuel made without the vein was reported from the north, and that she had not seen it. That is the first mention of dubhan in the royal record, and she spelled the word three ways in two lines.

In the capital the poorer streets were left dark to save ore. By {{year:V-0242}} ore was sold at the fair only by the sack, and at last only by the lump, for altars.

Cathal was crowned on {{date:V-0243}}, the feast of Lùnastal, by Macha's rite. Mairead's reign had ended while Dubhan's experiments still failed more often than they worked, and Cathal did not yet know how near his kingdom's answer lay. The new dies at the mint still pressed the obsidian ink into the seal.

The fuel came south within three years. In {{year:V-0245}} a company of shipmasters set up a dubhan works at Seann Skell on the southern coast, the first outside the north, and how they came by the making of it they never told. The high custodian wrote to the houses of the Rite that the new fuel was not forbidden, since it was not ore, and not blessed, since it was not ore. It sold its first year's making to the south-coast boats and the capital's lamp-houses, and the dark streets were the first to be lit again.

In {{year:V-0251}} the five coastal beacons, which had burned obsidian-lit flame for more than a century, were the first works of the kingdom to be turned over to dubhan by decree.

Ciorstaidh nic Artair was grey by then, but nobody else was let carry the lantern. Inside its thick glass the flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg burned with the dark, coloured edge that only obsidian gives. She carried it into the Rite's house at Seann Skell in the late spring, down the hall between the custodians of four shires, who stood in their rows and did not speak. Her boots were loud on the flags.

At Brìde's altar she set the lantern down and opened its little door. The flame leaned towards the air, and steadied. Nobody told her to go, so she stayed, and so did they, while the last of the ore burned down. It took longer than anyone had thought. When the flame shrank to a blue bead and then to nothing, a thread of smoke went up and hung under the roof, and one of the custodians let out a breath as if he had been holding it the whole while.

The south coast has a song of that day in more than one form. This is the shortest:

> *Dh'fhalbh an lasair às an tùr,*
> *tha solas ann, ach chan ann leinn.*
> The flame has gone out of the tower;
> there is a light there, but it is not ours.

At Baile chrom, on the Tuathaich coast, the change was made by Tuathaich fitters from Caol mhòr, because the Lighthouse Board had none of its own to send so far. The keeper's report names none of them.

After that the change came to everything, and it came fast. On {{date:V-0259}}, Bealltainn, the Rail Board ran its first dubhan engine on the ore line. It carried no ore. The drovers sold their oxen for lorries. On {{date:V-0263a}} the fitters at Seann Skell set a lorry's engine under a closed carriage for the high custodian of the Rite, the first carriage on the island that needed no horse, and on {{date:V-0278a}} buses began to run out of the capital to Inis mhòr and Cathair mhòr. The roadside inns put in dubhan lamps, and centuries of oil and wick were gone within a decade. At An Leòmhann Buidhe the landlord kept one wick lamp burning over the door beside the new ones, and a wick lamp over an inn door is still called a Leòmhann.

In {{year:V-0257}} Daniel Frayne died, and the Moot chose Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas, a mill manager. His old pupils rebound the Moot's minute-books from the first meeting onward. The Moot's minute says that a second set was made and hidden elsewhere. The library notes this and has not gone looking.

In {{year:V-0269}} the last working face under Muileann dhearg was closed. Its families were moved to the Hewers' Row at Seann Toll, and most of them went to work on the dubhan tanks at the river landing. Muileann dhearg was left with its chapel, its graves and a caretaker.

By {{year:V-0272}} *dubhan* meant the fuel and no longer the man, years before the trade built on it was whole. A letter of the Fèidh school at Caol shean coined *dubhan-tùsail* for a first attempt that looks like a dead end until it turns out to be the road. On {{date:V-0275}} the Guild of Hewers dissolved itself. Its rolls, its oath-stone of ore and its sick-roll went to the library. Its last master, Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh, wrote that the guild had outlived the mountain's patience.

By {{year:V-0278}} the markets, the army's supply lines and the shipping were all going over to the new fuel. The obsidian caste watched its own source of power fail and did not object. The house of Mac Ùisdein sold the last of its share in the spoil and put its money into dubhan shipping at Ros dhomhain, the only family of the vein that moved before the vein failed it.

In {{year:V-0282}} the fitters of dubhan engines were chartered as a guild at Seann Skell. Like every guild before it, its rolls were open to Ròdaich only, and the Tuathaich fitters were let go. They went home and fitted engines without a guild. That winter the Moot voted to take up the new fuel, and the delegates of the western pit towns got up and walked out. When the northern depots opened, Martha Greaves wrote in the minute that the north now bought its fire from the south, and she did not say whether that was good.

On {{date:V-0286}} the coinage was pegged to the making of dubhan in place of the obsidian reserves, which was a quiet way of owning which of the two now ran the kingdom. The obsidian ink was left out of the seal, and the seal was plain. Fearchar Bonn's first dies went to the library, where they lie beside the Hewers' oath-stone. The high custodian protested that a plain seal showed the vein was no longer held sacred. The council answered that the kingdom had never said it did. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh copied both letters into the chronicle. It is one of the few places in the whole record where the kingdom said plainly what it was.

The north has a rhyme from those years, heard there in both tongues. This is its Ròdais form:

> *Dubh an gual, 's dubh an dubhan,*
> *ach cò leis an teine?*
> Black the coal, and black the dubhan;
> but whose is the fire?

## XI. Of the Holdouts of the Western Hills

In the spring of {{year:V-0289}} the western pit towns met at Muileann àrsaidh, apart from the Moot, and agreed to go on working their seams and to sell their coal to any household in the north that would buy it. Àth naomh, with only a few old shafts to its name, joined them, saying it would rather be cold with its neighbours than warm alone. So a knot of northern towns refused to fold themselves into the new trade. They kept to the last workable coal seams sooner than hang their fires on one man's fuel. They were Àth leathan, Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg, and their spokeswoman was Ruth Calder, a pit captain's daughter from Àth leathan. The Moot neither joined nor condemned them. At Cnoc thais Tom Varley opened again two shafts that had lain abandoned since the humans' time. The coal was poor. It was theirs, and it kept the town warm.

In {{year:V-0295}} Ellen Marsh, a schoolteacher of Doire ghlas, finished eleven books of Tuathaich accounts of the Severance and the settling, taken down from the old people of the north. She asked Leabharlann Muileann chaol, formally, to keep the Tuathaich tellings beside the Ròdaich record. The request was tabled, and it lies on the table yet. Raghnall mac Mhuirich replied that it awaited the council's view on the standing of spoken witness. The council has never given one. Ellen Marsh wrote back that spoken witness had been good enough for the census clerks when they took the Moot's figures. Her letter is filed with the request. It is short, and nobody has answered it.

The holdout pits undersold dubhan across the north, and the Moot's own mills bought their coal. In {{year:V-0300}} the Board's northern revenue fell by a quarter, the first fall since the Severance, and its report named the western pits as the cause and the Leòid ruling as the cure. Eighty years after Eilidh nic Leòid gave it, her ruling was put to the use the north had always feared. The Board declared the western seams Crown property, holding that the right to work them had lapsed when the holders stopped paying the fifth. The holders answered that they had stopped paying when the Board stopped carrying the coal. Both were true. The pits were ordered shut.

A company of enforcers was posted to Seann Skell in the west, which is another town from the Rite's Seann Skell in the south. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh noted that it was the first armed company the kingdom had sent toward the north since the Severance. Martha Greaves wrote that the western towns would not shut their pits because of a letter, and asked for a year while the Moot talked with them. Her letter was received and filed.

In the autumn of {{year:V-0305}} the first skirmishes began. Each was too small to name and none was written down, and together they ran straight into open war. At the foot of that year Beathag wrote that clashes were reported in the west, and that she had asked for the Board's reports of them. They were not given. At Muileann àrsaidh the pit-heads were walled and the road from the south was dug across. On {{date:V-0308}}, which was Christmas Day, Ruth Calder wrote to the council for the western towns and offered the Crown's fifth in coal, if the Board would carry it and let the pits work until the seams were spent. The letter reached the library. No answer to it did.

In {{year:V-0309}}, in the depth of winter, the Moot resolved by nine votes that it was no party to the quarrel and would feed anyone who came to its towns hungry. That summer it filled its granaries at Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas beyond the usual. The minute gives no reason. It needed none.

In August Beathag laid down the office, and her clerk Sìleas nic Rath became royal chronicler. It is her record of the war that the library would later seal. In October came the order to mobilise. Regiments that had not been called out since the Severance were formed again and armed again, openly, months before anyone in power would admit that a war was coming. Command went to Somhairle mac Lachlainn, who had risen among the Board's enforcers. The rolls of the Severance companies were brought out of the council's chests to name the new regiments, and the old names were read aloud before new men. The list had been kept for nearly ninety years. Now it was used.

On {{date:V-0314}}, Christmas Day again, Martha Greaves came to Cathair dhearg with the Moot's petition.

She had kept the feast on the road, in a cold carriage, and she came into the capital in the grey forenoon with the frost still white on the bridge. The Ròdaich keep no feast that day, and the council was sitting. She waited on a bench in a passage for most of an hour, the petition on her knee. Then a clerk said her name, in the Ròdais way, so that for a moment she did not know it for hers.

She was the first speaker of the Moot ever to walk into the council chamber. It was smaller than she had thought, and warm with a dubhan stove. Twelve faces turned to her. She read the petition standing. It asked that the western pits be let alone to work out their seams, which would be spent within a few years in any case. She read it to the end without hurrying, and laid it on the table.

"Received," said the clerk, and wrote.

It was filed. That winter the Rail Board carried arms and dubhan west to Seann Bhral, and the yard at Muileann chrom worked on the trains day and night.

## XII. Of An Cogadh Fada, the Long War

*What is told here of the war comes from the open record, the towns' reports and the Tuathaich tellings; the rest is sealed.*

Late in January of {{year:V-0316}} the hewers of the five towns came together at Àth leathan, where the road from the south goes up into the hills. In the north they tell it like this. It was bitter weather, with a thin snow blowing that would not lie, and the men and women came in by the hill paths with their pit-lamps lit at noon, the sky was so dark. They slept in the pit-sheds, and in the byres with the cattle for the warmth. Ruth Calder went from fire to fire with a list, asking who was here, and who was coming. The Board's lists name her among them, and Tom Varley too.

From Cathair dhearg the regiments marched west, and their carts and lorries ran on dubhan.

The Battle of Àth leathan was fought on {{date:V-0318}}, in the spring, the first great battle of An Cogadh Fada. The state, already leaning on Dubhan's fuel to prop its fading power, moved against the holdouts with its own hand. The open record counts the kingdom's dead and not the holdouts'. The Tuathaich tellings put theirs in the hundreds. Ruth Calder's name does not appear in the open record after that day. Whether she fell there, no teller in the north will say for certain. Her name simply stops.

The holdouts gave up the southern pits and fell back into the hills round Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais. The pits of Àth leathan were flooded on the Board's order, and the field was left unploughed. The seventh regiment was raised on the west coast to hold the road behind the army. Before the month was out a wartime tithe was laid on every sale of dubhan in the kingdom, for the war's sake. It was never lifted. Fleets were raised and sent round the western cape to close the Tuathaich ports of the north-west to every boat but the fishing boats, and to search those.

In June the regiments invested Muileann àrsaidh. The town held out for weeks on stored coal and potatoes, and its pit-heads were taken one at a time. Sìleas nic Rath's open record gives the day it fell and nothing more. The army's dubhan was served before any other use, and the capital's poorer streets went dark again, as they had been before the fuel came.

The families of the western pits came into Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas with what they could carry. The Moot fed them from its own stores, as it had resolved to. The council sent nothing and forbade nothing. The Moot's minute records the families' names, and it is among the few Tuathaich records of the war that the library does not hold. Agnes Pike's old school at Doire ghlas was given over to them, and Ellen Marsh, who taught there, took down their accounts. This book would give a good deal to read them.

In August Cnoc thais was taken, after the regiments cut the road to its pits, and Tom Varley's two shafts were fired and sealed. The open record gives the number of holdouts taken and not the number who went on into the hills. Some went on past the hills and out onto the windy coast, beyond the shire lines, and the regiments did not follow them there. With the fleets away in the north-west, raiders out of the east took two salt-boats off Àth bheag and burned a third.

Through the autumn the kingdom went on raising regiments, as if it looked for a longer war. The fourth regiment was quartered at Ceann mhòr in the old human magistrate's house, and the Moot's protest was received.

In December the regiments came to Baile chrom on the night of the vigil.

The corporal at the head of the file had come up the coast road expecting shutters and dogs and shouting. There was nothing. Not a lamp showed in any window. No dog barked. The only sound was the sea on the stones and his own men's boots, and even they began to tread softer without being told.

He tried the first door. It was unlatched. Inside, by the grey of the window, a man and a woman and three children sat round a cold hearth with their hands in their laps. None of them spoke. He asked them, in Ròdais and then in the humans' tongue, who else was in the house, and whether there were arms. The man only shook his head, once, slowly, as a man does at a child who has asked a foolish thing in church.

It was the same in the next house, and the next, until the small hours. He wrote in the end that the whole town had been dark and silent, and that it had been searched and nothing found. The regiments' return for that month says as much, and no more.

That month Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg opened their roads without a siege. Their pits were flooded. The open record names no holdouts taken at either.

Late in October of the next year a column of holdouts, several hundred strong, came out of the western hills, and through November it moved south-east through the middle of the island toward the capital. The reason lies in the sealed archive. The Tuathaich tellings give its leader the name Owen Barrow; the open record gives him none. On {{date:V-0350}} the column passed Muileann òg and made camp on the fields by Muileann ghlas, a day's walk from Cathair dhearg, and the first and third regiments were brought down the fair road to meet it. The booths of the fair-ground were cleared for their camp, and the fair court's books for that year end without a closing entry.

The Battle of Muileann ghlas was fought on {{date:V-0352}}, four days after, in the short grey days before midwinter. It was the second great battle of the war, and the last. The holdouts were broken within a single year's campaigning. They were the children of humans once pushed to the island's edge, and now they were pushed back again, and the fuel that drove the lorries against them had been made by one of their own. The open record lists the first and third regiments on the field and does not count the column's dead. When word reached the hills, the holdouts still there came down to the seventh regiment's posts on the Àth leathan road. They were disarmed and sent home, and the open record counts their weapons.

So An Cogadh Fada ended on {{date:V-0355}}, twenty months after it began, and it has been called the Long War all the same. Nine regiments and four fleets were raised in it. All are under arms yet, and each is named for the town where it is stationed.

Before the year was out the Tuathaich had a new saying. *Chaidh am fadachadh*, they got long-warred, was said first of the defeat in the west, and then of any defeat that comes after holding out too long. It is said as a joke, and the people who say it do not laugh.

## XIII. Of the Peace, and of the Present Year

The regiments were not stood down. The council kept each at the town where it had been raised and paid them out of the wartime tithe. With the last organised resistance gone, Dubhan's fuel became, in all but law, compulsory across the whole island. The last western seams were flooded and capped with concrete, and after that no coal was dug on Rodos outside the Board's own works. The holdouts taken at Muileann ghlas were marched north as far as the boundary stones, the stones set in {{year:V-0003}} that nobody had ever been paid to move. The Moot's register gave their names a column of their own. Somhairle mac Lachlainn was given a seat on the council, the first soldier to sit there since the captains of the Severance war.

In {{year:V-0363}} the kingdom raised a stone at Cathair mhòr, beside the Mac Ùisdein market cross, with the names of the regiments' dead. On the field of Àth leathan the Tuathaich families built a cairn without leave. The seventh regiment's patrols have let it stand, and it grows by a stone or two every time they pass. At Muileann ghlas the field was sown again, and for years its farmer turned up weapons in the furrows and handed them to the first regiment, which keeps a list.

On {{date:V-0365}} the library's whole record of the Long War was sealed from the public for fifty years. It is the only sealed archive in the kingdom's history. What is still open for the war years is Sìleas nic Rath's list of places, the regiments present, and the counts she was allowed to give. Her full record went into the sealed room with everything else. Ailean Leabhar said once that the library settles nothing and only keeps. He never thought it would one day keep a thing it was not let read.

The war left its accounts. The Treasury carried the wartime tithe forward as a standing line under the regiments' pay, and no council minute records a decision to keep it. The fuel-fitters took the contract for the regiments' engines and doubled their rolls, and every new fitter was Ròdaich.

In June of {{year:V-0374}} Anna nic Phàil, councillor for Cathair dhomhain, brought the Moot's petition before the council: that the Tuathaich towns be paid for what they had lost in the war. She spoke for it once. On {{date:V-0375}} the proposal was raised in the capital and quietly let die without a vote. The Moot's minute on it is one line long: *Allowed to die.* Martha Greaves laid down the chair that year, and the Moot chose Peter Hale, great-grandson of its first speaker. The fourth regiment left the magistrate's house at Ceann mhòr. The Moot asked that the house be given to the town. It was given to the maor.

By {{year:V-0380}} the Tuathaich had one name for the tabled request and the dead motion together: *seann-chunntas*, an old reckoning that nobody in power will settle and nobody has struck out. Official Ròdaich usage has not taken up the word. This book uses it, since the record should hold the words of both peoples.

That year the mountain was shut, or nearly. The Board closed the upper galleries of the Sloc Mòr and kept one face open, in the Rite's custody, to cut ore for the altars. Twelve hewers were kept on to work it, the last of their trade on the island. On {{date:V-0385}} the last ore train ran to Cathair dhearg. The ore line has kept its name and carries dubhan, wool and soldiers. The second regiment set a guard at the library over the sealed archive, and Raghnall mac Mhuirich wrote to the council that the library had not asked for one. On the northern road, where the boundary stones stand, the sixth regiment keeps posts, and Tuathaich going south show a pass for the first time since the Severance. The council's minute calls the passes temporary. The reader will know by now what the kingdom's temporary measures tend to become.

The last year and this one have been like other years. Tuathaich wool was sold at Muileann òg on its northern day, through Ròdaich factors, as at the first fair. The Còmhrag was fought with staves and fists, and one man died. Ellen Marsh's eleven books have been copied by hand in the northern schools and are kept there; the library's catalogue lists the tabled request and not the books. The Moot met at Caol mhòr under Peter Hale, its minute-books unbroken since Walter Hale's wet afternoon, with no standing in Ròdaich law and no request for any. Every lamp and engine on the island, every ship and every lorry, runs on dubhan; the coinage is pegged to it, and the tithe is taken on every sale. The Treasury's roll for the year stood at 9,568 purses, two parts in three of it from the head-due. In the summer the Rite's twelve hewers cut the year's altar-ore. It filled four baskets, and the high custodian shared it among the houses by lot.

On {{date:V-0391b}}, as the summer went out, Dubhan himself, an old man now in his sixty-ninth year, won from the council at Cathair dhearg the right to sell dubhan off the island. He had written to the council for a year and sat before it three times. It is the first leave the kingdom has given for any good of Rodos to go beyond its waters since the Severance, and it is narrowly drawn. The Treasury sets each year's measure. The fuel is loaded only at Ros dhomhain, where a clerk of the Treasury takes the tithe on every barrel. The kingdom's depots and the Moot's are served first. The custodians of Òrd Bhrìde seal every hold, since nothing of the vein may be sold, and the Small-Burning Law is not touched by it. Dubhan has told neither the council nor the chronicler who his buyers are. The first tank-hull loaded under the charter went out past the western capes in the autumn, and at Seann Skell the priests of Manannan said the old prayer to keep the sea shut on the day she sailed. Dubhan goes out under that charter and no other.

On {{date:V-0393}}, with the lamps lit by mid-afternoon at Muileann chaol, Sìleas nic Rath wrote at the foot of the year that the kingdom's record is whole in every part but two: the war, which is sealed, and the north, which was not asked.

So the account stands on {{date:V-0394}}, at the turning of the year's shortest days. The coal is all but gone. The kingdom's trade runs on a name that once belonged to one man, and since the autumn a little of it has gone out past the western capes under his charter. The Ròdaich hold the capital and the sea. The Tuathaich hold the north, and their Church, and a grievance five years old, and a memory of the First Canoe that the people who twice defeated them still call folklore. The restored kingdom promised the island its own again. It kept the humans' tariff, their surveys, their rails and their terms for the mountain, and it kept the humans' children where the humans had put them. It kept faith as well with the drowned towns of the south, and its wells at Achadh shean still draw water.

Here the Fifth Book stops, at the present year, for the chronicle itself goes on. The age that follows has no name yet, because nobody can yet say what began it. It is left to the next chronicler, who will be handed the whole record and, with it, the things still missing from it: the war in its guarded room, which is to be opened fifty years from its sealing if the kingdom keeps its word; the fourth floor at Muileann chaol, built for what was not sent; the eleven books in the north; and the Moot's minutes with their hidden second set. The old reckoning is still owed. Whoever writes the next book should begin by asking for them.
