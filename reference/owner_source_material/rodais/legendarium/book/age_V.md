# The Book of the Five Ages

# Book the Fifth: An Aois Ùr, the New Age

*1930 – 2026 DE*

> *Tha e scrìobhte a-nis.*
> It is written now.
>
> — a minute of the Moot at Caol mhòr, 6 an t-Samhain 1993 DE, entire, as the library renders it

## I. Of the Severance and the Settling of the North

The Age of Strangers ended with the crossing shut. Whatever had let the humans come over the western water closed behind the last of their ships, and those of them still on Rodos were marched north under the council's guard into the north-western country. That was the Severance. The Ròdaich called it a deliverance and the northerners called it nothing at all for a generation. The New Age begins in its wake, and the first thing the record says of it is that in 1930 the Tuathaich homeland was fixed: the human descendants stranded on the island were formally settled in the north. It was a confinement and not a homecoming. Their grandchildren would call it home anyway, because it was the only home they had. Two of the immortal generation, Cian and Lorccan, were not yet twenty and grew up in the wake of it, and the chronicle has nothing more to say of them in this age.

The victors began by putting things away. At Cathair dhearg the war-companies that had fought the last campaign against the human administration were stood down, and their rolls were handed to the council rather than burned. The companies were never abolished. They survived as names on a list, and the list was kept, and nearly ninety years later someone would take it out. On the roads running north, at Caol mhòr and elsewhere, stones were set where Tuathaich territory began. No wall followed them. Many are still standing at the roadside, because nobody has been paid to move them.

The north went short in its first year alone. It had lived on stores that came over the water from an Tìr Thall, and nothing came now. Tuathaich memory, written down long afterwards, puts the dead at Doire ghlas that winter above two hundred. No Ròdaich count was made. The library has learned to mark such places in the record, where one side counted and the other did not bother to.

In the south, council clerks went into the human counting-houses at Cathair dhearg, Ros dhomhain and Seann Skell and carried off everything written. Whatever the war had left of the tithe ledgers was boxed with the rest and left unread, because few of the clerks could read the humans' hand. In the north-west the human harbour officers at Ceann mhòr, Cathair gheal and Inis àrsaidh had gone with the last ships and taken their keys. The Tuathaich fishermen broke the locks on the harbour stores and shared out what they found, household by household. There is no record that anyone objected.

On 27 an t-Iuchar 1931 DE the state styled itself Rìoghachd Ròdais, a kingdom. It was a kingdom in form, whatever it remained in function, and its own records still carry that contradiction. That autumn a royal council of twelve was seated at Cathair dhearg, drawn from the custodian families and the captains of the Severance war. Its minute-books are the oldest continuous record of the state in this age. They are not the oldest continuous record of the age, though. A month earlier, at Caol mhòr, Tuathaich householders from the northern towns had met and chosen a speaker, Walter Hale, a former harbour clerk. The Moot had no standing in Ròdaich law. It met anyway, and kept minutes, and those minutes run unbroken from that meeting to the present year. For most of this age the two books were kept a long way apart, and they seldom mention each other.

The kingdom found out quickly how much of the humans' work it would keep. At Ros dhomhain, its largest port, the customs sheds passed to Ròdaich harbour-masters. The first of them, Dùghall mac Iain, kept the humans' tariff in force because he had no other, and spent years translating it line by line. In 1932 the council sent a ship out past the western capes to look for the humans' crossing. It came back after a month of open water and reported nothing: no land, no ship, no passage. No second ship was sent. The chroniclers do not say whether the council was relieved.

## II. Of Niall and the Making of the Shires

Niall was crowned at Cathair dhearg on 7 an Dùbhlachd 1932 DE, the first ruler of the restored kingdom and the first name in a line that the state's chronicles keep unbroken from then on. The crowning was so free of dispute that *mar a thàinig Niall*, "the way Niall came", became a saying for any smooth handing-over of power. Its first written use is not in any council book. It is in a tavern ledger in the capital, beside a debt settled without argument.

The kingdom was then drawn into shires. The council named each *siorrachd* for its seat and kept most of the human district lines under the new names. The humans' surveys were the only complete ones on the island. The kingdom used them and did not say so. A *maor* was appointed to each shire to collect the Crown's dues and keep its peace. In the Tuathaich shires the maoir were Ròdaich men lodged in the seat towns. At Ceann mhòr the maor took the old human magistrate's house, and the townspeople went on calling it by its old name. At Baile Mòr mhòr the first maor, Coinneach mac Ailein, held his court under the market roof because the humans' courthouse had burned. He kept it there after a new courthouse was built, and so has every maor after him.

Then the sea came in. On 2 an Gearran 1933 DE the Great Wave, Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol, struck the southern coast without warning and wrecked Cnoc chaol, Cuan dhearg and Achadh mhin in one night. It was the first disaster the new state met on its own terms. When the relief parties from Cathair dhearg reached Cuan dhearg they counted the dead of the three settlements at a little over nine hundred. The harbour was gone. The boats were found in the fields behind the town. The fields of Achadh mhin lay under salt and would not bear, and the council moved the survivors to higher ground and paid for their seed out of the treasury. That was the first relief the restored kingdom ever granted, and it was granted well. Cnoc chaol was rebuilt on the slope behind the old town, out of the water's reach. The old site was left as the water had left it. The townspeople call it an Tràigh Bhàthte, the drowned strand, and do not build there. The wave also crossed the low island of Eilean mhin, where no one then lived all year, and the shepherds who had summered there did not go back.

From the survivors' accounts the south took a word. *Tonn*, wave, came to mean anything sudden and overwhelming, and within a generation it was said across the south of a man caught in a change he could not stop: *tha e san tonn*, he is in the wave of it. The south would have need of the phrase.

In 1934 the human coinage was withdrawn, every remaining piece of it, and within a year a Rìoghachd coinage replaced it. A mint was set up in the old human counting-house at Cathair dhearg under Fearchar mac Dhòmhnaill, called Fearchar Bonn. The seal of each coin was pressed with an ink ground from obsidian dust, so that a clipped coin showed colour at its edge. The coin was honest, and it was also sacred, and the kingdom liked it to be both.

The north paid for it. Tuathaich households had kept their savings in human coin. With the withdrawal those savings were worth their weight in metal and nothing more. The Moot petitioned the council for an exchange, and the petition was received and filed. It is the first petition in this book to meet that end, and there will be many more. At Doire mhin a smith named Joseph Marlow melted his neighbours' dead coin and cast it into ploughshares, which he sold back to them for the price of the charcoal. The Tuathaich still sing of him, in their own tongue. A clerk of the library once took down a verse and gave it in Ròdais prose: *he turned the king's silence into iron, and the iron into bread.*

The new coin did not reach everywhere at once. At Inis thais on the south-west coast it was short for most of a year, and the merchants traded in chits written on the Wolf house's paper. When the coin came the chits were redeemed at the Wolf house door, and the house kept a tenth. The faith of Clach has always been good at arithmetic.

## III. Of the Rite, the Faiths and the Stones on the Road

In 1935 the Obsidian Rite became Creideamh nan Ròdach, an ordered doctrine and no longer a diffuse reverence. It was built around the leyline and the unbroken line of the obsidian-touched. Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was named Àrd-choimheadaiche, high custodian, and seated at Seann Skell on the southern coast, where the Rite's largest house stood. His first charge was a written order of worship. There had never been one.

The Hawk houses round Cnoc bheag would not take it. Creideamh an t-Seabhaig was the Rite's older form, and its houses held that it needed no custodian above the local altar. They were not compelled, and the long quarrel between the two forms of the Rite begins there. The Wolf houses of the south-west, whose god Clach was never the Rite's, were asked to send witnesses to the formalizing. They sent none, and a letter wishing it well. The house of Creideamh an Aon-adharcaich Dhuibh at Cathair dhearg, the capital's own faith, was given the keeping of the coronation regalia, as it had held them before the humans came. The high custodian asked for a share of that keeping and was refused. So within a year of its founding the orthodox Rite had one house that disowned it, one that ignored it politely, and one that outranked it at court. It was a fair picture of the kingdom's faiths for the rest of the age.

In the north the old ways were kept quietly. The Tuathaich went on with their own folk tradition, Creideamh nan Tuathach, and with it the fragment called the First Canoe, which the Ròdaich priesthood calls superstition. The library has never been shown the fragment. It records that it exists and that it is kept. At Baile chrom in the far north-west, in 1938, the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane held the rites of Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise in the open for the first time since the Severance. The cult keeps one night of silence a year for Sìne, the Peaceful Goose, and outsiders are not told which night it is. Most Tuathaich towns worshipped Cnut, the Yellow Scorpion, as the Ròdaich of the west coast did. How the settlers came to that god the record does not say, and the chroniclers have learned not to guess. A house of Creideamh na Scairpe Buidhe was built at Cnoc ghorm, the largest in the north, and in 1939 it opened a school for its priests that taught in the humans' tongue and read the scripture in the Ròdais text the west-coast houses used. For a long time it was the only place in the north where both were taught.

In the east, followers of Seann Spioradan nan Ròdach began to walk each summer to the holy well Allt an Àigh, whose water the old tales call healing. The custodians discouraged the walk and did not forbid it. The walkers lodged at Achadh gharbh, and the village built them a long hall. Its keeper wrote each walker's shire in a book, and by the end of the decade the book held names from fifty shires.

In those years the council considered, once, whether the two peoples should be divided by a wall. It was proposed in 1939 and rejected. The records suggest it was rejected as unnecessary rather than on principle: the two populations were already keeping apart. The minute that rejects it runs to four lines. The fourth reads *They do not come south.* Five of the twelve councillors initialled it, and the other seven did not sign against it. The library keeps that minute in a case. It is a small document, and a good deal of the age is in it.

The law came north in the same fashion. In 1936 the council appointed circuit justices, the first of them Eilidh nic Leòid of Cathair dhomhain, whose circuit covered eleven shires of the south-west and took most of a year to ride. Sitting at Seann Bhral in 1937 she ruled that land held by Tuathaich under human title passed to the Crown, while the right to work it stayed with the holders and their heirs. The Leòid ruling is cited in nearly every land case of the age. When she rode the northern circuit in 1944 and held court at Ceann mhòr, seventeen of the nineteen cases brought to her were Tuathaich suits over land. She found for the Crown in fifteen, citing her own ruling. She died at Cathair dhomhain the next spring. Her rulings were bound as one book, the first law-book of the restored kingdom, and every circuit justice after her has carried a copy. Nobody in the record calls her unjust. She was exact, and the law she was exact about had been written for one people.

Walter Hale asked in 1936 for leave to open schools in the north that taught in the humans' tongue. The council answered that the kingdom's schools taught in Ròdais and that it had no others. The Moot's minute calls the answer "clear, at least." In 1944 a Tuathaich teacher, Agnes Pike, opened a school at Doire ghlas anyway, with Moot money and without leave. Within ten years there were nine such schools in the north. The council never licensed them and never closed them. That was how the kingdom dealt with the north for most of the age. It did not forbid things there, and it did not help.

## IV. Of the Mountain, the Guilds and the Fair

In 1940 the mine's yield matched, and then passed, what it had been in the colonial years. The method had not changed. Only the owners had.

The Crown granted the working of the Sloc Mòr, the great shaft at Achadh dhomhain, to Uisdean mac Ùisdein of Cathair mhòr. His house had held the overseer's post under the humans. The terms gave the Crown a fifth of the yield; they were the humans' terms with the names changed. The council declined to end the continuous working of the vein, day and night, that the humans had ordered two generations before, so the mountain was still given no season of rest. The custodians of the Rite entered a protest and then blessed the shafts. The hewers had a saying for it, which is still heard in the pit-towns that remain:

> *Cha d' fhuair a' bheinn fois a-riamh,*
> *is cha d' fhuair sinne.*
> The mountain never got its rest,
> and neither did we.

Families from the hungry east came to the vein-towns for wages. Muileann dhearg doubled in three years, and its hall of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh was enlarged twice. Baile thais, where the ore road reached the east coast, filled with carters, inns and a ropewalk. The humans' railway had been broken in three places in the Severance war. It was repaired to Muileann chrom, and in 1941 it was carried up the valley to the pithead. The first ore train from the Sloc Mòr to Cathair dhearg ran under the kingdom's name on the humans' rails. At Muileann chrom the Rail Board laid sidings where the ore trains were broken up and made up again for the ports. The families who worked the small shunting engines there were counted separately by the Board and lived apart in rows by the lines. The spoil of the Sloc Mòr, a grey stone veined with black, was cut and sold under the house of Mac Ùisdein's quarry-mark, and for forty years the public buildings of the south were built of it. Nobody minded that the kingdom's courthouses were made of what the mountain had thrown away.

In 1942 the old apprentice-binding custom of the Holy Age was revived in a changed form, now reserved in so many words to Ròdaich applicants. The Guild of Hewers was chartered at Achadh dhomhain first. Its oath was taken with a hand on a piece of ore, and its rolls were open to Ròdaich only, as the charter required. The shipwrights of Ros dhomhain followed and bound thirty apprentices in one day on the slipway. Then came the chandlers of Ceann àrsaidh, whose monopoly on supplying the Crown's patrol boats outlasted the patrol boats; the lamp-makers of Muileann mhin, whose flame-over-hammer mark was stamped on every ore lamp on the island; and the weavers of Baile chiar. At Cuan shean and Seann Bhral, Tuathaich boys already bound to Ròdaich masters were released from their indentures under the charter rule. Some went north. Most stayed on as day-labour, outside the guilds and their wages. The charter rule did not drive them out. It kept them where the work was worst paid.

Out on the north-western water, ships that flew no flag began to raid the coast off Cathair gheal. Some were crewed by men who had served the human administration. The Tuathaich towns suffered first and were not defended. In 1949, having asked the council twice and been sent nothing, the fishermen of Cathair gheal armed their own boats and drove the raiders out of the bay. The council then fined the town for bearing arms without licence, and the town paid. In the same year the house of Mac Ùisdein raised a market cross at Cathair mhòr with its name cut on all four faces. The wool and ore markets were moved to its foot.

On 16 an Dàmhair 1950 DE the fair at Muileann òg was established, and it became the island's largest recurring gathering, drawing trade from every province the state controlled. Its first toll-mistress, Oighrig nic Mhathain, set its rules: a stall-fee in coin, a court of the fair for disputes, and Tuathaich goods sold only through Ròdaich factors. The rules are still read aloud when each fair opens. The road from the capital was widened and metalled for the fair's carts. Dùn chrom, which stood on it, took a toll at its gate and built a new southern wall with the money. The townspeople call it the fair's wall. The carters call it something else, and the library has chosen not to record what.

Two years later the Còmhrag began at Ceann leathan: a yearly melee that was called a martial tradition and served more and more as a release for a people with fewer and fewer other outlets. The first champion the record remembers is Calum mac Aonghais, called Calum Ruadh, a hewer from Muileann chrom. Two men died in the melee he won. The prize was a year's wages and the right to carry arms in any shire. Ceann leathan built four inns for the crowds, and the fair-wardens who took a share of every room became the Còmhrag's keepers in all but name.

On 7 an t-Ògmhios 1953 DE there was a cluster of births in the shunting families, the generation called Splatter and Dodge's. Seachran and Brean were among them. They would grow up to be the closest allies of a man not yet born. The record does not say in which yards they were born, and the compiler of this book will not guess.

## V. Of the Library at Muileann chaol

On 1 an Cèitean 1945 DE, which was Bealltainn, a great library was founded at Muileann chaol. It is the institution that now keeps the fullest surviving record of everything in this book, and the reader should remember that. Its first keeper was Ailean mac Iain, called Ailean Leabhar. He brought with him the council's minute-books and what survived of the humans' tithe ledgers, and spent his first years having them read. Within a few years *a chur san Leabharlann*, to put it in the Library, was said of anything made settled and beyond dispute. Ailean Leabhar is recorded as disliking the phrase. The library settles nothing, he said; it only keeps. His successors have repeated that, and the saying has outlasted them all.

The first reading room opened to anyone with a letter from a shire maor. The first reader in its book was a shipwright of Ros dhomhain who wanted the humans' hull plans. Copyists, binders and paper-makers settled round the library, the town took a market charter, and its first market was held in the library's yard. A paper-mill was built on the stream below Tobar dhìreach, and for half a century nearly every page of the royal chronicle was written on its paper.

In 1947 an office was created to keep the kingdom's record, the direct ancestor of the archive this book is drawn from. The first royal chronicler was Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, a council clerk. She kept her record at Muileann chaol and not at the capital, and wrote in her first entry that a record kept beside the throne is kept for the throne. She sent clerks to every shire to copy parish books, grave-lists and harbour rolls. The returns from the south filled forty chests. The returns from Siorrachd Àth àrsaidh and the other northern shires filled one, because few Tuathaich would speak to the clerks. At Inis bheag on the north-east coast the clerks were shown a parish book that went back before the humans came, kept in the Seann Spioradan house there. The keeper let them copy it and would not let them take it.

Other things came in with the returns. Fishermen of Achadh àrsaidh reported a creature longer than their boats in the waters off the north-west cape, and Gormshuil set down the report with a note that the raiders sheltered in the same waters. A royal clerk sent to count the flocks on Eilean dhubh, the largest of the outer isles, let the islanders count them for him, and the dues were set on their count. That same year a storm drowned eleven Tuathaich fishermen off Cuan bheag. The village had no lifeboat. The Moot bought one by subscription and asked the council to pay half, and the council paid a third.

The five lighthouses built under the humans were given Ròdaich keepers by royal warrant. At Taigh-solais Baile chrom, the one beacon on the Tuathaich coast, the keeper was a Ròdaich man lodged in a Tuathaich town, and his warrant told him to report what he saw on land as well as at sea. In 1961 he reported that the Grey Night cult kept its vigil in the dark of the lighthouse's own shadow. The council took no action. On the east coast the Seann Spioradan houses built a common hall at Seann Dunn, where the ancestors of forty families are named aloud every year by their living heads. The list was copied to the library the year after, and it has grown by a few names each year since.

The library also dug. In 1960 its diggers opened trenches at Làrach an Dùin on the south-east coast and found walls older than any Ròdaich building and no writing at all. Ailean Leabhar had the finds catalogued as belonging to the Old Ones, purpose unknown. At Làrach an Teampaill in the east they measured the ruin and did not dig it, because the Seann Spioradan houses of the district asked them not to, and the keeper entered it as *measured and let be.* A scribe copied the inscription on the obelisk near Cnoc bheag stroke by stroke. It matched no script known on the island. The copy was filed and the obelisk fenced. Near Doire uaine, in 1967, herders refused to graze the hills where old inscriptions tell of a thing that is dead and does not rest. A shepherd went missing there, and the magistrate recorded wolves. The Seann-Dhaoine appear in this age, as in every age, only as what they left and what the living would not do near it.

Ailean Leabhar died in 1969, and Mòrag nic Coinnich became keeper. She began the practice, kept ever since, of writing each item's source beside the item. Two years later Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill laid down the chronicle. Her last entry is a list of what the record did not hold: the northern towns' own accounts, the humans' treaties in their own tongue, and the Mine Board's surveys. Her successor, Tormod mac Ailein, called Tormod Pinn for the speed of his hand, copied the list into the front of his first book and did not add to it. The compiler of this book notes that he did not strike anything from it either.

## VI. Of Brìghde and Eòghan, and the First Word of the Depletion

Niall died in 1951. His daughter Brìghde was crowned on 31 an Lùnastal 1951 DE and succeeded without contest: a mortal succession in an aristocracy that stood very close to the undying. Nobody remarked on it then. It looks strange now. Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill wrote that the succession came *mar a thàinig Niall*. It was the first time the saying appeared in the royal record, and the only idiom she let into the chronicle in all her years.

In 1954 the Scorpion house at Seann Bhral, the oldest on the west coast, refused to admit Tuathaich worshippers who had come from the north for its feast. Its keeper said the god was the same and the house was not. The following year the northerners held the feast at Cnoc ghorm instead. They have kept it there every year since, and within a generation it was larger than the western one.

On 12 am Màrt 1955 DE the survey reports began to note, quietly, that the vein's yield was falling. The survey behind them was the work of Iain mac Ruairidh, called Iain Mapa, of the Mine Board. His measurements of the faces of the Sloc Mòr showed the vein thinning to the east and the south. The Board filed the survey without circulating it and moved Iain Mapa to the silver works at Muileann chiar. The next year the Board moved its own clerks from Cathair dhearg to Cathair mhòr, to be nearer the mountain, and made a rule that its surveys were kept there and not sent to the library. The state did not act on the reports for years. The library could not have known what it was not sent. It knows now, and it records the delay as part of the Depletion.

In 1956 a Ròdaich magistrate at Baile chrom forbade the Grey Night gatherings as disorderly and fined Hannah Crane. The vigil moved indoors and went on. In the same year the harbour at Cuan dhearg, lost to the Great Wave, reopened with a new mole of dressed stone. The first boat in was one of those found in the fields behind the old town, refloated. The harbour-master kept it as a relic. Early in 1957 the fields of Achadh mhin bore again, and some of the families moved after the Wave came back to them. The shire gave them their old boundaries out of the humans' survey.

Brìghde's reign ended young. No cause was made public. Her cousin Eòghan was crowned on 11 an Gearran 1957 DE, and for the first time the restored kingdom had a disputed succession. The royal chronicler's entry for it gives the fact and nothing more. No chronicler after Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill has added anything, and this book will not either, because there is nothing in the library to add. That December, for the first time, the council's minutes record votes against a succession. They name the councillors who voted and not their reasons. Two of them did not sit again. The library has those names. It does not have the reasons, and it does not guess at them.

On 9 an Cèitean 1958 DE Dubhan was born, a mortal, in the confinement the Severance had made, decades after the war that had closed the island to the people his line came from. The chronicle of that year does not mention him. No chronicle of that year could have.

The kingdom's affairs went on round the Depletion it had not announced. Uisdean mac Ùisdein's house at Cathair mhòr was rebuilt in the grey spoil-stone of the Sloc Mòr, the largest private house in the kingdom, and the Guild of Hewers held its feast in the new hall. Licences to fish the waters south and east of Caol mhòr were reserved to Ròdaich boats, and the Tuathaich boats were left the northern grounds, which the raiders also worked. Doire shean in the south-west built a stone harbour for the Wolf country's boats with money lent by the Wolf house, which took the dues. In 1962 Cathair fhada built a Crown grain-store big enough to feed the south for a season.

The second census, in 1962, measured the gap between Ròdaich and Tuathaich living for the first time instead of simply noticing it. The report was not made public. One census clerk, Seonag nic Ille-Mhoire, left a note among her papers saying that she had asked to see the report and been refused. The note reached the library after her death, and it is the only mention of the report outside the council. The library knows the report existed because a clerk wrote down that she had not been allowed to read it.

That spring Walter Hale died at Caol mhòr, and the Moot chose Mary Coyle, a fisherman's widow, to follow him. She was the first woman to hold the chair. In 1963 the Moot paid for two Tuathaich women to be taught medicine by a Ròdaich physician at Àth àrsaidh, who took them against his guild's rule. He was struck from the guild's rolls. They practised in the north for forty years. In the same year the Abhainn ìseal, the longest river on the island, broke its banks on its lower reach and carried away three bridges and a length of the ore line's embankment, and the ports lived on stored ore for most of a year.

In 1964 the Hawk houses of Siorrachd Cnoc bheag named a custodian of their own, Raonaid nic Iain, and seated her at Inis ìseal. The high custodian at Seann Skell refused to recognise her, and the houses carried on as though he had. She wrote out the Hawk's order of worship, which had always been spoken and never written. The houses read it aloud once a year and keep the written copy locked away.

In 1965 six fighters died at the Còmhrag, the most in any year yet. The custodians petitioned for it to end. The fair-wardens of Ceann leathan, whose town lived off the crowds, refused. An Siorcas Siubhail Do-thuigsinn, the travelling show first recorded near Seann Chwen in 1954, pitched at Ceann leathan that year and played to the crowds who stayed on after the melee. The fair-wardens are said to have paid it to come. On its first recorded showing the chronicler's clerk could not say what the acts were meant to show, and added that neither could the crowd, who came back the next night. The ore line reached Ceann leathan's quays that year. Ros bheag opened a shipyard for ore-ships, and the silver at Muileann chiar proved richer at depth than anyone had expected. Iain Mapa, still working there, measured the new faces.

Then, in 1968, a gallery gave way under Muileann dhearg in the reopened Cill ghlas district, along a line the surveyors had marked as sound. Thirty-one hewers died. It was small beside the Rending, but it was the same fault. New excavation rules followed. They applied to Ròdaich workers, and the records suggest that enforcement at the sites near the Tuathaich country was inconsistent on purpose. At the pits round Àth leathan, where Ròdaich overseers worked Tuathaich hands, the timbering rules were posted and never paid for. The overseers' books, which later reached the library, show the timber ordered and not delivered. Àth leathan will come into this book again.

## VII. Of the Search for a Second Vein

After Muileann dhearg forty hewers' families left Achadh dhomhain for the southern fishing ports. The Guild of Hewers bound sixty apprentices to fill its rolls, and none of them were Tuathaich. Fire ran through the stalls at Muileann òg in 1969. The fair's court sat in a field the next day and the fair went on, and Oighrig nic Mhathain, toll-mistress since the first fair, died of her burns. That winter the Rail Board refused to carry goods consigned by the Moot, citing the fair's rule on factors. So the north's wool went south by cart, a week on the road, by Cnoc ghorm, Àth fhada and the old road down to Inis àrsaidh, and the Tuathaich villages along the way opened inns for the carters. In the north it is called the Carters' Road. In the south it has no name.

In 1970 the Moot began a register of Tuathaich hewers killed or maimed in the western pits, since no Ròdaich book counted them. Mary Coyle's clerks entered the first year's names on the families' word. The register has been kept to this day and has never been sent south. In the same years Dubhan was a child in the north, watching an undying caste hold every real advantage on an island his own ancestors had been pushed to the edge of. The obsidian was never his to inherit. The chronicle records that, and nothing else of his childhood.

The age produced keepers of other kinds as well. When the dry years came back to Achadh shean in 1972, the council sent grain up the ore line from the store at Cathair fhada, paid for out of the Sloc Mòr's share. No one in the shire died of hunger, and the chronicler recorded that too, because it deserved recording. The Rail Board charged the council for the carriage. The maor had twelve wells dug to the rock along the drove road, and four of them are still in use. When raiders burned the salt-sheds near Baile ìseal in 1973 and the patrol boats came two days late, Murchadh mac Coinnich, keeper of Taigh-solais Baile ìseal, began entering in the lighthouse log every unlit ship he saw, with its heading and the hour. He kept it up for most of his life. His logs are the fullest record of the eastern raiders the library has, and the raiders' ships appear in the royal record under the names the fishermen of Àth bheag gave them, since they flew no flag. When the keeper of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg drowned going out to a boat on the reef, his daughter Ciorstaidh nic Artair saved its crew and was given his warrant. She had an iron post set on the reef, painted and hung with a bell. No boat went aground there while she kept the light.

In 1974 the Còmhrag drew its largest crowd yet, and the champion was Ealasaid nic Coinnich of Àth ghlas, the first woman to win it. She did not fight again. Cnoc bheag, the great Hawk port, passed Cathair mhòr in population. In 1976 the Rite offered the Hawk houses a place under the high custodian with their own order of worship. They accepted the place and kept the order. The quarrel was quieter after that. It was not over.

On 12 an t-Samhain 1975 DE Feallsanachd an Fhèidh woke from its long sleep. It had been dormant since the colonial years. Its founding question was why the vein answers some hands and not others, and that question mattered again now that the vein was running short. Its first teacher was Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain, who lectured in the sacred wood near Muileann chiar, where the school had last met before the humans came. She put the old question plainly. The houses of Siorrachd Baile gharbh reopened, and their teachers walked to the wood to hear her and walked home again. At Caol shean the school took a house for the wet months. Its students copied the census note on the obsidian-touched and argued over it openly, which the Rite did not do. In 1978 they tried to read the pillar near Cnoc chiar against the obelisk at Cnoc bheag and the column at Cnoc ghorm. They found signs in common and no meaning. Seonaid wrote that the Old Ones had been here and had said so, and that nobody living could hear it.

In 1979 Alasdair mac Ùisdein, grandson of the first holder of the Sloc Mòr, borrowed against his house's share to pay for a search for a second vein. The Mine Board lent its surveyors and the Crown lent its name. Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain wrote to the Board that the search asked the wrong question. The school had always asked why the vein answered some hands, she said, and nobody had asked why there was only one vein. The Board filed the letter.

The search failed. The borings ran from the hills above Dùn dhearg to the moors south of Baile ruadh. Forty-one shafts were sunk and none struck the vein. Afterwards Mòrag nic Coinnich found a Holy Age list in the library that named the same hills as searched and barren. The kingdom had repeated a failure its own library could have warned it about, if anyone had thought to ask the library. Dùn dhearg sued the house of Mac Ùisdein for spoiled grazing and won, and the house paid a third before it sold. In 1981 it sold half its share in the Sloc Mòr back to the Crown and let its stone house to the Mine Board. The dry shafts near Baile ruadh were left open. Sheep fell into them, and one child, before the shire fenced them at its own cost. The shire's claim for the fencing was never paid. The mills of Muileann dhomhain, which had ground grain for the vein-towns, turned to grinding bark for the tanners. At Seann Tarr the rope-makers took the last charter the restored custom gave to a trade the humans had never touched. The shire kept the charter in its own chest and sent only a list of what the chest held to Cathair dhearg.

Some things in the record do not add up to anything. In 1982 the circus pitched on the common near Inis àrsaidh, and the magistrate who licensed it entered its acts as "various" and its fee as paid. In 1983 a fighter was killed at the Còmhrag with a hewer's pick. Edged tools were banned after that, and staves and fists were still allowed, so the deaths went on, fewer. In 1984 Mary Coyle laid down the Moot's chair. Her successor, Daniel Frayne of Muileann leathan, was the first speaker born after the Severance. He had taught young Tuathaich to copy and bind books because the southern guilds would not take them, and Muileann leathan bindings have since been found in Ròdaich libraries without their makers' names. The same winter the fair's court at Muileann òg set aside one day for goods from the northern shires, still sold through Ròdaich factors but under the northern makers' own marks. It was the first time a Tuathaich name was written on anything sold there. The first such mark was the cloth of the Doire ghlas weavers, a grey twill with a white thread at the selvedge. Within the year the weavers of Baile chiar were weaving a white thread into their own.

On 11 am Màrt 1985 DE the Depletion could no longer be denied. The last untouched sections of the vein were identified, and they were smaller than anyone wanted to admit. The Board's surveyor-general, Catrìona nic Neacail, published the map of them, and it fitted on one sheet. She resigned within the year and went to teach at the Fèidh school at Caol shean. Among the Board's files she found Iain Mapa's survey of thirty years before, which showed the same thinning, and she sent a copy to the library with a note of the date the Board had received it. Iain Mapa died at Muileann chiar in 1986. His own copy of the survey was found among his papers with the Board's receipt pinned to it. He had kept the proof that he had told them.

Muileann dhearg lost a third of its households to the ports within a few years. Its Fèidh hall closed, and its bell was sold to the school at Caol shean, where it still rings for lectures. Ore ships at Ceann leathan waited for cargoes that came in half-loads, and the fair-wardens raised the Còmhrag's gate-price to make up the harbour dues.

## VIII. Of Mairead and the Failing of the Ore

Mairead was crowned on 29 an Dàmhair 1987 DE. Eòghan's reign had ended in the decade the Depletion became public, and she inherited a kingdom whose founding resource was visibly failing for the first time since the restoration. The Guild of Hewers bound no apprentices that year for the first time since its charter, and its masters voted to keep the rolls open to members' sons and daughters only. At Muileann shean in the north a school opened for hewers' children who would not be bound, teaching them to read, reckon and survey. Most of its first pupils went to the ports. Baile Mòr dhomhain in the northern hills became the market for the hill country's wool and hides, and for two years its market day drew more carts than the fair at Muileann òg.

The north began building things that did not depend on the mountain. In 1988 wool mills went up at Doire ghlas with Moot money. They were the north's first works tied to nothing in the vein. The Rail Board refused their cloth. At Seann Bhral the circuit justice Ruairidh mac Phàdraig ruled that the Rail Board was a carrier of the Crown and had to carry for anyone who paid the rate. It was the first ruling the Moot ever won in a Ròdaich court. The Board appealed, and the council let the appeal lapse. The library has sometimes been asked why the council let it go. The honest answer is that the minute does not say, and the likeliest reason is that by then cloth mattered more than ore.

In 1989 the Crown bought the last of the house of Mac Ùisdein's working shafts, and from then on the vein was worked by the Board in the Crown's name alone. Copper was found behind Àth fhiadhaich, and the Board opened a working there with men laid off from the Sloc Mòr. It paid wages. It was not the vein, and the town's Seann Spioradan house would not bless it. When the miners named their new street for the Sloc Mòr, the house objected to that as well.

On 1 an t-Iuchar 1990 DE Dubhan began his experiments: early attempts, all failures, to make a substitute fuel from the byproducts of mining. The historical record barely troubles to mention them. The chronicler of the day had no way to know of them. In the same month the lowest galleries of the Sloc Mòr were allowed to flood, because the pumps cost more to run than the ore under them was worth. The Board's minute calls it a pause. The next year the great pumps were sold to the harbour at Seann Bhral to drain its new dry dock, and they were the only machines of the vein still working a generation later.

The chronicle of those years is full of small alarms. In 1991 a great movement of panthers crossed the south-west near Baile dhomhain, and the herders lost stock for a month. Tormod Pinn recorded it as a thing named in the Holy Age lists and not seen since, and he set his entry, the longest of his year, beside the old list, which named the year after the panthers as a hungry one. The tanners of Baile ghorm bought the hides, and panther-skin saddles sold at the fair for a decade. The lamp-makers of Muileann mhin halved their output for lack of ore to burn in the lamps they made, and shaft-lamps were lent from pit to pit. The guild's minute-book records who lent to whom. At Muileann chrom the yard families stopped the ore trains for three days over cut wages. The Rail Board settled on the fourth day and dismissed eleven men on the fifth. Then the harvest after the panthers was a good one across the south, and Tormod Pinn drew a line through his own note of the year before. The compiler of this book has always liked him for that.

The third census, in 1993, published its figures openly for the first time in the kingdom's history. It was a small, symbolic reform and changed nothing in the structure. When the figures were read aloud at the Moot they showed the northern towns at about half the Ròdaich measure in every column the clerks had chosen to take. The Moot's minute says only: *Now written.* The census carried a line for the obsidian-touched, as the first had done. It gave a number and nothing more, and Tormod Pinn copied it into the chronicle without comment, as Gormshuil had done. In the Wolf shires the Wolf houses published their own count beside the kingdom's, and the two agreed to within a hundred.

Tormod Pinn died at his desk in 1994. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh became royal chronicler, and she was the first to add at the foot of each year a note of what the record could not learn. Her first such note concerned the northern shires. The census clerks there had been told the figures and had not counted them, and the figures they had been told were the Moot's. The kingdom's first honest census was honest in the north because the Moot had supplied the numbers.

Mòrag nic Coinnich now set out to gather the island's records into one place. In 1995 her clerks went to thirty shire towns for their record-chests. At Seann Tarr the shire keeper burned his chest rather than send it, saying that the shire's record belonged in the shire. He was fined, and the chest is lost. The keeper of Cathair naomh sent her shire's rolls in two carts, together with the town's own chronicle, which one family had kept for four generations, and Mòrag entered it as the best-kept shire record received. Seann Toll sent its chest willingly and asked for a copy of everything in it, and the copy made for Seann Toll became the model for every copy the library has made since. In 1996 the library doubled its holdings and took in several smaller archives. That is the point at which the history of Rodos began to be kept in one place instead of many.

No Tuathaich record was called in. The Moot's minute-books, the schools' registers and the vigil-books of Baile chrom stayed in the north in private hands, as they always had. The new wing at Muileann chaol was built of grey stone from the Sloc Mòr's spoil. The provincial chests filled three floors. The fourth floor was left empty for what had not been sent. The compiler of this book writes this book on that floor. It is still mostly empty.

In the west the Board was pulling out. It closed the branch line to Àth leathan in 1997, citing the falling yield of the western pits. The Tuathaich pits went on working, and their coal went by cart to the western ports and by boat from there. With the Board's overseers gone, the hewers of Àth leathan worked the pits under their own captains and paid the Crown's fifth by the Leòid ruling. The Board took the payments and entered them as arrears. At Cnoc thais in the western hills the pits passed to their hewers in the same way, and the father of the pit captain Tom Varley was chosen to keep the accounts. The town's own register of the pits begins then. Nobody in the south paid much attention, because there was nothing in the western hills the south wanted any longer.

In the east the herring failed off Seann Dunn two years running, and the east-coast boats went north to fish the grounds off Caol mhòr that the licence law reserved to them. The Tuathaich boats were pushed farther out. Those of Inis mhin fished the deep water past the northern isles. Two were lost in the first year and none after, and Inis mhin's catch came to pass Caol mhòr's. Mòrag nic Coinnich retired in 1999 after some thirty years, and her deputy Raghnall mac Mhuirich became keeper. That autumn the price of ore-fuel at the fair doubled as the Board cut its sales. The fair's court fixed a ceiling price and could not hold it. At Cnoc fhiadhaich, households were found picking ore out of the spoil-heaps to burn. The Board posted watchmen there. The watchmen were from the town, and they saw nothing.

On 19 an t-Ògmhios 2000 DE the first working dubhan was made. It was a crude synthetic fuel, but it burned. It was not yet an economy. For the time being it was one man's answer to a crisis that the caste in power had still not fully faced. At the foot of that year Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh wrote that a fuel made without the vein was reported from the north and that she had not seen it. It is the first mention of dubhan in the royal record, and she spelled the word three ways in two lines. The next year the Mine Board sent two surveyors north to find out whether it burned as people said. Their report reached the library. It says that the fuel burns, that it smells, and that it is not ore. In the capital the street-lamps of the poorer streets, which had burned ore since the humans put them up, were left dark to save fuel. In 2002 the fair's court recorded its last sale of ore-fuel by the cartload. After that ore was sold at the fair only by the sack, and in the end only by the lump, for altars.

Cathal was crowned on 1 an Lùnastal 2002 DE, on the feast itself. Mairead's reign had ended while Dubhan's experiments still failed more often than they worked. Cathal inherited a kingdom that did not yet know how close its answer was. The mint cut new dies for the reign, the first not cut by a pupil of Fearchar Bonn's workshop, and the obsidian ink was still pressed into the seal.

## IX. Of Dubhan's Fire

The fuel came south within three years, and it came on terms the record does not give. In 2003 a company of shipmasters set up a works for making dubhan at Seann Skell on the southern coast, the first outside the north. The record does not say how they came by the process, and the compiler of this book will not supply an answer the record lacks. The high custodian at Seann Skell wrote to every house of the Rite that the new fuel was not forbidden, because it was not ore, and not blessed, because it was not ore. The letter was read out in the houses and argued over in the Fèidh schools for years. The works sold its first year's output to the fishing boats of the south coast and the lamp-houses of Cathair dhearg, and its chimney became the tallest thing on that coast. The dark streets of the capital were the first to be lit again.

The change reached everything, and quickly. Peigi nic Ascaill, engineer to the Lighthouse Board, fitted a dubhan burner to a spare lamp in the yard below Taigh-solais Baile ìseal and ran it for a hundred nights. Old Murchadh mac Coinnich, retired from the light, kept the trial's log for her. The boatwrights of Caol fhiadhaich fitted dubhan engines to fishing boats. The Shipwrights' Guild called the work unlawful for its members, so the boatwrights left the guild and kept the work. The next year the guild court at Ros dhomhain ruled that fitting an engine was not shipwrighting and so not within its charter, and invited them back. They declined.

In 2005 the five coastal beacons, which had burned obsidian-lit flame for more than a century, became the first works formally converted to dubhan. The flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg was carried in a closed lantern to the Rite's house at Seann Skell and left to burn out on the altar. Custodians from four shires attended. Ciorstaidh nic Artair, keeper of the light, carried the lantern. A song was made for the occasion, or soon after; the library has it from the south coast in more than one form, and this is the shortest:

> *Dh'fhalbh an lasair às an tùr,*
> *tha solas ann, ach chan ann leinn.*
> The flame has gone out of the tower;
> there is a light there, but it is not ours.

The other lights followed. The beacon at Muileann bheag on the Tuathaich north coast was the only one of the five whose fuel came by cart from the north and not by sea from Seann Skell. At Àth dhearg on the north-east cape the stair was too narrow for the new tank, and it was lifted to the lamp-room from outside by a crane borrowed from the rope-walk at Seann Tarr. At Baile chrom the work was done by Tuathaich fitters from Caol mhòr, since the Lighthouse Board had none of its own to send so far. The keeper's report does not name any of them. After a year's argument the lamp-makers of Muileann mhin were allowed to put their guild mark on dubhan lamps. The flame over the hammer was kept.

In 2007 Daniel Frayne died, and the Moot chose Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas, a mill manager, to follow him. His pupils, the binders of Muileann leathan, rebound the Moot's minute-books from the first meeting onward. The rebound set is kept at Caol mhòr. The Moot's minute says that a second set was made and hidden elsewhere. The library notes it and has not gone looking. On 1 an Cèitean 2007 DE, Bealltainn again, the Rail Board ran its first dubhan engine on the ore line. It carried no ore. It carried dubhan to the capital's lamp-houses and came back empty. Two years later the ore-fired engines were laid up in a siding at Muileann chrom and never ran again, and the yard families who had driven them were retrained on the new engines, those of them who would be.

The roads changed next. Salt from Àth bheag went inland by dubhan lorry on the old drove road, and the east-coast drovers sold their oxen within a few years. The drovers of Achadh mhòr bought lorries, and the drove-inn at An Sligeanach Fad' às kept its trade by selling them dubhan. Within a single decade the roadside houses along the old mining roads put in dubhan lamps, ending centuries of oil-and-wick lighting. At An Leòmhann Buidhe on the north-east road the landlord kept one wick lamp burning over the door beside the new lamps, and the custom spread along the road, so that a wick lamp over an inn door is still called a Leòmhann. Am Buabhall Fortanach near Doire shean was the last of the great inns to put the lamps in, and its landlady's account-book gives the cost as a year's takings. An Taigh-òsta Grianach near Cnoc dhìreach became the first inn on the island to sell fuel as well as beds. The landlord of An Taigh-seinnse Reòta wrote to the Board that the smell of dubhan drove his guests away. It did not, and he put the lamps in the next year. At An Taigh-seinnse Mòr above Cuan chrom a new hall lit entirely by dubhan was let for weddings, wakes and meetings of the Guild of Hewers, whose own hall at Achadh dhomhain had been sold.

In 2009 the last working face under Muileann dhearg was closed. The Board moved the remaining families to Seann Toll, where they were given a new row at the edge of the town, called the Hewers' Row. Few of them found work at sea. Most worked on the dubhan tanks in the harbour. Muileann dhearg was left with its chapel, its graves and a caretaker. The Fèidh school at Caol shean sends a teacher once a year to give the town's one remaining lecture, and few people come to it.

In 2010 *dubhan* began to be used in common speech for the fuel itself and not the man, years before the economy built on it was finished. Some time later a letter of the Fèidh school at Caol shean used the word *dubhan-tùsail* for an early attempt that looked like a dead end until it turned out not to be. The letter used it of the school's own revival. The Rail Board dropped "ore" from the name of its main line in its own timetables. Everyone else went on calling it the ore line.

On 30 an Cèitean 2011 DE the Guild of Hewers dissolved itself. Its rolls, its oath-stone of ore and its sick-roll went to the library. Its last master, Tormod mac Coinnich, wrote that the guild had outlived the mountain's patience. The bath-house at the hot springs near Muileann ruadh, built by the Hewers for miners sick with the dust, went to the Rite's custodians and was opened to anyone who paid. It was the first time it had been open to Tuathaich. The Hewers' hall at Achadh dhomhain became a dubhan depot, and its carved lintel, with the guild's oath cut into it, was left in place above the pumps.

By 2012 markets, the army's supply and shipping were all moving to the new fuel. The obsidian caste watched its own source of power fail and did not object. The house of Mac Ùisdein sold what was left of its share in the spoil and the quarry and put its money into dubhan shipping at Ros dhomhain. Of all the families that had held the vein, it was the only one to move before the vein failed it. The patrol boats at Ros dhomhain were refitted for dubhan, and for the first time they could outrun the raiders. The crews of two raider hulks brought in under tow were tried at the court of the sea. Several of them were Tuathaich. The court hanged none of them. It sent the Ròdaich prisoners to the quarries at Achadh dhomhain and the Tuathaich ones home to the north. Nobody could tell the library which of the two sentences was meant as the lighter one.

In 2013 the fitters of dubhan engines, who until then had worked outside every guild, were chartered as the Guild of Fuel-Fitters at Seann Skell. Like every guild before it, its rolls were open to Ròdaich only. The Tuathaich fitters already in the trade were let go. They went home to the northern towns and fitted engines there without a guild, and within a few years Caol mhòr's harbour had more dubhan boats than any southern port of its size. That winter the Moot, under Martha Greaves, debated whether the north should take up the new fuel or keep to its own coal seams. It voted to take it up, and the delegates of the western pit towns walked out. In 2014 dubhan depots opened at Caol mhòr, Doire ghlas and Ceann mhòr, supplied by sea. Martha Greaves wrote in the minute that the north now bought its fire from the south, and did not say whether that was good.

On 5 an Cèitean 2014 DE the coinage was pegged to dubhan output instead of obsidian reserves. It was an admission, quietly made, of which resource now ran the kingdom. The mint stopped pressing obsidian ink into the seal, and the new seal was plain. Fearchar Bonn's first dies were given to the library, where they lie in the case beside the Hewers' oath-stone. The high custodian protested to the council that the plain seal showed the kingdom no longer held the vein sacred. The council answered that the kingdom had never said it did. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh copied both letters into the chronicle. It is one of the few places in the record where the kingdom said plainly what it was.

The north has a rhyme from these years, heard there in both tongues. This is its Ròdais form:

> *Dubh an gual, 's dubh an dubhan,*
> *ach cò leis an teine?*
> Black the coal, and black the dubhan;
> but whose is the fire?

## X. Of the Holdouts of the Western Hills

In the spring of 2015 the delegates of the western pit towns met at Muileann àrsaidh, apart from the Moot. They agreed to go on working their seams and to sell their coal to any household in the north that would buy it. Àth naomh, a town of the western hills with only a few old shafts, joined them, and its delegates said the town would rather be cold with its neighbours than warm alone. A group of northern communities refused to be folded into the new economy. They kept working the last workable coal seams, because they would not give up their independence to depend on one man's fuel. The towns were Àth leathan, Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg in the western hills. Their spokeswoman was Ruth Calder, a pit captain's daughter from Àth leathan. The Moot did not join them and did not condemn them. At Cnoc thais Tom Varley reopened two shafts abandoned since the humans' time. The coal from them was poor, but it was theirs, and it heated the town. Tobar dhearg, the smallest of the five, had one pit and forty hewers, and it sent its coal to Cathair gheal on the coast in exchange for fish.

In 2016 Ellen Marsh, a schoolteacher of Doire ghlas, finished a collection of Tuathaich accounts of the Severance and the settlement, taken down from the old people of the north over many years. It filled eleven books. She formally asked Leabharlann Muileann chaol to keep Tuathaich oral history beside the official Ròdaich record. The request was tabled. It was not rejected, and it is still tabled. Raghnall mac Mhuirich answered her that the request awaited the council's view on the standing of oral testimony. The council has never given one. Ellen Marsh wrote back that oral testimony had been good enough for the census clerks when they took the Moot's figures. Her letter is filed with the request. The compiler of this book has read it several times. It is short, and it has never been answered.

The holdout pits sold their coal across the north for less than dubhan cost, and the Moot's own wool mills at Doire ghlas bought it. In 2017 the Board's revenue from the north fell for the first time since the Severance, by a quarter. The Board's report named the western pits as the cause and the Leòid ruling as the remedy. So, eighty years after Eilidh nic Leòid gave it at Seann Bhral, her ruling was used for the purpose the north had always feared. The Board declared the western seams Crown property. It held that the right to work them had lapsed when the holders stopped paying the fifth. The holders said they had stopped paying when the Board stopped carrying the coal. Both statements were true. The pits were ordered closed.

A company of the Board's enforcers was posted to Seann Skell on the western coast, which is not the Rite's Seann Skell of the south. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh noted that it was the first armed company the kingdom had sent toward the north since the Severance. The enforcers built a depot for their lorries at the edge of the town. The town's Scorpion house refused to bless it, and the high custodian sent a priest from the south to do it instead. Martha Greaves wrote to the council that the western towns would not close their pits because of a letter, and asked for a year's delay while the Moot talked with them. Her letter was received and filed.

In the autumn of 2018 the first skirmishes began between the enforcers and the holdouts. They were small clashes, none of them recorded, none big enough to have a name, but taken together they led straight into open war. At the foot of that year Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh wrote that clashes were reported in the west, that the Board's reports of them had not been given to the chronicler, and that she had asked for them. They were not given. At Muileann àrsaidh the pit-heads were walled with spoil and timber and the road from the south was dug across. On 25 an Dùbhlachd 2018 DE Ruth Calder wrote to the council for the western towns. She offered to pay the Crown's fifth in coal if the Board would carry it, and to let the pits work until the seams were spent. That letter reached the library. No answer to it did.

In January 2019 the Moot resolved by nine votes that it was not a party to the quarrel and would feed anyone who came to its towns hungry. That summer it had its granaries at Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas filled beyond the usual, paid for out of the wool-mill money. The minute gives no reason. It did not need to.

Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh laid down the office that August, and her clerk Sìleas nic Rath became royal chronicler. Her record of the war is the one the library would later seal. The day after, the mobilization order was given. Regiments that had not been called to active service since the Severance were re-formed and re-equipped, openly, months before anyone in power admitted a war was coming. Command was given to Somhairle mac Lachlainn, who had risen among the Board's enforcers. The rolls of the Severance companies were brought out of the council's chests at Cathair dhearg to name the new regiments, and the old names were read aloud before new men. The list had been kept for nearly ninety years, and now it was used.

On 26 an t-Samhain 2019 DE Martha Greaves carried a petition from the Moot to the council in person. She asked that the western pits be left to work out their seams, which the Moot said would be spent within a few years in any case. She was the first speaker of the Moot ever to enter the council chamber. The petition was received and filed. That winter the Rail Board carried arms and dubhan to Seann Bhral on the ore line and its western branch, and the yard at Muileann chrom worked on the trains day and night.

## XI. Of An Cogadh Fada, the Long War

In March 2020 hewers from the five western towns gathered at Àth leathan, where the road from the south enters the hills. The Board's lists name Ruth Calder and Tom Varley among them. In April the re-formed regiments marched west from Cathair dhearg, their carts and lorries running on dubhan, to join the enforcers at Seann Skell on the west coast.

The Battle of Àth leathan was fought on 11 an Giblean 2020 DE, and it was the first great engagement of An Cogadh Fada. The state had already come to lean on Dubhan's fuel to hold up its fading power, and it now moved against the holdouts directly. The open record counts the kingdom's dead at Àth leathan and does not count the holdouts'. Tuathaich telling puts theirs in the hundreds. Ruth Calder's name does not appear in the open record after the battle. The library cannot say whether she was killed there. It records that her name stops.

The holdouts gave up the southern pits and fell back into the hills round Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais. The pits at Àth leathan were taken without a fight and flooded by the Board's order. The seventh regiment was raised from the levies of the west coast and stationed at Seann Skell, the port nearest the western hills, to hold the road behind the army and the field. The field of Àth leathan was left unploughed, and the regiment's patrols passing it on the road reported it empty.

The kingdom raised money as well as men. In June a temporary tithe was laid, justified by the war, on every dubhan sale in the kingdom. It was never repealed. A Treasury clerk sat beside every pump at the depots, and within a month the fair court at Muileann òg had recorded the first complaint against the tithe. The first regiment was raised at Inis mhòr from the capital's own companies to guard the council, the mint and the bridge over the Abhainn dhomhain. The first fleet was raised from the south-west harbours at Àth gharbh and sent round the western cape to stop coal leaving the Tuathaich ports by sea. The fourth fleet was raised at Cuan shean and joined it, and between them they closed the north-western ports of Seann Skell and Cnoc bhàn to every boat except fishing boats, and searched those.

In October the regiments invested Muileann àrsaidh. The town held out for weeks on stored coal and potatoes, and its pit-heads were taken one at a time. Sìleas nic Rath's open record gives the day it fell and nothing more. The third regiment was raised at Cathair mhòr and held its first muster under the house of Mac Ùisdein's market cross. The fifth was raised at Seann Warr and sent north to relieve the regiments before Muileann àrsaidh. Dubhan for the army was taken ahead of every other use, and the poorer streets of the capital went dark again, as they had been before the fuel came.

The families of the western pits came into Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas with what they could carry. The Moot fed them from its own stores, as it had said it would. The council sent nothing and forbade nothing. When the Moot opened its granaries, its minute recorded the amounts given and the names of the families. Those names are among the few Tuathaich records of the war that the library does not hold. The school Agnes Pike had founded at Doire ghlas was given over to the families, and its benches were used as beds. Ellen Marsh, who taught there, took down their accounts. The compiler of this book would give a good deal to read them and does not expect to.

Cnoc thais was taken in December after the regiments cut the road to its pits. Tom Varley's two shafts were fired and sealed. The open record gives the number of holdouts taken and not the number who went on into the hills. The second fleet was raised at Seann Skell on the southern coast to keep the eastern and southern waters while the other fleets were away in the north-west. The raiders of the east took their chance and seized two salt-boats off Àth bheag and burned a third. The second fleet ran one of them down off Baile ìseal.

In the new year the kingdom went on raising regiments as though it expected a longer war than it fought. The sixth was raised at Cnoc bheag, the Hawk port, to hold the northern road, and the Hawk houses blessed its colours in their own order. The second was raised at Àth dhìreach in the east to guard the ore line and the library road. Its camp was the first soldiery the town of Muileann chaol had ever seen. The fourth was raised at Caol bheag and shipped round the north coast in hired dubhan hulls to Ceann mhòr, where it was quartered in the old human magistrate's house. The Moot protested the quartering, and the protest was received. The fleets searched the boats of Cathair gheal every day they went out. The fishermen petitioned the Moot, the Moot petitioned the council, and the searching went on. The eighth regiment was raised at Inis uaine to guard the eastern dubhan tanks, which the council feared the holdouts might fire. The third fleet was raised at Baile ghorm to carry the army's dubhan up the west coast in tank-hulls. In July the Grey Night cult at Baile chrom kept its vigil as it always had. The regiments along the coast reported a night when the whole town was dark and silent, searched it, and found nothing. In the same month the last two western towns, Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg, opened their roads to the regiments without a siege. Their pits were flooded. The open record names no holdouts taken at either town. In August the ninth regiment was raised at the southern Seann Skell to guard the shipmasters' dubhan works. It was the second regiment named for a Seann Skell, and the regimental clerks told the two apart by their coasts.

Then, in September 2021, a column of holdouts several hundred strong came down out of the western hills and moved south-east through the middle of the island toward the capital. Why it did so is in the sealed archive. The name that Tuathaich telling gives its leader is Owen Barrow. The open record gives no name. Its road ran by Àth bheag, a small town of the west and not the salt town, then by Muileann mhin and to the edge of the capital's country. The towns it passed reported it, and the open record copied their reports and not the regiments'. In October it passed Muileann òg and camped on the fields by Muileann ghlas, a day's walk from Cathair dhearg. The first and third regiments were brought down the fair road to meet it. The standing booths of the fair-ground were cleared to give them room to camp, and the fair court's books for that year end without a closing entry.

The Battle of Muileann ghlas was fought on 10 an Dùbhlachd 2021 DE. It was the second and last great battle of the war. The holdouts were broken within a single year's campaign. They were descended from humans once pushed to the island's edge, and now they were pushed back again, this time by a fuel one of their own had made. The open record lists the first and third regiments on the field and gives no count of the column's dead. The regiments' own dead are named on a stone at Cathair mhòr. When word of the battle reached the hills, the holdouts still there came down to the seventh regiment's posts on the Àth leathan road. They were disarmed and sent home, and the open record counts their weapons.

So An Cogadh Fada ended on 10 an Dùbhlachd 2021 DE, a year and eight months after it began, and it was called the Long War all the same. Nine regiments and four fleets were raised for it. All of them are still active, and each is named for the town where it is stationed.

Before the year was out the Tuathaich had a new word. *Chaidh am fadachadh*, they got long-warred, was said first of the defeat in the west and then of any defeat that came after holding out too long. It is said as a joke, and the people who say it do not laugh.

## XII. Of the Peace, and of the Present Year

The regiments were not stood down. In January 2022 the council kept each one at the town where it was raised and paid them all out of the wartime tithe. With the last organized resistance gone, Dubhan's fuel became in practice compulsory across the whole of the island's economy. The Board flooded the last western seams and capped their shafts with concrete, and the seventh regiment stood by at each capping. After that no coal was dug anywhere on the island outside the Board's own works. The holdouts held since Muileann ghlas were released from the capital's gaol and sent north under the first regiment's escort as far as the boundary stones. Those were the stones set in 1930, which nobody had been paid to move. The Moot's register added their names to the dead and maimed it had kept since the Severance, in a separate column. At the end of 2022 Somhairle mac Lachlainn was given a seat on the council. He was the first soldier to sit there since the captains of the Severance war.

The victors set up their stone. In 2023 a stone was raised at Cathair mhòr with the names of the regiments' dead from Àth leathan and Muileann ghlas, beside the market cross of the house of Mac Ùisdein. The seventh regiment built a post on the road below Àth leathan and kept a company there. The regimental lists call it the Àth leathan post, and the town calls it something else. On the field itself Tuathaich families built a cairn without leave. The seventh regiment's patrols left it standing, and it grows by a stone or two every time they pass. The field at Muileann ghlas was sown again. For years the farmer who holds it found weapons in the furrows and handed them to the first regiment, which keeps a list.

On 26 an Cèitean 2023 DE the library's complete record of the Long War was sealed from public access for fifty years. It is the only sealed archive in the kingdom's history. What is still open of the royal chronicle for the war years is Sìleas nic Rath's list of places, the regiments present, and the counts she was allowed to give. Her full record went into the sealed archive with everything else. The compiler of this book therefore has to say plainly what the reader may already have noticed. The account of the war given above comes from the open record, from the towns' reports and from Tuathaich telling, and much of it cannot be checked against the sealed archive. The library keeps the war and cannot read it. Ailean Leabhar said the library settles nothing and only keeps. He did not foresee a thing kept that even the library could not read.

The war left its accounts behind it. The Treasury carried the wartime tithe forward as a standing line under the regiments' pay, and no council minute records a decision to keep it. The Guild of Fuel-Fitters at Seann Skell was given the contract for the regiments' engines and the fleets' hulls and doubled its rolls. The charter rule held, and every new fitter was Ròdaich. The second fleet caught the raiders who had taken the salt-boats and brought their hulls into Seann Skell, and the lighthouse log at Baile ìseal records the chase from the lamp-room, as Murchadh mac Coinnich would have wanted. The fourth fleet, kept on the Tuathaich coast after the war, searched the raiders' bays off Cathair gheal and found them empty. The fishermen there said the raiders had left when the fleets came and would come back when the fleets went. The same fleet's log for 2024 records a thing longer than a patrol boat off the north-west cape, where Tuathaich fishermen had reported one in Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill's day. The log records it as a whale. The third fleet kept its tank-hulls and ran dubhan up the west coast to the Tuathaich depots on the council's contract. The Moot pays the freight.

In June 2024 Anna nic Phàil, councillor for Cathair dhomhain, brought into the council a proposal to compensate the Tuathaich communities for their losses in the war, on a petition from the Moot under Martha Greaves. She spoke for it once. On 18 an t-Ògmhios 2024 DE the proposal was raised in the capital and quietly allowed to die without a vote. The Moot's minute on it is one line long: *Allowed to die.* Martha Greaves laid down the chair that year. The Moot chose Peter Hale, a great-grandson of its first speaker, to follow her. The fifth regiment built stone barracks at Seann Warr, the first permanent quarters of any of the nine. The eighth regiment's guard on the eastern tanks at Inis uaine was made permanent, and the town's Seann Spioradan house added the regiment to its prayers. The fourth regiment left the old magistrate's house at Ceann mhòr and went home to Caol bheag. The Moot asked for the house to be given to the town. It was given to the maor.

By 2025 the Tuathaich were speaking of the tabled archive request and the dead reparations motion together as *seann-chunntas*: an old reckoning that nobody in power will act on and nobody has cancelled. Official Ròdaich usage has not taken the word up. The compiler of this book uses it here because this record should hold both peoples' words.

The mountain was closed that year, or very nearly. The Mine Board closed the upper galleries of the Sloc Mòr at Achadh dhomhain and kept one face open under the Rite's custody to cut ore for the altars, a few baskets a year. Twelve hewers were kept on to do it. They are the last of their trade working the vein on the island, and the Rite gives them its own oath, not the dissolved guild's. On 14 an t-Iuchar 2025 DE the last ore train ran from Achadh dhomhain to Cathair dhearg. The ore line has kept its name, and it now carries dubhan, wool and soldiers.

The regiments settled into the life of the kingdom. That summer regimental fighters were entered in the Còmhrag for the first time, and the ninth regiment's man won it. The fair-wardens raised the gate-price again. The second regiment keeps a guard at the library over the sealed war archive. Raghnall mac Mhuirich wrote to the council that the library had not asked for one. On the northern road, where the boundary stones stand, the sixth regiment keeps posts, and Tuathaich going south have to show a pass for the first time since the Severance. The council's minute calls the passes temporary. The reader of this book will have seen what becomes of the kingdom's temporary measures.

This book ends in the present year, 2026, and the present looks much like any other year. The fair at Muileann òg drew traders from every shire the state controls, and Tuathaich wool was sold on its northern day through Ròdaich factors, as it has been since the first fair. The Còmhrag was fought with staves and fists by fighters from two regiments and nine shires, and one man died. Ellen Marsh's eleven books have been copied by hand in the Tuathaich schools and are kept in the north. The library's catalogue lists the tabled request and not the books. The Moot met at Caol mhòr under Peter Hale. Its minute-books run unbroken from Walter Hale's first meeting. It has no standing in Ròdaich law and has never asked for any. Dubhan is made at Seann Skell on the southern coast and at works in the east and the north. Every lamp, engine, ship and lorry on the island runs on it. The coinage is pegged to it, and the tithe is taken on every sale. In the summer the Rite's twelve hewers cut the year's altar-ore at the Sloc Mòr. It filled four baskets, and the high custodian at Seann Skell divided it among the houses of the Rite by lot.

On 22 an t-Samhain 2026 DE Sìleas nic Rath wrote at the foot of the year that the kingdom's record is complete in every part but two: the war, which is sealed, and the north, which was not asked.

So the account stands on 21 an Dùbhlachd 2026 DE. The coal is all but gone. The economy runs on a name that once belonged to a single man. The Ròdaich hold the capital and the sea. The Tuathaich hold the north, a grievance five years old, and a memory of the First Canoe that the people who have twice defeated them still call folklore. The restored kingdom promised a return, and what it delivered was a continuation. It kept the humans' tariff, the humans' surveys, the humans' rails and the humans' terms for the mountain, and it kept the humans' descendants where the humans had been driven. It also kept its word to the drowned towns of the south, and its wells at Achadh shean still draw water. This book records both, because both happened.

The chronicle does not end here. It stops at the present year, and the next age has no name yet, because nobody can yet say what began it. What comes next is left to the next person, who has been handed the whole record: the sealed war in its guarded room, which opens fifty years from its sealing if the kingdom keeps its word; the fourth floor at Muileann chaol, still empty, which was built for what was not sent; the eleven books in the north, the Moot's minute-books and the hidden second set, all waiting to be asked for. The old reckoning is still owed. Whoever writes the next book should begin by asking for them.
