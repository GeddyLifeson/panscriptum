# The Fifth Book: An Aois Choigreach, the Age of Strangers

*AE 1 – 151*

> *Thàinig na coigrich air an t-sàl, agus dh'fhàg iad an cnoc falamh.*
>
> The strangers came over the brine, and they left the hill empty.
>
> — a line sung in the camps, as the copyists at Muileann chaol set it down

*Here is told the history of Dia-thìr from the Crossing to the Severance: the hundred and fifty years in which men from over the western sea ruled the island, first by leave and then without it. It is drawn from four witnesses: the rolls of the custodians, most of which live now only in the copies made in a cellar of Muileann chaol; the letter-books of the humans' Administration, left lying in the Residency when its masters fled; the daybooks of the beacon-keepers, who wrote down every sail they saw; and the songs of the camps, which were forbidden, and were written down for that reason. Where the witnesses quarrel, both are heard. Where they fall silent, the telling falls silent with them.*

## I. Of the Three Ships and the Leave to Stay

The age begins with the Crossing, on {{date:IV-0001}}, when the humans first made landfall on Dia-thìr. That winter Manannan's mist lifted, which had lain on the western sea since the fleet of the Sundering sailed into it and had hidden the island from every ship beyond. The fishers of Achadh àrsaidh saw it go, and theirs is the first telling.

Two boats were out that morning, working the lines off the north-west cape, where the old men say the Sea-Watcher rises when it pleases. The mist stood on the water as it had stood all their lives, a grey wall a few oar-lengths off. But that morning the wall had gone to gauze, and behind the gauze there was a paleness that moved.

It was sail; then three sails, heaped high on masts taller than any tree the fishers knew, and under them black hulls, wet and shining, coming on slowly out of the west with the mist tearing off their bows in rags. A voice called down from high up in a tongue that was no tongue at all.

The steersman stood and gave back the old hail of the north-west boats. The voice answered. Neither understood a word of the other. Nothing in all the customs of Dia-thìr told a fisherman what to do when the sea opened. At last the steersman put his tiller over and turned south for Cuan shean, where there were custodians whose business it was to know. When he looked back, the three ships were following.

The place of that meeting is called Coinneachadh nan Long, the meeting of the ships ({{date:IV-0001a}}), and the boats of Achadh àrsaidh slow there still.

The roll of Cuan shean, which is the fuller witness, first sets the ships down on {{date:IV-0002}}: three hulls out of the west riding at anchor off the town, the first of their size seen from any shore of the island since the fleet of the Sundering went out sunwise from the harbour and did not come back. Their master came in through the surf in a small boat with eleven men and no weapon among them that anyone could see. The roll calls him Edmund Harrow. He waded the last of the way, stood on the shingle in front of the custodians of the town, and held out an empty cask and tipped his hand to his mouth.

They gave him water. More than that they would not give on their own word, for no custodian living had ever been asked such a thing, and they sent a rider inland to the capital for counsel. For forty days the strangers were fed on the beach and kept below the dunes.

On {{date:IV-0005}} the Council of Custodians at Cathair dhearg gave the strangers leave to stay. The council had sat in the crown's place since the crown fell vacant at the close of the Age of Sundering, and its high custodian in those years was Tormod mac Ailein. The roll enters the vote as unanimous. In the margin a later hand has written that three custodians were absent. The copyists kept the note, for it is the first of many places in the record of this age where what was written and what was so do not quite meet.

Soon the old sailors of Cuan shean began to say that the strangers had come in from the very quarter of the sea into which the Sundering fleet had sailed. The council forbade the remark in its hall. It was written down anyway, and never answered. Whether the humans were children of the departed, or of some other people of those waters, or of no one the Dia-thìrich ever knew, the island has never learned.

## II. Of the Season of Words

The strangers had a name for their homeland in their own tongue, and no Dia-thìreach scribe troubled to set it down. From the first season the rolls call it *an Tìr Thall*, the land beyond, and call its people simply the humans; and so they are called in this book. They had brought their god with them over the sea: Crìosd, one God and no other, with priests and a Bible and a great company of saints. The Dia-thìrich called that faith *an Eaglais*, the Church, after the word its clerics used, and the name has held.

Then came the season of words, when the clerics of the Church and the custodians sat down together to make a common tongue out of two. What was agreed in that season neither side has handed down whole. Two lists were kept, side by side.

The custodian Gormshuil nic Fhearchair kept the Dia-thìreach list. Through that season she sat across a table from the cleric Samuel Wren, a thin, courteous man who coughed in the damp, and the two of them pointed at things. He held up a cup and said his word, and she wrote it; she held up bread and said *aran*, and he wrote that. Before the season was out they had some hundreds of words each.

Then one morning Gormshuil tipped a basket of broken coal out on the table where the window-light fell, a double handful from the vein, black as a wet crow and bright with a sheen, the colours shining at the edges where it had split.

She began to name the colours. There was the green that lies under the black like deep water under weed, and the red at the break that deepens if you breathe on it, and the grey-violet of old cuttings, and the brown that means the piece came from near the surface; and she laid a finger on each and gave its word, as any child of the hills could have done. She gave forty, and there were more, and she stopped because Wren had stopped writing.

He picked up a piece and turned it in the light with his eyes narrowed, and looked at her in a puzzled, kindly way. Then he said a single word, and wrote a single word, and tapped the whole heap with his pen to show that the word stood for all of it.

When she understood the word she sat very still. It was the word for the fuel his people burned at home.

She waited for him to ask for hers. He smiled, and set the coal aside to make room on the table, and held up a candle.

Wren's list lies still in the Administration's letter-books. Gormshuil's was lost, and only its first leaf lives on, in a copy: forty words for the colours of broken coal. Her copyist wrote beneath it a thing the chroniclers have since set at the head of the whole age. The humans had one word for the coal of the vein, and it was their word for the black fuel of their own hearths. No Dia-thìris word in either list answered to it, for to Dia-thìreach eyes no coal of the vein is only black. And the humans did not ask for one.

## III. Of the Years of Fair Exchange

Let it be told plainly, since what came after has thrown its shadow back across them: the first years were good. The island had been alone for eighteen centuries and lacked much that the strangers had, and the giving ran both ways. For a few years the bargain looked as if it would last, and those who trusted it were no fools.

The humans set up a trading post at Ros dhomhain, the capital's harbour on the lake. Iron tools went across its counter, and window glass, lamp-oil and paper; wool came back, and salt fish, hides and dyed cloth. Its first tally-book, kept by the trader Thomas Pike, balances to the last line across its first year. The smiths of Cathair dhearg took the humans' bar-iron gladly and learned to temper it in a single winter, and within a few seasons the old bronze hoes of the capital's fields were melted down for bells. The salt-pans of Seann Warr sold to Pike by the barrel, and theirs was the first Dia-thìreach trade the humans came back asking for twice.

Harrow sailed west with hides and cloth in his holds and came back with four ships in place of three. He brought carpenters, and a surgeon, and a letter from a trading house in an Tìr Thall asking what else the island had. The surgeon, Jonas Tull, set the broken leg of a Cuan shean fisherman whom the whole town had given up for lame, and the custodian of Cuan shean entered it in the roll as the first debt owed to a stranger.

In {{year:IV-0022}} the small port of Baile dhìreach, on the south-west coast, took Dia-thìrich and humans alike under one council of six, three of either people. Its founding compact was written twice, once in each tongue, and it is the only such compact of the age. The chair passed in alternate years between the fisherman Iain mac Chaluim and the human carpenter William Rook, and the harbour dues went half to the custodians and half to the humans' post.

At Baile dhìreach the net-maker Eilidh nic Ruairidh married the human cooper Samuel Hale. The council entered the marriage in both its books. The custodians at Cathair dhearg, asked for a ruling on it, sent none. No other town tried what Baile dhìreach had tried, and the tellers will come back to that marriage when they reach the years in which the humans' law found a use for it.

In {{year:IV-0025}} a human supply ship struck the western reefs in fog and nine of her crew were drowned. Her master had been sailing, the letter-books complain, by a chart drawn from memory on a coast lit only by the old driftwood fires. The human navigators asked for beacons, and the Dia-thìreach engineers agreed, and did not yet see what the asking would bring. The navigator James Fenn and the Dia-thìreach mason Torcall Clachair walked the coast together that year to choose the ground. They marked the five headlands of the old fires. Fenn's chart survives. Torcall's cairns, for the most part, have gone back into the hills.

## IV. Of the Treaty in One Tongue, and the Company

In {{year:IV-0029}} the terms of the humans' presence were set down in writing for the first time, in the humans' tongue only, and no one on the Dia-thìreach side is known to have spoken against it.

Tormod mac Ailein came to the council hall at Cathair dhearg on the day of the signing, {{date:IV-0030}}, and sat down at the long table across from Josiah Pell, newly come over the sea as the humans' first Commissioner. Between them lay the treaty, covered edge to edge in the strangers' close black writing.

Tormod could not read a word of it. The letters stood on the page like a fence of thorn. Someone read it to him, and he sat with his hands folded and listened as the voice went down the sheets, and once he asked for a line to be read again. Outside the rain was coming off the lake.

When the reading was done Pell turned the last sheet round and held out a pen. Tormod took it. He looked a long moment at the place where his name should go, as a man looks at a ford before he wades it. Then he wrote, and Pell signed after him for the Administration.

The treaty was dated by the humans' day, month and year. The custodians' clerk entered it in the roll under its year of the Diosal and the moon in which it was signed, and left the day empty, having nothing to put there. It was the first of many such pages. Within a generation every clerk on the island wrote two dates at the head of his page, and the crews in the Company's pits had learned the humans' week before they learned their prayers, because they were paid by it.

Pell built a house of dressed stone on the rise above Ros dhomhain and called it the Residency, and from it the humans governed Dia-thìr for a hundred and forty years. Harrow went back to the sea, and with Pike he founded a trading house licensed by the Administration, which the island knew only as the Company. Its charter gave it first refusal on "all fuels, ores and minerals the island may yield." Its first building of its own was a stone warehouse at Cathair dhomhain, along the lake shore, where it kept its tools out of reach of the harbour thieves. The copyists ask the reader to remember that warehouse.

The strangers' fever came ashore at Cuan shean with a crowded ship in {{year:IV-0034}} and ran down the west coast. Ninety-one Dia-thìrich died of it, by the custodians' count, and eleven humans. The custodians shut Cuan shean to ships for a season, and Pell protested in writing.

In {{year:IV-0037}} the Company sent two prospectors, Abel Stone and Hugh Mercer, into the hills east of the capital, the country of Muileann chrom. They came down again with sacks of broken coal on their backs and word that the black seam ran "further than can be walked in a day." The custodians demanded the coal back, to be laid in the rock again with the proper words. The Company gave back half. The roll enters the other half as "carried west."

In {{year:IV-0039}} barter gave way to contract. Under the first contracts of extraction Dia-thìreach hands began to cut the vein on a scale that no reverent keeping had ever used. A crew of thirty opened the first pit at Muileann chrom, paid by the ton in iron tools and lamp-oil, where the custodians' own cuttings had never once been measured by weight. The Company built a coal quay at Ceann fhada, a fishing hamlet below the hills, and paid the fishermen for their foreshore in iron nails, by weight. Wren finished his grammar of Dia-thìris for the Mission, which was the Church's mission to the island. It is careful and right in most things, and it turns the name of the vein's custodians into "keepers of the coal." The Mission raised a chapel on the quay at Ros dhomhain and hung in it the first iron bell on Dia-thìr, and the custodians asked that it not be rung before the fishing fleet was out.

In {{year:IV-0045}} one settlement would not sign.

The Company's agent climbed to Doire mhòr, in the hills above Muileann chrom, with the contract under his coat. The custodian, Beathag Dhubh, met him at her door and did not ask him in. She let him say all he had come to say, and answered him in a few words, and shut the door.

Her words live only in his report, where he set them down in his own tongue: that the hill was not hers to sell, and not his to buy.

Doire mhòr was not punished. The Company opened its next working at Cnoc fhiadhaich instead, and ran its cart-road round Doire mhòr's fields rather than through them. The annals add two words: *for now*.

The first of the new beacons was finished in {{year:IV-0048}} on the southern headland at Tobar dhearg, a round tower of grey stone raised over the old fire-tower and paid for by the Company. Torcall Clachair built it and Fenn set its lens.

In {{year:IV-0052}} the humans brought in their coin beside the barter of the island, for convenience, they said: the mark and its hundred pence, struck at Ros dhomhain from metal shipped in from an Tìr Thall, with a ship on one face and nothing of Dia-thìr on either. At the capital the custodians' clerks began to keep two sets of books, and complained of it in both. Within ten years nothing that came across the sea could be had for anything but marks.

Wren spent a winter at Dùn thais among the druids of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the school of the deer. He asked them why the vein should be holy. They asked him why his fuel should be burned. Neither side's account names a winner. Wren wrote home that the druids told of a voyage west to an isle of the blessed very like his own Church's telling of Naomh Breandan, told from the other shore, and that he did not know what to make of it.

The Harbour Articles followed, written like the treaty in the humans' tongue alone, and they fixed the dues at Ros dhomhain in the Company's favour. This time two custodians refused to sign. The council granted the Administration upland grazing in the north-west, around Muileann àrsaidh, for human families who would not work in the pits. The grant was for grazing. The families built houses of stone.

Tormod mac Ailein died at Cathair dhearg in {{year:IV-0061}}, and the council chose Coinneach Glas of Ceann àrsaidh to follow him, a man who had never met a human before the Crossing and had dealt with little else since. In {{year:IV-0062}} Edmund Harrow's ship did not come home from the western crossing. The Company passed wholly into Pike's hands, and the letter-books mourned Harrow as the man who found Dia-thìr.

## V. Of the Lapse of the Law, and the First Steward

The keepers at the hearths of the hills tell the end of the Small-Burning Law as they would tell of a fire that went out for want of feeding. That Law had been kept since the Holy Age: that the vein be cut and burned sparingly, and that what the hill could give in a year be counted and never exceeded. The Age of Sundering ended with it still kept. Within a generation of the Crossing it was gone. No council repealed it. It stopped being enforced when it stopped paying to enforce it. At Dùn dhearg the custodian Fionnghal nic Artair wrote to the council the only defence of the Law that has come down. The Law, she wrote, was made for the people more than for the coal, for a people becomes whatever its burning makes it.

At Seann Skell the hawk-priest Murchadh Seabhag preached that Mòd, the Hungry Hawk of Acaill, starves those who eat faster than the land can feed them. The sermon was copied and carried into the camps, and it was the first Dia-thìreach writing the Company's agents were told to seize. At Muileann chrom the Company burned coal by the cartload to drive its pumps, and when Coinneach Glas's council asked that the old tally be kept, it was sent a copy of the Company's accounts.

Pell sailed home in {{year:IV-0069}} and did not return. Henry Maddox came in his place, with orders to double the yield and a letter in his pocket promising him a share of it. In {{year:IV-0071}} a gallery at Cnoc fhiadhaich, driven forward faster than any Dia-thìreach crew would have chosen and without the timbering the crews had begged for, came down and killed eleven. The custodians' roll names all eleven. The Company's report names none, and calls the gallery "unsound ground." Then a dry cough ran through the camps and killed thirty-eight, most of them boys who broke coal at the pit-mouth. The Mission's surgeon called it a winter complaint. It lasted through the summer.

The second beacon was lit at Baile ìseal on the eastern shore, with Torcall Clachair directing the work from a chair at the foot of the headland, too old now to climb. The Company founded a camp of timber huts at Àth ìseal, the first settlement on Dia-thìr built for no purpose but the pit. It dammed the stream that watered Doire mhòr to wash its coal. No order for it was ever found. Beathag Dhubh's people carried water up by hand for two summers, and then they began to leave. In {{year:IV-0079}} the hill villages lost their oats to rain that no one was home to cut ahead of, and the Company sold them meal for marks, and they had few marks.

From {{year:IV-0081}} the mountain was worked by the ton. The Company was chartered anew in an Tìr Thall under a name the Dia-thìrich never used, and at Ros dhomhain its agents outnumbered the Administration's clerks four to one. Coal barges loaded at Cidhe Beag on Abhainn uaine, where the old canoe landing was widened with cut stone and its shrine carried uphill out of the way. Within a few seasons the green river ran black with slurry and the salmon at its mouth failed, and the fishermen kept the river's name and said it bitterly. Baile dhomhain was laid out on a grid by a Company surveyor, the first Dia-thìreach town drawn on paper before a stone of it was set. On the coal road the old carters' house at the halfway stage was rebuilt and kept its name, An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, and its keeper Seònaid Bhàn took marks and barter both, the only keeper on the road who would. Seann Toll was made a coal landing for the barges on the Abhainn uaine, and its fishing boats were moved to the next reach.

In {{year:IV-0091}} a Dia-thìreach was set over Dia-thìreach miners to answer to human masters, and the chroniclers count it the first crack within the Dia-thìrich themselves. He was Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill of Muileann chrom, a crew-leader since the first contract. The Company gave him its staff and a house with a slate roof, and the Dia-thìrich gave him his byname: Raghnall Maor, Raghnall the steward. Soon there were overseers at every working in the district, and the roll stopped giving their names and gave only their number. At Cnoc mhòr the overseer Seumas Dubh was killed by his own crew after a boy was crushed on a haulage he had ordered hurried, and the Company hanged two men at Ros dhomhain. It was the first hanging the Administration did on Dia-thìr.

Maddox barred the priests of the hawk from the camps in {{year:IV-0095}}, on the ground that their preaching slowed the work. The overseers enforced it; several of them were hawk-faithful themselves, and the roll says so. A spotted pox came up the coal road from the quays of Ros dhomhain. The Mission counted four hundred and six dead. The custodians counted more, and wrote the difference in the margin of the Mission's list. The Administration moved its customs house from Baile dhìreach to Ros dhomhain, and the mixed council there, left with half an income, shrank from six to four. In {{year:IV-0099}} Maddox left Dia-thìr a rich man, and Arthur Lisle, a lawyer, came to the Residency. His first act was to have every agreement since the treaty copied into a single bound book.

## VI. Of the Silver Compact, and the Things the Clerks Could Not File

Reading that book closely, Lisle found in it nothing whatever about silver.

When the Company's surveyors came up the valley to Muileann chiar with their chains and pegs, Ùna nic Choinnich was waiting for them at the mouth of the adit. Behind her, filling the yard and the slope above it, stood the whole membership of the guild in their leather aprons, not one of them speaking, and two of the eldest held the guild's ledgers open across their forearms.

The chief surveyor began to read out his authority. Ùna let him finish. Then she turned the first ledger toward him, and the second, and asked him, in his own tongue and quite pleasantly, to find the humans' name anywhere in either.

He looked. The yard waited, and the wind came down off the hill with the smell of the smelting-floors in it. After a while the surveyor closed his book, and the Company's men went back down the valley.

On Là Fhèill Brìde of {{year:IV-0103}} Lisle signed a compact that left the seam to the town. It was the first writing of the age in which the Dia-thìreach side kept the better terms, and Muileann chiar held its silver in its own hand through all the rest of the age. When a Company coal ship was taken off the eastern coast by raiders in low black boats and burned to the waterline, the Company said the guild had paid them. The guild said nothing.

The third beacon was lit at Àth dhearg in {{year:IV-0106}}, and its keeper, Tormod mac Pheadair, kept a daybook of every ship he saw. From his book, and the books of the keepers after him, the sailings of the humans in this age are known; and the copyists have leaned on the daybooks more than on any other witness, for a keeper has nothing to gain from a ship that is not there.

In {{year:IV-0110}} the council of Baile dhìreach broke up in a quarrel over a boundary wall and never sat again. The human families went to Ros bheag and Ros dhomhain; the Dia-thìrich stayed. The households that were both split, or went quiet. The next year the rain failed for a single season around Achadh shean, and it was a warning nobody heeded.

At Dùn thais in {{year:IV-0114}} the Deer master Aonghas mac Coinnich put to his students a question the register calls new: why the humans could not see the colour in broken coal that every Dia-thìreach child sees. He did not answer it, and told them it was the wrong question. Its form in the Deer books has been quoted in every age since:

> *Carson a chì sinne an dath, is iadsan dall air?*
>
> Why do we see the colour, and they blind to it?

On the concession the human families founded a market town at Cathair gheal under a charter that made no mention of the custodians. In {{year:IV-0119}} the family of Eachann Mòr, a custodian of Ceann àrsaidh who had lost his seat, sent their son Calum west on a Company ship hoping his schooling would buy them better treatment. He never came home. The Mission opened a school at Ros dhomhain in the humans' tongue, and most of its pupils were the children of overseers. The Company rebuilt the old bridge over the river at Ros fhionn for its eastern coal road.

Two things the Administration's clerks never knew where to file. The crew of a Company brig reported a beast larger than their ship off the north-western headlands; the clerk put the report in with the complaints about pirates, and the sailors of that coast said they had wondered when the humans would see it. And two Company surveyors went up into the hills at Doire uaine against the villagers' advice and were not seen again.

In {{year:IV-0130}} the Administration's share of the coal rose sharply, and what the Dia-thìreach towns received did not rise with it. Lisle's schedule took the portion from one part in five to one part in three, and the custodians received it in the humans' tongue with a translation made by a Mission pupil. A bloody flux broke out on the Ros dhomhain quays; the Mission's nurse Anne Colby turned the chapel into a sick-house, and the record credits her with keeping the dead under two hundred. Lisle died at the Residency in {{year:IV-0135}}, and Walter Crane, the Company's chief agent at the pits, took the seal after him, the first man to hold both offices in turn.

Raghnall Maor died in {{year:IV-0137}}, and the Company gave his staff to his son Dòmhnall. The custodians had hoped the office would die with the man. It became a family's inheritance instead.

The fourth beacon was lit at Muileann bheag in {{year:IV-0138}}. It was the first to burn coal in place of oil, and its flame laid a colour on the water that no oil lamp ever had. The Mission cleric Edwin Lowe dug that season at Làrach an Teampaill and found cut stone, burnt bone and marks he could not read, and wrote that the ruin's builders "were not the present race." The custodians agreed with him, and added nothing. What became of the Seann-Dhaoine his spade did not turn up.

In {{year:IV-0143}} the Company agent Martha Gale offered the silver guild a partnership, on terms its own ledger calls generous. Ùna nic Choinnich was very old by then. She had the offer read aloud twice in the guild hall, slowly, and then put it to a show of hands, and the hands refused it. That same year a new camp was opened at Achadh dhomhain for the deep galleries. Its houses were built of spoil.

## VII. Of the Smoke in the Gallery, the Child in the Camps, and the Green Death

When the women of the camps tell of the Green Death, they begin with a child, for she was born in the same years and the same dust, and the one tale has grown into the other. And when they tell of the child, they begin with her father, and with what he met in the dark.

His name was Donnchadh mac Thormoid, and he was a hewer. His grandfather Ruairidh had stood behind Beathag Dhubh at Doire mhòr on the day she sent the Company's agent back down the hill. His father Tormod had carried water up to Doire mhòr for two summers after the Company took the stream, and then gone down with the rest to the huts at Àth ìseal; and there Donnchadh was born, and broke coal at the pit-mouth before he could read, and went below at twelve. When the Company opened its camp at Achadh dhomhain for the deep galleries under the hill ({{year:IV-0144}}), he went with the first crews. The pay was better below. It always is where the dying is.

In the first winter of that camp he was working a face at the far end of the deepest gallery, alone, with a coal-lamp hung on a nail behind him. The Company had sunk a shaft from the hillside to draw the air, and a draught came along the gallery from it all day long, cold and steady, so that the flame of his lamp lay over on its side and the dust of his cutting went away from him in a thin drift toward the dark.

He stopped to spit, and turned, and saw that the drift was parting.

Something was standing in the gallery between him and the shaft. It was the height of a tall man, and it was grey, and it was made of smoke, such smoke as a peat fire gives in a shut room. The draught went past it on either side. His lamp flame streamed and the dust streamed, and the smoke that stood in the middle of them did not stir by a thread.

Every child of the hills has heard of the smoke in the grove. Donnchadh had heard it on his grandmother's knee, with the Hollow Man and the white stag, and had put it away with them when he went below. He did not run. There was nowhere to run but past it, and he said afterward that he had not wanted to.

"You are Tormod's son," it said. Its voice was low and very clear, and the draught did not carry it off.

"I am."

"And Somhairle's, and Ailean's." It seemed to him to be looking about the gallery, at the black walls and the props and the cut face, as a guest looks round a house where things have gone badly. "They are taking the hill."

"They are," said Donnchadh.

"I will not fight for the kin. I never have; that is not my charge. But I was set to keep the kin, and a kin with its hill dug out from under it is not kept." It came nearer, and he smelt it: a hearth in the morning, when the fire has been banked all night. "I made a promise once, in the grove, to the one you come from. Breathe."

He breathed, because it had told him to. The smoke went into him with the dust of the gallery, and it was warm, and it went down into his chest and stayed there, like a coal carried home under a cloak.

"What is it?" he said, when he could speak.

"A gift. It is not for you. You will carry it, and the carrying will not be easy. It will wake in your daughter." The draught had begun to move through it, and it was thinning. "It is in the dust of the deep places now, for every one of the kin who goes down; and where it wakes, it wakes. Tell no one of it but the one who will bear the child."

Then the draught went through the place where it had stood, and his lamp flame lay over again on its side, and there was nothing in the gallery but the dust.

He coughed from that day. It was a deep cough, and he brought up black with it, and he grew thin through the spring, and the men of his crew moved their bread away from him at the break. The Mission's surgeon listened at his chest and wrote him down with the miners' lung, the dust-rot the humans knew from the pits of their own country, which the crews called the lung-rot; and he gave him a year. The coal of Dia-thìr rots the lungs of the humans as their own coal does. It does not rot the lungs of the kin in that way, and what Donnchadh carried was no rot at all. But it looked like one to a physician who could not see the colour in the coal, and nobody else was asked.

On {{date:IV-0146}} his daughter was born in a house of spoil at Achadh dhomhain. That night Donnchadh's cough stopped, and it never came back. He told his wife then what he had met in the gallery, and long afterward she told the women of the camp; and that is all the witness there is, and the camps have never asked for more.

The child's whole life long she was steeped in more raw coal than any Dia-thìreach in living memory, and she did not age as the people around her aged. Her name was Aisling, and she was the first of the generation that would be called immortal. Of her the rolls give three things, her birth, her name and her condition. The camps gave her more.

It was the women who laid out the camp's dead who first said it, over the washing, as they said most things: that the child was of the old king's blood. They meant Ailean Mòr, whom the gods crowned in the grove, and his children who lived six hundred years. Not one of them believed a word of the six hundred years. Ailean Mòr was a tale for the dark of the year, the scribes of the Hall had set him among the tellings long ago, and nobody in the camps had ever heard the Line of the Mason said. But the years went over the child and left her as they found her, and the whisper grew with her. In the camps they called her *nighean an rìgh*, the king's daughter, half in jest, as *Aisling-aois* is said now of anyone who keeps her youth; and half not. A people whose hill is going out in ships will take its hope where it finds it, and the camps found theirs in an old tale and a girl of the spoil-houses who did not grow old. Nobody could prove a word of it. That came later, and is told in its place.

The next year the Company set up a great steelyard on the quay at Ros dhomhain, and from then on the island's yield was known by the ton, and the custodians' old count, kept in seasons of labour, was no longer entered. What was happening to the child had no name in any tongue on the island, and decades passed before anyone tried to find one. At Cill ghlas the custodians of Òrd Bhrìde kept the old rites of the flame and the stone at the mouth of the oldest cutting, while the pits deepened all around them. It was the last shrine in the district the crews still climbed to on the old feast days, at Samhain and Imbolc, Bealltainn and Lùnastal. Remember it.

In {{year:IV-0152}} came A' Phlàigh Uaine, the Green Death.

It showed first at Achadh dhomhain. A man would go pale with a greenish cast, like a candle seen through bottle-glass; then came a cough that brought up dark dust; then weakness. The camp buried forty in the first month, in a field beyond the spoil-heaps that is called the green acre to this day. It went out along the coal roads from the camps at Muileann chrom, Àth ìseal and Cnoc fhiadhaich, and it fell hardest on Ros dhìreach, Ceann òg, Inis ìseal, Baile dhomhain, Achadh fhada, Cnoc bheag, Muileann uaine, Cuan àrsaidh and Inis bhàn. Twenty-six settlements set down cases. The roll lists them, and leaves a blank line after the last, as though waiting for another. At Àth ghlas the custodians burned every hut in which anyone had died, bedding and all, over the Company's protest at the loss of its property, and there the sickness stopped sooner than anywhere else.

The dust was blamed, and no study was ever finished. The Administration's physician Nathaniel Bright began one at Muileann chrom, weighing the dust in the lungs of the dead. He fell ill in its second season, and his notebooks break off in the middle of a table. Commissioner Crane, who had walked the camps through the worst of it, died of it at the Residency in {{year:IV-0159}}, and Martha Gale held his seal until George Tolley came over the sea, with orders that the yield must not fall. Tolley never visited the camps. His letter-book blames the dust, the Dia-thìreach habit of sleeping near the fire, and the custodians' shrines, in that order.

The hawk-priests were still barred, so the crews carried the sermons in their own mouths, and the overseers' reports of those years list hawk-feathers nailed above hut doors as a matter for discipline. The saying of the feathered doors outlived every report:

> *Ithidh Mòd an neach a dh'itheas an cnoc.*
>
> Mòd will eat whoever eats the hill.

Anne Colby opened sick-houses at Àth ìseal and Achadh dhomhain and nursed in them until the sickness ebbed. Of all the humans on the island she is the only one the camp people name in their own tellings of the plague, and they name her kindly.

## VIII. Of the Eleven Days of Baile thais

Catrìona nic Eachainn called the halt on a grey morning in {{year:IV-0165}}, standing on an upturned coal-tub at the pithead of Baile thais with the whole shift below her.

She had asked the Company to close the galleries where the Green Death had been worst, and had been told that the yield must not fall. So she told the crews what she had told the agent, and then she stepped down off the tub and sat on it, and folded her arms, and did not go below.

Nobody went below. By noon the word had gone round every hut in the town, and the winding-gear stood still, and the coal-carts stood in a line on the rails with nothing in them. The agent came up and shouted in two tongues, and the crews sat on the spoil-heaps in the thin rain and listened to him politely.

For eleven days no coal left Baile thais. Each evening Catrìona walked the town and counted the loaves in the windows, and knew what the agent knew: that there was one store in Baile thais, and it was the Company's. Each day the shelves were barer. On the twelfth morning she went down to it herself and looked through the shutters at the empty shelves, and then she walked back up to the pithead and told the crews to go below.

After it the Company built its own granary at Muileann chrom and forbade the camps to keep more grain than a week's ration, and the order was signed by Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill Maor. Catrìona was turned out of Baile thais and refused work at every Company pit. She went east over the hills to Muileann chiar, and the silver guild took her on as a refiner. The Company asked for her back. The guild's answer was filed, and has not been kept.

The Administration built a new bath-house at the hot springs near Muileann ruadh for officials recovering from fevers. The Dia-thìrich, who had bathed there for centuries, were let in on certain days for a penny. The silver town grew, for the humans wanted both metals, and in {{year:IV-0172}} the guild founded a daughter town to the west for its refiners and carters, under the same name; the record calls them the old town and the new, and does not always say which it means. The guild sold bar silver to the Company at a price the guild fixed, and it was the only thing on Dia-thìr in all the age priced by Dia-thìreach hands.

At Tolley's asking the priests of Òrd Mhacha at Cathair dhearg, the capital's own order and the crown's, blessed the Residency's new coin. Macha's priesthood teaches that the two horses of her rite, Dubh Sainglenn the black and Liath Macha the grey, each have their due, the dark and the light; the dissenting priests said the coin was only one of them. In {{year:IV-0176}} barter was cheapened against the mark a second time, on terms set wholly by the Administration. In {{year:IV-0179}} the dissenting priests left the capital's temple for the sacred forest at Cnoc bheag, where custodians who had lost their seats found a welcome. And Dòmhnall Maor died, and his son Iain took the staff: the first overseer schooled at the Mission, who kept his reports in the humans' tongue and his household in Dia-thìris.

## IX. Of the Levy, the Ring of Beacons, and the Great Hunger

The hunger of Baile chrom is told in the north-west by the beacon-keepers' families, and they begin it with a piece of paper. On {{date:IV-0181}} Tolley issued the ordinance the Dia-thìrich called simply the Levy. Every farming village in the west and north was to send one in five of its able men and women to the pits for two years, and be paid for them in marks, at the new rate. It fell hardest on the villages round Baile chrom and on the northern concession, which had never sent crews to the pits. The Dia-thìrich of the concession were taken. Its human farmers were exempt.

Tolley closed the hall of Feallsanachd an Fhèidh at Dùn thais in {{year:IV-0186}}, over a lecture on whether the humans' blindness to the colour of the coal might be a kind of sickness. The masters went on teaching in their own houses, to smaller rooms each year, and some went out under the trees of the sacred forest of Flidais near Muileann chiar, where there was no hall to shut.

Work began on the fifth and last beacon at Baile chrom, and the masons were taken by the Levy from the very villages whose fields they left unworked. The ring was finished in {{year:IV-0191}}: Taigh-solais Baile chrom, Baile ìseal, Muileann bheag, Àth dhearg and Tobar dhearg. It had been asked for so that ships should not be lost on the reefs. By the time it was done it served a trade that was plunder in all but name. The Baile chrom beacon was lit with a coal flame in {{year:IV-0193}}, and the daybooks show the sailings doubling in the years after.

In {{year:IV-0194}} the harvest failed. Gorta Baile chrom, the Great Hunger, went across thirty-four settlements, and it bit the deeper because the Levy had stripped the countryside of the hands to farm it. It was worst at Baile chrom itself. From their empty fields its people could see the coal flame of the new tower moving on the sea at night; and the custodians recorded that more died at Baile chrom than anywhere else, and the Administration, which kept its own count, did not dispute the figure. A relief ship from an Tìr Thall lay a month at Ros dhomhain with her holds full of grain while the Residency and the Company argued over who should pay to unload her. The Mission opened kitchens meanwhile at Cathair gheal and Baile chrom, and Anne Colby, come back to the island, ran the one at Baile chrom. The Levy was suspended in the famine districts in {{year:IV-0200}}, too late for the harvest. It was never repealed, and never used again, and the custodians note that it never needed to be, since the villages had learned what it meant.

Tolley retired with honours in {{year:IV-0202}}. Robert Haskins, who came after him, ordered that the custodians' council be consulted on the tithe, and then set the tithe before the council had met.

In {{year:IV-0204}} the custodians acted together for the first time. Nineteen settlements sent their custodians to Ceann àrsaidh, and there, meeting as one body, they drew up a formal grievance to lay before the Administration.

Peigi Ghlas, the custodian of Cill ghlas, carried it to the Residency. She was kept waiting on a bench in a stone passage that smelled of lamp-soot, and at last was shown to a young clerk at a high desk. He took the petition from her, and thanked her, and laid it on a pile, and bent to his work again. She stood a moment longer. Then she went home.

It was not answered.

From these years come the oldest Dia-thìreach writings of grievance that survive. They are grievances as yet and no more, the first written word that something had gone wrong. The earliest is Leabhar Baile thais, a book of complaints kept by many hands over some years: wages, deaths, the granary, the stream at Doire mhòr. At Muileann dhearg the poet Fearchar Bàrd, who had broken coal since boyhood, made songs of the Levy and the famine. The overseers forbade them, and the forbidding is why several were written down. They were sung in the roadside houses of the east, and An Taigh-òsta Grianach became known for them, and the Company's own carters sang them over their ale without always knowing what they sang. When the Company built its deep-water quay at Ros dhomhain for the new iron steamships, which burned the island's own coal to carry it away, Fearchar made the song that is quoted most of all his songs:

> *Tha an long ag ithe a' chnuic*
> *gus an cnoc a ghiùlan air falbh.*
>
> The ship is eating the hill
> to carry the hill away.

The Company walled its pithead at Baile thais and set a gate in the wall and a watchman at the gate to count who went in, and it was the first working on Dia-thìr walled against its own crews. Catrìona nic Eachainn died at Muileann chiar in {{year:IV-0214}}, a refiner of the silver guild to the end, and when the camp people of Baile thais sent a delegation to her burying, the Company docked each of them a day's pay. In {{year:IV-0215}} a fire in the Company's coal sheds burned three days and took two streets of the harbour quarter with it. The Company's inquiry found no cause. The harbour quarter's own account has several. That year the council, on the death of its high custodian, could not agree on another, and sat for years with the seat empty, and Haskins wrote to an Tìr Thall that this made it easier to deal with.

## X. Of the Long Drought, and the Law of One Line

In {{year:IV-0217}} came Tiormachd Achadh shean, the Long Drought: three dry seasons one after another, the dry year of the east come back threefold. At Achadh shean the wells failed, and the cattle were driven west in a column the roll says was a day long and fewer than half of them reached water. The roll counted ten districts as drought country, worst at Achadh shean, Àth uaine, Achadh àrsaidh and Muileann chiar, and reaching down to Cuan ruadh and west toward Baile chaol. The Administration kept no count of its own.

The spring of Allt an Àigh, which is said never to have failed, did not fail. Pilgrims of Na Seann Spioradan came to it from all across the east, the first of them at Bealltainn. The Administration posted a watchman at the spring to charge for the water. He was called back after a single season.

A fever of bad water followed the drought into the towns. It was worst at Baile Mòr ruadh, where the wells had been dug deeper into the old middens, and two hundred and sixty died there. The Mission's report was the first to name the cause rightly, and the Mission bored deep wells at Achadh shean and Àth chrom that held. The custodians of the east entered the Mission's wells and the Company's quotas on facing pages, and wrote nothing beside either. For the quota was not lowered. The custodians petitioned Haskins one by one, and he wrote back to each that the matter was under consideration.

In {{year:IV-0230}} a case came before the Administration that fixed the law of inheritance on Dia-thìr for the rest of the age. The claimant was Thomas Hale, grandson of the Baile dhìreach marriage of Eilidh nic Ruairidh and Samuel Hale, and he claimed a share in a customary cutting through his Dia-thìreach grandmother. The magistrate at Ros dhomhain, Oliver Dunstan, ruled that customary rights descend only through the line the Administration recognises, and that it recognises only its own. Within a few years that ruling had passed eleven customary cuttings to the Company, each because some owner in the line had married a human or been born to one. The custodians stopped entering such marriages in the roll. And so the marriage that Baile dhìreach had written into both its books became, a century on, the reason marriages were written into neither.

In {{year:IV-0234}} a society of pit crews that called itself the Lamp-Watch began to meet at Baile thais and Cnoc chaol under cover of a burial club. The first thing it is known to have done was to bury an old breaker who had died in the Company's lock-up.

Haskins was recalled in {{year:IV-0235}} after a quarrel with the Company over steamship berths, and Edward Vane came to the Residency. He was the first Commissioner born on Dia-thìr, at the Residency itself. He spoke Dia-thìris badly, but he spoke it. He laid the mule-drawn tram-road to Ros dhìreach, and before he died the Company's railway ran from the pits at Muileann chrom to the quay at Ros dhomhain, its engines burning the island's coal ({{date:IV-0243a}}).

In {{year:IV-0237}} came the night-shift order, and from then on the mountain was given no season of rest. It was felt first at Cnoc chaol, in the newest galleries, where the crews worked by coal-lamp in two shifts. The custodians' roll records that the hill was given no silence at all, and leaves the rest of the page empty. The crews at Cnoc chaol refused the night shift and were replaced within a week by crews brought from the coast. The Lamp-Watch paid the strikers' families for a season, and then its money ran out. The Mission's minister at Ros dhomhain, Matthew Dunne, preached from the chapel pulpit that the Dia-thìrich were right, and that even the ground must rest. The Company complained, and Dunne was called home to an Tìr Thall, and the Lamp-Watch copied his sermon and read it aloud in the camps.

A wasting sickness of the lungs spread among the night crews of Cnoc chaol and Baile thais. Unlike the Green Death it killed slowly. It killed the humans among the crews brought in from the coast, as the dust of their own country's pits had killed their fathers. The Dia-thìreach hewers coughed as hard, and the Mission's physicians wrote the same word against them; but the custodians' roll, which knew the crews by name, notes that of the Dia-thìrich who coughed few died of it, and the old women of the camps called it Donnchadh's cough and would say no more. The Company paid the widows a pension for a year, and then stopped. In {{year:IV-0243}} Iain Maor wrote to the Company that the night shift was killing his crews faster than it paid for them. The letter survives because it was filed with his dismissal, which was reversed a month later for want of anyone else to do the work.

## XI. Of the Second Child, and the Word that was Coined

In {{year:IV-0245}} a second child was born who showed the same bond with the coal that Aisling had shown half a century before. His name was Fionnan. To those who kept the rolls his birth meant that the first had been no single mischance, and that the thing was being made by the pits themselves.

Seònaid nic Dhùghaill, the last master of the Deer to teach openly in the age, died at Dùn thais in {{year:IV-0247}}, and the school's register ends with her. Nobody took it up again until long after the humans had gone.

In {{year:IV-0248}} the custodians of the east began to send copies of their rolls to Muileann chaol, which still kept Beathag nic Thòmais's loft of papers, well out of the Residency's reach. The copying was done in the houses of three families and the copies were kept in a cellar. The library at Muileann chaol, which keeps this book among its others, grew out of that loft and that cellar together.

On {{date:IV-0250}} the custodians met at the shrine of Cill ghlas to give the thing a name.

They came up the path in ones and twos through the evening, past the spoil-heaps, to where the oldest cutting still stood open in the hillside with Brìde's lamp burning in its mouth. They sat on the benches of the shrine with their cloaks steaming, and all around them in the dark they could hear the pumps of the Company's pits.

There were two cases now, and nobody could call it chance. A wonder that comes twice is a sickness, and a sickness must have a name if a physician is to write it down.

They argued late. Some wanted an old word out of the tellings; some wanted none, for a name is a door. At last one of them put forward the plainest word there was: *fuil-ghuail*, coal-blood. It was meant for a physician's register, a word to be written in a column, with no song in it. They took it by vote. In the humans' tongue it was rendered *coal-touched*.

It did not stay in the column.

The Residency, hearing of the word, ordered every Dia-thìreach child who showed the condition to be reported to the Commissioner's office. The custodians complied by reporting none.

In {{year:IV-0253}} barter was abolished as lawful tender, and every bargain on the island from then on ran through the humans' coin. The barter houses of Ros dhomhain and Cathair dhearg were shut by order, and a Residency clerk wrote that the ledgers were now "complete for the first time." The hill villages borrowed marks from the Company's stores to buy seed, at interest, and within a few years most of them owed the Company more than a year's crops.

Iain Maor died in {{year:IV-0258}}, and his daughter Mòrag took the staff, the first woman to hold it. The Company approved her in a letter that calls her "a safe pair of hands." Vane died at the Residency that year, and Charles Merriman came with a small company of armed constables, the first standing force the Administration had kept on Dia-thìr. His first posts were at Muileann shean, which the townsfolk called the humans' second Residency and did not mean it kindly, and at Tobar ghorm.

In {{year:IV-0262}} the Mission at Cathair gheal reported that human labourers near Baile chrom had been meeting at night in the fields to keep a silent vigil under the sign of a grey goose. They were of the Church, and took the goose for an Gèadh Fiadhaich, the wild goose of the Holy Spirit. The Mission called the vigil irregular, and supposed it a superstition picked up from the Dia-thìrich. The Dia-thìrich of Baile chrom disowned it. Manannan's priests at Ceann mhòr said the Mission was half right: the practice came from the Dia-thìrich, from Manannan's own harbours; Baile chrom had nothing to do with it. The human fishing families of Ceann mhòr had learned from them the night prayer to keep the sea shut, and the labourers inland had made of it a night of silence to a grey goose. The priests called it their prayer gone astray, and would not have its keepers in their house.

That year the dockers of Ros dhomhain, Dia-thìrich and human alike, refused for nine days to load coal over the new berth charges. It was the only strike of the age in which the two peoples stood together. Merriman's constables broke it, and the human dockers were shipped west.

In {{year:IV-0265}} a third child of the condition was born, Lorccan, the first of them born in a mining town outside the camps, and the custodians knew from it that the thing had outgrown any one place. In {{year:IV-0266}} a fourth was born, Cian, within a year of the third: the nearest the generation of the coal-blood ever came to being born together.

## XII. Of the Copyist and the Line of Aisling

Cailean mac Eachainn was a copyist in the cellar at Muileann chaol, one of the hands of the three households that were turning the rolls of the east into copies the Residency could not reach; and in the autumn after the word was agreed ({{date:IV-0251a}}) he was copying the roll of the camp at Àth ìseal, whose custodian had kept the new camp at Achadh dhomhain on the same pages from its first winter.

It was dull work, and he was glad of it. A camp's roll is births and burials, fines and fevers and shifts lost, set down in a custodian's hand that grows worse in the winters; and among them the marriages, which the custodians write as the ford has written them since the Age of Ailean, each side with its line seven names back. He had copied a hundred such lines that month and never read one.

This one he read. The name at the head of it was Donnchadh mac Thormoid, hewer; and further down the same roll, in the same hand, a birth was written with a mark beside it that he had seen before, on the other copies, against one name only. *Aisling.* Every copyist in the cellar knew what the camps said of her. Cailean had said it himself, as a boy, as boys said it, with a grin.

He sat with the seven names a long while by the lamp. The last of them was a name nobody gives a child now. Then he copied them onto a slip, and put it in his coat, and in the morning he walked out to Tobar dhìreach.

Clann na Ceiste were few by then, and fewer every winter, and the one of them who still said the lines at the hearth on the night of the lightning was an old woman named Ealasaid, in a house not far from the slab. She let him in, because it was raining. When he told her what he wanted she looked at him as the reciters look at a stranger, which is to say not unkindly and not at all as if she meant to help; but she let him read the seven names aloud.

She sat with her eyes shut while he read. At the seventh she opened them.

"Where did you get that?"

He told her.

"That is Somhairle's line," she said. "The youngest son's. We have kept it since Somhairle, and no one of it has come asking in four hundred years. They went down into the valleys and forgot us." She was quiet a while. "Whose is the child?"

"Donnchadh's. Her name is Aisling."

Ealasaid said nothing for so long that he thought she had not heard. Then she said, not to him, and as though she were reading it off the wall: "When strangers hold this island, the fire will wake again in your line." She looked at him. "That is the end of the vigil, as we have it. I have said it every winter of my life, and I never thought to see what it meant."

She stood up, for the lines are said standing, and she gave it him as it is given at a naming, from the child back to the fire. The Mason's line has kept the years of its dead beside their names since the mason, and keeps them still, but it says them to no stranger except where they are strange; and so in the short form only the long lives are given their years. Cailean wrote it down as she said it:

> "Aisling, daughter of Donnchadh, who hewed in the deep galleries of Achadh dhomhain;
> son of Tormod, who carried water up to Doire mhòr two summers, and then went down to Àth ìseal;
> son of Ruairidh of Doire mhòr, who stood behind Beathag Dhubh when she sent the Company down the hill;
> and seventy generations whose names we keep;
> son of Fionnlagh, who stood on the quay at Seann Skell when the ships went west, and did not go;
> and a hundred and nineteen generations whose names we keep;
> son of Gille-Brìde, who carried a coal of the First Flame home to the hills;
> and a hundred and thirty-nine generations whose names we keep;
> son of Beathan, who sat by the slab through the dark half of the year;
> and sixteen generations whose names we keep;
> son of Ceallach, the last of us whose years were long; and all the days of Ceallach were a hundred and forty years, and he died;
> and four generations whose names we keep, each shorter-lived than the one before, and their days were a hundred and seventy-three years, and two hundred and twenty-one, and two hundred and ninety-six, and four hundred and eight;
> son of Somhairle, the youngest child of Ailean, who outlived his father and all his father's children; and all the days of Somhairle were five hundred and forty years, and he died;
> son of Ailean Mòr, the first king, whom the gods crowned in the grove; and all the days of Ailean Mòr were six hundred and ninety-five years, and he died;
> son of Suibhne, and fourteen fathers whose names we keep;
> son of Seathan, who snapped his fingers, and the fire was in his hand; and ten fathers;
> son of Colla, in whose days they called us the Inquisitors of the Mystic Coal; and fifteen fathers;
> son of Eòghann, who tasted the river; and twenty-one fathers;
> son of Cormac of the Misstrike, who bled in the grove; and thirty fathers;
> son of Brian, who buried the five like the dead; and twenty-eight fathers;
> son of Fearghas, who climbed to the top of the island; and thirty-one fathers;
> son of Aodh, who threw the seven into the sea; and eighteen fathers;
> son of Diarmad, who spoke with the Keeper in the grove; and thirty-four fathers;
> son of Conall, who laid the slab over the burnt vein;
> son of Neachdan, who found the fire;
> son of the godfolk, who grew out of the island like grass;
> and the island was born of Caoran's fire."

When it was done she sat down again, and the rain went on against the shutter.

"They'll say it's a tale," said Cailean.

"The six hundred years, they will," said Ealasaid. "Let them. I never told you a man lived six hundred years. I told you whose son he was."

Cailean walked back to Muileann chaol, and wrote the line in the margin of the Àth ìseal roll, small, beside the birth; and it stands there still. So the whisper of the camps was found true, as far as a line of names can make a thing true: the girl of the spoil-houses was a daughter of the first king's line. What else it proved the copyists did not say, and the camps did not ask them to.

## XIII. Of the Rending of Cill ghlas, and the First Blood

In {{year:IV-0267}} the Company began to drive galleries under the hills around Cill ghlas with blasting-powder shipped from an Tìr Thall. The custodians of the shrine asked that no charge be set within sight of the old cutting. The Company's engineer marked their request on his plan, and set the charges below it.

On {{date:IV-0269}}, in the course of an ordinary day's work, the hills east of the capital broke.

The tellers of Cill ghlas say the ground spoke first, a long grinding under the feet like a quern turning, and then the fault tore open. That was Scàineadh Cill ghlas, the Rending. It killed dozens and shut a whole mining district for a generation. The old cutting where the rites had been kept through all the age, where Brìde's lamp had burned and the word *fuil-ghuail* had been agreed, went down into the earth with its shrine and was not seen again. The custodians' roll sets down the loss of the shrine before the loss of the dead, and a later copyist has put the order right; the copyists of Muileann chaol let both hands stand. The Company fenced the district and left Cill ghlas and its hamlets without work, and the closed country went to thorn.

Within days came the first blood between the two peoples. The fuller account of it in the roll, written up at the year's end, sets it at Muileann chrom.

Mòrag nic Iain Maor was at the pithead when they brought the seven up.

The pumps had been stopped the day before to save fuel, and in the night the gallery had filled. Now the seven lay on the wet boards in a row, and the women of the camp were coming up the track, and there was no sound but their feet and the rain.

At noon the Company's agent rode up with his constables and began to speak about the loss to the working.

The first stone took him on the shoulder. Then the air was full of them, and the constables were shouting, and Mòrag was shouting too. She saw a constable raise his rifle. She saw the smoke before she heard the sound. When it was over four Dia-thìrich lay in the mud beside the seven, and one constable with them.

At the inquiry she stood up in her good dark dress with the Company's staff in her hand, the staff her great-grandfather had taken from the Company, and testified that the pumps had been stopped on the agent's order. The clerk wrote it down, and then was told to strike it out.

The Lamp-Watch got a copy anyway.

In {{year:IV-0276}} the grey fever came in on the ships, a fever of the chest that took the young and strong before the old, and it ran from the capital to the eastern farms. The camps, already hungry, buried more than the towns.

In {{year:IV-0279}} a second petition went to the Residency, and where the first had held a plea, this one held a warning. It was drafted by Eilidh nic Raghnaill, a crew-leader of Baile thais and a member of the Lamp-Watch, and signed by custodians and crew-leaders of thirty-one settlements, and its last sentence was underlined at the Residency: that the hill would not be worked by people who were not asked. It was the first Dia-thìreach writing the Administration troubled to answer. It answered with constables. In {{year:IV-0282}} Merriman tripled the constabulary and posted companies at Muileann chrom, Baile thais, Ceann fhada and Cnoc chaol, each with a fortified store, all paid for out of the tithe; and a barracks for two hundred at Dùn chrom stood empty most of its first year for want of men to fill it.

At Seann Skell, where Murchadh Seabhag had preached the hawk's hunger in {{year:IV-0066}}, the hawk-priest Ìomhar Seabhag now preached that Mòd's hunger had turned at last toward those who had eaten the hill. The hawk-shrines of the west became meeting-houses for the Lamp-Watch, and the constables did not yet know it.

The grey fever came back in {{year:IV-0287}} and settled in the north-west, and there it killed humans as readily as Dia-thìrich. In the Mission's crowded hospital at Cathair gheal the two peoples lay in the same wards for the first time.

## XIV. Of the Houses on the Roads, and the Day the Pits Stopped

The carters of the old coal roads tell these years as a tale of inns. From {{year:IV-0290}} the roadside houses of the mining routes began, unbidden, to serve as waystations for a nameless militia in a war not yet declared. The singing house, An Taigh-òsta Grianach, walled its yard and set a watch on its gate. Am Buabhall Fortanach on the south-western road barred its gates at dusk and kept a man on its roof, and its keeper Tormod Buabhall sent a list of travellers up the road every week, and never told the constables whose hands it reached. At An Taigh-seinnse Reòta on the northern road the stables were rebuilt with a loft for sleeping men, and the carters noticed that the loft was never empty and never held the same men twice. An Sligeanach Fad' às, near the silver country, became the post-house through which the Lamp-Watch sent word to Muileann chiar and the silver guild paid its rent. And An Taigh-seinnse Mòr, the carters' house that Seònaid Bhàn had opened in the first years of the extraction, was kept now by her great-grandson Aonghas. It was the busiest of the fortified houses, the one the constables searched most often and found least in.

In {{year:IV-0301}} the pits stopped.

That morning the Company's chief agent stood at his window over the Ros dhomhain quay to watch the first coal-trains come down to the steamships. None came. By noon the ships still rode high at their moorings with their holds open to the rain.

Word came in by rider and by rail. Every working in the Muileann chrom district had halted at once. So had Baile thais, and Cnoc chaol, and the silver town, and the eastern pits at Ros fhionn. The signal had been a fire on the hills above Doire mhòr, which the Company took for a heath fire.

For six days the steamships lay at Ros dhomhain with empty holds. The agents wrote home that they had never known the harbour so quiet, and that the quiet frightened them more than the crowds ever had.

The stoppage was broken as the strike at Baile thais had been broken, when the constables shut the Company stores in every district at once. Forty crew-leaders were taken. Thirty-two were let go without charge. Eight were shipped west to an Tìr Thall for trial, and were never heard of again. The beacon of the stoppage had been lit on Doire mhòr's hill by the children's children of Beathag Dhubh's people, and the constables burned the heather there so that no fire could be lit on it again.

The keepers' daybooks were showing by now fewer ships from an Tìr Thall each year, and older ones. The Company's letters speak of troubles at home and do not say what they are. Of whatever was happening beyond the sea, the island saw only the emptiness in the daybooks.

In {{year:IV-0313}} the Administration began to issue its orders with no Dia-thìreach counsel sought at all, and the polite pretence of permission, kept up since the council's vote of {{year:IV-0005}}, was put away. Merriman left in ill health, and Lionel Strake, a soldier, took his place. His commission, read aloud at the Residency, made no mention of the Council of Custodians, and he did not call on it. He brought a flying machine over in the hold of a Company steamship and had it flown over the coal country to count the workings from the air. The Dia-thìrich called it *eun-iarainn*, the iron bird ({{date:IV-0315a}}). He had the council hall at Cathair dhearg locked and its keys lodged at the Residency. The custodians met instead in the temple of Macha, whose priests had once blessed the humans' coin and now sheltered those who had petitioned against it.

In {{year:IV-0316}} the Administration claimed the right to take any Dia-thìreach dwelling it judged needful for the widening of the workings. Of all the wrongs of the age, the edict of seizure is the one named most often in every telling of what came after. The first houses taken under it were those of Doire mhòr, for a new shaft beneath the hill, and the last households there were carried out by constables. The village that had refused the first contract was the first to be emptied by the last. At Baile dhomhain, the first camp laid out on paper, two streets were pulled down for a spoil-tip.

## XV. Of the Breaking of the Staff

In {{year:IV-0319}} the order came to Mòrag nic Iain Maor to clear the houses of her district.

She read it twice at her table in the steward's house under the slate roof. Then she took down the staff from over the hearth, the staff Raghnall had carried, and Dòmhnall after him, and her father Iain, and walked down through Muileann chrom to the Company's office, with the townsfolk coming out of their doors behind her.

At the Company's door she broke it across her knee. It was old wood and it went with a crack like a shot. She laid the two halves on the step and walked home.

Three other overseers resigned that month, and the Company put humans in their places. So ended the office that Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill had taken up more than a hundred years before, Raghnall, Dòmhnall, Iain and Mòrag each in turn; and the custodians' hope that it would lapse with the first of them was granted at last by the fourth.

That summer the silver guild of Muileann chiar, still holding its seam under the compact of a century before, began to buy rifles from the raiders of the eastern waters, where no gunboat had patrolled for years, and paid them in bar silver by weight. Its ledger enters the rifles as refining tools. Custodians, crew-leaders of the Lamp-Watch and the dissenting priests of Macha met in the sacred forest at Cnoc bheag and made themselves one council for the mining country, and chose Eilidh nic Raghnaill to speak for it. No human hand wrote down that meeting. Strake learned of it all the same, and ordered the arrest of Eilidh and the forest council; and the constables went to Baile thais, and found her house empty and the town silent.

## XVI. Of the War of the Hills

On {{date:IV-0324}} the grievance of the Dia-thìrich, joined at last under one council across the mining country, became war.

It opened in the hills. At first light crews under Mòrag nic Iain, who had broken the staff, rushed the fortified store at Muileann chrom, and its eleven constables gave themselves up without a shot; before noon the rifles and the grain were gone. Crews from the camps took the coal quay at Ceann fhada and sank two barges across its mouth, and no coal left the hills by water again in the age. At Baile thais the constables held the walled pithead for eleven days, the span of Catrìona's strike a lifetime before, and on the twelfth, their water gone, they came out. Eilidh nic Raghnaill sent them under guard to the coast unharmed. The Residency had not looked for that, and did not return it in kind. The northern pits at Baile gharbh rose in the same season, took the constable posts at Muileann shean and Tobar ghorm, and sent the constables south on foot. Aonghas shut the gates of An Taigh-seinnse Mòr across the coal road, and the men in its loft came down armed, and the road between the capital and the hills was cut at its halfway stage and stayed cut. At Muileann dhearg the smith Uilleam Gobha made grenades of the Company's own blasting-powder cased in tin from the coal sheds, and pike-heads from the rails of the galleries, and the council's roll says it could not have fought its first season without him.

Then the capital rose. The custodians broke the Residency's locks on their hall at Cathair dhearg and sat in it again. The townsfolk held the stone bridge against the constables sent up from the harbour, the bridge the Company had rebuilt for coal-carts, and they barricaded it with the carts. At Cathair dhomhain the townsfolk broke open the Company's warehouse, the first building the Company had ever owned on Dia-thìr, and carried its tools to the council's companies. Strake drew the Administration back into the Residency and fortified the Company's quarter of the harbour. The city below it, Dia-thìreach almost to a soul, neither joined the rising nor stood against it, and the constables did not trust it enough to leave their walls.

A column of constables and Company guards under Major Peter Ashdown marched up the coal road to relieve the hills, and on {{date:IV-0335}} it came to the ford of Àth chiar and found the rebels on the far bank. The water was high and brown, and his men went into it to the waist with their rifles over their heads, and the fire from the far bank came down on them. Ashdown lost forty men there and all his baggage, and turned back. The rebels lost nineteen, and the council's roll names every one, as the custodians' roll had named the eleven of Cnoc fhiadhaich when the Company's report named none. Ashdown's column halted a night at the barracks of Dùn chrom, and in the morning found the town's gates shut behind it, and went on to the coast without its wounded, whom the townsfolk kept and nursed.

In the east the silver guild declared for the council, opened its armoury, and escorted the Company's agents to the coast with their papers. The guild's ledger notes that this was the one place on the island where the rising took nothing, because the humans had never held anything there to take. The constables' post at Dùn ìseal gave itself up to the guild's riflemen after a single exchange of fire, and the east was clear of human arms. Dùn dhearg declared for the council and sent it grain, and its declaration quotes the defence of the Small-Burning Law that its custodian Fionnghal nic Artair had written a hundred and twenty-seven years before. The crews of the eastern workings at Ros fhionn stopped the pumps and walked out, and let the lower galleries flood, and the bridge on the eastern coal road fell silent.

The humans struck back from the sea. In the first month of {{year:IV-0342}} a Company steamship came up the Abhainn uaine and shelled the coal stage at Seann Toll to keep it from the rebels, and set the fishing town behind it burning, and twenty-three townsfolk died. It was the first time the humans turned the island's own coal, burning in their boilers, against a Dia-thìreach town. Fishing boats from Seann Chwen and Ros ruadh, sailing by night along the south coast, boarded a Company coaster at anchor and burned her, and the Company drew its coastal shipping back into Ros dhomhain and Ros bheag.

On the concession, Cathair gheal shut its gates and raised a militia and sent none of it south. Its magistrate asked both sides to leave it alone; the council granted it, and Strake did not reply.

That spring the council's letters to the Hawk coast went north by the hands of seven hawk-faithful crewmen of the river towns. They waited nine hours for the letters at An Cupa Dàna in Cuan dhearg ({{date:IV-0342a}}). Each wore at his throat a hawk cast in lead from the cartridges taken at the store at Muileann chrom, and the record calls them Buidheann an t-Seabhaig Luaidhe, the Leaden Hawk company. They left at dark.

They went on foot, past the burned stage at Seann Toll, over the hills by Achadh dhomhain, down the Abhainn ìseal to Cnoc bheag and along the Hawk coast to Inis mhin. At every hawk-house on the way one of them stood up among the benches and read the letter aloud. At Inis mhin they heard that Strake's constables rode the western roads out of Ros bheag every day, so they hired a carriage and left the roads altogether. In the grasslands above Tobar dhomhain they lay eleven hours in the long grass over the road from Ros bheag and counted the constables' riders as they passed ({{date:IV-0342d}}). There were forty-one, and none of them going north.

They came down to Inis thais eight days after they had set out. The priests of the Stone, the keepers of Clach, heard the letter in their temple and would send no men; but they shut the harbour of Inis thais to the Company's ships ({{date:IV-0342e}}), and gave the hawk-faithful the use of the Fianna's roads. At Seann Skell Ìomhar Seabhag declared that the war was Mòd's own hunger turned back on those who had fed it, and the hawk-shrines of the west became recruiting-houses.

The keepers at Tobar dhearg put out their beacon, and Àth dhearg and Baile ìseal followed. Company ships feeling their way along the coast by night ran onto the reefs the beacons had been built to light, and two were lost. The Administration begged an Tìr Thall for troops and ships. One ship came in the war's first year, with dispatches and a few crates of cartridges, and sailed again within the week.

Through the first winter the council held the hills, the east and the capital, and Strake held the Residency, the harbour quarter below it and the western ports, and neither had the strength to break the other. Cathair dhearg went short, its harbour held against it, and the custodians shared out the grain by household; and the roll records that they took the same share as everyone else.

In that stalemate the ledgers burned.

The first fire was at the Company's counting-house on the Ros dhomhain quay, inside Strake's lines. Dùghall Dubh and the men of the harbour quarter came to it over the roofs on a night of low cloud, in their stocking feet, and in at a garret window and down the stair in the dark. The tithe books stood in rows in the long room, calf-bound, heavy as flagstones. They carried them out into the yard in armfuls and heaped them on the cobbles, and Dùghall struck the light himself. The sentry at the front door never turned round until the whole yard was lit.

The second raid burned the tithe-house at Ceann àrsaidh, where the portion owed by every settlement of the hills had been entered since the first schedule. The clerks were let go. The custodians of Ceann àrsaidh asked that one ledger be spared for the record, and their own people refused them, and the library at Muileann chaol has been sorry for it ever since. The third burned the weigh-house at Muileann chrom, with the steelyard ledgers in which the coal of the hills had been set down by the ton since the first contract. The rebels' own chronicler writes that the fire burned green. Later chroniclers hold those fires great in what they meant, if small in what they did. The camps made a verse of them:

> *Loisc iad an leabhar, ach cha do loisc iad an cnoc.*
>
> They burned the book, but they did not burn the hill.

On {{date:IV-0359}} Company guards and constables sallying from the harbour to win back the eastern road were driven back at Cnoc ghorm, in the hills beyond the capital, and it was the Administration's last attempt of the war to reach the mining country. The next day the Dia-thìrich of the Ros dhomhain harbour quarter opened the lower gates to the council's companies, and Strake held only the Residency and the Company's quays. On {{date:IV-0362}} Strake took his officials, the Mission's clergy and the Company's agents away by water to Ros bheag. The council's companies came into the Residency the next morning and found it stripped of everything but its furniture and the letter-books, from which so much of this history is drawn, and which the humans had not troubled to take.

## XVII. Of the Truce of Àth àrsaidh, and the Closing of the Crossing

Ros bheag, the western port the humans had used since the early years, became the last seat of the Administration, and the human families of the west and south, a few thousand in all, gathered behind its walls. With the roads cut and no ships from the west the town went short within months, and Strake ordered the Company's stores opened to the human families and the Dia-thìreach townsfolk alike. It is the one deed of his that the Dia-thìreach rolls give him credit for.

The custodians of Cuan shean, the town where the first humans had waded ashore to beg for water, shut their harbour to Company ships. The council's roll marks the turn of it and says no more, and neither shall the tellers. At Baile dhìreach the descendants of the old joint council, many of them of both peoples, were asked by each side which people they belonged to. Most of them would not answer, and neither side made them.

On {{date:IV-0368}}, on Samhain, the last ship known to have come from an Tìr Thall anchored at Ros bheag. She brought no troops and no grain, only a sealed letter for the Commissioner, and she sailed on the next tide with as many passengers as she could hold. What the letter said, Strake kept to himself. On {{date:IV-0369}} envoys of the council and the Administration met at Àth àrsaidh, on the edge of the northern concession, and agreed a truce: the humans remaining on Dia-thìr would withdraw into the north-west under the council's safe-conduct, and the council would leave the concession towns standing.

On {{date:IV-0371}} the columns went north. The human families of Ros bheag, Cuan shean, Baile dhìreach and the south-west walked up the western roads with their carts and bundles under the guard of the council's companies, and few of them had ever lived in the north. The roll records that no column was attacked, and that none was helped. At Àth chrom the custodians stood at the ford and counted them over, and that count was the last the Dia-thìrich made of the humans on Dia-thìr in this age. At Àth leathan, the last stage before the northern country, the columns camped a night, and the custodians of the town record that the humans asked the name of the place, and that several of them wrote it down.

The keeper of the beacon at Baile chrom saw the last of it.

He was on the gallery of the tower on {{date:IV-0374}} with his glass to his eye when the ship came up along the coast. She had sailed from Cuan shean with Lionel Strake, the last Commissioner, and his officials, and she was the last ship on the island that could make the crossing. How the harbour that had shut itself to the Company came to let her go, the men of Cuan shean never said. The keeper watched her stand out to the west, smaller and greyer, until the sea had her. Then he went in out of the wind and wrote her down.

Nothing on Dia-thìr records her arriving anywhere.

The council ordered the five beacons lit again, from Baile chrom round to Tobar dhearg. The keepers kept their daybooks as before, and there was nothing to put in them. The line that stands in them, in the hands of five keepers, is this:

> *Cha tàinig long.*
>
> No ship came.

At the year's end the custodians sent what was left of the age's rolls down to the cellars at Muileann chaol: their copies of the petitions, the book of Baile thais, and the letter-books left in the Residency. What the Administration's own clerks carried west with Strake, no one on Dia-thìr saw again.

On {{date:IV-0377}} the war ended with the crossing itself closed. Whatever had let the humans reach Dia-thìr shut behind them. No one on the island knows what it was, or why it opened, or why it closed, and the chroniclers set it down as a thing that befell the sea. The humans still on the island were brought into the northern country their descendants hold today. This was the Severance. Cian and Lorccan, neither yet twenty, grew up in its wake.

*The masters of the humans on Dia-thìr, as the letter-books give them.* Edmund Harrow, captain, from the Crossing until the treaty; then the Commissioners at the Residency: Josiah Pell, {{year:IV-0030}} to {{year:IV-0069}}; Henry Maddox, {{year:IV-0069}} to {{year:IV-0099}}; Arthur Lisle, {{year:IV-0099}} to {{year:IV-0135}}; Walter Crane, {{year:IV-0135}} to {{year:IV-0159}}; George Tolley, {{year:IV-0160}} to {{year:IV-0202}}; Robert Haskins, {{year:IV-0202}} to {{year:IV-0235}}; Edward Vane, {{year:IV-0235}} to {{year:IV-0259}}; Charles Merriman, {{year:IV-0259}} to {{year:IV-0314}}; Lionel Strake, {{year:IV-0314}} to {{year:IV-0374}}, the last.

*The Stewards of Muileann chrom, as the custodians' roll gives them.* Raghnall mac Dhòmhnaill, Raghnall Maor, {{year:IV-0091}} to {{year:IV-0137}}; Dòmhnall mac Raghnaill, {{year:IV-0137}} to {{year:IV-0180}}; Iain Maor, {{year:IV-0180}} to {{year:IV-0258}}; Mòrag nic Iain, {{year:IV-0258}} to {{year:IV-0319}}, who broke the staff.

Thus ended the Age of Strangers. It began with a vote that three custodians did not attend, and with forty words for the colours of the coal that the strangers never asked to learn; and it ended with the hill cut to its roots, the Small-Burning Law a hundred and thirty years forgotten, and the tithe ledgers burning green at Muileann chrom. What was lost the rolls can count only in part: the shrine at Cill ghlas and the water of Doire mhòr, the salmon of the green river, the Deer school's hall and the barter houses, the eight who were shipped west, and all the names the Company's reports never gave. What was kept is shorter to tell: the silver of Muileann chiar, the copies in the cellar at Muileann chaol, and the four children of the coal-blood, whom the strangers' pits had woken and the strangers' registers never held. The hill was the island's again. But the island knew no way to use it save the way the strangers had taught, and of that, and of the kingdom restored at Cathair dhearg, and of the same digging under new masters, and of the northerners in their new country, the next book tells, which is the book of An Aois Rìoghachd, the Age of the Kingdom.
