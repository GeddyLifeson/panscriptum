# The Fifth Book: An Aois Ùr, the New Age

*1930 – 2026 DE*

> *Tha e scrìobhte a-nis.*
> It is written now.
>
> — a minute of the Moot at Caol mhòr, {{date:V-0217}}, entire, as the library renders it

## I. Of the Severance and the Settling of the North

The Age of Strangers ended with the crossing shut. Whatever had let the humans come over the western water closed behind their last ships, and those of them still on Rodos were sent north under the council's guard. That was the Severance, on the first day of {{year:IV-0377}}. The New Age begins in its wake. On {{date:V-0001}} the Tuathaich homeland was fixed: the human descendants stranded on the island were formally settled in the north. It was a confinement, not a homecoming. Their grandchildren would call it home anyway, because it was the only one they had. Cian and Lorccan of the immortal generation grew up in the wake of it; the chronicles of this age say nothing more of them.

The victors began by putting things away. At Cathair dhearg the war-companies of the Severance were stood down, and their rolls were handed to the council instead of being burned. The companies were never abolished. They survived as names on a list, and nearly ninety years later someone would take the list out. On the roads running north, at Caol mhòr and elsewhere, stones were set where Tuathaich territory began. No wall followed them. Many still stand at the roadside, since nobody has been paid to move them.

The north went short in its first year alone, cut off from the stores that had come over the water from an Tìr Thall. Tuathaich memory, taken down long afterwards, puts the dead at Doire ghlas that winter above two hundred. No Ròdaich count was made.

Council clerks carried off everything written from the human counting-houses at Cathair dhearg, Ros dhomhain and Seann Skell, and boxed the tithe ledgers unread, since few of them could read the humans' hand. In the north-west the human harbour officers had sailed with the last ships and taken their keys, and the Tuathaich fishermen broke the locks on the harbour stores and shared out what they found by household.

On {{date:V-0007}} the state styled itself Rìoghachd Ròdais: a kingdom in form, whatever it remained in function, a contradiction its own records still carry. That autumn a royal council of twelve was seated at Cathair dhearg, drawn from the custodian families and the captains of the Severance war. Its minute-books are the oldest continuous record of the state in this age. They are not quite the oldest record of the age. A month before, Tuathaich householders had met at Caol mhòr and chosen a speaker, Walter Hale, a former harbour clerk. The Moot had no standing in Ròdaich law. It met anyway and kept minutes, and they run unbroken from that meeting to the present year.

The kingdom soon learned how much of the humans' work it would keep. At Ros dhomhain the first Ròdaich harbour-master, Dùghall mac Iain, kept the humans' tariff in force for want of another and spent years translating it. In {{year:V-0011}} a ship was sent past the western capes to look for the humans' crossing. It came back after a month of open water with nothing to report, and no second ship was sent.

## II. Of Niall and the Making of the Shires

Niall was crowned at Cathair dhearg on {{date:V-0012}}, the first ruler of the restored kingdom and the first name in a line the state's chronicles keep unbroken from then on. The crowning was so free of dispute that *mar a thàinig Niall*, "the way Niall came", became a saying for any smooth handing-over of power. Its first written use is in a tavern ledger in the capital, set against a debt settled without argument.

The council drew the kingdom into shires, each *siorrachd* named for its seat, on the human district lines. The humans' surveys were the only complete ones on the island. The kingdom used them and did not say so. A *maor* was set over each shire to collect the Crown's dues. In the Tuathaich shires the maoir were Ròdaich, and at Ceann mhòr the maor took the old human magistrate's house, which the townspeople went on calling by its old name.

Then the sea came in. On {{date:V-0017}} the Great Wave, Tonn Mhòr Cnoc chaol, struck the southern coast without warning and wrecked Cnoc chaol, Cuan dhearg and Achadh mhin in a single night. It was the first disaster the new state met on its own terms, and it met it well. The relief parties counted a little over nine hundred dead, and found the boats of Cuan dhearg in the fields behind the town. The council moved the survivors of Achadh mhin off their salted fields and paid for their seed from the treasury, the first relief the restored kingdom ever granted. The old site is called an Tràigh Bhàthte, the drowned strand, and nobody builds there. From the survivors the south took a word: *tonn*, wave, for anything sudden and overwhelming. *Tha e san tonn*, they say, he is in the wave of it. The south would need the phrase again.

In {{year:V-0023}} the human coinage was withdrawn. At the mint in the old counting-house at Cathair dhearg, Fearchar mac Dhòmhnaill, called Fearchar Bonn, struck the Rìoghachd coin, and each coin's seal was pressed with ink ground from obsidian dust, so that a clipped coin showed colour at its edge. The coin was honest and it was sacred, and the kingdom liked it to be both.

The north paid for it. Tuathaich households had kept their savings in human coin, and those savings were now worth their metal by weight and no more. The Moot petitioned for an exchange. The petition was received and filed. It is the first petition in this book to end that way, and not the last. At Doire mhin a smith named Joseph Marlow melted his neighbours' dead coin into ploughshares and sold them back for the cost of the charcoal. The Tuathaich still sing of him in their own tongue; a library clerk once gave a verse of it in plain prose: *he turned the king's silence into iron, and the iron into bread.*

## III. Of the Rite, the Faiths and the Law

In {{year:V-0028}} the Obsidian Rite became Creideamh nan Ròdach, ordered doctrine in place of diffuse reverence, built around the leyline and the unbroken line of the obsidian-touched. Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was named Àrd-choimheadaiche, the first high custodian of the Rite, and seated at Seann Skell on the southern coast. His first task was a written order of worship. There had never been one.

The Hawk houses round Cnoc bheag would not take it. Creideamh an t-Seabhaig, the Rite's older form, needed no custodian above the local altar, they said. They were not compelled, and the long quarrel between the two forms of the Rite begins here. The Wolf houses of the south-west, whose god Clach was never the Rite's, sent no witnesses, only a letter wishing the Rite well. The capital's own faith, Creideamh an Aon-adharcaich Dhuibh, was given the coronation regalia to keep, as it had kept them before the humans came, and refused the high custodian a share. Within a year the orthodox Rite had one house that disowned it, one that ignored it politely and one that outranked it at court. That was a fair picture of the kingdom's faiths for the rest of the age.

In the north the old ways were kept quietly. The Tuathaich held to Creideamh nan Tuathach and to the fragment called the First Canoe, which the Ròdaich priesthood calls superstition. The library has never been shown the fragment and records only that it is kept. At Baile chrom the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane held the rites of Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise openly for the first time since the Severance. The cult keeps one night of silence a year for Sìne, the Peaceful Goose, and does not tell outsiders which. Most Tuathaich towns worshipped Cnut, the Yellow Scorpion, as the west-coast Ròdaich did. How they came to that god the record does not say.

In {{year:V-0046}} a wall between the two peoples was proposed and rejected. It was rejected as unnecessary, not on principle; the two peoples were already keeping apart. The council minute that rejects it runs to four lines. The fourth reads *They do not come south.* Five of the twelve initialled it, and the other seven did not sign against it. The library keeps that minute in a case. It is a small document, and a good deal of the age is in it.

The law came north in the same way. Eilidh nic Leòid, first of the circuit justices, ruled at Seann Bhral in {{year:V-0038}} that land held by Tuathaich under human title passed to the Crown, while the right to work it stayed with the holders and their heirs. The Leòid ruling is cited in nearly every land case of the age. On her northern circuit in {{year:V-0068}} she heard nineteen cases at Ceann mhòr, seventeen of them Tuathaich suits over land, and found for the Crown in fifteen, citing her own ruling. Her rulings became the kingdom's first law-book. Nobody in the record calls her unjust. She was exact, and the law she was exact about had been written for one people.

In {{year:V-0032}} Walter Hale asked leave to open schools in the north that taught in the humans' tongue. The council answered that the kingdom's schools taught in Ròdais and it had no others. The Moot's minute calls the answer "clear, at least." In {{year:V-0066}} Agnes Pike opened such a school at Doire ghlas anyway, with Moot money and without leave, and within ten years there were nine. The council never licensed them and never closed them. For most of the age that was the kingdom's way with the north: it forbade little there and helped with less.

## IV. Of the Mountain, the Guilds and the Fair

By {{year:V-0050}} the mine's yield had matched, and then passed, its colonial output. The method had not changed. Only the owners had.

The Crown granted the Sloc Mòr, the great shaft at Achadh dhomhain, to Uisdean mac Ùisdein of Cathair mhòr, whose house had held an overseer's post under the humans. The terms gave the Crown a fifth. They were the humans' terms with the names changed. The council would not end the continuous working the humans had ordered, day and night, so the mountain was still given no season of rest. The custodians entered a protest and then blessed the shafts. The hewers had a saying for it:

> *Cha d' fhuair a' bheinn fois a-riamh,*
> *is cha d' fhuair sinne.*
> The mountain never got its rest,
> and neither did we.

Families from the hungry east came to the vein-towns for wages, and Muileann dhearg doubled in three years. The humans' railway was mended to the pithead, and in {{year:V-0056}} the first ore train ran from the Sloc Mòr to Cathair dhearg under the kingdom's name on the humans' rails. At Muileann chrom the families who worked the small shunting engines were counted separately and lived apart in rows by the lines. For forty years the public buildings of the south were built of the Sloc Mòr's grey spoil-stone, sold under the Mac Ùisdein quarry-mark: what the mountain had thrown away.

In {{year:V-0059}} the apprentice-binding custom of the Holy Age was revived, now reserved in so many words to Ròdaich applicants. The Guild of Hewers came first, its oath sworn with a hand on a piece of ore, and shipwrights, chandlers, lamp-makers and weavers followed. Tuathaich boys already bound to Ròdaich masters were released from their indentures. Some went north. Most stayed on as day-labour, outside the guilds and their wages. The rule did not drive them away. It kept them where the pay was worst.

Off Cathair gheal, ships that flew no flag, some crewed by men who had served the human administration, began raiding the north-western coast. The Tuathaich towns suffered first and were not defended. In {{year:V-0085}}, having asked twice and been sent nothing, the fishermen of Cathair gheal armed their own boats and drove the raiders from the bay. The council then fined the town for bearing arms without licence, and the town paid.

On {{date:V-0088}} the fair at Muileann òg was established, and it became the largest recurring gathering on the island. Its first toll-mistress, Oighrig nic Mhathain, set its rules, including this one: Tuathaich goods to be sold only through Ròdaich factors. The rules are still read aloud when each fair opens. Two years later the Còmhrag began at Ceann leathan, a yearly melee called a martial tradition that served more and more as an outlet for a people with few others. Two men died in the first one the record remembers.

On {{date:V-0100}} there was a cluster of births in the shunting families, the generation called Splatter and Dodge's. Seachran and Brean were among them. They would grow up to be the closest allies of a man not yet born.

## V. Of the Library at Muileann chaol

On {{date:V-0070}}, which was Bealltainn, a great library was founded at Muileann chaol. It now keeps the fullest surviving record of everything in this book, and the reader should keep that in mind. Its first keeper, Ailean mac Iain, called Ailean Leabhar, brought the council's minute-books and the remains of the humans' tithe ledgers with him, took in the custodians' rolls that had lain in the town's cellars since the Severance war, and spent years having them read. Before the year was out *a chur san Leabharlann*, to put it in the Library, meant to make a thing settled and beyond dispute. Ailean Leabhar disliked the phrase. The library settles nothing, he said; it only keeps.

In {{year:V-0078}} an office was made to keep the kingdom's record, the direct ancestor of the archive this book is drawn from. The first royal chronicler, Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill, a council clerk, kept her record at Muileann chaol and not at the capital, and wrote in her first entry that a record kept beside the throne is kept for the throne. She sent clerks to every shire to copy parish books, grave-lists and harbour rolls. The south filled forty chests. The northern shires filled one, because few Tuathaich would speak to the clerks.

The five lighthouses of the humans were given Ròdaich keepers. At Baile chrom, on the Tuathaich coast, the keeper's warrant told him to report what he saw on land as well as at sea. In time he reported that the Grey Night cult kept its vigil in the lighthouse's own shadow. The council took no action.

The library also dug. At Làrach an Dùin it found walls older than any Ròdaich building and no writing at all, and catalogued the finds as belonging to the Old Ones, purpose unknown. At Làrach an Teampaill the Seann Spioradan houses asked it not to dig, and the keeper entered the ruin as *measured and let be.* The inscription on the obelisk near Cnoc bheag matched no script known on the island. In this age, as in every age, the Seann-Dhaoine appear only in what they left and in what the living will not do near it.

When Ailean Leabhar died in {{year:V-0144}}, Mòrag nic Coinnich became keeper. She began the practice, kept ever since, of writing each item's source beside it. In {{year:V-0152}} Gormshuil laid down the chronicle. Her last entry is a list of what the record did not hold: the northern towns' own accounts, the humans' treaties in their own tongue, and the Board's surveys. Her successor, Tormod mac Sheumais, called Tormod Pinn for the speed of his hand, copied the list into the front of his first book and added nothing to it. He struck nothing out, either.

## VI. Of Brìghde and Eòghan, and the First Word of the Depletion

Niall died, and his daughter Brìghde was crowned on {{date:V-0092}} without contest: a mortal succession in an aristocracy that stood very near the undying. It was unremarkable then. It looks strange now. Gormshuil wrote that the succession came *mar a thàinig Niall*, the only idiom she let into the chronicle in all her years.

On {{date:V-0105}} the survey reports began quietly to note that the vein's yield was falling. The survey behind them was the work of Iain mac Ruairidh, called Iain Mapa, of the Mine Board, and it showed the Sloc Mòr thinning to the east and south. The Board filed it without circulating it and moved Iain Mapa to the silver works at Muileann chiar. The next year the Board moved its clerks to Cathair mhòr and made a rule that its surveys were kept there and not sent to the library. The state did not act for years. The library could not know what it was not sent. It knows now, and it counts the delay as part of the Depletion.

Brìghde's reign ended young, and no cause was made public. Her cousin Eòghan was crowned on {{date:V-0111}}, amid the first real tension over a succession that the restored kingdom had known. The chronicler's entry gives the fact and nothing more. No chronicler since has added anything, and this book adds nothing either, because the library holds nothing to add. That December the council's minutes recorded votes against a succession for the first time. They name the councillors and not their reasons. Two of them did not sit again.

On {{date:V-0115}} Dubhan was born, mortal, into the confinement the Severance had made, decades after the war that had closed the island to the people his line came from. No chronicle of that year mentions him, and none could have.

The kingdom went on round the Depletion it had not announced. In {{year:V-0126}} the second census measured the gap between Ròdaich and Tuathaich living for the first time. The report was not made public. A census clerk, Seonag nic Ille-Mhoire, left a note among her papers saying that she had asked to see it and been refused, and that note is the only mention of the report outside the council. The library knows the report existed only because a clerk wrote down that she had been refused it.

That year Walter Hale died, and the Moot chose Mary Coyle, a fisherman's widow, the first woman in its chair. The Còmhrag grew bloodier. In {{year:V-0133}} six fighters died, and the custodians asked for it to end. The fair-wardens of Ceann leathan, whose town lived off the crowds, refused.

In {{year:V-0141}} a gallery gave way under Muileann dhearg, in the reopened Cill ghlas district, along a line the surveyors had marked as sound. Thirty-one hewers died. It was small beside the Rending. It was the same fault. New excavation rules followed. They applied to Ròdaich workers, and the records suggest that their enforcement near the Tuathaich country was inconsistent on purpose. At the pits round Àth leathan, where Ròdaich overseers worked Tuathaich hands, the timbering rules were posted and never paid for. The overseers' books show the timber ordered and not delivered. Àth leathan will come into this book again.

## VII. Of the Search for a Second Vein

After Muileann dhearg the Guild of Hewers bound sixty apprentices to fill its rolls, and none of them were Tuathaich. In {{year:V-0146}} fire ran through the fair at Muileann òg. Its court sat in a field the next day and the fair went on, and Oighrig nic Mhathain died of her burns. That winter the Rail Board refused to carry the Moot's goods, so the north's wool went south by cart, a week on the road. In the north that way is called the Carters' Road. In the south it has no name.

In {{year:V-0150}} the Moot began a register of Tuathaich hewers killed or maimed in the western pits, since no Ròdaich book counted them. It has been kept ever since and never sent south. In those years Dubhan was a child in the north, watching an undying caste hold every real advantage on an island his own ancestors had been pushed to the edge of. The obsidian was never his to inherit.

The age had its good keepers too. When dry years came back to Achadh shean in {{year:V-0154}}, the council sent grain up the ore line out of the Sloc Mòr's share, and nobody in the shire died of hunger. The chronicler recorded it because it deserved recording. The maor had twelve wells dug to the rock, and four are still in use. At Tobar dhearg a keeper drowned going out to a boat on the reef. His daughter Ciorstaidh nic Artair saved its crew, took his warrant, and set a belled post on the reef. No boat went aground there while she kept the light.

On {{date:V-0162}} Feallsanachd an Fhèidh woke from its long sleep. Its founding question, why the vein answers some hands and not others, mattered again now that the vein was running short. Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain lectured in the sacred wood near Muileann chiar, and at Caol shean her students copied the census line on the obsidian-touched and argued over it openly, which the Rite did not do. They set the old pillars and the obelisk side by side and found signs in common and no meaning. Seonaid wrote that the Old Ones had been here and had said so, and that nobody living could hear them.

In {{year:V-0174}} Alasdair mac Ùisdein, grandson of the first holder of the Sloc Mòr, borrowed against his house's share to search for a second vein. The Board lent its surveyors, and the Crown lent its name. Seonaid wrote to the Board that the search asked the wrong question. Her school had always asked why the vein answered some hands, and nobody had asked why there was only one vein. The Board filed the letter.

The search failed. Forty-one shafts were sunk between the hills above Dùn dhearg and the moors south of Baile ruadh, and none struck the vein. Mòrag nic Coinnich afterwards found a Holy Age list in the library naming the same hills as searched and barren. The kingdom had repeated a failure its own library could have warned it of, if anyone had asked. The house of Mac Ùisdein paid part of Dùn dhearg's damages and sold half its share back to the Crown. The dry shafts at Baile ruadh were left open, and sheep and one child fell into them before the shire fenced them at its own cost.

In {{year:V-0189}} Mary Coyle laid down the chair, and Daniel Frayne of Muileann leathan followed her, the first speaker born after the Severance. He had taught young Tuathaich to bind books when the southern guilds would not take them. That winter the fair gave one day to northern goods, still sold through factors but under the makers' own marks, the first time a Tuathaich name was written on anything sold there. The first such mark was the Doire ghlas weavers' grey twill with a white thread at the selvedge. Within the year the weavers of Baile chiar were weaving a white thread into theirs.

On {{date:V-0192}} the Depletion could no longer be denied. The last untouched sections of the vein were smaller than anyone wanted to admit. The surveyor-general, Catrìona nic Neacail, published the map of them, and it fitted on one sheet. Among the Board's files she found Iain Mapa's survey of thirty years before, showing the same thinning, and she sent the library a copy with a note of the day the Board had received it. She resigned within the year. Iain Mapa died at Muileann chiar the next spring. His own copy of the survey was found among his papers with the Board's receipt pinned to it. He had kept the proof that he had told them.

Muileann dhearg lost a third of its households. Its Fèidh hall closed, and its bell was sold to Caol shean, where it still rings for lectures.

## VIII. Of Mairead and the Failing of the Ore

Mairead was crowned on {{date:V-0199}}. Eòghan's reign had ended in the decade the Depletion became public, and she inherited a kingdom whose founding resource was visibly failing for the first time since the restoration. That year the Guild of Hewers bound no apprentices for the first time since its charter.

The north began building things that did not depend on the mountain. In {{year:V-0202}} wool mills went up at Doire ghlas with Moot money. The Rail Board refused their cloth until the circuit justice at Seann Bhral, Ruairidh mac Phàdraig, ruled that the Board was a carrier of the Crown and must carry for any payer. It was the first ruling the Moot ever won in a Ròdaich court. The Board appealed, and the council let the appeal lapse. The minute does not say why.

In {{year:V-0204}} the Crown bought the last of the house of Mac Ùisdein's working shafts, and from then on the Board worked the vein in the Crown's name alone.

On {{date:V-0207}} Dubhan began his experiments: early attempts to make a substitute fuel from the byproducts of mining, and all of them failed. The record barely troubles to name them. That same month the lowest galleries of the Sloc Mòr were allowed to flood, because the pumps cost more to run than the ore beneath them was worth. The Board's minute calls it a pause.

The chronicle of those years is full of small alarms. Panthers crossed the south-west, a thing named in the Holy Age lists and not seen in living memory, and the old list named the year after them as a hungry one. At Muileann chrom the yard families stopped the ore trains for three days over cut wages; the Board settled on the fourth day and dismissed eleven men on the fifth. Then the harvest after the panthers came in good, and Tormod Pinn drew a line through his own note of the year before. This book has always liked him for it.

In {{year:V-0216}} the third census published its figures openly, the first time in the kingdom's history. It was a small, symbolic reform and changed nothing in the structure. Read aloud at the Moot, the figures showed the northern towns at about half the Ròdaich measure in every column the clerks had chosen to take. The Moot's minute says only: *Now written.* The census carried a line for the obsidian-touched, as the first one had, giving a number and nothing else. Tormod Pinn copied it into the chronicle without comment, as Gormshuil had done.

Tormod Pinn died at his desk in {{year:V-0220}}, and Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh became royal chronicler. She was the first to add, at the foot of each year, a note of what the record could not learn. Her first such note concerned the northern shires: the census clerks there had been told the figures and had not counted them, and the figures they were told were the Moot's. The kingdom's first honest census was honest in the north because the Moot had supplied the numbers.

Mòrag nic Coinnich now called the shires' record-chests to Muileann chaol. At Seann Tarr the shire keeper burned his chest rather than send it, saying the shire's record belonged in the shire, and the chest is lost. In {{year:V-0226}} the library doubled its holdings, and from then on the history of Rodos was kept in one place instead of many.

No Tuathaich record was called in. The Moot's minute-books, the schools' registers and the vigil-books of Baile chrom stayed in the north in private keeping. The library's new wing was built of Sloc Mòr stone. The provincial chests filled three floors, and the fourth was left empty for what had not been sent. It is still mostly empty.

In the west the Board was pulling out. In {{year:V-0229}} it closed the branch line to Àth leathan, citing the falling yield of the western pits. The Tuathaich hewers there kept working under their own pit captains and paid the Crown's fifth by the Leòid ruling. The Board took the payments and entered them as arrears. At Cnoc thais the pits passed to their hewers in the same way. The south did not notice. There was nothing in the western hills it wanted any longer.

In {{year:V-0235}} Raghnall mac Mhuirich became keeper of the library. The price of ore-fuel at the fair doubled, and at Cnoc fhiadhaich households were found picking ore from the spoil-heaps to burn. The Board posted watchmen. The watchmen were from the town and saw nothing.

On {{date:V-0238}} the first working dubhan was made. It was crude, but it burned. It was not yet an economy. For the time being it was one man's answer to a crisis the caste in power had still not fully faced. At the foot of that year Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh wrote that a fuel made without the vein was reported from the north and that she had not seen it. It is the first mention of dubhan in the royal record, and she spelled the word three ways in two lines. In the capital the poorer streets were left dark to save ore. By {{year:V-0242}} ore was sold at the fair only by the sack, and in the end only by the lump, for altars.

Cathal was crowned on {{date:V-0243}}, the feast day. Mairead's reign had ended while Dubhan's experiments were still failing more often than they worked, and Cathal inherited a kingdom that did not yet know how close its answer was. The new dies at the mint still pressed the obsidian ink into the seal.

## IX. Of Dubhan's Fire

The fuel came south within three years. In {{year:V-0245}} a company of shipmasters set up a dubhan works at Seann Skell on the southern coast, the first outside the north. The record does not say how they came by the process, and this book will not supply what the record lacks. The high custodian wrote to the houses of the Rite that the new fuel was not forbidden, since it was not ore, and not blessed, since it was not ore. The works sold its first year's output to the south-coast boats and the capital's lamp-houses, and the dark streets were the first to be lit again.

In {{year:V-0251}} the five coastal beacons, which had burned obsidian-lit flame for more than a century, became the first works formally converted to dubhan. The flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg was carried in a closed lantern to the Rite's house at Seann Skell and left to burn out on the altar. Custodians from four shires attended, and Ciorstaidh nic Artair, keeper of the light, carried the lantern. The south coast has a song about it in more than one form. This is the shortest:

> *Dh'fhalbh an lasair às an tùr,*
> *tha solas ann, ach chan ann leinn.*
> The flame has gone out of the tower;
> there is a light there, but it is not ours.

At Baile chrom, on the Tuathaich coast, the conversion was done by Tuathaich fitters from Caol mhòr, because the Lighthouse Board had none of its own to send so far. The keeper's report names none of them.

The change reached everything, and quickly. On {{date:V-0259}}, Bealltainn, the Rail Board ran its first dubhan engine on the ore line. It carried no ore. The roadside inns put in dubhan lamps, ending centuries of oil and wick within a decade. At An Leòmhann Buidhe the landlord kept one wick lamp burning over the door beside the new ones, and a wick lamp over an inn door is still called a Leòmhann.

In {{year:V-0257}} Daniel Frayne died, and the Moot chose Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas, a mill manager. His pupils rebound the Moot's minute-books from the first meeting. The Moot's minute says that a second set was made and hidden elsewhere. The library notes this and has not gone looking.

In {{year:V-0269}} the last working face under Muileann dhearg was closed. Its families were moved to the Hewers' Row at Seann Toll, and most of them worked on the dubhan tanks in the harbour. Muileann dhearg was left with its chapel, its graves and a caretaker.

By {{year:V-0272}} *dubhan* meant the fuel and not the man, years before the economy built on it was complete. A letter of the Fèidh school at Caol shean coined *dubhan-tùsail* for a first attempt that looked like a dead end until it turned out not to be. On {{date:V-0275}} the Guild of Hewers dissolved itself. Its rolls, its oath-stone of ore and its sick-roll went to the library. Its last master, Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh, wrote that the guild had outlived the mountain's patience.

By {{year:V-0278}} markets, the army's supply lines and shipping were all moving to the new fuel. The obsidian caste watched its own source of power fail and did not object. The house of Mac Ùisdein sold the last of its share in the spoil and put its money into dubhan shipping at Ros dhomhain, the only family of the vein that moved before the vein failed it.

In {{year:V-0282}} the fitters of dubhan engines were chartered as a guild at Seann Skell. Like every guild before it, its rolls were open to Ròdaich only, and the Tuathaich fitters were let go. They went home and fitted engines without a guild. That winter the Moot voted to take up the new fuel, and the delegates of the western pit towns walked out. When the northern depots opened, Martha Greaves wrote in the minute that the north now bought its fire from the south, and did not say whether that was good.

On {{date:V-0286}} the coinage was pegged to dubhan output instead of obsidian reserves, a quiet admission of which resource now ran the kingdom. The obsidian ink was left out of the seal, and the seal was plain. Fearchar Bonn's first dies went to the library, where they lie beside the Hewers' oath-stone. The high custodian protested that the plain seal showed the kingdom no longer held the vein sacred. The council answered that the kingdom had never said it did. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh copied both letters into the chronicle. It is one of the few places in the record where the kingdom said plainly what it was.

The north has a rhyme from these years, heard there in both tongues. This is its Ròdais form:

> *Dubh an gual, 's dubh an dubhan,*
> *ach cò leis an teine?*
> Black the coal, and black the dubhan;
> but whose is the fire?

## X. Of the Holdouts of the Western Hills

In {{year:V-0289}} the western pit towns met at Muileann àrsaidh, apart from the Moot, and agreed to keep working their seams and sell their coal to any northern household that would buy it. Àth naomh, with only a few old shafts, joined them, saying it would rather be cold with its neighbours than warm alone. So a group of northern communities refused to fold into the new economy, and kept working the last workable coal seams rather than trade independence for dependence on one man's fuel. They were Àth leathan, Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg. Their spokeswoman was Ruth Calder, a pit captain's daughter from Àth leathan. The Moot did not join them and did not condemn them. At Cnoc thais Tom Varley reopened two shafts abandoned since the humans' time. The coal was poor. It was theirs, and it heated the town.

In {{year:V-0295}} Ellen Marsh, a schoolteacher of Doire ghlas, finished eleven books of Tuathaich accounts of the Severance and the settlement, taken down from the old people of the north. She formally asked Leabharlann Muileann chaol to keep Tuathaich oral history beside the Ròdaich record. The request was tabled, not rejected, and it is tabled still. Raghnall mac Mhuirich replied that it awaited the council's view on the standing of oral testimony. The council has never given one. Ellen Marsh wrote back that oral testimony had been good enough for the census clerks when they took the Moot's figures. Her letter is filed with the request. It is short, and nobody has answered it.

The holdout pits undersold dubhan across the north, and the Moot's own mills bought their coal. In {{year:V-0300}} the Board's northern revenue fell by a quarter, the first fall since the Severance, and its report named the western pits as the cause and the Leòid ruling as the remedy. Eighty years after Eilidh nic Leòid gave it, her ruling was put to the use the north had always feared. The Board declared the western seams Crown property, holding that the right to work them had lapsed when the holders stopped paying the fifth. The holders said they had stopped paying when the Board stopped carrying the coal. Both were true. The pits were ordered closed.

A company of enforcers was posted to Seann Skell on the western coast, which is not the Rite's Seann Skell in the south. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh noted that it was the first armed company the kingdom had sent toward the north since the Severance. Martha Greaves wrote that the western towns would not close their pits because of a letter, and asked for a year while the Moot talked with them. Her letter was received and filed.

In the autumn of {{year:V-0305}} the first skirmishes began. Each was too small to name and none was recorded, but together they led straight into open war. At the foot of the year Beathag wrote that clashes were reported in the west, that the Board's reports of them had not been given to her, and that she had asked for them. They were not given. At Muileann àrsaidh the pit-heads were walled and the road from the south dug across. On {{date:V-0308}} Ruth Calder offered the council the Crown's fifth in coal if the Board would carry it and let the pits work until the seams were spent. The letter reached the library. No answer to it did.

In {{year:V-0309}} the Moot resolved, by nine votes, that it was no party to the quarrel and would feed anyone who came to its towns hungry. That summer it filled its granaries at Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas beyond the usual. The minute gives no reason. It did not need to.

In August Beathag laid down the office, and her clerk Sìleas nic Rath became royal chronicler. It is her record of the war that the library would later seal. In October came the mobilization order. Regiments not called out since the Severance were re-formed and re-equipped, openly, months before anyone in power admitted that a war was coming. Command went to Somhairle mac Lachlainn, who had risen among the Board's enforcers. The rolls of the Severance companies were brought out of the council's chests to name the new regiments, and the old names were read aloud before new men. The list had been kept for nearly ninety years, and now it was used.

On {{date:V-0314}} Martha Greaves carried the Moot's petition to the council in person. She asked that the western pits be left to work out their seams, which would be spent within a few years in any case. She was the first speaker of the Moot ever to enter the council chamber. The petition was received and filed. That winter the Rail Board carried arms and dubhan west to Seann Bhral, and the yard at Muileann chrom worked on the trains day and night.

## XI. Of An Cogadh Fada, the Long War

Late in January {{year:V-0316}} hewers from the five towns gathered at Àth leathan, where the road from the south enters the hills. The Board's lists name Ruth Calder and Tom Varley among them. The regiments marched west from Cathair dhearg with their carts and lorries running on dubhan.

The Battle of Àth leathan was fought on {{date:V-0318}}, the first great engagement of An Cogadh Fada. The state, already leaning on Dubhan's fuel to prop up its fading power, moved against the holdouts directly. The open record counts the kingdom's dead and not the holdouts'. Tuathaich telling puts theirs in the hundreds. Ruth Calder's name does not appear in the open record after the battle. The library cannot say whether she fell there. It records only that her name stops.

The holdouts gave up the southern pits and fell back into the hills round Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais. The pits of Àth leathan were flooded on the Board's order, and the field was left unploughed. The seventh regiment was raised on the west coast to hold the road behind the army. Before the month was out a temporary tithe, justified by the war, was laid on every sale of dubhan in the kingdom. It was never repealed. Fleets were raised and sent round the western cape to close the Tuathaich ports of the north-west to every boat except fishing boats, and to search those.

In June the regiments invested Muileann àrsaidh. The town held out for weeks on stored coal and potatoes, and its pit-heads were taken one at a time. Sìleas nic Rath's open record gives the day it fell and nothing more. The army's dubhan was taken ahead of every other use, and the capital's poorer streets went dark again, as they had been before the fuel came.

The families of the western pits came into Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas with what they could carry. The Moot fed them from its own stores, as it had resolved to. The council sent nothing and forbade nothing. The Moot's minute records the names of the families, among the few Tuathaich records of the war the library does not hold. Agnes Pike's old school at Doire ghlas was given over to them, and Ellen Marsh, who taught there, took down their accounts. This book would give a good deal to read them.

In August Cnoc thais was taken after the regiments cut the road to its pits, and Tom Varley's two shafts were fired and sealed. The open record gives the number of holdouts taken and not the number who went on into the hills. With the fleets away in the north-west, eastern raiders took two salt-boats off Àth bheag and burned a third.

Through the autumn the kingdom went on raising regiments as if it expected a longer war than it fought. The fourth regiment was quartered at Ceann mhòr in the old human magistrate's house, and the Moot's protest was received. In December the Grey Night cult at Baile chrom kept its vigil as always, and the regiments reported a night when the whole town was dark and silent, and searched it, and found nothing. That month Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg opened their roads without a siege. Their pits were flooded. The open record names no holdouts taken at either.

Late in October of the next year a column of holdouts, several hundred strong, came out of the western hills, and through November it moved south-east through the middle of the island toward the capital. Why it did so is in the sealed archive. Tuathaich telling gives its leader the name Owen Barrow. The open record gives no name. On {{date:V-0350}} it camped on the fields by Muileann ghlas, a day's walk from Cathair dhearg, and the first and third regiments were brought down the fair road to meet it. The booths of the fair-ground were cleared for their camp, and the fair court's books for that year end without a closing entry.

The Battle of Muileann ghlas was fought on {{date:V-0352}}, the second and last great battle of the war. The holdouts were broken within a single year's campaign, the column's last march through the autumn. They were descended from humans once pushed to the island's edge, and now they were pushed back again, this time by a fuel one of their own had made. The open record lists the first and third regiments on the field and gives no count of the column's dead. When word reached the hills, the holdouts still there came down to the seventh regiment's posts on the Àth leathan road. They were disarmed and sent home, and the open record counts their weapons.

So An Cogadh Fada ended on {{date:V-0355}}, twenty months after it began, and it has been called the Long War all the same. Nine regiments and four fleets were raised in it. All are still active, and each is named for the town where it is stationed.

Before the year was out the Tuathaich had a new saying. *Chaidh am fadachadh*, they got long-warred, was said first of the defeat in the west and then of any defeat after holding out too long. It is said as a joke, and the people who say it do not laugh.

## XII. Of the Peace, and of the Present Year

The regiments were not stood down. The council kept each at the town where it was raised and paid them out of the wartime tithe. With the last organized resistance gone, Dubhan's fuel became in practice compulsory across the whole island. The last western seams were flooded and capped with concrete, and no coal was dug on the island after that outside the Board's own works. The holdouts taken at Muileann ghlas were escorted north as far as the boundary stones, the stones set in {{year:V-0003}} that nobody had been paid to move. The Moot's register added their names in a column of their own. Somhairle mac Lachlainn was given a seat on the council, the first soldier to sit there since the captains of the Severance war.

In {{year:V-0363}} the kingdom raised a stone at Cathair mhòr, beside the Mac Ùisdein market cross, with the names of the regiments' dead. On the field of Àth leathan Tuathaich families built a cairn without leave. The seventh regiment's patrols have left it standing, and it grows by a stone or two every time they pass. At Muileann ghlas the field was sown again, and for years its farmer turned weapons up in the furrows and handed them to the first regiment, which keeps a list.

On {{date:V-0365}} the library's complete record of the Long War was sealed from public access for fifty years. It is the only sealed archive in the kingdom's history. What is still open of the chronicle for the war years is Sìleas nic Rath's list of places, the regiments present and the counts she was allowed to give. Her full record went into the sealed archive with everything else. The reader should therefore know what has been done here. The account of the war given above is drawn from the open record, the towns' reports and Tuathaich telling, and none of it can be checked against what is sealed. Ailean Leabhar said the library settles nothing and only keeps. He did not foresee that it would one day keep something it was not allowed to read.

The war left its accounts. The Treasury carried the wartime tithe forward as a standing line under the regiments' pay, and no council minute records a decision to keep it. The fuel-fitters took the contract for the regiments' engines and doubled their rolls, and every new fitter was Ròdaich.

In June {{year:V-0374}} Anna nic Phàil, councillor for Cathair dhomhain, brought into the council the Moot's petition to compensate the Tuathaich communities for their losses in the war. She spoke for it once. On {{date:V-0375}} the proposal was raised in the capital and quietly allowed to die without a vote. The Moot's minute on it is one line long: *Allowed to die.* Martha Greaves laid down the chair that year, and the Moot chose Peter Hale, great-grandson of its first speaker. The fourth regiment left the magistrate's house at Ceann mhòr. The Moot asked for the house to be given to the town. It was given to the maor.

By {{year:V-0380}} the Tuathaich were calling the tabled archive request and the dead reparations motion, together, *seann-chunntas*: an old reckoning nobody in power will act on and nobody has cancelled. Official Ròdaich usage has not taken up the word. This book uses it, because the record should hold both peoples' words.

That year the mountain was closed, or nearly. The Board shut the upper galleries of the Sloc Mòr and kept one face open under the Rite's custody to cut ore for the altars. Twelve hewers were kept on to work it, the last of their trade on the island. On {{date:V-0385}} the last ore train ran to Cathair dhearg. The ore line has kept its name and carries dubhan, wool and soldiers. The second regiment set a guard at the library over the sealed archive, and Raghnall mac Mhuirich wrote to the council that the library had not asked for one. On the northern road, where the boundary stones stand, the sixth regiment keeps posts, and Tuathaich going south show a pass for the first time since the Severance. The council's minute calls the passes temporary. The reader will know by now what the kingdom's temporary measures tend to become.

The last year and the present one have been much like other years. Tuathaich wool was sold at Muileann òg on its northern day through Ròdaich factors, as at the first fair. The Còmhrag was fought with staves and fists, and one man died. Ellen Marsh's eleven books have been copied by hand in the northern schools and are kept there; the library's catalogue lists the tabled request and not the books. The Moot met at Caol mhòr under Peter Hale, its minute-books unbroken from Walter Hale's first meeting, with no standing in Ròdaich law and no request for any. Every lamp, engine, ship and lorry on the island runs on dubhan, the coinage is pegged to it, and the tithe is taken on every sale. In the summer the Rite's twelve hewers cut the year's altar-ore. It filled four baskets, and the high custodian divided it among the houses by lot.

On {{date:V-0393}} Sìleas nic Rath wrote at the foot of the year that the kingdom's record is complete in every part but two: the war, which is sealed, and the north, which was not asked.

So the account stands on {{date:V-0394}}. The coal is all but gone. The economy runs on a name that once belonged to one man. The Ròdaich hold the capital and the sea. The Tuathaich hold the north, a grievance five years old, and a memory of the First Canoe that the people who twice defeated them still call folklore. The restored kingdom promised a return, and what it delivered was a continuation. It kept the humans' tariff, their surveys, their rails and their terms for the mountain, and it kept the humans' descendants where the humans had put them. It also kept faith with the drowned towns of the south, and its wells at Achadh shean still draw water. This book records both, because both are true.

The chronicle does not end here. It stops at the present year. The age that follows has no name yet, because nobody can yet say what began it. It is left to the next chronicler, who will be handed the whole record, and with it the things still missing from it: the war in its guarded room, which is to be opened fifty years from its sealing if the kingdom keeps its word; the fourth floor at Muileann chaol, built for what was not sent; the eleven books in the north; and the Moot's minutes with their hidden second set. The old reckoning is still owed. Whoever writes the next book should begin by asking for them.
