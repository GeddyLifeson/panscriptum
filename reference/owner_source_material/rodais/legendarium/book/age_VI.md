# The Sixth Book: An Aois Dhubhain, the Age of Dubhan

*DE 1 – 27*

> *Bho latha a' chiad dubhain cunntar na bliadhnaichean às ùr.*
> From the day of the first dubhan the years shall be counted anew.
>
> — the council's proclamation of the Dubhan Era, {{date:VI-0049a}}

## I. Of Dubhan's Fire

On {{date:VI-0001}}, about midsummer, the first working dubhan was made, and from that morning the island counts the years of its sixth age, the Dubhan Era, though nobody counted them so for fourteen years. It was crude, and it burned. Nobody yet traded in it; for the while it was one man's answer to a crisis that the caste in power had still not faced. At the foot of that year Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh wrote that a fuel made without the vein was reported from the north, and that she had not seen it. That is the first mention of dubhan in the royal record, and she spelled the word three ways in two lines.

In the capital the poorer streets were left dark to save coal. By {{year:VI-0005}} coal was sold at the fair only by the sack, and at last only by the lump, for altars.

Cathal was crowned on {{date:VI-0006}}, the feast of Lùnastal, by Macha's rite. Mairead's reign had ended while Dubhan's experiments still failed more often than they worked, and Cathal did not yet know how near his kingdom's answer lay. The new dies at the mint still pressed the coal ink into the seal.

The fuel came south within three years. In {{year:VI-0008}} a company of shipmasters set up a dubhan works at Seann Skell on the southern coast, the first outside the north, and how they came by the making of it they never told. The high custodian wrote to the houses of the Rite that the new fuel was not forbidden, since it was not coal, and not blessed, since it was not coal. It sold its first year's making to the south-coast boats and the capital's lamp-houses, and the dark streets were the first to be lit again.

In {{year:VI-0014}} the five coastal beacons, which had burned coal-lit flame for more than a century, were the first works of the kingdom to be turned over to dubhan by decree.

Ciorstaidh nic Artair was grey by then, but nobody else was let carry the lantern. Inside its thick glass the flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg burned with the dark, coloured edge that only the island's coal gives. She carried it into the Rite's house at Seann Skell in the late spring, down the hall between the custodians of four shires, who stood in their rows and did not speak. Her boots were loud on the flags.

At Brìde's altar she set the lantern down and opened its little door. The flame leaned towards the air, and steadied. Nobody told her to go, so she stayed, and so did they, while the last of the coal burned down. It took longer than anyone had thought. When the flame shrank to a blue bead and then to nothing, a thread of smoke went up and hung under the roof, and one of the custodians let out a breath as if he had been holding it the whole while.

The south coast has a song of that day in more than one form. This is the shortest:

> *Dh'fhalbh an lasair às an tùr,*
> *tha solas ann, ach chan ann leinn.*
> The flame has gone out of the tower;
> there is a light there, but it is not ours.

At Baile chrom, on the Tuathaich coast, the change was made by Tuathaich fitters from Caol mhòr, because the Lighthouse Board had none of its own to send so far. The keeper's report names none of them.

After that the change came to everything, and it came fast. On {{date:VI-0022}}, Bealltainn, the Rail Board ran its first dubhan engine on the coal line. It carried no coal. The drovers sold their oxen for lorries. On {{date:VI-0026a}} the fitters at Seann Skell set a lorry's engine under a closed carriage for the high custodian of the Rite, the first carriage on the island that needed no horse, and on {{date:VI-0041a}} buses began to run out of the capital to Inis mhòr and Cathair mhòr. The roadside inns put in dubhan lamps, and centuries of oil and wick were gone within a decade. At An Leòmhann Buidhe the landlord kept one wick lamp burning over the door beside the new ones, and a wick lamp over an inn door is still called a Leòmhann.

In {{year:VI-0020}} Daniel Frayne died, and the Moot chose Martha Greaves of Doire ghlas, a mill manager. His old pupils rebound the Moot's minute-books from the first meeting onward. The Moot's minute says that a second set was made and hidden elsewhere. The library notes this and has not gone looking.

In {{year:VI-0032}} the last working face under Muileann dhearg was closed. Its families were moved to the Hewers' Row at Seann Toll, and most of them went to work on the dubhan tanks at the river landing. Muileann dhearg was left with its chapel, its graves and a caretaker.

By {{year:VI-0035}} *dubhan* meant the fuel and no longer the man, years before the trade built on it was whole. A letter of the Fèidh school at Caol shean coined *dubhan-tùsail* for a first attempt that looks like a dead end until it turns out to be the road. On {{date:VI-0038}} the Guild of Hewers dissolved itself. Its rolls, its oath-stone of coal and its sick-roll went to the library. Its last master, Tormod mac Dhonnchaidh, wrote that the guild had outlived the mountain's patience.

By {{year:VI-0041}} the markets, the army's supply lines and the shipping were all going over to the new fuel. The coal caste watched its own source of power fail and did not object. The house of Mac Ùisdein sold the last of its share in the spoil and put its money into dubhan shipping at Ros dhomhain, the only family of the vein that moved before the vein failed it.

In {{year:VI-0045}} the fitters of dubhan engines were chartered as a guild at Seann Skell. Like every guild before it, its rolls were open to Dia-thìrich only, and the Tuathaich fitters were let go. They went home and fitted engines without a guild. That winter the Moot voted to take up the new fuel, and the delegates of the western pit towns got up and walked out. When the northern depots opened, Martha Greaves wrote in the minute that the north now bought its fire from the south, and she did not say whether that was good.

On {{date:VI-0049}} the coinage was pegged to the making of dubhan in place of the coal reserves, which was a quiet way of owning which of the two now ran the kingdom. At the same sitting the council proclaimed that the Age of the Kingdom had ended with the first working dubhan, and that the kingdom's years were counted anew from that day. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh re-headed fourteen years of her chronicle, and wrote at the foot of the page that she had never before had to move a year she was living in. The coal ink was left out of the seal, and the seal was plain. Fearchar Bonn's first dies went to the library, where they lie beside the Hewers' oath-stone. The high custodian protested that a plain seal showed the vein was no longer held sacred. The council answered that the kingdom had never said it did. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh copied both letters into the chronicle. It is one of the few places in the whole record where the kingdom said plainly what it was.

The north has a rhyme from those years, heard there in both tongues. This is its Dia-thìris form:

> *Dubh an gual, 's dubh an dubhan,*
> *ach cò leis an teine?*
> Black the coal, and black the dubhan;
> but whose is the fire?

## II. Of the Holdouts of the Western Hills

In the spring of {{year:VI-0052}} the western pit towns met at Muileann àrsaidh, apart from the Moot, and agreed to go on working their seams and to sell their coal to any household in the north that would buy it. Àth naomh, with only a few old shafts to its name, joined them, saying it would rather be cold with its neighbours than warm alone. So a knot of northern towns refused to fold themselves into the new trade. They kept to the last workable coal seams sooner than hang their fires on one man's fuel. They were Àth leathan, Muileann àrsaidh, Cnoc thais, Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg, and their spokeswoman was Ruth Calder, a pit captain's daughter from Àth leathan. The Moot neither joined nor condemned them. At Cnoc thais Tom Varley opened again two shafts that had lain abandoned since the humans' time. The coal was poor. It was theirs, and it kept the town warm.

In {{year:VI-0058}} Ellen Marsh, a schoolteacher of Doire ghlas, finished eleven books of Tuathaich accounts of the Severance and the settling, taken down from the old people of the north. She asked Leabharlann Muileann chaol, formally, to keep the Tuathaich tellings beside the Dia-thìrich record. The request was tabled, and it lies on the table yet. Raghnall mac Mhuirich replied that it awaited the council's view on the standing of spoken witness. The council has never given one. Ellen Marsh wrote back that spoken witness had been good enough for the census clerks when they took the Moot's figures. Her letter is filed with the request. It is short, and nobody has answered it.

The holdout pits undersold dubhan across the north, and the Moot's own mills bought their coal. In {{year:VI-0063}} the Board's northern revenue fell by a quarter, the first fall since the Severance, and its report named the western pits as the cause and the Leòid ruling as the cure. Eighty years after Eilidh nic Leòid gave it, her ruling was put to the use the north had always feared. The Board declared the western seams Crown property, holding that the right to work them had lapsed when the holders stopped paying the fifth. The holders answered that they had stopped paying when the Board stopped carrying the coal. Both were true. The pits were ordered shut.

A company of enforcers was posted to Seann Skell in the west, which is another town from the Rite's Seann Skell in the south. Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh noted that it was the first armed company the kingdom had sent toward the north since the Severance. Martha Greaves wrote that the western towns would not shut their pits because of a letter, and asked for a year while the Moot talked with them. Her letter was received and filed.

In the autumn of {{year:VI-0068}} the first skirmishes began. Each was too small to name and none was written down, and together they ran straight into open war. At the foot of that year Beathag wrote that clashes were reported in the west, and that she had asked for the Board's reports of them. They were not given. At Muileann àrsaidh the pit-heads were walled and the road from the south was dug across. On {{date:VI-0071}}, which was Christmas Day, Ruth Calder wrote to the council for the western towns and offered the Crown's fifth in coal, if the Board would carry it and let the pits work until the seams were spent. The letter reached the library. No answer to it did.

In {{year:VI-0072}}, in the depth of winter, the Moot resolved by nine votes that it was no party to the quarrel and would feed anyone who came to its towns hungry. That summer it filled its granaries at Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas beyond the usual. The minute gives no reason. It needed none.

In an Lùnastal Beathag laid down the office, and her clerk Sìleas nic Rath became royal chronicler. It is her record of the war that the library would later seal. In an Dàmhair came the order to mobilise. Regiments that had not been called out since the Severance were formed again and armed again, openly, months before anyone in power would admit that a war was coming. Command went to Somhairle mac Lachlainn, who had risen among the Board's enforcers. The rolls of the Severance companies were brought out of the council's chests to name the new regiments, and the old names were read aloud before new men. The list had been kept for nearly ninety years. Now it was used.

On {{date:VI-0077}}, Christmas Day again, Martha Greaves came to Cathair dhearg with the Moot's petition.

She had kept the feast on the road, in a cold carriage, and she came into the capital in the grey forenoon with the frost still white on the bridge. The Dia-thìrich keep no feast that day, and the council was sitting. She waited on a bench in a passage for most of an hour, the petition on her knee. Then a clerk said her name, in the Dia-thìris way, so that for a moment she did not know it for hers.

She was the first speaker of the Moot ever to walk into the council chamber. It was smaller than she had thought, and warm with a dubhan stove. Twelve faces turned to her. She read the petition standing. It asked that the western pits be let alone to work out their seams, which would be spent within a few years in any case. She read it to the end without hurrying, and laid it on the table.

"Received," said the clerk, and wrote.

It was filed. That winter the Rail Board carried arms and dubhan west to Seann Bhral, and the yard at Muileann chrom worked on the trains day and night.

## III. Of An Cogadh Fada, the Long War

*What is told here of the war comes from the open record, the towns' reports and the Tuathaich tellings; the rest is sealed.*

Late in am Faoilleach of {{year:VI-0079}} the hewers of the five towns came together at Àth leathan, where the road from the south goes up into the hills. In the north they tell it like this. It was bitter weather, with a thin snow blowing that would not lie, and the men and women came in by the hill paths with their pit-lamps lit at noon, the sky was so dark. They slept in the pit-sheds, and in the byres with the cattle for the warmth. Ruth Calder went from fire to fire with a list, asking who was here, and who was coming. The Board's lists name her among them, and Tom Varley too.

From Cathair dhearg the regiments marched west, and their carts and lorries ran on dubhan.

The Battle of Àth leathan was fought on {{date:VI-0081}}, in the spring, the first great battle of An Cogadh Fada. The state, already leaning on Dubhan's fuel to prop its fading power, moved against the holdouts with its own hand. The open record counts the kingdom's dead and not the holdouts'. The Tuathaich tellings put theirs in the hundreds. Ruth Calder's name does not appear in the open record after that day. Whether she fell there, no teller in the north will say for certain. Her name simply stops.

The holdouts gave up the southern pits and fell back into the hills round Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais. The pits of Àth leathan were flooded on the Board's order, and the field was left unploughed. The seventh regiment was raised on the west coast to hold the road behind the army. Before the month was out a wartime tithe was laid on every sale of dubhan in the kingdom, for the war's sake. It was never lifted. Fleets were raised and sent round the western cape to close the Tuathaich ports of the north-west to every boat but the fishing boats, and to search those.

In an t-Ògmhios the regiments invested Muileann àrsaidh. The town held out for weeks on stored coal and potatoes, and its pit-heads were taken one at a time. Sìleas nic Rath's open record gives the day it fell and nothing more. The army's dubhan was served before any other use, and the capital's poorer streets went dark again, as they had been before the fuel came.

The families of the western pits came into Caol mhòr and Doire ghlas with what they could carry. The Moot fed them from its own stores, as it had resolved to. The council sent nothing and forbade nothing. The Moot's minute records the families' names, and it is among the few Tuathaich records of the war that the library does not hold. Agnes Pike's old school at Doire ghlas was given over to them, and Ellen Marsh, who taught there, took down their accounts. This book would give a good deal to read them.

In an Lùnastal Cnoc thais was taken, after the regiments cut the road to its pits, and Tom Varley's two shafts were fired and sealed. The open record gives the number of holdouts taken and not the number who went on into the hills. Some went on past the hills and out onto the windy coast, beyond the shire lines, and the regiments did not follow them there. With the fleets away in the north-west, raiders out of the east took two salt-boats off Àth bheag and burned a third.

Through the autumn the kingdom went on raising regiments, as if it looked for a longer war. The fourth regiment was quartered at Ceann mhòr in the old human magistrate's house, and the Moot's protest was received.

In an Dùbhlachd the regiments came to Baile chrom on the night of the vigil.

The corporal at the head of the file had come up the coast road expecting shutters and dogs and shouting. There was nothing. Not a lamp showed in any window. No dog barked. The only sound was the sea on the stones and his own men's boots, and even they began to tread softer without being told.

He tried the first door. It was unlatched. Inside, by the grey of the window, a man and a woman and three children sat round a cold hearth with their hands in their laps. None of them spoke. He asked them, in Dia-thìris and then in the humans' tongue, who else was in the house, and whether there were arms. The man only shook his head, once, slowly, as a man does at a child who has asked a foolish thing in church.

It was the same in the next house, and the next, until the small hours. He wrote in the end that the whole town had been dark and silent, and that it had been searched and nothing found. The regiments' return for that month says as much, and no more.

That month Àth naomh and Tobar dhearg opened their roads without a siege. Their pits were flooded. The open record names no holdouts taken at either.

Late in an Dàmhair of the next year a column of holdouts, several hundred strong, came out of the western hills, and through an t-Samhain it moved south-east through the middle of the island toward the capital. The reason lies in the sealed archive. The Tuathaich tellings give its leader the name Owen Barrow; the open record gives him none. On {{date:VI-0113}} the column passed Muileann òg and made camp on the fields by Muileann ghlas, a day's walk from Cathair dhearg, and the first and third regiments were brought down the fair road to meet it. The booths of the fair-ground were cleared for their camp, and the fair court's books for that year end without a closing entry.

The Battle of Muileann ghlas was fought on {{date:VI-0115}}, four days after, in the short grey days before midwinter. It was the second great battle of the war, and the last. The holdouts were broken in two summers' campaigning. They were the children of humans once pushed to the island's edge, and now they were pushed back again, and the fuel that drove the lorries against them had been made by one of their own. The open record lists the first and third regiments on the field and does not count the column's dead. When word reached the hills, the holdouts still there came down to the seventh regiment's posts on the Àth leathan road. They were disarmed and sent home, and the open record counts their weapons.

So An Cogadh Fada ended on {{date:VI-0118}}, twenty months after it began, and it has been called the Long War all the same. Nine regiments and four fleets were raised in it. All are under arms yet, and each is named for the town where it is stationed.

Before the year was out the Tuathaich had a new saying. *Chaidh am fadachadh*, they got long-warred, was said first of the defeat in the west, and then of any defeat that comes after holding out too long. It is said as a joke, and the people who say it do not laugh.

## IV. Of the Peace, and of the Present Year

The regiments were not stood down. The council kept each at the town where it had been raised and paid them out of the wartime tithe. With the last organised resistance gone, Dubhan's fuel became, in all but law, compulsory across the whole island. The last western seams were flooded and capped with concrete, and after that no coal was dug on Dia-thìr outside the Board's own works. The holdouts taken at Muileann ghlas were marched north as far as the boundary stones, the stones set in {{year:V-0003}} that nobody had ever been paid to move. The Moot's register gave their names a column of their own. Somhairle mac Lachlainn was given a seat on the council, the first soldier to sit there since the captains of the Severance war.

In {{year:VI-0126}} the kingdom raised a stone at Cathair mhòr, beside the Mac Ùisdein market cross, with the names of the regiments' dead. On the field of Àth leathan the Tuathaich families built a cairn without leave. The seventh regiment's patrols have let it stand, and it grows by a stone or two every time they pass. At Muileann ghlas the field was sown again, and for years its farmer turned up weapons in the furrows and handed them to the first regiment, which keeps a list.

On {{date:VI-0128}} the library's whole record of the Long War was sealed from the public for fifty years. It is the only sealed archive in the kingdom's history. What is still open for the war years is Sìleas nic Rath's list of places, the regiments present, and the counts she was allowed to give. Her full record went into the sealed room with everything else. Ailean Leabhar said once that the library settles nothing and only keeps. He never thought it would one day keep a thing it was not let read.

The war left its accounts. The Treasury carried the wartime tithe forward as a standing line under the regiments' pay, and no council minute records a decision to keep it. The fuel-fitters took the contract for the regiments' engines and doubled their rolls, and every new fitter was Dia-thìrich.

In an t-Ògmhios of {{year:VI-0137}} Anna nic Phàil, councillor for Cathair dhomhain, brought the Moot's petition before the council: that the Tuathaich towns be paid for what they had lost in the war. She spoke for it once. On {{date:VI-0138}} the proposal was raised in the capital and quietly let die without a vote. The Moot's minute on it is one line long: *Allowed to die.* Martha Greaves laid down the chair that year, and the Moot chose Peter Hale, great-grandson of its first speaker. The fourth regiment left the magistrate's house at Ceann mhòr. The Moot asked that the house be given to the town. It was given to the maor.

By {{year:VI-0143}} the Tuathaich had one name for the tabled request and the dead motion together: *seann-chunntas*, an old reckoning that nobody in power will settle and nobody has struck out. Official Dia-thìrich usage has not taken up the word. This book uses it, since the record should hold the words of both peoples.

That year the mountain was shut, or nearly. The Board closed the upper galleries of the Sloc Mòr and kept one face open, in the Rite's custody, to cut coal for the altars. Twelve hewers were kept on to work it, the last of their trade on the island. On {{date:VI-0148}} the last coal train ran to Cathair dhearg. The coal line has kept its name and carries dubhan, wool and soldiers. The second regiment set a guard at the library over the sealed archive, and Raghnall mac Mhuirich wrote to the council that the library had not asked for one. On the northern road, where the boundary stones stand, the sixth regiment keeps posts, and Tuathaich going south show a pass for the first time since the Severance. The council's minute calls the passes temporary. The reader will know by now what the kingdom's temporary measures tend to become.

The last year and this one have been like other years. Tuathaich wool was sold at Muileann òg on its northern day, through Dia-thìrich factors, as at the first fair. The Còmhrag was fought with staves and fists, and one man died. Ellen Marsh's eleven books have been copied by hand in the northern schools and are kept there; the library's catalogue lists the tabled request and not the books. The Moot met at Caol mhòr under Peter Hale, its minute-books unbroken since Walter Hale's wet afternoon, with no standing in Dia-thìrich law and no request for any. Every lamp and engine on the island, every ship and every lorry, runs on dubhan; the coinage is pegged to it, and the tithe is taken on every sale. The Treasury's roll for the year stood at 9,568 purses, two parts in three of it from the head-due. In the summer the Rite's twelve hewers cut the year's altar-coal. It filled four baskets, and the high custodian shared it among the houses by lot.

On {{date:VI-0154b}}, as the summer went out, Dubhan himself, an old man now in his sixty-ninth year, won from the council at Cathair dhearg the right to sell dubhan off the island. He had written to the council for a year and sat before it three times. It is the first leave the kingdom has given for any good of Dia-thìr to go beyond its waters since the Severance, and it is narrowly drawn. The Treasury sets each year's measure. The fuel is loaded only at Ros dhomhain, where a clerk of the Treasury takes the tithe on every barrel. The kingdom's depots and the Moot's are served first. The custodians of Òrd Bhrìde seal every hold, since nothing of the vein may be sold, and the Small-Burning Law is not touched by it. Dubhan has told neither the council nor the chronicler who his buyers are. The first tank-hull loaded under the charter went out past the western capes in the autumn, and at Seann Skell the priests of Manannan said the old prayer to keep the sea shut on the day she sailed. Dubhan goes out under that charter and no other.

On {{date:VI-0156}}, with the lamps lit by mid-afternoon at Muileann chaol, Sìleas nic Rath wrote at the foot of the year that the kingdom's record is whole in every part but two: the war, which is sealed, and the north, which was not asked.

So the account stands on {{date:VI-0157}}, at the turning of the year's shortest days. The coal is all but gone. The kingdom's trade runs on a name that once belonged to one man, and since the autumn a little of it has gone out past the western capes under his charter. The Dia-thìrich hold the capital and the sea. The Tuathaich hold the north, and their Church, and a grievance five years old, and a memory of the First Canoe that the people who twice defeated them still call folklore. The restored kingdom promised the island its own again. It kept the humans' tariff, their surveys, their rails and their terms for the mountain, and it kept the humans' children where the humans had put them. It kept faith as well with the drowned towns of the south, and its wells at Achadh shean still draw water.

Here the Sixth Book stops, at the present year, for the chronicle itself goes on. The age that follows has no name yet, because nobody can yet say what began it. It is left to the next chronicler, who will be handed the whole record and, with it, the things still missing from it: the war in its guarded room, which is to be opened fifty years from its sealing if the kingdom keeps its word; the fourth floor at Muileann chaol, built for what was not sent; the eleven books in the north; and the Moot's minutes with their hidden second set. The old reckoning is still owed. Whoever writes the next book should begin by asking for them.
