## Appendix F — The Tongues and Peoples of Dia-thìr

Here is gathered what the rolls and chronicles hold of the peoples who have lived on Dia-thìr and of the
speech they used. It is drawn from the royal chronicles, from the rolls and custody-books copied into
Leabharlann Muileann chaol, from the map of the kingdom, and from the word-lists that survive from the Age
of Strangers. Where the sources are silent, that is said, and the silence is left as it was found.

### I. The Peoples

Five peoples, or kinds of people, stand in the island's history. Two of them are gone. One, the
*fuil-ghuail*, is hardly a people in the common sense of the word; yet the chronicles have come to speak
of it as one, and so it is set down here with the rest.

| People | Dia-thìris name | In the chronicles | On the map today |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Old Ones | *na Seann-Dhaoine* | Ages I and II; their works end before the Holy Age | no town; thirty towns and thirteen shires bear their names |
| The people of Dia-thìr | *na Dia-thìrich* (sg. *Dia-thìreach*) | every age | 441 of 505 towns; the capital and the sea |
| The humans | the chronicles' plain name for them | Age V | none by that name; their descendants are the Tuathaich |
| The northerners | *na Tuathaich* (sg. *Tuathach*) | Ages VI and VII | 64 towns in the north and north-west |
| The coal-blooded | *an fhuil-ghuail*; in the humans' tongue, *coal-touched* | from Age V | not counted on the map; counted, briefly, in the censuses |

#### The Seann-Dhaoine

The Dia-thìrich call the people before them *na Seann-Dhaoine*, the Old Ones. The name is a Dia-thìris
one, given from outside. What the Old Ones called themselves is not known, and cannot now be
known.

**What survives of them** is stone, shell and names. The oldest sure trace is the midden at
Seann Dunn ({{date:I-0010}}): limpet shell, seal bone and burnt weed, laid down over many
lifetimes, with no tool in it shaped the way any later Dia-thìreach shaped tools. After it come the
notched net-weights of Seann Chwen, every stone cut with the same three strokes
({{date:I-0011}}); the unmortared mole at Seann Skell on which every later quay wall there
stands ({{date:I-0012}}); the two hundred and twelve steps of Seann Bhral ({{date:I-0013}}); the
stone boxes on Eilean dhearg to which the dead of Seann Vell were rowed ({{date:I-0014}}); the
fish-channel of Seann Toll ({{date:I-0015}}); the shell causeway of Seann Warr, the first made road
on the island that can still be walked ({{date:I-0016}}); the fish-kilns of Seann Mhorn
({{date:I-0018}}); and the slipways of Seann Bhrenn, wide enough for hulls bigger than any hide
boat; the hulls are gone, and so is any telling of where they went ({{date:I-0019}}).

At their widest reach ({{date:I-0056}}) they held every coast of the island: the ten places whose
names begin with *Seann*, and the islands off them. Their largest landing was at the place now
called Seann Skell, on the Abhainn naomh.

**The ruins inland** are theirs by the oldest tellings. The telling of the counting of the
dead ({{date:I-0091}}) names the Seann-Dhaoine as "the people who built Làrach an Dùin-fhaire and
Làrach an Dùin". Làrach an Dùin-fhaire, the walled outpost on the spur above the ground where
Cathair mhòr now stands, has a single gate, and it faces inland, away from the sea
({{date:I-0032}}); Làrach an Dùin, near Ceann mhin, had three rings of wall and room inside for
some four hundred people with their beasts ({{date:I-0033}}); and Làrach an Teampaill, in the
eastern hills, has its altar set square to the central mountains ({{date:I-0035}}), toward the
vein and away from the sea. All three were already ruined when the first reliable chronicles begin
({{date:I-0059}}, {{date:I-0061}}, {{date:I-0063}}). Whether the builders of the outpost built the
fortress too has never been settled. After the high places were left, every new work of the
Seann-Dhaoine lies within sight of salt water ({{date:I-0064}}).

**The standing stones** are harder. Carragh Cnoc bheag, Colbh Cnoc chiar and Calbh Cnoc ghorm were
raised in the Ancient Age ({{date:I-0069}}, {{date:I-0071}}, {{date:I-0073}}), and no telling
says by whom. The upper face of Carragh Cnoc bheag bears a line of the unread script; the
four Dia-thìris words, *Bha teine sa chloich*, "there was fire in the stone", lie lower down, cut with
a narrower tool by another hand, and "when that second hand came is not known"
({{date:I-0070}}). So on Carragh Cnoc bheag the marks of two peoples lie on the same face. In few
other places do they touch; one is the wind-stone at the central Seann Chwen, where a Dia-thìris wind-name
is cut over an older mark of the Seann-Dhaoine ({{date:I-0180}}).

**Their counting.** Late in their time the Seann-Dhaoine began cutting single strokes on flat
stones at the edges of their middens, one stone to a household ({{date:I-0090}}). At the eastern
Seann Warr the counting stones were built into a wall of some three thousand strokes
({{date:I-0092}}); at Seann Tarr in the north-east they were cut in fives and set in the floor of a
house ({{date:I-0093}}). At the Seann Chwen near Cuan dhearg the last tally stone was left
half-cut: two strokes finished, a third begun and abandoned ({{date:I-0095}}).

**Their end.** After the half-cut stone no new work of the Seann-Dhaoine can be dated. Their
middens stop growing, their harbours silt, and their script is not cut again
({{date:I-0096}}). How they ended no telling knows, and no guess is offered here.
The chronicles do not say that they were driven out, or that they died, or that their blood ran on in
some later people; nor do they say otherwise.

**The hill-folk.** Alongside, or after, the Seann-Dhaoine the tellings set a second people: the
hill-folk of the high valleys around the crack ({{date:I-0065}}), who buried their dead in the
ground rather than on islands, and cut no script. They burned the coal, though sparingly: one sliver,
one fire, at Àth gharbh ({{date:I-0066}}). Whether they shared the island with the Old Ones or
came after them no telling knows. Their own answer, given later, is that they were always
there, and it is the answer the Dia-thìrich give of themselves in the doctrine carried on Calbh Cnoc
ghorm: *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann*, "we did not come; we were here." When
stag-scholars came to Calbh Cnoc ghorm in the Holy Age and could not read it, the people of Cnoc
ghorm told them what it said, and "the scholars write that the people say so"
({{date:II-0167}}).

#### The Dia-thìrich

The Dia-thìrich are the people of Dia-thìr: the majority in every age of the island's history, the holders of the
capital, {{place:burg:19}}, and of the sea. Their name is the island's name with the
people-ending, written *-ich* after the slender vowel of *thìr*; a single person is a *Dia-thìreach*. Writers of the humans' tongue called them
*Dia-thìrian*, and that word is kept in this history for the adjective.

**Custodians.** The Dia-thìrich enter history as custodians. In the Age of Ailean a custom set in across
the hill settlements that no fire should take more of the coal than one cupped hand could hold. The
Binding of the First Flame ({{date:II-0001}}) made the custodianship of the vein a formal order,
whose keepers were "older by centuries than any rìgh after Ailean", and the Small-Burning Law ({{date:II-0027}}) made the
custom into rule. The Binding was sworn by the heirs of Ailean Mòr, gathered to part his kingdom, and the
parting they swore after it, *Roinn na Rìoghachd* ({{date:II-0001b}}), cut the Dia-thìrich into
great shares, provinces, counties and holdings under the king's kin, whose feuds, *Aimhreit nan
Oighrean*, ran until the Peace of the Threshold ({{date:II-0064c}}). The first after Ailean to be
called *rìgh* was Goraidh mac Ìomhair at Dùn dhearg ({{date:II-0077}}), and he claimed nothing over the vein. For the whole of the Holy Age the
kings ruled the roads and the granaries, and the Keepers at Dùn ìseal ruled the mountain. The
Holy Age closes on the last age of even population ({{date:II-0249}}). After it, the chroniclers
note with some unease, there was never again a time on Dia-thìr when no family held more years of life
than any other.

**The kingdom.** After the Severance the state restyled itself Rìoghachd Dia-thìr, the Kingdom of
Dia-thìr ({{date:V-0007}}), and Niall was crowned at Cathair dhearg ({{date:V-0012}}). The rulers since
are set down in Appendix A. Here it is enough to say that the kingdom is a Dia-thìreach kingdom: its schools
teach in Dia-thìris "and it has no others" ({{date:V-0032}}), and the old apprentice-binding custom,
when it was revived, was reserved for Dia-thìrich applicants only ({{date:V-0059}}).

**The sea.** The Dia-thìrich have always been a people of the coast as much as of the mountain. The largest
towns on the map today stand on the sea or on the rivers that run down to it. The largest of all is the capital, and the next two bear a name older than Dia-thìris:

dhomhain.

**The departed.** Part of the Dia-thìrich people is not on Dia-thìr. Land was found across the water
({{date:III-0001}}); volunteers chose to go, by "a vote, or what the later chroniclers call a
vote" ({{date:III-0003}}); the fleet set out, turning sunwise round the river pool at Seann Skell, in the year
from which the old count of the Diosal ran ({{date:III-0023}}). A broken report told of two landfalls
({{date:III-0027}}), and of the thinning: cut off from the vein, "by the third generation over the water
nothing is left that Dia-thìr would know" ({{date:III-0029}}). The departed stand in the
chronicles only as a gap. The island kept them in rites (the empty-harbour rite,
{{date:III-0036}}; the white sorrow, *an Tùrsa Geal*, {{date:III-0032}}) and in words, of which more
below. When the humans came, old sailors at Cuan shean remarked that the strangers had come in from
the same quarter of sea the Sundering fleet once sailed into. The council forbade the remark in its
hall; it was written down all the same, and never answered ({{date:IV-0006}}). Whether the humans
descend from the departed, no one on Dia-thìr knows.

#### The humans of an Tìr Thall

**The Crossing.** Humans made landfall on Dia-thìr and were granted leave to stay
({{date:IV-0001}}): the first contact since the Sundering. Three ships down from the north anchored off
Cuan shean, and their master, whom the chronicles call Edmund Harrow, came ashore unarmed and asked by
signs for water ({{date:IV-0002}}). So the first dealing between the two peoples was made without
words.

**Their homeland.** The strangers named their homeland in their own tongue, but no Dia-thìreach scribe
set the word down. From the first season the chronicles call it *an Tìr Thall*, the land beyond, and
its people simply the humans ({{date:IV-0007}}). Their own name for their country is written on
Dia-thìr in no hand, and it cannot now be recovered.

**What they called things.** The humans' names for the island's things were few, plain and, to
Dia-thìreach ears, careless. They had a single word for the coloured coal, and it was the word for the black fuel
they burned at home; Gormshuil nic Fhearchair's copyist noted that no Dia-thìris word answered to it,
and that the humans did not ask for one ({{date:IV-0010}}). So *an gual dathach*, the coloured coal of the vein, became in their tongue plain
*coal*, and the custodians of the vein became, in Samuel Wren's otherwise careful grammar, "keepers
of the coal" ({{date:IV-0042}}). Their offices and bodies came with names in the humans' tongue and kept them (they are given here in English): the
Company, the Administration, the Mission, the Residency above Ros dhomhain ({{date:IV-0031}}), the
Commissioner, the Levy, the constable posts. Their faith was the Church, which the Dia-thìrich call *an
Eaglais*, and the Mission was its house on Dia-thìr. Their coin was the *mark*. The townsfolk of Muileann
shean called the constable post there "the humans' second Residency", and did not mean it kindly
({{date:IV-0260}}).

**The camps.** What the humans built for themselves on Dia-thìr they built for the pit. The camp of
timber huts at Àth ìseal was the first settlement on the island raised for the pit and for nothing else
({{date:IV-0077}}); Baile dhomhain was laid out on a grid by a Company surveyor, the first Dia-thìreach
town drawn on paper before it was built ({{date:IV-0087}}); the camp at Achadh dhomhain was built
of spoil ({{date:IV-0144}}). Dia-thìreach hands worked the camps and human officers ruled them, and the
record of them is kept in two tongues that seldom agree: the Mission counts four hundred and six
dead of the spotted pox, and the custodians count more, and write the difference in the margin of
the Mission's list ({{date:IV-0096}}). Human families lived on Dia-thìr as well as human officers:
upland pasture in the north-west, around Muileann àrsaidh, was granted for human families who would
not work in the pits ({{date:IV-0060}}). This was the first human settlement in the north; and to
the north, in the end, their descendants were sent.

**Mixing.** The first mixed settlement ({{date:IV-0021}}) was Baile dhìreach on the south-west
coast, under a council of three Dia-thìrich and three humans, whose founding compact was written twice,
once in each tongue, the only such compact on record in the age ({{date:IV-0022}}). There the
net-maker Eilidh nic Ruairidh married the human cooper Samuel Hale, and the council entered the
marriage in both its books ({{date:IV-0028}}). It was not tried again, and the
first inheritance case of mixed blood ({{date:IV-0230}}) the Administration decided wholly in
its own favour.

**The going north.** War was declared in {{year:IV-0324}}. Before the end, the human families of
Ros bheag, Cuan shean, Baile dhìreach and the south-west marched north along the western roads under
guard of the council's companies; few of them had ever lived in the north; no column was attacked,
and none was helped ({{date:IV-0371}}). The war ended with the crossing itself closed
({{date:IV-0377}}). Whatever way had let the humans reach Dia-thìr was shut, and those still on the island were
herded to the northern country their descendants hold today.

#### The Tuathaich

From the Severance the chronicles stop calling them humans. The descendants of those left on Dia-thìr are
*na Tuathaich*, the northerners, from *tuath*, north, with the same people-ending that makes *Dia-thìrich*;
on the page the two names stand like brothers, though the two peoples' fortunes have been far apart. Their
homeland in the north was fixed formally ({{date:V-0001}}): "It is a confinement;
yet their children's children call it home." A formal wall between the two peoples was
proposed and rejected as unnecessary, because the two were already keeping apart on their own
({{date:V-0046}}).

What the Tuathaich call themselves in their own speech the chronicles do not say. The name used here
is the kingdom's name for them.

**Their towns.** The kingdom's map counts sixty-four Tuathaich towns, all in the north and north-west, with
some 54,500 people in them. The Dia-thìrich hold the other 441. The Tuathaich country is some fifteen parts in a hundred of the island's land, the windy coast of the far north-west among it, and the Dia-thìrich country the other eighty-five (Appendix H). The largest Tuathaich towns are these:

| {{place:burg:209}} | Doire ghlas | An Eaglais (Eaglais nan Tuathach) | 7,660 |
| {{place:burg:143}} | Ceann mhòr | An Eaglais | 4,323 |
| {{place:burg:305}} | Baile chrom | An Eaglais (the vigil of the Grey Night) | 3,825 |

Every Tuathaich town but one bears a Dia-thìris name, and most of them were Dia-thìreach towns before the
Severance; the north-west had been thinly held since the dearth on that shore in the Age of
Sundering ({{date:III-0134}}). One of them, {{place:burg:16}} in the shire of
Muileann àrsaidh, bears an Old One's name. The Tuathaich have added no names of their own to the
map.

**Their assembly.** Tuathaich householders from the northern towns met at Caol mhòr and chose a
speaker, Walter Hale, a former harbour clerk ({{date:V-0008}}). The Moot has no standing in
Dia-thìreach law; it meets anyway, and its minutes run unbroken from that meeting to the present.

**Their faith.** All sixty-four Tuathaich towns keep An Eaglais, the Church, which their forebears
brought over the sea at the Crossing; no Dia-thìreach town keeps it. Fifty-one keep it in the line the
Mission left, and their largest church is at Cnoc ghorm. Twelve are the old congregations of the
north coast, Eaglais nan Tuathach, whose towns lie together in the shires of Doire ghlas,
Muileann leathan, Inis mhin and Àth àrsaidh. One, Baile chrom, keeps the vigil of the Grey Night,
Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise, which the Church's bishops call irregular. The old congregations
keep, quietly, the First Canoe fragment ({{date:V-0042}}): among coastal Tuathaich families, "a
steersman with a name, and a first crossing", which their church tells as the voyage of Naomh Breandan to
the Isle of the Blessed, and which the druids of the Old Faith dismiss as superstition
({{date:I-0038}}). The fragment is set in the Ancient Age, for there its
tellers place it.
The Library has neither upheld it nor struck it out. It is one of the two suppressed histories
that stand at either end of the island's story, and it is left here as it was found.

#### The fuil-ghuail

This section holds to what the rolls and chronicles say.

A child born into the mining camps was exposed to more raw coal across her lifetime than any Dia-thìreach
in living memory, and did not age the way the people around her did. Her name is Aisling
({{date:IV-0146}}). What was happening to her had no word, in any tongue spoken on the island
({{date:IV-0149}}). A second, Fionnan, was born half a century later ({{date:IV-0245}}), and
it became plain that this was no single mischance: it came of the working of the pits, and would come
again while they were worked. With two sure cases before them, the Dia-thìreach custodians settled on a word
({{date:IV-0250}}): *fuil-ghuail*, "coal-blood", rendered in the humans' tongue as *coal-touched*.
It was meant as a physician's word. It was agreed at a meeting of custodians at the shrine of Cill ghlas, and the roll records the vote but not
who proposed it ({{date:IV-0251}}). The Residency ordered every Dia-thìreach child showing the condition to
be reported; the custodians complied by reporting none ({{date:IV-0252}}). A third case, Lorccan,
was born in a mining town ({{date:IV-0265}}), and a fourth, Cian, within a year of him
({{date:IV-0266}}).

The camps say more than the rolls. They tell that Aisling's father, Donnchadh mac Thormoid, a hewer
of the deep galleries at Achadh dhomhain, coughed a year with what the Mission's surgeon wrote down as
the lung-rot, and that the cough left him on the night she was born; that what he carried was no rot
but the Keeper's gift, *Tìodhlac a' Choimhdich* (Appendix B); and that she was of the first king's
line. That last the copyists of Muileann chaol found to stand in the lines ({{date:IV-0251a}};
Appendix A, *The Line of Aisling*). The rest is the camps' own, and is set down here as theirs.

After the Severance the Coal Rite, Òrd Bhrìde, was set in order within the Old Faith, built around the
leyline and "the unbroken line of the coal-touched" ({{date:V-0028}}). The first census of the
kingdom noted, without further comment, exactly how few such people exist ({{date:V-0034}}), and
the published census of a later generation gave a number and nothing else ({{date:V-0218}}). The
number is printed nowhere else, and it is not printed here.

Whether the *fuil-ghuail* are a people is a question of words. They are born to Dia-thìreach families;
in Dia-thìreach speech the word names the gift that sets the Dia-thìrich apart from the Tuathaich; and the druid schools have asked since the Holy Age why the vein answers some hands and
not others. The chronicles do not settle it.

### II. The Tongues

#### Dia-thìris

Dia-thìris is the tongue of Dia-thìr, spoken by the Dia-thìrich in every age. Three
things mark it at once on the page:

| Feature | Dia-thìris | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Long vowels | **grave accent only**: *à è ì ò ù* | *mòr, sìth, Dia-thìris* |
| The cluster /sk/ | written **sc**, never *sg* | *uisce, scoil, sceul, Scàineadh* |
| "City" | *cathair* | *Cathair dhearg* |

Its greeting of thanks is *gu robh math agad*. Its verbs come first in the sentence
(*Chunnaic Cian an long*, "Cian saw the ship"); it has no word for "a", no words for yes and no,
and two initial mutations.

**Lenition.** After many words an initial consonant softens, and the change is written with *h*:
*b > bh, c > ch, d > dh, f > fh, g > gh, m > mh, p > ph, s > sh, t > th*. The possessives, the past
tense, a feminine noun after the article, an adjective after a feminine noun, most simple
prepositions and the second element of a name all cause it. It does not touch vowels, nor *l, n,
r* in writing, nor the clusters *sc, sp, st, sm*; and after a word ending in *n* it spares *d, t*
and *s*, which is why the map has *Seann Dunn* and *Seann Toll* but *Seann Bhral*.

**Broad and slender.** Vowels are broad (*a o u à ò ù*) or slender (*e i è ì*), and within one
word a consonant takes vowels of the same class on both sides: *caol le caol, leathan le
leathan*, slender with slender, broad with broad. That is why so many endings come in pairs, and why
the small rivers are *Ciaras* but *Àrsaidheas*.

**Sounds.** For the reader who would say these names aloud:

| written | said | example |
|---|---|---|
| bh, mh | v | *Seann Bhral* |
| ch | as in *loch* | *Seann Chwen* |
| dh, gh | a voiced *ch* before broad vowels; *y* before slender | *Cathair dhearg* |
| fh | silent | *Àth fhada* |
| sh, th | h | *Dùn thais* |
| sc | sk | *Scàineadh* |

The stress falls on the first syllable.

**How Dia-thìris names new things.** Dia-thìris was long spoken on an island that few new things reached, and
it was slow to borrow. The humans brought many new things, and the kingdom after them more. In the first
generations after the Crossing the Dia-thìrich named nearly all of them from old roots, calling the thing by
what it does or what it is like: head first, the describer after, hyphenated, two elements and seldom
three. Many of these kennings are still the common words. The loans Dia-thìris did take came mostly later:
*bus*, the bus; *tacsaidh*, the taxi; *rèidio*, the radio; and many words of the sciences, such as
*ogsaidean*, oxygen, and the names of the elements. Some of the kennings, with their literal sense:

The kennings fall into families: machines are *iarainn*, of iron; screens and pictures are *scàil* and
*scàthan*, shadow and mirror; electricity is *dealan*, spark, and *dealanach*, lightning. A
crossing for feet is a ford, *àth*, the same word the map uses for a town at a river.

**Words born of the island's history.** Old words have also taken new senses from what the island lived
through. The royal chroniclers keep a list of these, and the rolls date the first use of most.

| *mar a thàinig Niall* | the way Niall came | any smooth handing-over of power | {{date:V-0013}} |
| *tonn* | wave | a sudden, overwhelming change: *tha e san tonn* | {{date:V-0021}} |
| *a chur san Leabharlann* | to put it in the Library | to make a thing settled and official | {{date:V-0072}} |
| *dubhan* | a man's name | the synthetic fuel | {{date:VI-0035}} |
| *dubhan-tùsail* | dubhan of the beginning | a first attempt that seemed a dead end and proved otherwise | {{date:VI-0036}} |
| *fadachadh* | lengthening | to be crushed after holding out too long: *chaidh am fadachadh* | {{date:VI-0119}} |
| *seann-chunntas* | old account | a grievance nobody in power acts on and nobody cancels | {{date:VI-0143}} |

Of the Sundering the language also keeps one strange relic. A work-song of the rope-makers of Cuan
shean names the parts of a deep keel, and half its words have no meaning to anyone who sings it
({{date:III-0190}}). Nothing else of the fleet's craft survives in any form. Whether the lost words
are old Dia-thìris, or the cant of the wrights, or something else, no one has shown.

#### The tongue of the Seann-Dhaoine

Of the Old Ones' speech nothing survives but names. Ten roots are known, each standing after the
Dia-thìris word *seann*, "old", which lenites it where Dia-thìris lenites:

| Root | As written on the map | Towns bearing it | Shires named from it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dunn | Seann Dunn | 3 | yes |
| Cwen | Seann Chwen | 3 | yes |
| Skell | Seann Skell | 4 | yes (three) |
| Bral | Seann Bhral | 3 | yes |
| Tarr | Seann Tarr | 3 | yes |
| Vell | Seann Vell | 5 | yes (three) |
| Morn | Seann Mhorn | 2 | — |
| Brenn | Seann Bhrenn | 2 | yes |
| Toll | Seann Toll | 3 | yes |
| Warr | Seann Warr | 2 | yes |

Thirty towns on the map bear these names, and thirteen shires are named from them.

The Dia-thìrich did not translate these names; they took them over. The fishers who resettled Seann
Mhorn in the Holy Age kept "the name they find there, which is not of their own tongue", and said
the old name came with the stones ({{date:II-0039}}). It is the first such taking-over on
record. So the harbour-names of an older people outlived them inside a newer tongue, kept whole and
hard like shells in a stone, and never put into Dia-thìris.

**What can be said.** The roots are single syllables. Seven of the ten end in a doubled consonant
(*-nn, -ll, -rr*). Four of them hold letters that Dia-thìris writes nowhere else: *k* in *Skell*, *v* in
*Vell*, *w* in *Cwen* and *Warr*. Everywhere else Dia-thìris writes the sound *sk* as *sc*, yet no scribe
has ever written Seann Scell; they kept the foreign *k* as they found it, and the grammarians count
*sk-* among the clusters that do not lenite.

**What cannot be known.** What any root meant. Whether *seann* was ever the Old Ones' own element,
or only the Dia-thìrich's word for places they found already named (the second is the common
reading, and the likelier; but it is no more than a reading). Whether the roots come from one tongue or several.
Two of them look like Dia-thìris words, *toll*, a hole, and *dunn* beside *dùn*, a fort. The
likeness has tempted scholars in every age and proves nothing, and the Library takes no side.

**The script.** The Seann-Dhaoine also cut signs. Rows of them stand on the cliff above Seann Tarr
({{date:I-0017}}), in the script that is found again on the standing stones. It is not Dia-thìris. It
has never been read, and not one sign of it has an agreed meaning. The stag-scholars of the Holy Age
set their own new letters against the obelisk at Carragh Cnoc bheag and found that they matched
nothing, and wrote down that the stone's writers were not their teachers ({{date:II-0089}}).
Samuel Wren copied the obelisk and sent it over the sea to the scholars of an Tìr Thall; no reply is
recorded ({{date:IV-0051}}). Edwin Lowe copied the pillar near Cnoc chiar, compared it with Wren's
copy, and decided that the two were not the same script and that neither was Dia-thìreach; his notes stop
there ({{date:IV-0141}}). The strokes on the midden stones are a count, and have nothing to do with
this script. Whether the script records the Old Ones' tongue, or some other, or no tongue at all, is
not known.

#### The humans' tongue

The humans spoke a plain tongue, and this history renders it in English (see *Of Translation*
below). The names they carried were names of that tongue: Edmund Harrow, Thomas Pike, Samuel Wren,
Josiah Pell, Henry Maddox, Arthur Lisle, Samuel Hale, Edwin Lowe.

**The season of translation.** Human clerics and Dia-thìreach custodians spent a full season trying to
build a shared vocabulary, and neither side's account of what was agreed survives intact
({{date:IV-0008}}). Samuel Wren and the custodian Gormshuil nic Fhearchair kept parallel
word-lists. Wren's survives in the Administration's letter-books; Gormshuil's was lost, and only its
first leaf is known from a copy: forty words for the colours of broken coal ({{date:IV-0009}}).
Against those forty the humans had one word, and it was *coal*.

**The tongue of rule.** The humans wrote their agreements in their own tongue only. The first
written treaty ({{date:IV-0029}}) was signed at Cathair dhearg by Tormod mac Ailein, who could not
read the tongue it was written in; who read it to him, no roll says ({{date:IV-0030}}).
The Harbour Articles were written the same way, and that time two custodians refused to sign
({{date:IV-0058}}). The Mission's school at Ros dhomhain taught Dia-thìreach children in the humans'
tongue ({{date:IV-0121}}); a generation later Iain Maor, the first overseer schooled there, kept his
reports in the humans' tongue and his household in Dia-thìris ({{date:IV-0180}}). The Levy's officers
read the names of the chosen at Caol mhòr from a list in the humans' tongue, and the custodians took
the names down again in Dia-thìris, "that they may know whom to look for" ({{date:IV-0182}}).

**Loanwords.** Few crossed, and most of those late. In the first generations Dia-thìris took the
humans' goods and named them in its own way, as told above, and kept few of the humans' words for them.
The humans for their part, so far as the rolls show, used the Dia-thìris names of places as they found them;
*fuil-ghuail* they did not borrow but turned into their own tongue, *coal-touched*, and that word,
rather than the Dia-thìris, is the one the kingdom's own later entries use of the Rite's "unbroken line".
What passed the other way, into the humans' speech, is best seen after the Severance, in the north.

The Dia-thìris word for the humans' country, *an Tìr Thall*, is the only name the island has for it.

#### Tuathaich speech

The speech of the north is a mixed speech, and the chronicles speak of it only in passing. This much
they say.

The Tuathaich kept the humans' tongue. Walter Hale asked the council for leave to open schools in
the north teaching in it, and was refused ({{date:V-0032}}); Agnes Pike opened one at Doire ghlas
anyway, with Moot money and without the council's leave, and within a decade there were nine
({{date:V-0066}}). The Moot later counted eleven such schools, and three that had begun to teach
Dia-thìris as well, at the parents' asking ({{date:V-0116}}). The church at Cnoc ghorm taught its
priests in the humans' tongue while reading the Bible in the Dia-thìris text its preachers use on the
west coast: the only place in the north where both are taught ({{date:V-0045}}).

The Tuathaich live among Dia-thìris names and use Dia-thìris words. The words the north has added to the
island's speech in the present age are Dia-thìris in form: *fadachadh*, from *fada*, long, the name of
the war they lost ({{date:VI-0119}}), and *seann-chunntas*, an old reckoning, which official Dia-thìreach
usage pointedly does not take up ({{date:VI-0143}}). The word *dubhan* for the fuel came out of the
north, from the name of Dubhan, a Tuathach, and it spread before the fuel did ({{date:VI-0035}});
the royal chronicler who first set it down spelled it three ways in two lines ({{date:VI-0002}}).

Beyond this the chronicles do not go. The northern towns' own accounts are among the things the royal
chroniclers say they do not hold ({{date:V-0153}}). How far the humans' tongue has drifted in the north
since the crossing closed, how much Dia-thìris is spoken at a Tuathach hearth, and in which tongue the
First Canoe is told, the Library cannot say.

#### Names and naming

**Personal names.** Dia-thìreach names are the old given names of the island: Ailean, Beathag, Catrìona, Dòmhnall,
Eilidh, Fearchar, Gormshuil, Iain, Mòrag, Raghnall, Sìleas, Tormod. A person is further named from
the father, with *mac*, son, or *nic*, daughter, and the father's name in the genitive, lenited
where Dia-thìris lenites: Tormod mac Iain; Eilidh nic Iain; Gormshuil nic Fhearchair; Donnchadh mac
Thormoid; Mòrag nic Dhòmhnaill; Oighrig nic Lachlainn.

**Bynames** are as common as patronymics and often outlast them. They name a colour, a trade, a
size or a deed. After a woman's name the byname is lenited, after a man's it is not: Seònaid Mhòr,
Eilidh Dhubh, Beathag Bhàn, but Ailean Mòr, Calum Liath, Dùghall Garbh. Trade-names are nouns and
stand as they are: Alasdair Saor, the wright; Iain Maor, the overseer. Some bynames are earned:
Tormod mac Iain, the first of the royal kin to read, was called Tormod Scrìobhaiche, Tormod the
Writer, for the rest of his life ({{date:II-0109}}).

**Kindreds** take *Sliochd*, the seed or line, or *Clann*, the children, with a founder's name in the
genitive: Sliochd Ìomhair, Sliochd Mhuirich, Clann Choinnich.

**Human and Tuathaich names** are of the humans' tongue and are kept in their own spelling: Walter
Hale, Agnes Pike. The humans' names for the island's things were lost at the Severance; their names
for themselves lived through it.

**Place names.** Dia-thìreach places are named by a generic noun and a describing word after it. On the
map the describer is always lenited, whatever the gender of the generic (*Baile ghorm, Cnoc bheag,
Dùn thais*). In speech it would not be lenited after a masculine noun; the names keep an old fixed
usage, as old compounds are kept. The generics are these:

| Generic | Sense | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| *Cathair* | stone fort; city | capital, city |
| *Dùn* | fortified hill | capital, city |
| *Baile Mòr* | great settlement | city |
| *Baile* | settlement, town | town |
| *Àth* | ford | town |
| *Cnoc* | hill | town |
| *Muileann* | mill | town |
| *Cill* | holy cell, shrine | village, hamlet |
| *Tobar* | well | village, hamlet |
| *Achadh* | field | village |
| *Doire* | oak grove | village |
| *Carraig* | rock | hamlet |
| *Ros* | headland | port |
| *Ceann* | head, point | port |
| *Cuan* | harbour | port |
| *Caol* | strait | port |
| *Inis* | island | port |

The describers are colour, size and character words: *mòr, beag, dubh, geal, bàn, dearg, ruadh,
uaine, glas, fada, domhain, ìseal, fiadhaich, naomh, fionn, ciar, gorm, sean, òg, garbh, min, caol,
leathan, crom, dìreach, àrsaidh*. The great rivers are *Abhainn* with a describer; the small ones
take a root and the ending *-as* or *-eas* by broad and slender. A shire, *siorrachd*, takes the name
of its seat, or, where it has no town, of its island (Appendix H). Names repeat freely: there are four Baile chrom and four Seann Skell, and the rolls
tell them apart by where they lie.

Beside these stand the Old Ones' names, which follow none of the rules above except that the Dia-thìrich
put *seann* before them.

#### Writing

**Before letters.** The first marks on Dia-thìr made on purpose are the Seann-Dhaoine's: the notched
net-weights of Seann Chwen, their unread script, and their tally strokes. The Dia-thìrich's forebears
kept counts too, but not in letters. Someone began marking the high-water line against a fixed rock
in the Age of Ailean ({{date:I-0126}}), and at Ros dhomhain Seònaid Mhòr cut her marks in pairs, one
for the high water and one for the low ({{date:I-0169}}). The king-lists of that age were oral, and
the Small-Burning custom was "nowhere written and everywhere kept".

**The Dia-thìris words on the stones.** *Bha teine sa chloich* on Carragh Cnoc bheag, *Cuimhnich an
teine* on Colbh Cnoc chiar, *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann* on Calbh Cnoc ghorm: these are the
oldest Dia-thìris words in stone. When they were cut no roll tells. The stones were raised in
the Ancient Age; the Dia-thìris on Carragh Cnoc bheag was cut later, by another hand. The words on Calbh
Cnoc ghorm were recut along their old grooves by a hand from the Stone Kings' hill, and some later
readers say the recutting changed a letter ({{date:I-0251}}). This does not prove that there were
letters to change in that day; the Library keeps the claim and passes no judgement on it.

**The stag-letters.** The first letters in use by the Dia-thìrich were made at Muileann chiar in the far
east, where the teachers of the stag's people, the first of the druid schools, later called
Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, turned the cutters' tally-notches into signs for sounds, "so that a teaching
can be kept without a teacher" ({{date:II-0088}}). The druids call them the ogham, the gift of
Ogma, god of letters. From them nearly every later hand on the island descends. From them too
came the first written king-list ({{date:II-0111}}), kept by Tormod Scrìobhaiche at Dùn dhearg; the
Keepers' own roll, written down at Dùn ìseal in answer to it ({{date:II-0113}}); boundary stones
cut with treaty terms ({{date:II-0118}}); and the first teaching of letters for their own sake, to
fishers' children at Inis bheag, which the Hall recorded with the remark that fish do not need
reading ({{date:II-0156}}).

**Books and paper.** The Age of Sundering is the age of the custody-book, kept by the vein-house,
and of the first books: Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, the sayings of Gormshuil ({{date:III-0065}}), and
Seònaid nic Coinnich's Book of the Three Claimants, the oldest surviving history on Dia-thìr written as
one narrative ({{date:III-0117}}). Aonghas mac Uisdein was the first king to learn his letters as a
child ({{date:III-0084}}). A mill at Muileann chaol began making paper from rags, the first on Dia-thìr,
and the custody-book was copied onto paper in that generation ({{date:III-0218}}).

**Two tongues of record.** The humans brought paper in quantity, and the custodians of
Cathair naomh asked for their first payment in paper and ink ({{date:IV-0013}}). They brought, too, a
written tongue the Dia-thìrich could not read, and for a century and a half the island kept its records in two.
Whether the humans' letters and the stag-letters share any shapes, no roll says. The
earliest Dia-thìreach grievance text to survive, Leabhar Baile thais, was set down in Dia-thìris by many
hands ({{date:IV-0208}}).

**The kingdom's record.** After the Severance the kingdom founded Leabharlann Muileann chaol
({{date:V-0070}}) and the office of royal chronicler ({{date:V-0078}}), and in time drew the
provincial archives into one place ({{date:V-0226}}). This account is written in that Library.

### Of Translation

This history is written in English for readers who have no Dia-thìris, and some account is owed of what
that has done to the tongues it tells of.

**The humans' speech is rendered in English.** The humans did not speak English. They spoke the
tongue of an Tìr Thall, whose name for itself, like the name of their country, is not recorded on
Dia-thìr. It was a plain tongue, as English is plain beside Dia-thìris. Its speakers were strangers to the
island and its things, and it has become, in the north, the everyday speech of the Tuathaich. For
those reasons it is given here as English, and its names are given as English names, as the letter-
books would have them. When this history says the humans called the coloured coal *coal*, it means that they
used their ordinary word for their ordinary fuel, and saw in the vein nothing that their own hearths
did not burn. When it gives a custodian's title as "keepers of the coal", it is rendering Wren's
rendering. *Coal-touched* is likewise an English stand-in for the humans' word, chosen because
it keeps the plainness of the original and the error in it. The coal of the vein is coal, *gual*,
but it is not only black.

**Dia-thìris is kept as it is.** The Dia-thìrich are the people of this history and Dia-thìris is its first
tongue, and it is not translated into something else here. Names of places, persons, faiths,
laws and offices are left in Dia-thìris spelling, with the grave accent and the *sc*, and with their
lenitions: *Cathair dhearg*, not "Red City"; *fuil-ghuail*, not "coal-blood", except where a gloss is
wanted. Where a Dia-thìris phrase is translated, the translation follows it in quotation marks, as a help
to the reader; the Dia-thìris stands first. The seven ages have Dia-thìris names and are also given English ones
for convenience; the English names are this archive's, not the chroniclers'. The adjective *Dia-thìrian*
is English, and is used as such.

**The island's own name.** It is *Dia-thìr*, the god's land, of *dia*, god, and *tìr*, land, and it is written
the same in the English text of this history as in Dia-thìris, accent, hyphen and all. The hyphen is
the law of the spelling: the broad *a* of *dia* may not stand against the slender *ì* of *thìr* within one
word, so the two are written apart and each keeps its own stress.

**The Seann tongue is left untranslated, for it cannot be translated.** Its ten roots are given as the
Dia-thìrich wrote them, with the foreign letters the Dia-thìrich kept. No meaning is offered for any of
them.

**What has been lost in the rendering.** Some things do not cross. The forty colour-words on
Gormshuil's leaf are one; the Tuathaich jokes about being long-warred are another, since *fadachadh*
is funny, and not funny, only in a tongue where *fada* is also the name of the war. Where there was a
choice between a rendering that reads easily and one that keeps what the words did, the second has
been taken.
