## Appendix H — The Shires of Dia-thìr

Here is set out how Rìoghachd Dia-thìr is divided and governed: the shires, their seats and how they came to be; the Crown's dues and the Treasury; the windy coast of the far north-west, which no shire holds; and the extent of the island's peoples in the present year. It is drawn from the council's minute-books, the Treasury's rolls, the shire record-chests called in to Leabharlann Muileann chaol ({{date:V-0223}}), and the humans' district surveys the kingdom inherited.

### I. The Kingdom

The state styled itself Rìoghachd Dia-thìr, the Kingdom of Dia-thìr, on {{date:V-0007}}, and Niall was crowned at {{place:burg:19}} on {{date:V-0012}}. In form it is a monarchy. The crown, a royal council of twelve ({{date:V-0009}}) and the Treasury sit at Cathair dhearg, and Cathal reigns in the present year. The kingdom counts its years by the eras of the seven ages, and the present year is {{year:VI-0157}} (Appendix D).

The kingdom holds every coast of Dia-thìr and every island off it but one stretch of windswept shore in the far north-west (§ V). It has no neighbour but that shore and the sea. Since the crossing closed there has been no other state within reach of its ships ({{date:V-0011}}), and it keeps no envoys and makes no treaties. Its one war has been fought on its own ground, against its own people: An Cogadh Fada, the Long War of {{year:VI-0081}} and {{year:VI-0115}} (Appendix C). The regiments raised for it were not stood down ({{date:VI-0120}}).

The kingdom grew along the island's waters, as the island's people had always done. Three shire seats in five stand on the sea or on a river, and so do nearly half of all the towns.

### II. How the Shires Came to Be

**The shares of the Roinn.** The first divisions of the island were made by the heirs of Ailean Mòr, and they were made for the sake of fairness among kin, which is the hardest of all reasons to divide land by. At the parting of the kingdom, *Roinn na Rìoghachd* ({{date:II-0001b}}), the heirs cut the island below the mountain into nine great shares, *earrannan mòra*, with the Red Share of Dùn dhearg beside them and the ground of the Hall at Dùn ìseal in none. Each great share was parted again into countries, provinces, counties and holdings ({{date:II-0001d}}). The clerks of the Hall counted nine great shares, forty-four countries and a hundred and thirty-one provinces, and gave up at the counties. Every share was bounded with stones, *clachan-crìche*, cut on each face with the sign of the share it looked into; and at every death of a holder the share was parted again, until the Peace of the Threshold ({{date:II-0064c}}) forbade it and fixed the stones where they stood. From then on the holdings passed whole, and the shares gathered by marriage and by lords dying without heirs. Goraidh mac Ìomhair, the first rìgh after Ailean, began as a gatherer of three old counties of the east ({{date:II-0076a}}).

Most of the shires founded in the Age of Ailean and the Holy Age can be traced to a share of the Roinn. The provinces of the rìghrean were, most of them, the provinces of the Roinn under new masters, and the seats the rìghrean gave granaries to were seats the heirs had already quarrelled over. When the humans' surveyors ran their chains across the island they took the old boundary stones for marks without knowing what they were, and so a good many of the district lines the council kept at the drawing, and many shire bounds of the present day, run on the stones set at the Peace of the Threshold. A few of the stones still show a share's sign, worn shallow. The shire of Achadh mhòr keeps one in the wall of its maor's house, cut on its two faces with the signs of two shares, and says it is the stone Fearghas Dà-Mhionn was sworn by.

**The provinces of the Holy Age.** After the Peace the rìghrean made provinces of the gathered shares, for the sake of grain and roads. The census-takers of Beathag Mhòr worked from the waypoint houses, since every household on the island was within a day of one ({{date:II-0141}}). Lachlann Bàn built a granary in every province seat that would take one ({{date:II-0170}}), and under Coinneach Bàn the clerks of the red hall copied the rolls for the provinces, so that every seat had its own copy of the laws ({{date:II-0213}}). The Library holds several of those provincial rolls where the originals are lost ({{date:II-0229}}). Most of the shire seats of the present day were already towns in the Holy Age, and many of them were seats then.

**The shires of the Sundering.** The word *siorrachd* first appears in the sail-cloth levy, when the weaving households of Siorrachd Baile àrsaidh were asked for cloth for the fleet, reckoned on tally-sticks at the shire seat ({{date:III-0013}}). Through that age the shire was the unit in which the crown's measures were kept. The coppice law of Doire fhionn spread shire by shire ({{date:III-0048}}); Queen Sìleas fixed the firlot of Cathair dhearg so that the shires need not quarrel over baskets ({{date:III-0069}}); Iain Ciar ordered a year's grain in store at every shire seat ({{date:III-0182}}). Two rulers, Sìleas Òg and Gormshuil nic Thormoid, went round every shire seat in person ({{date:III-0205}}, {{date:III-0232}}). Doire shean became a shire seat on the wealth of its oyster beds ({{date:III-0119}}), the only raising of a seat in that age that the annals record.

**The districts of the humans.** The Administration did not use the shires. It governed by districts drawn for the pits, the customs and the Levy, counted the people by district for the first time ({{date:IV-0246}}), and set district seats where its clerks found it convenient: at Cnoc bhàn and at Baile Mòr ruadh on the eastern road, among others. One of its districts was hardly land at all. The outer coasts, the islands and the southern edge of the windy coast in the far north-west, where its revenue cutters chased the raiders, were governed from the harbour office at Seann Vell on Eilean ruadh ({{date:IV-0246a}}). Its surveyors went no farther north-west than the edge of the great moss. Beyond it the district map is empty.

**The drawing.** On {{date:V-0014}} the council divided the kingdom into shires, each named for its seat, and kept most of the human district lines under the new names. No other survey of the whole island had ever been made. Where the council did not keep a district seat it chose the old shire seat instead; so in the shire of Seann Tarr the seat went back from Baile Mòr ruadh, the humans' district town, to the old harbour. A *maor* was appointed to each shire to collect the Crown's dues and keep its peace ({{date:V-0015}}). In the Tuathaich shires the maoir were Dia-thìrich, lodged in the seat towns. Circuit justices were set to ride among the shires a few years later ({{date:V-0036}}).

The drawing made one hundred and twenty-two shires. One more was cut later: Doire chaol, the village of the royal household south of the capital, was taken out of the shire of Baile chiar once it had grown large enough to need its own maor ({{date:V-0139a}}). Since then Rìoghachd Dia-thìr has had one hundred and twenty-three shires, and no more.

### III. The Shape of the Shires

**Names.** A shire takes the name of its seat, and the island's names repeat, so the shires' names repeat too. Eleven names belong to more than one shire. There are three shires of Seann Skell, three of Seann Vell and three of Ceann mhin, and two each of Caol bheag, Àth shean, Baile ghorm, Achadh naomh, Muileann dhearg, Muileann dhomhain, Doire fhionn and Muileann naomh. The rolls tell them apart by where they lie, as the table in § VII does. Thirteen shires are named from the Old Ones' harbours (Appendix F).

**The island shires.** Two shires have no town at all. Eilean dhìreach, a rock off the Tuathaich coast, and Eilean ìseal, off the east coast, each kept a customs station of the Administration and was a district of its own. The council kept both as shires, for it had no rule by which a district might be folded into its neighbour, and since neither has a town to name it, each is named for its island ({{date:V-0014b}}). They are the only shires on Dia-thìr not named for a seat.

**Scattered shires.** The shire of Seann Vell of the west is the Administration's district of the outer coasts, kept whole. Its seat is a small harbour on Eilean ruadh off the west coast. Its towns, Cuan dhearg and Cnoc chaol, stand on Eilean bhàn, hard by the southern coast, where the Great Wave struck ({{date:V-0017}}). Its largest land is the southern edge of the windy coast in the far north-west, where no town stands and the maor goes by boat. With it goes Eilean ghorm. The shire of Seann Vell of the south-east holds Eilean dhearg, the old island of the dead (Appendix F), with Eilean gheal and Eilean mhin, except for a corner of Eilean dhearg that belongs to Àth fhionn. Caol bheag of the north-east lies on Eilean fhada with Eilean bheag, and Caol mhin on Eilean chrom with Eilean dhubh. On the main island, one town of the shire of Tobar ghorm, Baile gheal, lies apart from the rest.

**Seats that are not the largest town.** In five shires the seat is smaller than another town in the same shire. Caol leathan, the strait town, is the seat and not Baile Mòr fhionn, the lords' city of the western plain. Baile àrsaidh is the seat and not Seann Bhral of the west coast. Cnoc dhubh of the charcoal-burners is the seat and not Doire fhiadhaich, the citadel village of the same hills. Baile Mòr dhomhain is the seat and not Cuan bheag. Seann Vell of the west is the seat and not Cuan dhearg. In each case the council kept the seat the humans' district had, or the older shire had, and did not follow the growth of the towns.

**The Tuathaich shires.** Eleven shires lie wholly or mostly in the Tuathaich north and north-west: Caol mhòr, Ceann mhòr, Doire ghlas, Baile chrom, Cnoc ghorm, Cnoc thais, Àth àrsaidh, Muileann leathan, Muileann àrsaidh, Baile ìseal and the island shire of Eilean dhìreach. Two of them keep one Dia-thìrich town each: Baile dhubh in Cnoc ghorm, and Achadh fhiadhaich in Muileann àrsaidh. Three Dia-thìrich shires have one Tuathaich town: Seann Skell of the west has Àth leathan, Muileann naomh of the west has Àth chrom, and Inis mhin on the northern shore has one town of each people. The windy coast in Seann Vell of the west is Tuathaich country too, though the shire's towns are Dia-thìrich. The boundary stones on the roads north ({{date:V-0003}}) mark where the Tuathaich country begins; they are the bounds of no shire. The Moot at Caol mhòr has no standing in any shire court ({{date:V-0008}}).

**One kind of shire.** Every shire is a *siorrachd*, and the kingdom knows no other kind of division. The capital's shire, the Tuathaich shires, the island shires and the scattered shires have the same standing, the same officer and the same dues. The humans' distinctions of district, concession and Residency ground were dropped at the drawing.

### IV. The Crown's Dues and the Treasury

The maor of each shire collects two dues for the Crown, set by the council in the first weeks of the kingdom ({{date:V-0015a}}). The head-due, *cìs-cinn*, is laid on every household by the number of its heads, and so falls on the whole people of the kingdom, in town and country alike. The market-due, *cìs-margaidh*, is taken on every sale made by a seller of the kingdom, in a market or out of one. The humans' tariff, which the harbour-masters of Ros dhomhain kept in force for want of another ({{date:V-0010}}), was folded into the market-due when the kingdom's own coin replaced the humans' mark ({{date:V-0023}}).

Beside the two dues the Treasury carries the wartime tithe on every sale of dubhan, laid "for the war" in {{year:VI-0086}} and never repealed ({{date:VI-0131}}), which pays the regiments. The shires keep what the council allows them for their own works: Cnoc leathan paid for a school in every parish from the shire's dues ({{date:V-0173}}), and the shire of Baile ruadh fenced the open shafts at its own cost ({{date:V-0180}}). The first relief the kingdom ever granted came from the Treasury: seed for the survivors of Achadh mhin when their fields lay under salt ({{date:V-0019}}).

The Treasury reckons in purses, *sporain*, of the realm's coin. Its roll for the present year ({{date:VI-0154a}}) stands as follows:

| Due | Rate | Yield in the present year |
|---|---|---|
| Head-due (*cìs-cinn*) | eighteen purses on every hundred thousand heads | 6,375 purses |
| Market-due (*cìs-margaidh*) | seventeen in the hundred of the price | 3,193 purses |
| **The roll** | | **9,568 purses** |

Two parts in three of the Crown's money come from heads and one from trade. The kingdom counted 35,415,000 heads for the head-due in the present year. The head-due has stood at its rate since it was first collected ({{date:V-0036a}}), and the market-due at seventeen in the hundred since the Treasury fixed it after the Long War ({{date:VI-0131a}}). The windy coast pays neither due, and the roll carries no line for it.

### V. The Windy Coast

In the far north-west, beyond the Tuathaich shires and past the northern edge of Seann Vell's lands, lies a low shore of heather moor, blanket bog and bare rock, open to the gales off the north-western sea, that no shire holds. The Dia-thìrich call it *an Oirthir Ghaothach*, the windy coast. It is a little under two parts in a hundred of the island's land, some 190 square miles, in two pieces: a long stretch of shore, and a small cape to the east of it. Its bays open on the waters where the raiders have sheltered since the Age of Strangers ({{date:IV-0050}}, {{date:V-0055}}), and the sea-beast of the north-west cape is reported off it ({{date:IV-0127}}, {{date:V-0083}}).

**Why no one holds it.** The humans' surveyors stopped at the edge of the great moss, which no chain can cross ({{date:IV-0246a}}), and when the council drew the shires on the humans' lines it drew nothing where there were no lines. Its minute says only that the windy coast "is not in the survey" ({{date:V-0014a}}). No council since has taken it in. It has no coal, no field and no harbour the kingdom has any use for. A shire there would need a maor, and a maor there would need the fleet to keep the raiders' bays, and when the raiders came to Cathair gheal the council, asked twice, sent no help ({{date:V-0085}}). The Moot claims no land of its own and has never asked for it.

**Who lives there.** Tuathaich families keep the windy coast: sealers, fowlers, and herders on the moor's edge, in no town. The kingdom has never counted them. The Moot's tallies put them near nine thousand, a figure the royal clerks do not accept. No maor, justice or census clerk has gone there, and they pay no due.

**In the Long War.** When Cnoc thais fell, some of its holdouts went on past the hills and out onto the windy coast, and the regiments did not follow them beyond the shire line ({{date:VI-0098a}}). The open record of the war names the kingdom's enemy only as those "of no shire". The holdouts who came down to the seventh regiment's posts after Muileann ghlas ({{date:VI-0117}}) came from the hills; whether any came back from the coast, no roll says.

### VI. The Peoples on the Land

The land of Dia-thìr, with its islands, is some 10,900 square miles, of which the shires hold some 10,720. The two living peoples share it between them much as they share the towns. The Old Ones hold none of it. Their names remain on thirty towns and thirteen shires, and no household of theirs is counted anywhere (Appendix F).

| People | Towns | Share of the island's land | In the towns | In the country |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dia-thìrich | 441 | some eighty-five parts in a hundred (about 9,220 square miles) | 4,411,000 | 27,224,000 |
| Tuathaich | 64 | some fifteen parts in a hundred (about 1,680 square miles), of which the windy coast is nearly two | 543,000 | 3,237,000 within the shires; the windy coast uncounted |
| Seann-Dhaoine | none | none | — | — |

The figures are the kingdom's count for the present year, on which the head-due is laid. The Dia-thìrich country is the capital's shire and every shire of the south, the east and the centre. The Tuathaich country is the north and north-west, and its middle lies in the shire of Cnoc ghorm. Together the two peoples number some 35,415,000 within the shires, one in seven of them in a town.
