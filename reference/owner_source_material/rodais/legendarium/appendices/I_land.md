## Appendix I — The Land and Waters of Rodos

Here is described the ground on which these things were done: the size and shape of the island, its countries and what covers them, the central mountains and the lesser hills, the rivers and their courses, Loch chrom, the offshore islands, the climate, and the sea and its mist. The measurements are those of the humans' surveys, which the council kept when it drew the shires, and the heights are the surveyors' heights above the sea. Where the annals record a change in the land, the date is given.

### The island

Rodos is a little over two hundred miles from the capes of the far north-west to the eastern cape, and nowhere much more than a hundred miles from the north coast to the south. The main island covers about eleven thousand square miles. Twelve small islands lie off its coasts, and the largest of them is under seventeen square miles. One sea surrounds all of it, and the Ròdaich call it by one name on every coast, north and south: *Muir Mhanannain*, Manannan's sea, for the lord of the sea whose mist lay on it and hid the island from every ship beyond until the humans came out of the west ({{date:IV-0001a}}, {{date:IV-0002}}).

The coasts are unlike one another. The north coast is long and broken, cut by straits and sounds into headlands and near-islands, and most of the places named *Caol* (strait), *Ros* (headland) and *Ceann* (point) lie along it. Its one wide bay is the Hawk bay, shared by {{place:burg:24}}, {{place:burg:161}} and {{place:burg:132}}. The south coast is shorter and more sheltered. Its great inlet is the gulf, which the Abhainn uaine reaches at {{place:burg:23}} and whose mouth {{place:burg:40}} guards from its white headland. On the west coast the Abhainn gheal comes down to the sea at {{place:burg:166}} and {{place:burg:1}}, and every boat bound for the capital's waters passes between them. The east coast runs from the eastern narrows round the eastern cape, where the light of Baile ìseal stands ({{place:marker:13}}).

The sea has taken land more than once. In the Ancient Age it rose over the low neck that joined Eilean dhubh to the main island ({{date:I-0007}}). A storm drove it over the harbour of Seann Chwen ({{date:II-0087}}), and in the first years of the new kingdom the Great Wave came in over the southern coast in a single night ({{date:V-0017}}).

### The countries and what covers them

About two parts in five of Rodos is open moor, heather, bog and short grass with no trees on it except in the valleys. Three parts in ten are birch and pine. A fifth is wet woodland of oak, alder and hazel. Something under a tenth is the drier oak woods of the east and the capital's country. The fens, the grass plateau of Dùn dhearg and the rough grazing at the north-western end of the shire of Seann Vell make up the rest.

**The northern moors.** The whole northern third of the island, from the Tuathaich country in the north-west to the harbours of the north-east, is low moor, most of it under a hundred and fifty feet. The winters there are long, wet and windy, snow lies on the moor for days at a time and on the higher ground for weeks, and the summers are short and dry. Trees grow only in the sheltered valleys. The groves that give so many northern villages their *Doire* names are some of these: oak, birch and hazel in the hollows, with bog and heather between them. The pollen in the bog under {{place:burg:209}} shows that the oak came to these lowlands after the cold years ({{date:I-0006}}), and that it fell back again when the winters lengthened ({{date:I-0220a}}). The groves have stood in the valleys ever since. The moor is sheep country. It keeps the wool fairs of {{place:burg:438}} and {{place:burg:131}}, the peat banks of {{place:burg:467}} and {{place:burg:210}}, and barley and oats in every hollow that will take them. The north's largest towns are all on the coast or close to it: Caol mhòr, Doire ghlas, Baile Mòr mhòr on its northern plain ({{date:I-0200}}), and the harbours of the Hawk bay.

**The windy coast.** At the far north-western horn of the island the moor gives out onto *an Oirthir Ghaothach*, the windy coast, about a hundred and ninety square miles that no shire holds, and onto the rough grazing at the northern end of Seann Vell's shire beside it. It is the barest ground on Rodos: low heather moor and blanket bog with no tree on it, open to the gales off the north-western sea, which have kept it bare since the oak came to the rest of the island ({{date:I-0006a}}). Behind the shore lies the great moss, bog that no cart and no surveyor's chain can cross. The shore itself has seal-rocks, skerries and fowling cliffs, and sea mist lies on it for days together in summer. The windy coast keeps about nine thousand people, sealers, fowlers and herders, in no town, and the northern end of Seann Vell's shire about twice as many.

**The birch and pine country.** South of the moors a broad belt of birch and pine crosses the island from the western plain to the eastern hills, rising from under two hundred feet in the west to the skirts of the central mountains. It is the largest single stretch of woodland on Rodos, with hill pasture in its clearings. The Abhainn ìseal runs through the middle of it, past {{place:burg:371}} and {{place:burg:20}}, and the western hills rise at its south-western edge.

**The central mountains.** East of the middle of the island the ground rises into the central mountains, a single massif about seventy miles from west to east and thirty-five from north to south, from the hills behind the capital's country to the eastern foothills. The Doire uaine hills are its heart. The highest ground stands a little under three thousand six hundred feet, above {{place:burg:493}}. {{place:burg:315}}, where the flame was kept, stands at about two thousand six hundred feet under the summit ridge ({{date:II-0002}}), and the silver town of {{place:burg:419}} at nearly three thousand. The vein lies under this massif. The crack at {{place:burg:276}} is at about two thousand six hundred feet, and the high valleys round it, where the first hearths lie under the turf at Achadh dhìreach, are the oldest ground on the island known to have been settled ({{date:I-0005}}). The high ground is birch and pine below and bare moor above. Snowfields lay on the summits in the cold years ({{date:I-0004}}), and snow still lies late on the high passes in a hard spring ({{date:II-0177}}). The warm springs below Muileann ruadh ({{place:marker:0}}) come up on the south-eastern side of the massif. On the eastern shoulder, round {{place:burg:422}} and Cnoc ghlas, is the only open grassland on the island, a dry plateau between fifteen hundred and two thousand feet in the lee of the mountains. It is the eastern plain of the annals ({{date:I-0224}}), and its granaries fed the mountain districts in hard years ({{date:II-0056}}, {{date:II-0180}}). The ground in the massif has moved in every age, most of all in the hills round {{place:burg:123}} east of the capital.

**The lesser hills.** The hills east of the capital, the country of {{place:burg:354}} and Cill ghlas, are the western foothills of the massif. The towns there stand between a thousand and thirteen hundred feet, with higher ground behind them. The western hills are low: round Àth leathan, where the road from the south enters them, and north to Muileann àrsaidh and Cnoc thais, they rarely rise above four hundred feet, but they are broken, wooded and easy to hide in, as the holdouts found ({{date:VI-0083}}).

**The capital's country.** Around {{place:burg:19}} and Loch chrom the land is low and fertile, under wet oak woods to the west and south and drier oak woods to the east. The capital itself stands on a low rise above the water ({{date:II-0137}}).

**The wet west.** West of the lake and along the west coast the woods are wet oak, alder and hazel, and the island's largest fens lie behind the coast from Muileann naomh north to Baile fhiadhaich and Muileann àrsaidh. The heath of the west is wet heath on thin soil, good for goats and not worth ploughing ({{date:II-0071}}). With the south, this coast has the most rain on the island.

**The south.** The south coast from Inis chrom to Seann Bhral is the warmest and wettest part of Rodos, almost all of it wet woodland. Behind {{place:burg:170}} lie the fens of the south-west, whose marsh plants gave the dyers of Baile ghorm their blue, and whose salt-pans the Stone Kings fought over ({{date:I-0099}}).

**The eastern oak country.** From the eastern foothills of the massif to the east coast the woods are broad-leaved and the ground is drier: oak and hazel with pasture between, and the best cattle ground on the island. The Black Wind came in on this coast ({{date:I-0159}}).

### The rivers

A hundred and fifty-seven rivers and streams are named on Rodos. A hundred and fifteen are rivers in their own right. The rest are forks and branches that join a larger river, and creeks, brooks and streams too short to carry a boat. The larger rivers are all called *Abhainn* with a describing word after it, and the small ones take the endings *-as* and *-eas*. The describing words are few, so the names repeat: there are three rivers called Abhainn ìseal and three called Abhainn naomh, and the rolls name each by the towns on it. Most of the rivers are short. They rise in the central mountains or the moors and reach the sea within a day or two's walk.

**Abhainn ìseal**, the longest river on Rodos, about fifty-three miles. It rises in the northern foothills of the massif near Àth ghlas, at about seven hundred and forty feet, and runs north-west through the birch and pine country past {{place:burg:6}}, {{place:burg:371}}, the river-island of {{place:burg:25}}, {{place:burg:20}} and {{place:burg:450}}, to the north coast below {{place:burg:24}}. It settled into its present wide bed in the Ancient Age ({{date:I-0009}}), the dawn-burning of the Hawk went up it from the coast ({{date:II-0159}}), and it last broke its banks along its lower reach in {{year:V-0130}}.

**Abhainn fhionn**, about forty-five miles. It rises in the east near Baile chrom, at about nine hundred and fifty feet, and runs west past {{place:burg:88}}, {{place:burg:418}}, {{place:burg:137}}, {{place:burg:2}}, {{place:burg:104}} and {{place:burg:283}} into Loch chrom at {{place:burg:26}}. It is the capital's river road to the east. Its shallows had the first made crossing of any river on the island ({{date:I-0221}}), and King Tormod Bàn's sons drowned in it in spate ({{date:III-0102}}).

**Abhainn uaine**, about forty miles. It rises in the central mountains near Doire mhòr and runs south past {{place:burg:351}}, {{place:burg:44}}, the landing of {{place:marker:39}}, {{place:burg:313}}, {{place:burg:10}} and {{place:burg:40}} to the gulf at {{place:burg:23}}. Meltwater off the mountains cut its bed ({{date:I-0004}}), and the washing of the Company's ore upstream silted it black ({{date:IV-0085}}).

**Abhainn chaol**, about thirty-four miles. It rises high in the massif near Baile dhìreach, at nearly three thousand feet, and runs east to the sea below {{place:burg:18}}. Its fork, the eastern Abhainn ìseal, joins it below {{place:burg:197}}. The river towns of {{place:burg:57}}, {{place:burg:43}} and the river-island of {{place:burg:103}} stand on it. It is fast and dangerous in the wet season ({{date:I-0222}}).

**Abhainn gheal**, the northern, about thirty-one miles. It rises in the massif near Àth gharbh and runs north to the north coast near Muileann fhada.

**Abhainn dhomhain**, the capital's river, about thirty miles. It rises in the low country south of the capital near Baile ìseal and runs north past {{place:burg:500}} and {{place:burg:164}} to {{place:burg:19}}, where {{place:marker:3}} crosses it. A little below the capital it joins the Abhainn gheal under Loch chrom. Its fords took the carriers in the Holy Age ({{date:II-0031}}), and the grain barges came down it to the capital ({{date:III-0020}}).

**Abhainn naomh**, the western, about thirty miles. It rises on the low ground of the west near Achadh gheal and runs past {{place:burg:55}}, {{place:burg:489}} and {{place:burg:100}} to the sea at {{place:burg:122}}. After the Abhainn gheal it carries more water than any river on the island, and its deep pool at Seann Skell made that town the great river port of the west ({{date:I-0012}}).

**Abhainn gheal**, the lake river, about twenty-four miles. It is the outflow of Loch chrom. It leaves the lake by Seann Warr and runs short, deep and slow past {{place:burg:45}} to the sea between {{place:burg:166}} and {{place:burg:1}}. It carries more water than any other river on Rodos, and sea-going hulls can come up it into the lake ({{date:IV-0012a}}). The pilots of Caol leathan have taken a toll on it since the Holy Age.

**Abhainn ghorm**, the north-eastern, about twenty-seven miles. It rises in the northern massif and runs north past {{place:burg:17}} and {{place:burg:475}} to the sea at {{place:burg:215}}, and the north-eastern grain went down it in barges ({{date:III-0053}}).

**The mountain rivers of the south.** The Abhainn mhòr, Abhainn dhubh, Abhainn bhàn and Abhainn chiar all rise high in the massif and fall south to the south coast in twenty miles or less. The Abhainn gheal of the east joins the Abhainn bhàn at {{place:burg:203}}. They rise fast after rain on the eastern hills ({{date:II-0228}}).

**The rivers of the north-east.** The Abhainn bheag rises below the library at Muileann chaol and runs north-east past {{place:burg:76}} of the old bridge and {{place:burg:62}} to the sea at {{place:burg:133}}. The north-eastern Abhainn naomh runs past {{place:burg:180}} to the library's landing at {{place:burg:239}}.

### Loch chrom

Loch chrom is the island's only lake of any size ({{date:V-0170}}). It lies in the low country just north of the capital, about seventy-four square miles of fresh water. The Abhainn fhionn and the lesser Abhainn ìseal come into it from the east, the lesser Abhainn naomh from the north, and a dozen streams from the moors. Its one outflow is the Abhainn gheal.

The lake is the capital's harbour. The towns of the capital shore stand round its southern and western edge: {{place:burg:26}} at the mouth of the Abhainn fhionn, {{place:burg:27}}, {{place:burg:29}}, {{place:burg:35}}, {{place:burg:149}}, {{place:burg:45}}, and {{place:burg:64}} on the western shore, where brine springs rise in the reed marsh behind the town. {{place:burg:80}} stands on the northern shore. Sea-going ships reach the lake from the west coast by the Abhainn gheal, and the lake towns have fitted, loaded and built ships for the capital since the Holy Age.

The lake rises and falls with the seasons. The keepers at Ros dhomhain began to cut its high-water line on a boulder in the harbour mouth in the Ancient Age ({{date:I-0127}}), and the rock is still called the tide-rock, though Loch chrom has no tide. By the end of that age the lake stood a hand higher on the rock than at the first cut ({{date:I-0128}}). The oldest works on the lake are the platforms on oak piles in its shallows ({{date:I-0020}}), which burned to the waterline ({{date:I-0122}}). Its trout were kept for the Crown's table until {{year:V-0170}}.

### The climate

For so northerly a place Rodos is a mild island, wet and windy, with cool summers and winters that are seldom hard. At sea level the year averages about forty-eight degrees of the humans' scale on every coast, a little more on the south coast than on the north, and the harbours do not freeze. The cold is in the height. The towns of the hills east of the capital, at about a thousand feet, average near forty-four; Dùn ìseal, under the summit ridge, near thirty-nine; and the summits of the massif near thirty-six. Snow lies on the high tops and the high passes through the winter and on the northern moors for days at a time, and frost is common inland from the first of an t-Samhain to the end of the spring. Winter is the long season in the north, and it is a season of gales more than of frost: a gale that drove the sea up the river before the grain fleet had sailed was remembered at Inis mhòr for generations ({{date:III-0173}}).

The rain comes in off the western ocean. The west coast and the south get the most, and the fens lie there. The central mountains take much of what is left, so the eastern plateau and the northern moors lie in their lee and are dry. In the north a summer may pass without rain, and so the springs there that never fail are prized, and the towns built round them are remembered for them ({{date:I-0200}}). Droughts come to the east: the springs of Achadh shean failed in the Ancient Age ({{date:I-0135}}), and the Long Drought lay over the island for three years in the Age of Strangers ({{date:IV-0217}}). The storm season comes at the end of the year, and the oldest custom of sharing on Rodos was made for it ({{date:I-0146}}).

### The sea and the mist

Muir Mhanannain is a grey, short-tempered sea. The gales come in off it from the west and north-west, most of all between Samhain and the spring, and sea mist lies on the coasts in still weather, thickest on the north and north-west in early summer. The hunters of the north shore took seals on the skerries off Eilean fhada in the autumn gales in the Ancient Age ({{date:I-0008a}}), and the whale-boats of the north coast still go out in summer. The northern passage is dangerous in winter. After a Company brig was driven onto the skerries off Eilean fhada in mist and a gale ({{date:IV-0127a}}), it was held closed from midwinter to the end of spring.

Beyond the headlands to the west lay Manannan's own mist. The Ròdaich tell that the fleet of the Sundering sailed into it ({{date:III-0023}}), and the order of Manannan teaches that he shut the western sea behind the fleet with it and hid the island from every ship beyond (Appendix B; {{date:III-0120}}). The priests of Manannan recorded it thinning in the last years of the Age of Sundering ({{date:III-0239a}}), and it lifted in the winter of the Crossing, when the humans' ships came out of it ({{date:IV-0001a}}). Since the Severance no ship has come from the west, and the priests say the lord of the sea has shut it again ({{date:V-0011}}).

There is no lasting ice on Rodos. The snowfields that lay on the central mountains in the cold years have been seasonal since the oak came ({{date:I-0004}}), and the snow of the high tops is gone by the summer.
