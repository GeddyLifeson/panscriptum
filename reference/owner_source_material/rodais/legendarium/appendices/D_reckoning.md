## Appendix D — The Reckoning of Years

Here it is told how the years of Dia-thìr are now counted, by the seven eras, and how that reckoning grew out of the older counts. After that comes the island's own reckoning of the moon, which the Dia-thìrich kept before the humans came and the Old Faith keeps yet; then the humans' calendar, and how it became the civil calendar of the island, and how its months and the days of its week got their Dia-thìris names; then the feasts. Last it is told how the Library at Muileann chaol turned every old date into the civil calendar, and that is why the annals give a civil day and month to every age alike.

### I. The Seven Eras

The island counts its years by eras, *linntean*, one to each of the seven ages of *Clàr nan Aoisean*, the table of the ages, as the Library received and extended it. Each era is counted from the first thing remembered in its age. That first year is year 1, and there is no year nought. A date is written with the day, the month and the year of its era, *19 an t-Ògmhios, DE 1*, and a year alone as *DE 27*; in Dia-thìris it is read *air an naoidheamh latha deug den Ògmhios, sa chiad bhliadhna de Linn an Dubhain*. In running prose the chroniclers are content to write "in the year 214 of the Flame".

The names are plain. *Linn na Fèithe* is the era of the vein, counted from the mason's fire. Its year 1 is the year in which a mason of the hills, splitting rock for a well, broke into the vein of coal, and the coal showed every colour at the break; and in the storm of that same night the lightning struck the broken vein and it caught fire, so that the mason carried home the first fire of the Dia-thìrich. That is the oldest thing the island dates. The making of the world by Caoran, the godfolk, and their first finding of the coloured coal, which they did not understand and let lie, are told in *Aithris a' Chruthachaidh*, the Telling of the Making, and have no year. *Linn na Doire* is the era of the grove, from the night in Doire ghlas when the gods came to the seven cold coals and named Ailean Mòr king of the godkin; it is counted from his crowning and not from his birth, as a reign is counted. *Linn an Teine* is the era of the fire, from the year of the Binding of Brìde's flame at Dùn ìseal, which opens with the fall of the first king at the Ford of Blood three weeks before it; the fire it names is the First Flame that was sworn to, and not the mason's fire, which is older by the whole of the Ancient Age and the Age of Ailean. *Linn na Tìre* is the era of the land, from the word that came back of a land across open water. *Linn an Acair* is the era of the anchor, from the day the humans' ships came to anchor at the Crossing. *Linn an Dealachaidh* is the era of the parting, from the Severance and the settling of the north. *Linn an Dubhain* is the era of Dubhan, from the first fuel that burned without the vein. It is the only era of the seven named for a living man, and the north is apt to observe that he was one of theirs.

Five things in the table need a word.

An era's last year is also the first year of the next. The year in which an age opens is counted in the old era until the day of its first event, and in the new era from that day.

The Ancient Age closes with the lighting of the rainbow coal ({{date:I-0080a}}), and the Age of Ailean opens a week later with the vigil and the crowning in the same grove ({{date:I-0080b}}); so the Grove Era's first year is the last year of the Vein Era, and the week between them is the Vein Era's last.

The Holy Age is named for the flame and dated from it, but its first event is the fall of Ailean Mòr at Àth na Fala ({{date:I-0098a}}), three weeks before the Binding ({{date:II-0001}}). The copyists who first cut the past into ages began the Holy Age at the Binding; when the Library counted the Age of Ailean apart, its reckoners ended that age where the king ended, and gave the three weeks between to the Holy Age, so that no age should outlast the man it was named for.

The Age of Sundering is usually said to open with the Choosing, but the Choosing ({{date:III-0003}}) is not the first thing in it. The age opens with the finding of the land ({{date:III-0001}}), without which there would have been nothing to choose.

The Severance closes the Age of Strangers on the first day of AE 151, and the first events of the Age of the Kingdom follow it within that same year, which is therefore also SE 1. The copyists of the cellar entered the Severance as the last act of the age it ended, and the Library has left it there.

### II. How the Ages Counted Their Years

**The Ancient Age and the Age of Ailean.** Neither the Ancient Age nor the Age of Ailean kept a count of years that anyone has found. Their people counted other things. The Seann-Dhaoine cut strokes on stones at the edges of their middens, one stone to a household, and the first strokes were cut on {{date:I-0090}}. The tally wall at Seann Warr grew to some three thousand strokes, and in one stretch the tallies of three coasts thicken together ({{date:I-0094}}). These are counts of the dead. None of them has been read as a count of years.

The first tide-count ({{date:I-0126}}), a rock on which someone marked the high-water line, comes nearer. Its marks fall at intervals regular enough that later scholars have suspected a reckoning of the moon in them. The custom was carried to Ros dhomhain with the interval unchanged. What the interval was for no one knows, and the Library has declined to guess. The hill-folk also knew the crossing season of the great cats and kept off the passes while the panthers went over ({{date:I-0079}}). That season they kept year by year, but the years they did not number.

The oldest oral king-list, *Rìghrean na Cloiche*, begins with Ailean Mòr ({{date:I-0081}}) and "gives no lengths of reign." The Red Hill list is the same. When both lists were recited together at the ford gathering ({{date:I-0245}}), the recital lasted from dusk to dawn; yet it measured nothing but a line of names. Here and there the tellings of those ages bind a few deeds fast to one another, and where they do, those deeds are remembered as falling in one year or within a few. Between such points the tellings keep the order of things and little of the distance between them, and the years of those ages are known only so well as the tellings know them.

**The Holy Age.** The Holy Age counted in two ways, and neither of them ran from a fixed beginning.

The first was **by reigns**. From the Binding of the First Flame ({{date:II-0001}}) the Keepers of the flame were remembered in a recited roll that begins with Beathag Bhàn. After Goraidh mac Ìomhair was first called *rìgh* at Dùn dhearg ({{date:II-0077}}), the rulers of the lowlands had a list of their own. A year was named by whoever held it: "the ninth year of Iain Garbh," "the third winter of the Keeper Seumas Bàn." That served well enough for a debt within living memory; for the distance between two reigns it was of no use, since the recited lists gave no lengths. The first written king-list, Tormod Scrìobhaiche's roll ({{date:II-0111}}), marks which names came from reciters and which from living memory. The Keepers had their own roll set down in answer ({{date:II-0113}}), and it proved several centuries longer than Tormod's. From the reign of Seonag Chrom the clerks of the red hall kept the king-list year by year. Between Tormod Òg and Anna Ruadh the list gives nine rulers of the house of Tormod, "their names and years and little else" ({{date:II-0189}}). Those years were counted within each reign and did not run on from one reign to the next.

The second was **by burnings**. From the Small-Burning Law ({{date:II-0027}}), a household might burn no more than its measured handful of coal in a season, and the Hall at Dùn ìseal kept the record of those seasons. It counted them from the Law: the first burning, the second, and on through the centuries. A Small-Burning year ran from one filling of the baskets to the next, and the Hall filled them at Samhain, when the galleries closed and the hearths began to need the coal. The first sentence under the Law ({{date:II-0028}}) took from a household its right "for the season after," and it was entered by the burning in which it fell. Of all the island's counts the count of burnings came nearest to an era, because it ran from a single act and was meant never to stop. It long outlived the age. The Law was never repealed; it lapsed ({{date:IV-0063}}), and the custodians of Doire mhin kept the old count one season longer than anyone else ({{date:IV-0064}}), with the note that nobody had asked for it.

By the close of the age the Keepers' roll held one hundred and forty names and the king-list more than ninety ({{date:II-0248}}). The two did not agree in every place, and the Hall's count of burnings did not always agree with either.

**The Age of Sundering.** In this age the island first numbered its years from a single day, and the count it made was the count of the Diosal.

### III. The Count of the Diosal, and How It Was Given Up

*Diosal* is the island's word for the sunwise turn: the way a hand moves, a boat rounds a point, or a procession goes about a cairn, with the right shoulder toward the centre and the course following the sun. Before going down the Abhainn naomh to open water the fleet of the Sundering went once round the river pool of {{place:burg:489}} sunwise, for luck, and the watchers on the quay counted the hulls as they passed. The harbour scribe's entry for the turn is the last thing any roll says of the departed.

The count was not made at the time. On {{date:III-0025}} Donnchadh mac Thormoid, scribe of the vein-house above Muileann dhearg, began to number the years from the sunwise turn and called them *bliadhnaichean an Diosail*, the years of the Diosal. By his own reckoning only a few of them had yet passed. There was no year nought, and the years before the turn were counted backwards, *ron Diosal*, "before the Diosal". The count was his alone at first. The crown's scribes at Cathair dhearg took it up about a generation later.

The harbour scribe sets the Setting-Out and the sunwise turn on one day, the sailing on {{date:III-0023}} and the turn on {{date:III-0024}}, the same morning. The custody-book, however, enters the turn under a later day than the sailing, after the next Samhain. No one has accounted for that gap of half a year, and the keepers have left both entries as they stand. Donnchadh counted from the turn as the custody-book entered it. The crown's scribes, taking up the count when the court remembered the departure as a single thing, counted from the year the ships sailed. Both began each year at Samhain, by the moon, as the Hall had done; so by the old year the sailing and the turn lay in different years, and the two counts stood a year apart. So, the Library believes, the counts came to differ. The rolls do not prove it, and it leaves unexplained why the turn is entered so long after the sailing.

The difference stood for more than six hundred years, until {{date:III-0127}}, when the vein-house and the crown's scribes found that their counts disagreed and settled the matter by agreement. The same agreement fixed the day of the empty-harbour rite, which the two counts had placed differently. A later hand in the custody-book adds that the scribes afterwards "cease to pretend it was fixed by proof." From that day there was one Diosal count on Dia-thìr, and it followed the harbour scribe, setting the sailing and the turn both in its first year. The vein-house marked its thousandth year ({{date:III-0156}}) with a single measured burning on Cidhe an Diosail, the quay of the sunwise turn.

For the rest of the Age of Sundering, through the Age of Strangers and into the first years of the kingdom, the island dated its writings by the Diosal. The custodians set its year beneath the humans' year at the head of their pages (section VI). The keeper's proclamation at the founding of the Library ({{date:V-0070}}) is dated "the year one thousand nine hundred and forty-five of the Diosal", and it is among the last public writings so dated.

The count had served the scribes of the rolls well enough. It served the Library ill. Everything older than the sunwise turn had to be counted backwards in years that ran into the thousands: the Binding fell nearly three thousand years before the Diosal, and the mason's fire nearly thirteen thousand, a number no keeper could write with a steady hand, since no roll and no telling measured it. The count ran, moreover, from an absence; and the Library's first keeper said that a people ought not to have to name every year of its life after the day its kin went away.

The copyists of the cellar had already cut the past into ages (section XII), and Ailean Leabhar, when he came to the Library ({{date:V-0071}}), took their table as the frame of his work. He counted each age from its own first event; and in doing so he and his reckoners parted the eldest age of the table in two (section XII), so that the Age of Ailean was counted apart from the Ancient Age, and he wrote the dates of the ledgers he was having read in the years of their age and not of the Diosal. The council followed him. With the creation of the office of royal chronicler ({{date:V-0078}}) it ordered that the kingdom's records should count their years by the ages of the Library's table ({{date:V-0078a}}), six as the table then stood, and seven since. Gormshuil nic Dhùghaill entered her first year as the eighteenth of the Severance Era. The Diosal count was kept thereafter only in old books. Its name lives on in the day of the empty-harbour rite, *Là an Diosail*, and in the quay at Seann Skell, and nobody has proposed to change either.

No one foresaw the seventh era. The first working dubhan was made on {{date:VI-0001}}, and for years it was one man's fuel and no more; Beathag nic Fhionnlaigh, then chronicler, noted it at the foot of that year and spelled it three ways. When the council pegged the coinage to the making of dubhan in place of the coal reserves, it proclaimed at the same sitting that the Age of the Kingdom had ended with the first working dubhan, and that the kingdom's years were counted anew from that day ({{date:VI-0049a}}). The Severance Era closed with its seventy-first year, and the year of the proclamation became the fifteenth of the Dubhan Era. Beathag re-headed fourteen years of her own chronicle, and wrote at the foot of the page that she had never before had to move a year she was living in.

The Tuathaich were not asked about either change. The northern parish books, so far as the Library has seen them, give the civil day and month and the kingdom's era in their dealings with the kingdom. What count they keep among themselves the Library cannot say, for few of those books come to it.

### IV. The Reckoning of the Moon

Before the humans came the Dia-thìrich had no calendar fixed to the sun. They reckoned by the moon, and they counted time by nights and not by days. The night went before its day, as the dark half of the year went before the bright: a feast began at dusk on its eve, *Oidhche Shamhna* was the first night of Samhain and not the night before it, and a debt of "ten nights" fell due at the tenth dusk. The custody-book calls this reckoning *cunntas nan oidhcheannan*, the count of the nights, and the Library's word for it, *cunntas-oidhche*, the night-count, is shortened from the custody-book's.

The year began at Samhain, with the dark half of the year. It held twelve moon-months, *mìosan-gealaich*, of thirty nights and twenty-nine nights in turn. Each moon-month was halved. It began on the night of the full moon and waned through its dark half, *an leth dubh*; at the dark of the moon it turned, and its bright half, *an leth geal*, waxed to the next full. Below the month the Dia-thìrich had the half-moon, *cola-deug*, "fourteen nights", though the halves were of fourteen or fifteen nights as the moon ran. They had no week. In the rolls of the Holy Age and of the Sundering a debt, a sentence or a turn in the galleries is set by the half-moon, and the markets met at the full; Queen Sìleas's charter to Cathair fhada ({{date:III-0070a}}) grants a market at every full moon. The night of the full and the night of the dark were rest-nights, *oidhcheannan-tàmh*, on which the Hall let no coal be cut and the fishing households set no nets.

Twelve moons make three hundred and fifty-four nights, some eleven fewer than the sun's year, so that without help the feasts would have walked backwards through the seasons. About every thirty moons, two and a half years, the keepers of the reckoning put in a thirteenth moon of thirty nights at the end of the year, after Damhrach and before Samhnas, so that Samhain should not come before the summer had ended. They called it *Eadrach*, the between-moon. In the Holy Age the Hall at Dùn ìseal declared it; after the Hall's day the vein-house did, and since the vein-house's day the druid schools declare it, and the orders of the Old Faith follow them. The Hall's roll of the years in which Eadrach was put in is the thread on which every older date of the island hangs (section XII), and the thread is broken in places.

The four fire-feasts were fixed by the moon, each on the first night of its moon: Samhain at the opening of Samhnas, Là Fhèill Brìde of Gearras, Bealltainn of Bealltach, Lùnastal of Lùghnas. Against the sun they wander by as much as half a month from year to year, and the between-moon brings them back.

The names of the thirteen moons are old, and some of them were in dispute already when the Hall first wrote them down. The meanings given below are those the Library holds most likely.

### V. The Humans' Calendar

The humans who came at the Crossing ({{date:IV-0001}}) brought a calendar of another kind. It was fixed to the sun and paid no heed to the moon. Its year began in the dead of winter and had twelve months of set lengths, thirty or thirty-one days and one of twenty-eight, with a twenty-ninth day put into that short month every fourth year. They counted by days from midnight, not by nights from dusk, and they divided the month into weeks of seven days, one of which was kept for rest and worship. Their years they numbered from an epoch in their own land, which they named and did not explain, and their numbers were far larger than the island's. No Dia-thìrich scribe set down the name of that land ({{date:IV-0007}}), and the letter-books do not explain the epoch either.

In the season of translation Samuel Wren and the custodian Gormshuil nic Fhearchair kept their word-lists side by side ({{date:IV-0009}}). Wren's list, which survives in the Administration's letter-books, sets each of the humans' months against the moon it most nearly matched, and it is the first writing on Dia-thìr in which the two reckonings meet. From that pairing the Dia-thìrich named the humans' months (section VII).

### VI. How the Civil Calendar Came In

The humans' calendar was never proclaimed on Dia-thìr. It came in by paper. The first written treaty ({{date:IV-0029}}) was drawn up in the humans' tongue only and dated by the humans' day, month and year. The custodians entered it in their roll under its Diosal year and the moon in which it was signed, and left the day to the Administration. The Harbour Articles ({{date:IV-0058}}), the Company's charter and every contract for extraction ({{date:IV-0039}}) were dated the same way, and the contracts paid their crews by the week and let them rest on the humans' day of rest. So the Dia-thìrich who worked for the Company learned the week before any of them was christened. The same clerks who kept two sets of books for coin and barter ({{date:IV-0055}}) came to keep two dates at the head of every page, the humans' year and day above and the Diosal year and the night of the moon beneath, and they complained about both.

A contract that fell due on the humans' day could not be settled by the moon, and the clerks who had to settle it went over to the humans' months first and to the humans' new year after. By the third currency reform ({{date:IV-0253}}) every contract on the island ran from am Faoilleach, and the Dia-thìrich civil year had moved to the humans' beginning without any decree. The moon-year survived in the shrines, in the Hall's count of burnings until it lapsed, and among the custodians of Inis bhàn, who taught "the old reckoning" in their school ({{date:IV-0122}}). The Administration's census ({{date:IV-0246}}) and its register ({{date:IV-0252}}) were dated by the humans' reckoning alone.

After the Severance the kingdom kept the civil calendar, since a century of contracts, ledgers and letters already stood on it, and no one on the council wished to return the kingdom's debts to the moon. The kingdom has kept it since. Its months have the names the Dia-thìrich gave them, and its years are counted by the eras (section III).

### VII. The Civil Months

The civil months are the humans' months under Dia-thìris names. For ten of them the clerks took the name of the moon that Wren's list set beside the month, in the form it had in the speech of their day; two names they took from the humans themselves. Beside each month is set the island's work in it, as the rolls of every age describe that work, reckoned into the civil months.

An Gearran takes a twenty-ninth day in every fourth year, as the humans' calendar has it. The reckoning of the moon had no such day and needed none, for the between-moon did its work.

### VIII. The Week

The week, *seachdain*, came to Dia-thìr with the Mission. The humans' Church kept seven days, the first of them for rest and worship and two others for fasting, and when the Mission raised its chapel at Ros dhomhain ({{date:IV-0044}}) and opened its school ({{date:IV-0121}}) it taught the week with its prayers. The Company's contracts had already taught it to the crews (section VI).

The names came with it. Each begins with *Di-*, the Mission's church-tongue word for a day, written solid with the rest of the name. Four are the church-tongue's own names put into Dia-thìris mouths. Three the first converts made for themselves, from the Mission's fasts. The names below are given in the forms the royal chronicler uses.

| Day | Dia-thìris | The name |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Didòmhnaich | the Lord's day, from the church-tongue; the Mission's day of rest and worship. The Company's contracts let no coal be cut on it |
| 2 | Diluain | the moon's day, from the church-tongue |
| 3 | Dimàirt | the day of the war-god of the humans' forebears, from the church-tongue; the same name as the month am Màrt |
| 4 | Diciadain | the day of the first fast |
| 5 | Diardaoin | the day between the fasts |
| 6 | Dihaoine | the day of the fast |
| 7 | Disathairne | the day of another old god of the humans' forebears, from the church-tongue. The Mission kept his name and not his story |

The two fasts are the Mission's own: on the fourth day and the sixth the Church abstained from meat, and in the north it abstains still. Nobody else on Dia-thìr keeps them. An older opinion of the Library, that they were fasts from fire kept by Holy Age households under the Small-Burning Law, did not survive the reading of the Mission's letter-books; and the unlit hearth found under the peat ({{date:I-0108}}), which was once cited for it, proves nothing about weeks.

The Dia-thìrich had rested by the moon, on the nights of the full and the dark, and the Mission made a great deal of the difference. The custodians made little of it. It was the Mission's own minister Matthew Dunne who told the Company, against the night-shift order, that the Dia-thìrich were right that even the ground must rest ({{date:IV-0241}}), and the Mission recalled him for it.

### IX. The Three Faiths and the Two Reckonings

The kingdom lives by the civil calendar; but the moon was never put away, and it runs on beside the civil months as the reckoning of the Old Faith.

*An Creideamh Sean*, the Old Faith, keeps its feasts by the moon. Its four fire-feasts fall on the first nights of Samhnas, Gearras, Bealltach and Lùghnas, and its orders reckon the moons as the druid schools declare them, between-moon and all. Òrd Bhrìde lights its first lamp of the year on the first night of Gearras and not on the civil day, and in a year when the moon runs late its lamp is lit nearly a fortnight after the Church's candles.

*Na Seann Spioradan*, the Old Spirits, keep the night-count at the hearth. They count a journey, a mourning or a guest's stay in nights, and their dead are waked for three nights and not three days. They keep the feasts with the Old Faith, by the moon, at the hearth and at the mounds.

*An Eaglais*, the Church, keeps the civil calendar and the week it brought, with its day of rest, its two fasts and its own feasts on their civil days. The kingdom keeps the quarter-days on the first day of their civil months (section X), and the council, the courts and the markets follow the kingdom.

So a feast of the Old Faith and the same feast in the kingdom's calendar may fall a fortnight apart, and no one on Dia-thìr thinks it strange.

### X. The Quarter-Days

Four feasts divide the year into quarters. They are the great feasts of the Old Faith and older than any order now on the island, and all three faiths keep them in some fashion, though each for its own reasons (Appendix B). The kingdom keeps them on the first day of their civil months. The Old Faith keeps them on the first night of their moons (section IV).

**Là Fhèill Brìde**, 1 an Gearran, also called Imbolc, is the feast of Brìde, whose flame was bound at Dùn ìseal. Every hearth is swept and relit and the year's first work in the fields begins. The followers of Na Seann Spioradan set a sheaf by the door. Òrd Bhrìde and the Hawk order, the two forms of her rite, light their first lamp of the year from the altar-flame. The Church keeps the day as the feast of a saint of the same name.

**Bealltainn**, 1 an Cèitean, takes its name from a bright fire, and it is the feast of summer's beginning, when the Otherworld stands near. Cattle go up to the high pastures between two fires. In the Holy Age this was the day the Hall asked the cairn-watchers of Cnoc ghlas whether the galleries might open. The first sentence under the Small-Burning Law is dated {{date:II-0028}}, on Bealltainn itself. Whether the day was chosen for the sentence the roll does not say.

**Lùnastal**, 1 an Lùnastal, is Lugh's feast of first-fruits and gathering, which he made in memory of his foster-mother Tailtiu, and the month is named for it. Markets open, contests are held, and oaths are taken before witnesses. Cathal was crowned on the feast itself, {{date:VI-0006}}.

**Samhain**, 1 an t-Samhain, marks summer's end, when the Otherworld stands open, the sìth of the mounds are abroad, and the Old Faith names Donn and the dead of the year. Before the humans came it was also the beginning of the Dia-thìrich year. The Hall filled the baskets of the Small-Burning Law at Samhain, the galleries closed, and the dead were thought nearest. The Tuathaich, whose calendar came to them from an Tìr Thall, keep the day with the rest of the island, in the Church's way, as the feast of all the saints and the day of the dead. They have never counted the year from it.

The days of the older customs are given as the Library turned them (section XII). The night of lamps and the empty-harbour rite were fixed by the moon, and since the civil calendar came in the harbour towns have kept them on the civil days that the Library's tables give.

The day of the Setting-Out has never been kept as a feast. The chronicles say nothing more of the departed once the ships clear the harbour, and the island has kept its remembrance of them there, at the harbour where the rolls fall silent, on the day the custody-book gives for the turn and not on the day they sailed. Since {{date:III-0127}} the empty-harbour rite has fallen on that day, though the harbour scribe, whose count the island followed, sets the turn on the day of the sailing. The Library does not forget that the custody-book may have taken its day from the rite, and not the rite from the book. By the last entry of the Age of Sundering the rite was kept by eleven people ({{date:III-0238}}). How many keep it now the chronicles of the later ages do not say.

**The Severance**, {{date:IV-0377}}, is kept at Cathair dhearg by the council as the day on which the latter history of the kingdom begins, and the era named for it bore its memory in every date for seventy-one years. The Mourning Custom of the Sundering, first made for a parting that was chosen, was used again, uneasily, for this parting that was not, and the council's keeping of this day is the use most often meant. In the north the day is not kept. It is worked through like any other, and the Tuathaich say that this is their way of keeping it.

**The Long War** left two days. The Battle of Àth leathan, {{date:VI-0081}}, opened *An Cogadh Fada*. Tuathaich families have raised a cairn without leave on the field at {{place:marker:18}}, and it grows by a stone or two whenever the patrols pass; whether they come on the day of the battle nobody has written down. The Battle of Muileann ghlas, {{date:VI-0115}}, ended the war, and the regiments raised in the campaign keep that day at their stations. So the two days are kept by two peoples; and the Library, whose record of the war is sealed ({{date:VI-0128}}), holds what it can of both.

### XII. How the Library Turned the Old Dates

Every date in these annals is given in the civil calendar, with a day of the civil month and a year of the era, though for most of the island's history nobody who lived it counted so. The Library made it so, and the labour took three generations.

It began with Beathag nic Thòmais, the mill-owner's widow who bought up old copies of the custody-book and the harbour rolls and kept them in a loft over her mill at Muileann chaol ({{date:III-0217}}). She did not reckon years. But she set copies that disagreed side by side and declined to choose between them ({{date:III-0219}}), and that became the way of all who worked in Muileann chaol after her. She left her loft to the town on condition that nothing in it be burned ({{date:III-0221}}).

The joining of the counts was done in the cellar. From {{date:IV-0248}}, the custodians of the east sent copies of their rolls to Muileann chaol for safekeeping, away from the Residency's reach, and three families did the copying. While they copied, they laid the counts against each other. Where the king-list gave a ruler's years and the Hall's roll gave the burnings of the same reign, the two could be made to meet, and each meeting fixed a stretch of the older count against the newer. The copyists called the result *Clàr nan Aoisean*, the table of the ages. It is the first writing that runs one numbered line of years from the Binding to the copyists' own day, in the years of the Diosal. It is also the first to divide the island's past into ages, and to set before the Holy Age an older age whose length, the table owns, it cannot measure. The copyists made that older age one, from the mason's fire to the Binding. When the custodians sent what survived of the Age of Strangers to the same cellars ({{date:IV-0376}}), the table was carried through to the Severance, and in that form it gave the ages down to the Severance the bounds that historians of Dia-thìr still use.

Leabharlann Muileann chaol, founded {{date:V-0070}}, took the table as one of its first possessions. The council's clerks had emptied the humans' counting-houses ({{date:V-0005}}) and boxed the ledgers unread, because few of them could read the humans' hand. Ailean Leabhar, the Library's first keeper, spent his first years having them read ({{date:V-0071}}), and he had every date in them, and in the custodians' rolls, turned into the civil calendar. For the Age of Strangers the double-dated pages of the custodians' clerks were his guide, since they set the humans' day above the night of the moon. For the ages before the Crossing he had only the moons, and he turned them by the Hall's roll of the between-moons, reckoning moon by moon back from the first double-dated page to the Binding. Each date so turned he set in the era of its age, as the council afterwards ordered for all the kingdom's records (section III).

It was in this work that the Library's reckoners counted the Age of Ailean apart from the Ancient Age. The copyists' eldest age ran from the mason's fire to the Binding, and Ailean Leabhar's reckoners, laying the tellings of Clann na Ceiste against the king-lists, found that nearly half of it lay within the days of one man. Ailean Mòr was crowned in the grove at Doire ghlas and was king until he fell, and the Line of the Mason, which keeps the years of its dead beside their names, gives him more than four thousand six hundred of them. The reckoners held that a life so long was an age in itself, and that a count which ran over it in the same years as the mason's lightning and the Seven Coals would hide it. So they parted the eldest age at the crowning. What went before it, the mason and the Keeper and the lightings of the coals, they left as the Ancient Age, under the old name; what came after it they called *An Aois Ailein*, the Age of Ailean, and its era *Linn na Doire*, the Grove Era, from the grove where the king was made. Some of the Library's own scholars thought it a fancy to give one man an age, and said so in the margins; but the council took the table as the Library gave it, and the Age of Ailean has stood in it since. When the Library absorbed the provincial archives ({{date:V-0226}}), the table was checked against them and corrected where the archives knew better. When the seventh era was proclaimed, the Library re-headed the years since the first working dubhan as the chronicler had done.

So a reader of these annals who finds a Holy Age deed set on 3 an t-Samhain should remember that those who did it knew no such day. They knew a night of the moon Samhnas, and they began their year at Samhain and counted it by nights. The Library's day is the civil day on which that night would have fallen, as near as the roll of the between-moons allows. Where the roll has gaps, the day may be a half-moon out, and the Library says so. Ailean Leabhar disliked the saying *a chur san Leabharlann*, "to put it in the Library," on the ground that the Library settles nothing and only keeps it ({{date:V-0072}}). The turned dates should be read in the same spirit.

**Where the dates come from.** For the Age of Dubhan, the Age of the Kingdom and the Age of Strangers the days are remembered: they come from rolls, ledgers, letter-books and the chroniclers' own entries, and from the Crossing on they were written in the civil calendar or beside it. For the Age of Sundering they are remembered more thinly, from the custody-book, the harbour rolls and the crown's king-list, all dated by the moon, and a good many were settled by agreement, as the day of the empty-harbour rite was. For the Holy Age the years come from the Keepers' roll, the king-lists and the Hall's count of burnings, which do not always agree, and the days mostly from the feasts and moons the rolls name. For the Ancient Age and the Age of Ailean all rests on the tellings, the king-lists without lengths of reign, the tally walls and the tide-count. The tellings keep the order of things well and the distance between them poorly. Where they agree, the date is as sure as they are; where they disagree or fall silent it is not, and such a date should be trusted for the order it keeps rather than for its year.
