## Appendix B — The Faiths of Dia-thìr

Here is set down what is known of the faiths of Dia-thìr: the three that the kingdom's map names; the older reverence out of which the first two grew; the gods and the orders of the Old Faith; the Church that the humans brought; and the two matters that every faith on the island has had to answer, the holiness of the vein and the riddle of why it answers some hands and not others. The account is drawn from the annals, the Keepers' roll of Dùn ìseal, the custody-book of the vein-house, the registers that the schools, orders and churches sent to Leabharlann Muileann chaol, and the map. Several faiths and orders have kept their own counsel, and where they have, the gap is left open.

Each faith is entered under the Dia-thìris name the map gives it, with its god or powers as the map gives them, and each order of the Old Faith under its own name. Where it is known when a name first appears, that is noted; where it is not known, nothing is guessed.

### I. The Faiths in Brief

The kingdom's map counts 505 towns and sets each under one faith. The seat is the house held first among a faith's houses in the present age.

| Faith | In the common tongue | Kind (as the map has it) | God | Towns | Seat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| An Creideamh Sean | the Old Faith | organised; polytheist | an Dagda, the Good God, first of the Tuath Dè | 315 | Dùn ìseal, where Brìde's flame was bound; each order has its own seat |
| Na Seann Spioradan | the Old Spirits | folk; shamanic | na Sìthichean, the people of the mounds | 126 | Seann Dunn of the east coast, where the houses name the ancestors of forty families in one hall |
| An Eaglais | the Church | organised; monotheist | Crìosd, Christ | 64 | Doire ghlas (called its heart; no bishop's seat recorded) |

The Old Faith and the Old Spirits are Dia-thìrich faiths. The Church is the faith of the Tuathaich of the north, and it holds all 64 Tuathaich towns. The Old Faith is kept in six orders (*ùird*, singular *òrd*), each with its own houses, seat, sayings and history:

The Church is kept in three bodies. The churches of the Mission's line hold 51 towns, with eight churches the map marks as temples; their largest house is at Cnoc ghorm. The old congregations of the north coast, Eaglais nan Tuathach, hold twelve, with three. The vigil of the Grey Night, which the Church's bishops call irregular, holds one town and its one house.

Counted by towns, the Old Faith is the most widely held, and within it the druid schools are the most widely held order, with Macha's next. Counted by the people in their towns, the Old Faith comes first by far, at nearly three million two hundred thousand. The Old Spirits come next, at a little over one million two hundred thousand, and the Church holds some five hundred and forty thousand. Among the orders Macha's is the largest in people, at nearly one million two hundred thousand, close behind the Old Spirits, and the schools come next. Òrd Bhrìde, which the kingdom holds to be the rightful keeper of the vein, has nine towns. This is no error in the count; the reason is told in section VIII.

**Shires and temples.** Counted by land rather than by towns, the faiths divide the island's 123 shires as follows. A faith *holds* a shire where it has more of the shire's land than any other faith, and *shares* one where it has a smaller part. The temples are the houses the map marks as temples.

| Faith | Shires held | Shires shared | Temples |
|---|---|---|---|
| An Creideamh Sean | 77 | 13 | 65 |
| Na Seann Spioradan | 34 | 11 | 24 |
| An Eaglais | 11 | 5 | 12 |

That accounts for 122 shires. In {{place:province:120}}, the shire whose lands take in the far north-western cape, most of the land keeps no faith at all. The orders between them stand first in seventy-six of the Old Faith's shires. In the seventy-seventh, {{place:province:90}} in the south-east, no order has the greater part: the Old Spirits, Òrd Bhrìde and Macha's order have almost equal shares and the schools a small one, so that the orders together hold two-thirds of the land.

**Descent.** The Old Spirits are the old reverence itself, kept at the hearths without a break, and nothing older is known from which they came. The Old Faith grew out of the same reverence: its six orders began as the companies of the Hall's council at the end of the Holy Age (section II), though the gods they serve are older than any company. The Church came over the sea with the humans ({{date:IV-0001}}) and has no root on Dia-thìr older than the Crossing. The vigil of the Grey Night is the only rite on the island that grew out of two faiths at once: its prayer came from the harbours of Manannan's order, and its goose from the Church (section VII).

**How they spread.** The Old Spirits have never sought converts beyond their own people, and neither have Òrd Bhrìde, the Hawk order or the old congregations of the north coast. Each is held where its people live and nowhere else. The druid schools and Macha's order spread with the kingdom, the one through its schools and the other through the crown's favour, and neither has gone beyond the Dia-thìrich. Since King Aonghas took it up, Macha's order has spread faster than any other. The Church sought converts from the day the Mission landed, and counted its first Dia-thìrich converts among the children of its school at Ros dhomhain ({{date:IV-0121}}). It holds no Dia-thìrich town today. Manannan's order, the Stone and the Grey Night take in anyone of any people who comes to them. Only Manannan's has taught its prayers to a second people: the first human fishing families at Ceann mhòr learned its harbour prayers ({{date:IV-0118}}). The Stone, for all its open doors, is still held only in Dia-thìrich towns of the south-west.

**Land without a faith.** No faith holds the far north-western cape beyond Baile chrom. It has no town, and most of it lies outside any shire. Its few families, herders and shore-folk, most of them Tuathaich, have no house of any faith, and the first census of the restored kingdom entered them as *gun chreideamh*, without a faith ({{date:V-0034a}}). The same entry covers Eilean ghorm, where the grey geese winter and are never hunted ({{date:I-0055}}), Eilean dhubh, where the sea-thieves' boats were burned ({{date:III-0133}}), and the islet of Eilean gheal off the south-east coast. The cape has been thinly held since the dearth on the north-west shore ({{date:III-0134}}).

### II. The Old Reverence and Its Parting

The chronicle says plainly that the Dia-thìrich reverence was once one. On {{date:II-0221}}, it says, that one reverence began to part into the several threads that later ages named each for itself. What it had been while it was one may be gathered from the Ancient Age and the Age of Ailean, whose tellings name the slab, the cup and the handful, and set no god over them. The one telling of those ages that names the gods, the Telling of the Making, was kept within a single kindred (section III).

**The slab and the handful.** A mason broke into a vein of coal that showed every colour where it should have given dust ({{date:I-0001}}). That night lightning struck the broken vein and set it burning, and he carried the fire home, the first fire of the Dia-thìrich. His people dug no further. When the fire was out they laid a slab over the break and cut their well a stone's throw away ({{date:I-0003}}). Families took turns sitting by the slab ({{date:I-0112}}) and later chose a keeper by lot ({{date:I-0151}}). The first was Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill, who had a cup ground out of grey stone to the size of her cupped hand ({{date:I-0153}}), because by then no fire took more coal than one hand could hold ({{date:I-0110}}). When a keeper was found with a basket of coal in his house, it was neither burned nor kept. It was laid back under the slab: *the coal went home* ({{date:I-0156}}). By the end of the age every household of the hills burned its handful on one night of the dark of the year, so that the low fires of the handful showed on every hillside at once ({{date:I-0248}}).

**The doctrine.** The oldest saying on the island is cut on the column near Cnoc ghorm ({{date:I-0073}}), and it was first spoken as a rule at the ford gathering ({{date:I-0168}}):

> *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.*
> We did not come; we were here.

No order or house on Dia-thìr has disowned it since, though each reads it in its own way.

**What the Old Ones left.** {{place:marker:33}} has its altar set square to the central mountains ({{date:I-0035}}), and every later temple on Dia-thìr faces some other way. Its top stone was prised off and never found ({{date:I-0062}}). The chronicle calls it older than any faith of which there is record, and says that it was turned toward the vein and not toward the sky's rim ({{date:I-0063}}). The Dia-thìrich walked round it and took no stone from it ({{date:I-0216}}). Whether its builders held the vein holy, or only knew where it lay, no one can now say.

**The Binding and the first parting.** The Holy Age opens with the fall of Ailean Mòr at the Ford of Blood ({{date:I-0098a}}), and three weeks after it his heirs swore the Binding of the First Flame ({{date:II-0001}}), the flame the Old Faith calls Brìde's. At Dùn ìseal Beathag Bhàn was chosen to hold the flame ({{date:II-0002}}), and Talla na Lasrach was raised over it with one door facing the vein and none facing the sea ({{date:II-0004}}). The custom of the handful hardened into the Small-Burning Law ({{date:II-0027}}). The Keepers also judged what belonged to the vein, and each judgement drew a line. When Muireach Liath held that the fortune-stream Allt an Àigh belonged "to the ancestors and the weather and not to the vein" ({{date:II-0013}}), the Hall's reverence and the older reverence of the hearths could be seen for the first time as two things. The first became the Old Faith, and the second the Old Spirits.

By the end of the Holy Age the Hall could no longer hold the island to one rite. Seumas Bàn's clerks listed every rite that burned the handful or claimed the vein ({{date:II-0215}}): the Hall's own, the ancestor-keeping of the old folk, the dawn-hawk of the north coast, the wood-watchers of the west, the wolf-sworn, the sting-oath, and both schools of the stag. The council then began to sit in companies by rite. Seumas Bàn complained that he kept the flame for eight peoples who each thought themselves its only keepers ({{date:II-0222}}). The ancestor-keepers are the Old Spirits of the map. The other companies are the roots of the six orders of the Old Faith. The Church grew from no root on Dia-thìr.

**What stayed common.** Pilgrims of every rite went on walking to {{place:marker:1}} ({{date:II-0223}}), and the sick of every rite were bathed at the springs of Muileann ruadh ({{place:marker:0}}) without being asked which rite they kept ({{date:II-0224}}). The Age of Sundering added two rites that belong to no single order. One is the Mourning Custom for the departed, which is neither wholly grief nor wholly rejoicing ({{date:III-0031}}). The other is the empty-harbour rite on Cidhe an Diosail at Seann Skell ({{date:III-0036}}). The harbour saying spoken at that rite belongs to no order of worship:

> *Dh'fhalbh iad, agus cha do dh'fhalbh iad.*
> They went, and they did not go.

### III. The Gods

The gods of the Old Faith are *na Dè*, and their people are the *Tuath Dè*, the people of the gods. The tellings of Dia-thìr are the oldest that hold them. The Dia-thìrich say the gods were on the island before the people were, for the Tuath Dè walked it with the Young God in its first days, before the godfolk grew; and the people, in their turn, were on it before anyone else came: *bha sinn ann*. The priests of the Old Faith are the *draoidhean*, the druids; its poets are the *filidhean* and its judges the *britheamhan*. Most of the gods have no house of their own. The orders each serve one god or one pair above the rest, and honour the others at the feasts and in need.

**Caoran**, *an Dia Òg*, the Young God, is the maker of this world and the god of the coal. His name is *ember*. He is the youngest son of an Dagda and Danu, of a long line of gods and goddesses, each of whom on coming of age went out and made a world of their own out of some other thing: one of song, one of salt and bone, one of wind and horn. Caoran chose coal, and took his first coal from the ash under his father's cauldron. Of it he made all that is, then the earth. The island he did not make as he made the rest: it was born. He held the last coal of all over the empty sea and let drops of fire fall from it, and they hardened where they fell, one on another, until the island stood up out of the water; and that, the Telling says, is why its coal is not the coal of the wide earth. He gave a spirit to every spring and hill on it, to the old trees, the standing stones and the pools under the falls, and these are the old spirits of the land that the hearths honour, whom Na Seann Spioradan keep (section VI). Then he laid his palms on the warm ground and called, and the first people grew up out of the island like grass after spring rain: *an Dia-shluagh*, the godfolk, whom the tellings call *feur uaine an eilein*, the green grass of the island, and from whom the Dia-thìrich, *Clann nan Dè*, the godkin, are descended. The island is the elder of their line. He gave them the island to keep, and as a hidden gift he laid the elements in the coal under its mountains, each in its own colour. These are *na Seachd Guail*, the Seven Coals; together they are *an gual dathach*, the coloured coal:

| Coal | Dia-thìris | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| black | *gual dubh* | earth |
| fire | *gual teine* | fire |
| snowflake | *gual sneachda* | air |
| gold sheen | *gual òir* | the sea |
| silver sheen | *gual airgid* | river water |
| mahogany | *gual donn-ruadh* | life |
| rainbow | *gual bogha-froise* | spirit |

There is coloured coal nowhere but on Dia-thìr, and only the eyes of the godkin see its colours; to all others it is black. The whole of this is the Telling of the Making, *Aithris a' Chruthachaidh*, which stands before the Seven Books. It was told by Coimhdeach na Fine to one of the mason's line, and the mason's kindred, *Clann a' Chlachair*, afterward called *Clann na Ceiste*, the Children of the Question, kept it among themselves; when it passed to the hearths at large is not known. The Telling and the Second Book say that Caoran came to the island once more, with the Tuath Dè about him, to make Ailean Mòr *Rìgh Chlann nan Dè*, king of the godkin ({{date:I-0080b}}), who was king of the godkin from that night until he fell at the Ford of Blood four thousand six hundred years after ({{date:I-0098a}}). Caoran has no order, no temple and no feast of his own. The Old Faith honours an Dagda first, as the father of the gods, and names Caoran after him as the maker of the world.

**An Dagda**, the Good God, is the father of the gods. His is the cauldron from which no company goes away hungry, the club that kills with one end and wakes the dead with the other, and the harp that calls the seasons in their order. He stands first among the gods on the map and in every order, yet he has no order and no temple of his own. Every house of the Old Faith fills a cauldron for him at the four feasts.

**Brìde** is the goddess of the hearth-fire, of poetry, of healing and of smithcraft. The first flame bound at Dùn ìseal ({{date:II-0001}}), three weeks after the first king fell, is hers, and the Keepers from Beathag Bhàn on ({{date:II-0002}}) kept it in her name. Her order, Òrd Bhrìde, keeps the flame still, and every hearth on the island is swept and relit on her feast.

**Crom Cruaich**, the bent one of the mound, is the god of the vein. The vein of coloured coal broken at the crack ({{date:I-0001}}) is his, for the Telling of the Making says that Caoran set him at its door, and so is the slab over it ({{date:I-0003}}). He gives, and his price is measured; the handful, the cup and the Small-Burning Law ({{date:II-0027}}) are that measure. The tellings of Magh Slèacht, where his stone stood covered in gold among twelve stones, say that his due there was the firstlings of every kind, paid at Samhain; the Law holds that due to a handful. His oldest byname is Dòrsair, Uilebheist nan Geataichean, the Monster of the Gates, who stands at the door between the mountain and those who take from it. He is honoured by Òrd Bhrìde at the southern Seann Skell and at the last open face of the Sloc Mòr.

**Fionntan mac Bòchra** is the oldest of men. He outlived the great flood of the old tellings, and passed the long ages as a salmon, an eagle and a hawk, and he forgets nothing. His fellow in memory is the Hawk of Acaill, the grey hawk of time, as old as he is, which took one of his eyes when he swam as a salmon; the two have told each other the ages. The Hawk order takes its name from that hawk and keeps them both, and on the north coast it calls the hawk Mòd. The oldest hawk on the island, over a door at Àth mhòr ({{date:I-0131}}), is read as the Hawk of Acaill.

**Flidais** is the lady of the deer and the wild. The white stag first told of at the edge of Coille Naomh Muileann chiar ({{date:I-0050}}), the wood marked sacred before any order claimed it ({{date:I-0048}}), is hers in the tellings of the later ages, though the oldest tellings give it no god. The druid schools keep the wood in her name.

**Macha** is the goddess of sovereignty, of kingship and of horses. It is told that when she was great with child she was made to race against a king's horses. She won, and bore twins at the finish, and she laid on the men who made her run the pangs of a woman in labour, to fall on them in their hour of need. Eamhain Mhacha is named for her twins. The grey of the two horses that drew Cù Chulainn's chariot bears her name, Liath Macha, the Grey of Macha, and his yoke-fellow is Dubh Sainglenn, the black. The two were foaled on the night the hero was born; other tellings have them rise out of a grey lake in the mountains. Before his last battle the grey wept tears of blood, and when the hero fell the grey fought over him while the black ran on. Her order keeps the pair as its sign. Her wood is Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag, and her order makes the kings of Dia-thìr: King Aonghas was the first king buried by her rite ({{date:III-0096}}), and Niall was crowned by it at Cathair dhearg ({{date:V-0012}}).

**Lia Fàil**, the stone that cries out under the rightful king, which the Tuath Dè brought out of the north of the world and set up at Teamhair, is honoured as a power in its own right by the order of the Stone. The order holds that the Stone is one and its stones are many. Its first stone stood at the gate of Caol leathan ({{date:II-0100}}), and its second was dragged from the mountain to Inis thais ({{date:II-0161}}). Some read the name of the oldest king-list, Rìghrean na Cloiche, the kings of the stone ({{date:I-0081}}), as the kings of this stone. The list itself does not say.

**Manannan mac Lir** is the lord of the sea, and the sea round Dia-thìr is his, Muir Mhanannain. His mist hides the Otherworld, and the Dia-thìrich tell that the fleet of the Sundering sailed into it ({{date:III-0023}}). Until the Crossing the same mist hid Dia-thìr from every ship beyond it. His Cup of Truth breaks in three when three lies are spoken over it, and three truths make it whole again. His order keeps the western coast, and its first seat is the western Seann Skell ({{date:III-0120}}).

**Lugh Làmhfhada**, Lugh of the long arm, is the master of every craft. Lùnastal is his feast, which he made in memory of his foster-mother Tailtiu. On it markets open, games are held and oaths are sworn before witnesses, and on the feast itself Cathal was crowned ({{date:VI-0006}}).

**Goibhniu** is the smith of the gods. The smiths of the old vein country, where the forges of Muileann chrom, Muileann dhearg and Muileann dhomhain now make arms, swear by him. No smith on Dia-thìr could place the nails of the timber that came ashore at Ceann mhòr from beyond Manannan's mist ({{date:III-0236}}).

**Dian Cècht** is the healer of the gods, who made Nuadha's hand of silver. The springs of Muileann ruadh, where the sick of every rite are bathed ({{date:II-0224}}), are his.

**The Morrìgan** is the goddess of war and the crows. The battlefields are hers, among them the ford of Àth shean, where the custody-book counts three hundred dead ({{date:III-0106}}).

**Ogma** is the god of letters and of eloquence. The first Dia-thìrich letters, the stag-letters made from the cutters' tally-notches ({{date:II-0088}}), are the ogham, which the druids call his gift.

**Nuadha** is the first king of the gods, who lost his hand in battle and ruled again with the hand of silver that Dian Cècht made him. Dia-thìris still calls an artificial limb *ball-airgid*, silver-limb, after him.

**Danu** is the mother of the gods, and the Tuath Dè are named from her. She has no house; the tellings give her the rivers.

**Donn** is the lord of the dead. His house, *Taigh Dhuinn*, is a rock in the sea to the south-west, and the dead go to it. The Old Faith names him at Samhain and at every burial.

**Coimhdeach na Fine**, the Keeper of the Kin, is neither god nor ghost. It is the spirit that watches over the island's people for Caoran, made, the Telling says, of the smoke of the first coal he woke. It shows itself seldom and generations apart, as a figure of grey smoke of a tall man's height that stands still in the wind. It has no order, no house and no feast, and it is not counted among the Tuath Dè. In the grove of Doire ghlas it told the Telling of the Making to one of the mason's line, and when he asked how the colours might be woken, it answered with a riddle, which the Children of the Question kept:

> *Tha spiorad fa leth san speur, san talamh agus sa mhuir. Meascaich na spioradan agus bidh iad uile-chumhachdach.*
> The sky, the earth and the sea each carry their own spirit. Merge the spirits, and they become almighty.

It came again to the grove to light the rainbow coal before Ailean Mòr ({{date:I-0080a}}), and at his vigil, the Children of the Question say, it told him that the power of the coals would be his alone, that his children would have a little of it and their children less, until it faded out of his line, and that when strangers held the island the fire would wake again in it. Once more, and in secret, it came in the Age of Strangers. The camps tell that it stood in the draught of a deep gallery at Achadh dhomhain before Donnchadh mac Thormoid, a hewer of Ailean's blood by the line of his son Somhairle, and bade him breathe; and what went into him with the dust the Mission's surgeon took for the lung-rot. It was a gift, *Tìodhlac a' Choimhdich*, given in the dust of the deep places to the kin who went down, so that a people with its hill dug out from under it should have something of its own to stand against the strangers with; for the Keeper does not fight for the kin. It woke in Donnchadh's daughter Aisling ({{date:IV-0146}}), the first of the *fuil-ghuail*, and the camps hold that the coal-blood children after her came of it. No order keeps this as doctrine. The Rite, which holds the vein itself holy and no spirit in it, neither affirms the gift nor denies it (section VIII).

### IV. The Four Feasts and the Otherworld

**The feasts.** Four feasts divide the year into quarters (Appendix D). They are older than any order now on the island, and all three faiths keep them in some fashion, each for its own reasons. The Old Faith keeps them as the island kept them before the Crossing, by the moon, each on the first night of its moon; the kingdom and the Church keep them on the first day of the civil month, and the two may fall as much as a fortnight apart.

- **Samhain**, the first night of the moon Samhnas and 1 an t-Samhain in the civil calendar, is summer's end, and was the Dia-thìrich new year before the Crossing, when the Otherworld stands open and the dead are nearest. The Old Faith names Donn and the dead of the year. In the Holy Age the Hall filled the baskets of the Small-Burning Law at Samhain and the galleries closed.
- **Là Fhèill Brìde**, or Imbolc, the first night of Gearras and 1 an Gearran, is Brìde's feast. Hearths are swept and relit, and Òrd Bhrìde lights its first lamp of the year from the altar-flame.
- **Bealltainn**, the first night of Bealltach and 1 an Cèitean, is summer's beginning, the feast of the bright fire, when the cattle go up to the high pastures between two fires. The first sentence under the Small-Burning Law was given on Bealltainn itself ({{date:II-0028}}).
- **Lùnastal**, the first night of Lùghnas and 1 an Lùnastal, is Lugh's feast of first-fruits and gathering.

The Old Spirits keep the same four nights at the hearth and at the mounds, counting to them by nights as the old reckoning did, and the Church keeps the civil days as saints' days and the feasts of the Church's year.

**The Otherworld.** The Old Faith holds that the Otherworld lies west over the open sea, past the sight of any headland. Its tellings give it two names: *Tìr nan Òg*, the land of the young, where no one ages, and *Magh Meall*, the plain of delight. On the way to it, on its rock in the south-western sea, stands *Taigh Dhuinn*, the house of Donn, where the dead gather. The fleet of the Sundering went out north-about into Manannan's mist on the eastern sea ({{date:III-0023}}), not west, but the tellers say it went to find the Otherworld all the same, and the saying at the empty-harbour rite, *they went, and they did not go*, is read in the light of it. When the humans came in from the same quarter of sea, old sailors at Cuan shean said so aloud, and the council forbade the remark ({{date:IV-0006}}).

**The sìth.** Nearer home the Otherworld opens in the hills. The *sìth*, the mounds, belong to the *sìthichean*, the people of the mounds, whom the Old Spirits honour at the hearth and the Old Faith leaves alone.

### V. An Creideamh Sean and Its Orders

The Old Faith is the faith of the gods, and it is kept in its orders. Each order has its houses, temples and custodians and its own history, and some have had schisms of their own. All of them honour an Dagda and keep the four feasts. They differ on the vein, on the measure and on which god stands nearest to it, and they have differed since the Holy Age. The druids of every order are trained in the schools or in the order's own houses. No single council speaks for the Old Faith as a whole.

#### Òrd Bhrìde

Òrd Bhrìde, the order of Brìde's flame, is the Hall of the Holy Age and the Coal Rite of the chronicle. It keeps Brìde's flame, bound at Dùn ìseal, and Crom's stone, the vein. In its settled form it is a teaching set in order within the Old Faith, where before there was only the scattered reverence of the hearths, and it is built about the leyline and the unbroken line of the coal-touched ({{date:V-0028}}). As an ordered body it is the youngest of the orders; as a rite it claims to be the oldest, and the claim is just. Its seat is at the southern {{place:burg:431}}, where its largest house stands ({{date:V-0029}}). Its saying is the Law's own:

> *Làn an dà bhois, agus gun tuilleadh.*
> The fill of the two palms, and no more.

It has nine towns, all in the south-east, and holds four shires: {{place:province:4}}, {{place:province:63}}, {{place:province:116}} and {{place:province:80}}. It has a third of {{place:province:90}}, and the Old Spirits hold the land on every side. The largest towns are Seann Skell, {{place:burg:398}}, {{place:burg:326}}, {{place:burg:15}} and {{place:burg:93}}. The map marks five of its houses as temples: Seann Skell, Tobar dhearg, Muileann naomh, {{place:burg:299}} on the coast road and {{place:burg:205}}, the pilgrims' landing. Muileann naomh was the southernmost house of the Hawk until it took the order of worship of Òrd Bhrìde ({{date:V-0030a}}). The order's history is given in section VIII.

#### Òrd an t-Seabhaig

**What it holds.** The Hawk order is the older form of Brìde's rite on the north coast. It teaches that the hawk of the vein is Mòd, the Hawk of Acaill, who is as old as Fionntan mac Bòchra and remembers the ages with him, and who must be fed the measured handful and no more, "for a hawk overfed will not hunt and a hawk starved will turn on the hand" ({{date:III-0062}}). Too much ruins the hawk, and so does too little; the measure lies between. The Hawk holds, moreover, that the measure is the hawk's to keep, and no custodian's.

**Rites and sayings.** Each household feeds its hawk before it eats, and the handful is burned at dawn with the hawk on the wrist. The fowlers who began the rite gave its first saying ({{date:II-0158}}):

> *Is acras an lasair.*
> The flame is a hunger.

The sayings of Gormshuil nic Artair, who gathered the rite into its first house, were written down after her death as Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, the Book of the Hawk. The copyist marked every saying he had not heard from her own mouth, and the marked outnumbered the rest ({{date:III-0065}}). The saying most often cut over hawk-house doors is:

> *Cha shealg seabhag sàthach.*
> A sated hawk will not hunt.

**Holy places.** The oldest hawk carved on the island is over a door at Àth mhòr ({{date:I-0131}}), and it is older than any rite. The rite itself began at the Seann Skell of the northern coast ({{place:burg:132}}). The first house of the order is at {{place:burg:20}}.

**History.** In the Holy Age the dawn-hawk spread along the north coast, and while it spread the Keeper wrote that it was no concern of the Hall's ({{date:II-0159}}). In time it was the first rite to refuse the Hall's measure-check outright ({{date:II-0216}}). The rite was old already when Gormshuil nic Artair gathered it at Cnoc bheag into its first house ({{date:III-0062}}), and from that day it was an order. A second house followed at Ros gheal ({{date:III-0063}}) and a southern one at Muileann naomh ({{date:III-0101}}), which is now a house of Òrd Bhrìde. The schism came when a council at Cnoc bheag split over whether Mòd's measure could ever be raised ({{date:III-0140}}). Those who called it fixed kept the name of the Hungry Hawk. The Fed Hawk were turned out to the northern Seann Skell ({{date:III-0142}}), and their petition to raise the handful by a quarter was the first recorded request to alter the Small-Burning Law ({{date:III-0143}}). It was refused, and after that the Fed Hawk are not heard of as a house of their own.

Under the humans the Hawk resisted before there was any ordered resistance for it to join. Murchadh Seabhag preached that Mòd starves those who eat faster than the land can feed them ({{date:IV-0066}}), and his sermon was the first Dia-thìrich text the Company's agents were told to confiscate. When hawk-priests were barred from the camps ({{date:IV-0095}}), the crews carried the sermons in themselves ({{date:IV-0162}}). Ìomhar Seabhag's shrines served as meeting houses for the Lamp-Watch ({{date:IV-0284}}), and when the war came he called it Mòd's own hunger turned back on those who fed it ({{date:IV-0348}}). In the Age of the Kingdom the Hawk houses refused the written order of worship of Òrd Bhrìde ({{date:V-0030}}); only the far southern house at Muileann naomh took it ({{date:V-0030a}}). The others named their own custodian at Inis ìseal ({{date:V-0131}}). They then wrote down their order, which until that day had been spoken and never written, and they keep the book locked away ({{date:V-0132}}). They accepted a place under the high custodian only on condition that they kept that order ({{date:V-0166}}). The quarrel with Òrd Bhrìde has grown quiet, but it is not ended.

**Where it is held now.** Fourteen towns, all Dia-thìrich, along the north-central coast and up the Abhainn ìseal. It holds {{place:province:9}} and {{place:province:45}}. It is the greatest order, though with less than half the land, in {{place:province:6}}, where Manannan's order and Macha's hold the rest, and in the northern {{place:province:51}}, which it shares with the Old Spirits and Manannan's order. It also has a quarter of the northern {{place:province:113}}. Cnoc bheag is the largest, and its Hawk house is the richest house of any order outside the capital ({{date:V-0161}}). After it come {{place:burg:371}}, {{place:burg:24}}, the northern Seann Skell and {{place:burg:161}}. The map marks eight of its houses as temples: Cnoc bheag, Inis bhàn, Ros gheal and the northern Seann Skell; Àth shean on the northern march ({{place:burg:368}}); the Hawk mill of {{place:burg:274}} and its field village {{place:burg:336}}; and {{place:burg:25}}, where the Hawk's own custodian sits.

#### Scoiltean nan Draoidhean

**What it holds.** The druid schools are the reasoning order of the Old Faith, and they call themselves by their oldest name, Feallsanachd an Fhèidh, the Philosophy of the Deer. They are schools more than a church. They gathered about one question, why the vein answers some hands and not others ({{date:II-0021}}), and they were the first within the Old Faith who reasoned about the coal as well as revering it. They do not deny that the vein is holy. They ask what its holiness does. Their patron is Flidais, lady of the deer and the wild, whose white stag walks in their sacred wood. In none of the schools' records kept in the Library is the stag, Camaran, worshipped, and his name is first found in writings much younger than the first schools ({{date:II-0023}}). The schools are counted among the orders of the Old Faith, and they have not objected.

**Rites and sayings.** Their one public observance is the yearly walking of the bounds of Flidais's wood ({{date:III-0092}}). All else they do is argument. Their oldest question, as the masters put it to beginners, is:

> *Carson an làmh seo?*
> Why this hand?

A student who thinks he has solved it is given Aonghas mac Coinnich's answer ({{date:IV-0114}}):

> *Chan e sin a' cheist.*
> That is not the question.

**Holy places.** {{place:marker:23}}, which was held sacred long before any order of the Old Faith laid claim to it ({{date:I-0048}}), and where the white stag was first seen ({{date:I-0050}}). The wood lies outside the schools' own country, among Old Spirits towns in the shire of {{place:province:58}}, and the people of {{place:burg:456}} and {{place:burg:380}} on its edge, who keep the ancestors, share its keeping. {{place:burg:77}} has the first stone temple of the stag's people ({{date:II-0233}}). {{place:burg:197}} is the schools' house in the Age of the Kingdom.

**Where it is held now.** 133 towns, all Dia-thìrich, in the north-east and the eastern hills; the east coast itself belongs to the Old Spirits. The schools stand first in twenty-three shires, a block running from {{place:province:56}} and {{place:province:115}} in the north through {{place:province:8}}, {{place:province:20}} and {{place:province:16}} to {{place:province:48}}, {{place:province:112}} and {{place:province:96}} in the hills, and west to {{place:province:111}} in the middle of the island. They share ten more, most of them with the Old Spirits along the line where the two faiths meet, and they have nearly half of {{place:province:110}}, {{place:province:35}} and {{place:province:102}}. The largest towns are {{place:burg:475}}, {{place:burg:131}}, Dùn thais, Caol shean and {{place:burg:443}}. The map marks sixteen of the schools' houses as temples, though the schools call them halls: among them Dùn thais, Caol shean, Caol bheag, {{place:burg:76}} and the church villages whose chapels served the silver-miners and the old bridge. {{place:burg:246}}, the Library town, is a school town. The schools' history is given in section IX.

#### Òrd Mhacha

**What it holds.** Macha's order keeps "two horses, one yoke, light and dark two powers in balance, and the vein belonging to both" ({{date:III-0089}}). The wood-watchers held that their black beast has two natures, one that keeps and one that devours, and that the vein has the same two ({{date:II-0220}}); the order tells it of the two horses that drew one chariot, the black Dubh Sainglenn and the grey Liath Macha. It is the order of the capital and the court, and the kings of Dia-thìr are made by its rite. Its oldest saying is the wood-watchers':

> *Tha dà nàdar aig a' bhèist, agus aig an fhèith.*
> The beast has two natures, and so has the vein.

Its own saying is of the pair:

> *Dà each, aon chuing.*
> Two horses, one yoke.

**Rites and holy places.** Its priests read omens for rulers and bury the dead by their own rite. King Aonghas mac Uisdein was the first king buried that way ({{date:III-0096}}). Its floors are laid in black and white, and the floor of its great house at {{place:burg:23}} took eleven years ({{date:III-0198}}). Its sacred wood is {{place:marker:24}}, near the lesser Cnoc bheag south of the capital ({{place:burg:504}}). That town is not the river port of the Hawk that bears the same name.

**History.** The order began as a watch. A small company settled at the edge of a wood no one had ever cleared and watched for a black horned beast that walked in it ({{date:II-0114}}), the beast the order takes for a sign of Dubh Sainglenn, the black horse. Its quarrel with the stag's people over both sacred woods became the holy-wood dispute, which was settled by dividing the keeping of the woods, and no side was named the winner ({{date:II-0117}}). Anna Ruadh took a wood-watcher into her household at Cathair dhearg to read the omens ({{date:II-0210}}). Cathair dhomhain refused one, and from that day the rivalry of the two towns has been a matter of faith as well ({{date:II-0211}}). Cathair dhomhain is a town of Macha's order now, with its own temple. The order took its settled form when King Aonghas took up the wood-watchers' rite of Macha ({{date:III-0089}}). By royal grant its priests then had sole keeping of the western wood, and the other claimants withdrew, for reasons that are not told ({{date:III-0091}}).

Under the humans it split over the Residency's coin. The priests at Cathair dhearg blessed it ({{date:IV-0175}}). Those who refused went to the sacred forest, where they received custodians who had lost their seats ({{date:IV-0179}}). Sixty years later the capital's temple sheltered the council that Strake had locked out ({{date:IV-0315}}). In the forest the dissenters sat with custodians and the Lamp-Watch when the council of the mining regions was formed ({{date:IV-0322}}). In the restored kingdom the house at Cathair dhearg got back the keeping of the regalia and refused Òrd Bhrìde a share of it ({{date:V-0033}}).

**Where it is held now.** 103 towns, all Dia-thìrich, across the middle and south of the island. It stands first in twenty-nine shires in one block, from the capital's own shire ({{place:province:1}}) and its neighbours in the middle of the island south to {{place:province:5}}, {{place:province:49}} and {{place:province:19}}, and east to {{place:province:24}} and {{place:province:31}}. Manannan's order holds more than a third of {{place:province:32}}, the shire of the capital's old rival, and in {{place:province:22}} the Stone holds most of the land, though the harbour town is Macha's. The largest towns are {{place:burg:27}}, Cathair mhòr, {{place:burg:67}}, {{place:burg:36}} and {{place:burg:45}}. The map marks seventeen of its temples. Five are the great houses of Cathair mhòr, {{place:burg:26}}, {{place:burg:19}}, {{place:burg:22}} and {{place:burg:104}}; the others are the priests' towns, such as {{place:burg:231}} and {{place:burg:183}}, where acolytes are trained for the capital's temples, and the chapels along the roads to the capital. Cnoc naomh, whose people once kept the old reverence "with no hawk, wolf, sting or stag in it" ({{date:II-0246}}), is a town of Macha's order on the map.

#### Òrd na Cloiche

**What it holds.** The order of the Stone holds that the Stone watches the whole island as a guard watches a road, and will cry out under the rightful king ({{date:II-0217}}). The Stone is Lia Fàil, and its sworn guards are the Fianna, "who keep the island as a wolf keeps its range" ({{date:III-0086}}). The Stone's plain name is Clach, the common word for *stone*. The first stone was a white stone carved as a wolf at the gate of Caol leathan, on which the road-guards swore their watch, and later ages read more into it than the road-guards ever did ({{date:II-0100}}). The tales of Fionn and his Fianna are the order's own. The Stone is not known ever to have claimed the vein. When Òrd Bhrìde was set in order, the houses of the Stone sent no witnesses, only "a letter wishing it well" ({{date:V-0031}}).

**Rites and sayings.** Oaths are taken before the Stone. The houses of the Stone nurse the sick. When a spotted fever came to Inis chrom the Fianna nursed the sick and many of them died of it, and thereafter the town was the Stone's ({{date:III-0118}}). Its saying comes from the road-guards:

> *Mar a ghleidheas am freiceadan an rathad, gleidhidh Clach an t-eilean.*
> As the guard keeps the road, Clach keeps the island.

**Holy places.** {{place:burg:1}}, whose house of the Stone is the largest of the order and rings a bell for the hours ({{date:V-0087}}); {{place:burg:166}}, where a second stone of white quartz was dragged from the mountain ({{date:II-0161}}) and the first temple to Lia Fàil by name was built ({{date:III-0087}}); and {{place:burg:232}}.

**History.** The wolf-sworn were soldiers before they were an order. The order took its form when Dòmhnall mac Ailein, a net-maker of Inis thais, preached that their stone was Lia Fàil and its guards the Fianna ({{date:III-0086}}). Its schism was over the dead. Those who would not pray to the Stone for the dead kept the temple at Inis thais, and those who would built a house at Baile gheal ({{date:III-0191}}, {{date:III-0193}}). Of any reconciling of the two houses nothing is told. In the Age of the Kingdom the houses of the Stone have served as banks as well as churches. They issued chits on house paper when coin ran short ({{date:V-0027}}) and lent the money for Doire shean's harbour ({{date:V-0119}}). They also published a count of their own people that matched the kingdom's to within a hundred ({{date:V-0219}}).

**Where it is held now.** 21 towns, all Dia-thìrich, in the south-west. It holds six shires outright, {{place:province:10}}, {{place:province:17}}, {{place:province:25}}, {{place:province:72}}, {{place:province:78}} and {{place:province:108}}, and most of {{place:province:2}} and {{place:province:22}}. In {{place:province:15}}, the shire of its chief shrine, Manannan's order holds three-fifths of the land. The Stone also has a share of {{place:province:100}}, {{place:province:52}} and {{place:province:95}}, where Seann Warr is a town of the Stone in Manannan's country. The largest are {{place:burg:324}}, Inis thais, {{place:burg:92}}, {{place:burg:170}} and {{place:burg:64}}. The map marks temples in eight of its towns: Inis thais, still the chief shrine of the order, Caol leathan, Baile gheal, Doire shean, Inis chrom, {{place:burg:191}}, {{place:burg:204}} and {{place:burg:433}}, which holds the oldest shrines of the Stone.

#### Òrd Mhanannain

**What it holds.** Manannan's order holds that the lord of the sea sees the liar wherever he is, and that his Cup of Truth breaks at the lies spoken over it and is whole again at the truth; the yellow scorpion is the old sign on the Cup, and the order's badge. It was the first teaching on the island that called itself the only true one ({{date:II-0218}}). Its central teaching is about the sea: Manannan shut the eastern sea behind the fleet of the Sundering with his mist, which hid the island from every ship beyond it, and it must not be tried again ({{date:III-0120}}). The order keeps the sea shut by prayer. Its priests recorded the mist thinning in the last years of the Age of Sundering ({{date:III-0239a}}), and it lifted in the winter the humans' ships came out of it ({{date:IV-0001a}}). They held the Crossing to be the price of a prayer grown slack. When no ship came from an Tìr Thall after the Severance, and the council's ship found only open water, they said the lord of the sea had shut the sea again ({{date:V-0011}}).

**Rites and sayings.** Oaths of trade were first sworn on a scorpion's sting kept in a box ({{date:II-0086}}). Since the sting-oath became a creed they have been sworn over a cup marked with the scorpion, Manannan's Cup of Truth. No boat of the faithful may go out of sight of the headlands, and on the west coast steering by the stars was lost entirely ({{date:III-0123}}). At the harbour mouth the priests pray ({{date:III-0212}}):

> *A Mhanannain, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Manannan, keep the sea shut.

Since {{year:III-0223}} that prayer has been said on the day of the empty-harbour rite. So on one quay, on one day, some mourn the departed and some pray that no one follows them. Neither side is pleased.

**Holy places.** Seann Skell in the west ({{place:burg:489}}), where the teaching was declared; {{place:burg:420}}, where the sting-oath began; and {{place:burg:127}}, which has the first scorpion carved in yellow stone for worship ({{date:II-0219}}).

**History.** The sting-oath went from Baile àrsaidh up the coast to Ros bheag ({{date:II-0160}}) and became a creed at Seann Skell. The order took its form when Mànas mac Ruairidh named its power Manannan mac Lir ({{date:III-0120}}). A worked timber longer than any tree on the island, held with nails no smith on Dia-thìr could place, came ashore at Ceann mhòr. It was carried to Seann Skell, shown to Manannan's priests, and burned ({{date:III-0236}}). This was twenty-five years before the humans came. The first human fishing families on the north-west coast learned the order's harbour prayers at Ceann mhòr ({{date:IV-0118}}), and its priests there say the Grey Night grew from them (section VII). In the Age of the Kingdom the house at Seann Bhral turned away the Tuathaich of the north who came for its feast of the sea, and the northerners made a feast of their own at Cnoc ghorm ({{date:V-0103}}, {{date:V-0104}}); that quarrel is told with the Church. On the eve of the Long War Manannan's house at Seann Skell refused to bless the enforcers' depot ({{date:VI-0066}}). In the present year its priests said the prayer to keep the sea shut on the day the first dubhan was shipped off the island under Dubhan's charter ({{date:VI-0154b}}).

**Where it is held now.** 35 towns, all Dia-thìrich, on the west coast. Its seat, the western Seann Skell, is the largest town on Dia-thìr. It stands first in eight shires in the west, from {{place:province:117}} and {{place:province:73}} south through {{place:province:3}}, {{place:province:77}} and {{place:province:64}} to {{place:province:38}} and the Stone's holy shire of {{place:province:15}}, with {{place:province:95}} inland. It also has more than a third of {{place:province:6}} and {{place:province:32}} in the middle of the island and a quarter of {{place:province:2}}. The map marks eleven of its houses as temples; besides the holy places above they include {{place:burg:63}} and the western {{place:burg:47}}. After Seann Skell its largest towns are {{place:burg:282}} and {{place:burg:134}}.

### VI. Na Seann Spioradan

**What it holds.** The Old Spirits keep the reverence of the hearths as it was before the Hall: "reverence for the ancestors, for the vein, for the weather that rules the mining season" ({{date:II-0005}}). The chronicle calls it the oldest custom on the island that has never been broken. It has no priests, no order of worship and no council. Its powers, as the map gives them, are na Sìthichean, the people of the mounds, and its tellings speak of the ancestors, the sìth of the hills and mounds, and the spirits of weather, wells and streams, not of any one power over them. The Telling of the Making says that Caoran gave a spirit to every spring and hill when the island was born; the hearths of the Old Spirits do not deny it, and do not name him at their rites. The gods belong to the Old Faith. When the hearths first gave the powers of the mounds that name, or who first gave it, is not known.

**Rites and sayings.** The living heads of families name their ancestors aloud. The names of the dead are cut on wooden posts, which are recut each generation as the old ones rot ({{date:III-0056}}). The faith keeps the walk to Allt an Àigh as its own, and after the Keepers declined the stream it took Muireach Liath's ruling for its saying:

> *Buinidh an t-allt do na sinnsearan agus don t-sìde.*
> The stream belongs to the ancestors and to the weather.

The old night-count is kept at its hearths: a journey, a mourning or a guest's stay is reckoned in nights, and the dead are waked for three nights (Appendix D). It blesses what comes from the vein, and nothing else. When copper was found behind Àth fhiadhaich, the town's Seann Spioradan house held no blessing for it ({{date:V-0205}}).

**Holy places.** {{place:burg:315}} and its spirit-house; {{place:burg:18}}, where the east-coast houses built a common hall for naming the ancestors of forty families ({{date:V-0094}}); Allt an Àigh, which lies in the schools' country behind Caol shean, with the walkers' hall at Achadh gharbh, a school village ({{date:V-0049}}); and Làrach an Teampaill, which at the houses' request was entered by the Library as "measured and let be" ({{date:V-0121}}).

**History.** By the end of the Holy Age the Hall's list counted the old folk as "a company of its own" ({{date:II-0246}}). In the Age of Sundering they built a guest-house at the stream, and its book records pilgrims from every shire ({{date:III-0175}}). Under the humans this was the first faith to refuse the Company's coal on grounds of belief. The households of Achadh naomh burned peat, and their elders taught "that coal burned for wages is burned without the ancestors' leave" ({{date:IV-0067}}). In the Long Drought the Administration's watchman at Allt an Àigh, posted to charge pilgrims for the water, was recalled after one season ({{date:IV-0222}}). In the Age of the Kingdom the custodians of Òrd Bhrìde discouraged the walk and did not forbid it ({{date:V-0048}}).

**Where it is held now.** 126 towns, all Dia-thìrich, and more land than any single order of the Old Faith. It holds thirty-four shires: the whole south-east, from {{place:province:13}} and {{place:province:12}} to {{place:province:43}} and {{place:province:109}}; the east coast from {{place:province:101}} north through {{place:province:60}} and {{place:province:61}} to {{place:province:69}}; the hills round Dùn ìseal, where the schools hold nearly as much ({{place:province:35}}, {{place:province:110}}, {{place:province:87}}); and a band of the north coast between the Hawk and the schools, from {{place:province:27}} and {{place:province:83}} to {{place:province:68}} and {{place:province:92}}. It has a share of eleven more, among them {{place:province:103}}, which it divides almost evenly with the schools. The largest are {{place:burg:110}}, {{place:burg:395}}, {{place:burg:365}}, {{place:burg:502}}, {{place:burg:145}} and {{place:burg:422}}. The map marks temples in twenty-four of its towns, more than for any single order. The chief are Dùn ìseal, Seann Dunn, {{place:burg:34}}, whose shrine draws the whole north-east at the great feasts, and {{place:burg:192}}, whose elders the shrine-keepers of the north answer to. The rest are the shrines of wells, springs, fords and holy woods, and the houses of the largest towns.

### VII. An Eaglais

**What it holds.** The Church is the faith the humans brought over the sea at the Crossing ({{date:IV-0001}}). It holds that there is one God, *Dia*, and that Crìosd, Christ, is his son. Its book is *am Bìoball*, the Bible. Its clergy are *sagairt* and *ministearan*, priests and ministers, under *easbaigean*, bishops; it has had *manaich*, monks, and it keeps the days of its *naoimh*, its saints. On Dia-thìr it is the faith of the human labourers and the Residency in the Age of Strangers, and of the Tuathaich, their descendants, since. It brought the week with it, and keeps it: its day of rest is Didòmhnaich, the Lord's day, and its fasts from meat are on Diciadain and Dihaoine, which take their names from them. Its feasts fall on their days in the civil calendar, and it has never kept the moon (Appendix D).

**The Mission.** The Church came to the Dia-thìrich as the Mission. It raised a timber chapel on the quay at Ros dhomhain, the first church on the island ({{date:IV-0044}}), and ran a school there that taught Dia-thìreach children in the humans' tongue ({{date:IV-0121}}). Samuel Wren made his grammar of Dia-thìris for its use ({{date:IV-0042}}), and the hawk-priests of Cnoc bheag asked it for a copy ({{date:IV-0043}}). The Mission kept lists of the dead in the camps, and the custodians wrote their own count in its margins ({{date:IV-0096}}). Samuel Wren asked the masters of the schools why the vein should be holy, and they asked him why his fuel should be burned ({{date:IV-0056}}). The Mission's house in the north was at {{place:burg:476}}.

**The churches of the north.** Most Tuathaich towns keep the Church their forebears brought at the Crossing, in the line the Mission left, while the Dia-thìrich of the west coast beside them keep Manannan's order ({{date:V-0044}}). How the churches of the Mission's line came to outnumber the older congregations of the north coast is not told. Their largest house is at {{place:burg:142}}. It opened a school for its priests that taught in the humans' tongue and read the Bible in the Dia-thìris text its preachers use on the west coast; it is the only place in the north where both are taught ({{date:V-0045}}). In the early Age of the Kingdom the northerners came south to the feast of the sea at Seann Bhral, as their forebears had learned to from the Dia-thìrich harbours. The house of Manannan's order there turned them away, and its keeper said "the sea is the same and the house is not" ({{date:V-0103}}). They kept the feast at the church at Cnoc ghorm instead, as a blessing of the boats, and within a generation it was the larger of the two ({{date:V-0104}}).

These churches hold 51 towns. They hold seven shires: in the north-west {{place:province:36}}, {{place:province:14}}, {{place:province:75}}, {{place:province:105}} and {{place:province:107}}, and on the north coast {{place:province:7}} and {{place:province:86}}. The map marks eight of their houses as temples, among them Cnoc ghorm and Cathair gheal. Their largest towns are {{place:burg:95}}, the Moot's town, {{place:burg:143}} and Cnoc ghorm.

**Eaglais nan Tuathach and the voyage of Naomh Breandan.** The old congregations of the north coast keep the Church in their own way ({{date:V-0042}}), and among their ways, quietly kept, is the telling of the First Canoe, the voyage of Naomh Breandan to the Isle of the Blessed, which the Dia-thìrich priests still call superstition. Beyond this almost nothing is known of them, and what is known comes from outside. Few Tuathaich would speak to the chronicle clerks ({{date:V-0080}}); the north's books stayed in private keeping ({{date:V-0227}}); and the request that Tuathaich memory be kept in the Library was laid on the table, neither granted nor refused, and it lies there still ({{date:VI-0059}}). Their rites and their sayings are not known here, nor how far they differ from the churches of the Mission's line. Of their holy places three are known, the houses the map marks as temples: Doire ghlas, called the heart of the Church in the north, whose keepers are said to hold the First Canoe fragment and have not let the Library copy it; Muileann leathan; and {{place:burg:468}}, a Dia-thìrich chapel village whose chapel the Tuathaich made a church, leaving the old carvings on its doorposts beside their own.

The only one of their tellings known outside the north is the fragment its tellers set in the Ancient Age: "a steersman with a name, and a first crossing" ({{date:I-0038}}). The steersman is Naomh Breandan, Saint Brendan the Navigator of the humans' Church, who sailed west from an Tìr Thall in a boat of hides to the Isle of the Blessed, and the north's tellers say that isle was Dia-thìr. His name is in no Dia-thìrich record. In his voyage there is a mist on the sea and an island of the blessed in the west, as there is in the Dia-thìrich tellings of Manannan's sea; the Library notes the likeness and draws nothing from it. How families whose forebears came over the water in {{year:IV-0001}} came to keep the telling of a crossing set so long before the Sundering, no one has explained. It is left here where its tellers set it; the north keeps it still in the present day ({{date:VI-0157}}).

The old congregations hold twelve towns, all Tuathaich, along the north. They hold three shires, {{place:province:23}}, {{place:province:88}} and the island shire of {{place:province:122}} off the coast to the west of them, and have a share of six more, among them the land west of Baile chrom on the north-western cape, a quarter of {{place:province:86}} and a sixth of {{place:province:91}} and {{place:province:7}}. The largest are {{place:burg:209}}, {{place:burg:234}}, {{place:burg:9}} and Baile ghlas in Siorrachd Àth àrsaidh ({{place:burg:235}}).

**Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise.** Of the Mystery of the Grey Night four things are known: its name, its goose, one custom and one town. The goose is Sìne, an Gèadh Sìtheil, the Peaceful Goose, the keepers' name for an Gèadh Fiadhaich, the wild goose of the Holy Spirit. The custom is "one night of silence in the year", a vigil, and outsiders are not told which night it is ({{date:V-0043}}). No saying is known.

The vigil first appears in a Mission report that human labourers near Baile chrom were keeping a vigil at night in the fields, praying in silence under the sign of a grey goose, which they called the wild goose of the Spirit ({{date:IV-0262}}). The Mission called it irregular, a superstition learned from the Dia-thìrich, and the Dia-thìrich of Baile chrom disowned it. Manannan's priests at Ceann mhòr said the Mission was half right. The first human fishing families on that coast had settled at Ceann mhòr ({{date:IV-0118}}) and learned Manannan's night prayer to keep the sea shut there, and the labourers inland kept it as a Christian vigil, a night of silence under the wild goose of the Spirit instead of a prayer to Manannan. The priests called it Manannan's prayer gone astray and would not have its keepers in their house ({{date:IV-0262a}}). The Church's bishops call the vigil irregular and have not put it down. From the Ancient Age there is a telling of the grey geese that winter on Eilean ghorm and are never hunted there ({{date:I-0055}}); no one has shown that the two are joined, and it is not claimed here. After the Severance the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane held the vigil openly ({{date:V-0043}}). A magistrate fined her, and the vigil moved indoors ({{date:V-0108}}). The keeper of {{place:marker:12}} reported that the vigil was kept in the lighthouse's shadow ({{date:V-0123}}). In the Long War soldiers searched the town on a night when it lay wholly dark and silent, and found nothing ({{date:VI-0108}}).

The vigil holds one town, Baile chrom in the far north-west ({{place:burg:305}}), and with it the whole of its shire ({{place:province:28}}) and a tenth of {{place:province:14}} next door. It is not the Baile chrom in the hills ({{place:burg:187}}), and the rolls take care to keep the two apart ({{date:IV-0155}}).

**Where the Church is held now.** 64 towns, every Tuathaich town on the map, and eleven shires, all in the north and north-west. It has a share of five more: a quarter of {{place:province:3}} and of {{place:province:120}}, a sixth of {{place:province:91}}, and smaller parts of {{place:province:73}} and {{place:province:117}}. The map marks twelve of its churches as temples.

### VIII. Of the Coal Rite

The Rite is older than any name it has borne. Its root is the slab over the crack and the handful in the cup. What the chronicle calls the Coal Rite, and the Old Faith calls Òrd Bhrìde, is the part of the old reverence that holds the vein itself to be sacred. The vein is Crom's, and Brìde's flame is lit from what it gives; yet the Rite does not hold it to be the token of a god or the dwelling of a spirit. It is itself the holy thing.

**The vein as sacred.** The Rite holds that the vein bleeds because it is alive, in some fashion the Dia-thìrich are not asked to understand. What is broken from it must be burned in measure or returned. The vein answers a line of people, and that answering is itself holy. The doctrine of {{year:V-0028}} is "built upon the leyline and the unbroken line of the coal-touched" ({{date:V-0028}}): the vein under the ground and the blood of those it has answered. Fionnghal nic Artair's defence of the Small-Burning Law, the only one that survives, argued that "the Law was never for the coal's sake but for the people, who would become whatever the burning made them" ({{date:IV-0065}}). She wrote it two generations before Aisling was born, and it has been read as prophecy since; she made no such claim herself. Since the copyists found Aisling's line ({{date:IV-0251a}}), some of the Rite have read "the unbroken line" as the line that runs from the first king to her; the doctrine does not say which line it means.

**Under the Keepers.** In the Holy Age the Rite belonged to the Hall. The Keepers held the cup, took the census of hands ({{date:II-0142}}) and sentenced households that burned beyond the measure ({{date:II-0028}}). Their roll holds one hundred and forty names ({{date:II-0248}}).

**Under the vein-house.** Each ship of the Sundering carried one sealed measure of coal and no more ({{date:III-0016}}). The vein-house above Muileann dhearg kept the custody-book, and in {{year:III-0138}} the crown and the custody were joined. The Rite's worst hour in that age came in war. Raghnall Ruadh, holding the vein-house, refused the handful to every hearth in the shires that stood against him, "and this deed turns more against him than any battle" ({{date:III-0109}}). The council refused the Fed Hawk's petition, and refused Clann Choinnich leave to smelt silver with the coal ({{date:III-0208}}), which was the first time any asked to burn the coal for work and not for worship.

**Under the humans.** The Small-Burning Law was never repealed; it lapsed when it no longer paid anyone to enforce it ({{date:IV-0063}}). The Rite lived on in shrines at the pit mouths. At Cill dhìreach the crews left the first piece of coal from each new gallery, and the Company allowed it, "for it costs a stone a gallery" ({{date:IV-0150}}). At Cill ghlas the custodians kept the rites at the mouth of the oldest cutting ({{date:IV-0151}}). They agreed the word *fuil-ghuail* there ({{date:IV-0251}}), and the Rending swallowed that shrine ({{date:IV-0271}}).

**The Rite set in order.** The Rite was given its settled form on {{date:V-0028}}. Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was seated at the southern Seann Skell as Àrd-choimheadaiche, and his first charge was a written order of worship: "there had never been one" ({{date:V-0029}}). The Hawk refused the order and the Stone stood aside. When the council kept the night shift, the custodians "enter a protest, and then bless the shafts" ({{date:V-0052}}). So the Rite has done ever since: it protests at the kingdom's use of the vein, and then it blesses it.

**The Depletion.** The high custodian ruled that dubhan was "not forbidden, since it is not coal, and not blessed, since it is not coal" ({{date:VI-0009}}). When the beacons were converted ({{date:VI-0014}}), the coal flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg was carried to Seann Skell and left to burn out on the altar ({{date:VI-0015}}). When the coinage seal lost its coal ink, the high custodian protested. The council replied that the kingdom had never said it held the vein sacred ({{date:VI-0051}}).

**The Rite now.** One face of the Sloc Mòr is kept open under the Rite's custody ({{date:VI-0144}}). Twelve hewers work it, the last of their trade ({{date:VI-0145}}). On {{date:VI-0155}} they cut the year's altar-coal. It filled four baskets, and the high custodian divided it among the houses by lot.

That is why Òrd Bhrìde holds only nine towns. Most Dia-thìrich who hold the vein sacred keep it at the hearth, as the Old Spirits do, or at the local altar, as the Hawk does. In name the Rite is the right faith of them all; in only nine towns is it the whole of their practice. It was set in order after the vein had begun to fail. In less than a century it went from an order of worship for the whole island to four baskets of coal.

### IX. Of the Schools' Question

> *Carson an làmh seo?*

The druid schools began with one question, asked at Muileann chiar in the Holy Age: why does the vein answer some hands and not others ({{date:II-0021}})? Some have made much of the nearness of *fèidh*, deer, to *fèith*, vein. The schools' own writings make nothing of it.

**Forbidden and allowed.** The Hall shut the galleries to the stag's people, "holding that a question asked of the vein is a demand made of it" ({{date:II-0024}}). They were later let back in on an oath "to ask nothing of the vein aloud" ({{date:II-0066}}). The Hall called it a reconciliation, and the schools a truce. Long after, the stag's people made the first Dia-thìrich letters out of the cutters' tally-notches ({{date:II-0088}}), the letters the schools call ogham, and nearly every later hand on the island descends from them.

**Birth or effort.** Apprentices kept strike-books, written accounts of every blow ({{date:II-0125}}). Reading them, Donnchadh mac Iain found that the coal bled brighter for older cutters, whatever house they came from ({{date:II-0162}}). There followed a parting of the schools. A school broke away holding that the vein answers to effort and not to birth ({{date:II-0164}}); and over that same question, in another shape and many centuries later, the Long War was fought. The breakaway school at Muileann chaol did not write a cutter's parentage in the book at all ({{date:II-0165}}). The Keeper Raonaid Dhubh heard both schools and gave no ruling, "saying that the vein has not asked the Hall to decide" ({{date:II-0166}}).

**The years of disputation.** At Dùn thais the question was argued by rule ({{date:III-0074}}). Eilidh Dhubh's tracts for the effort-school were read aloud at harvest gatherings across the north-east ({{date:III-0094}}). Then Raghnall mac Chaluim showed that the heads of the custodian houses lived a few years longer than their tenants, and asked whether the vein answers the hand that holds it ({{date:III-0171}}). For that the house was shut, and when it was opened again it was only on condition that the question of longer lives was not raised ({{date:III-0230}}). His table was made nearly seven hundred years before the first of the immortal generation was born.

**Under the humans.** Samuel Wren asked the masters why the vein should be holy, and they asked him why his fuel should be burned ({{date:IV-0056}}). Aonghas mac Coinnich asked why the humans cannot see the colour in broken coal, and said it was the wrong question ({{date:IV-0114}}). Tolley closed the hall at Dùn thais ({{date:IV-0186}}). The last public disputation asked whether a question may be forbidden, and the Administration's clerk recorded that it may ({{date:IV-0187}}). After that the masters taught under the trees ({{date:IV-0188}}), until the last of them died in {{year:IV-0247}}.

**The revival.** The schools reopened in {{year:V-0162}}, when the vein itself began to run short and their question was all at once pressing again. Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain lectured in the sacred wood ({{date:V-0163}}), and the schools took a house at Caol shean, where students debated the census note on the coal-touched openly ({{date:V-0165}}). When the Mine Board searched for a second vein, she wrote that "no one has asked why there is one vein" ({{date:V-0175}}). The Board filed her letter, and the search found nothing ({{date:V-0176}}). The schools' own word for their revival is *dubhan-tùsail* ({{date:VI-0036}}).

**What the question has come to.** In the Holy Age it asked whether the coal bled for birth or for practice. In the Age of Sundering it asked whether the vein lengthened lives. Under the humans it asked why the humans could not see the colour. Since {{year:IV-0250}}, when the custodians agreed a plain word for what the vein had begun to do to a few children, the answer has had a name but no reason. The masters do not say that their question brought on the Long War, and no one has laid it to their charge. They say only that it was asked in the Holy Age, and has not been answered.
