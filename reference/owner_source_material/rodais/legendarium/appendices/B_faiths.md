## Appendix B — The Faiths of Rodos

This appendix sets down what the record holds about the faiths of Rodos: the nine the map names, the older reverence they grew out of, and the two matters every one of them has had to answer, the holiness of the vein and the question of why it answers some hands and not others. It draws on the annals, the Keepers' roll of Dùn ìseal, the custody-book of the vein-house, the registers that the schools and houses sent to Leabharlann Muileann chaol, and the map. Where a faith kept its own counsel, and several did, this appendix says so and leaves the gap open.

Each faith is entered under the Ròdais name the map gives it, with its god as the map gives the god. Where the record shows when a name first appears, that is noted. Where it does not, nothing more is claimed.

### I. The Faiths in Brief

The map counts 505 burgs and sets each under one faith. The seat is the house the record treats as first among a faith's houses in the present age.

| Creideamh nan Ròdach | the Obsidian Rite | organised; pantheist | Dòrsair, Uilebheist nan Geataichean, the Monster of the Gates | 9 | Seann Skell (south coast) |

Seven of the nine are Ròdaich faiths. The Faith of the Tuathaich and the Grey Night belong to the Tuathaich of the north. The Yellow Scorpion belongs to both peoples: 35 of its burgs are Ròdaich towns of the west coast and 51 are Tuathaich towns of the north. Between them the three faiths of the north hold all 64 Tuathaich burgs.

Counted by burgs, the Deer is the most widespread faith and the Old Spirits come next. Counted by the people in their towns, the Old Spirits come first, at a little over one million two hundred thousand, with the Dark Unicorn close behind. The Obsidian Rite, which the kingdom treats as orthodox, holds nine towns. That is not a mistake in the count, and section XII explains it.

### II. The Old Reverence and Its Parting

The chronicle says plainly that the faiths were once one. On {{date:II-0221}} "a single unified folk practice begins visibly splitting into the distinct threads later ages will separately name." What that one practice was can be put together from the Ancient Age. No god is named anywhere in it.

**The slab and the handful.** A mason broke into a black vein that bled colour instead of dust ({{date:I-0001}}). His people did not dig on. They laid a slab over the break and cut their well a stone's throw away ({{date:I-0003}}). Families took turns sitting by the slab ({{date:I-0112}}) and later chose a keeper by lot ({{date:I-0151}}). The first was Oighrig nic Dhòmhnaill, who had a cup ground out of grey stone to the size of her cupped hand ({{date:I-0153}}), because by then no fire took more ore than one hand could hold ({{date:I-0110}}). When a keeper was found with a basket of ore in his house, it was neither burned nor kept. It was laid back under the slab: *the ore went home* ({{date:I-0156}}). By the end of the age every household of the hills burned its handful on one night of the dark of the year, so that the colour showed on every hillside at once ({{date:I-0248}}).

**The doctrine.** The oldest saying on the island is cut on the column near Cnoc ghorm ({{date:I-0073}}), and it was first spoken as a rule at the ford gathering ({{date:I-0168}}):

> *Cha do thàinig sinn; bha sinn ann.*
> We did not come; we were here.

No faith since has disowned it, though each reads it its own way.

**What the Old Ones left.** Làrach an Teampaill ({{place:marker:33}}) has its altar set square to the central mountains ({{date:I-0035}}), and every later temple on Rodos faces some other way. Its top stone was prised off and never found ({{date:I-0062}}). The chronicle calls it "a temple older than any recorded religion ... toward the vein, not the horizon" ({{date:I-0063}}). The Ròdaich walked round it and took no stone from it ({{date:I-0216}}). Whether its builders held the vein holy, or only knew where it lay, is not known.

**The Binding and the first parting.** The Holy Age opens with the Binding of the First Flame ({{date:II-0001}}). At Dùn ìseal Beathag Bhàn was chosen to hold the flame ({{date:II-0002}}), and Talla na Lasrach was raised over it with one door facing the vein and none facing the sea ({{date:II-0004}}). The custom of the handful hardened into the Small-Burning Law ({{date:II-0027}}). The Keepers also ruled on what belonged to the vein, and each ruling drew a line. When Muireach Liath held that the fortune-stream Allt an Àigh belonged "to the ancestors and the weather, not to the vein" ({{date:II-0013}}), the Hall's reverence and the older reverence of the hearths could be seen for the first time as two things.

By the end of the Holy Age the Hall could no longer keep one rite. Seumas Bàn's clerks listed every rite that burned the handful or claimed the vein ({{date:II-0215}}): the Hall's own, the ancestor-keeping of the old folk, the dawn-hawk of the north coast, the wood-watchers of the west, the wolf-sworn, the sting-oath, and both schools of the stag. The council then began to sit in companies by rite. Seumas Bàn complained that he kept the flame for eight peoples who each thought themselves its only keepers ({{date:II-0222}}). Those companies are the roots of seven of the nine faiths on the map. The faiths of the Tuathaich grew from other roots.

**What stayed common.** Pilgrims of every rite went on walking to Allt an Àigh ({{place:marker:1}}) ({{date:II-0223}}), and the sick of every rite were bathed at the springs of Muileann ruadh ({{place:marker:0}}) without being asked which rite they kept ({{date:II-0224}}). The Age of Sundering added two rites that belong to no single faith. One is the Mourning Custom for the departed, "not quite grief and not quite celebration" ({{date:III-0031}}). The other is the empty-harbour rite on Cidhe an Diosail at Seann Skell ({{date:III-0036}}). The harbour saying at that rite is in no order of worship:

> *Dh'fhalbh iad, agus cha do dh'fhalbh iad.*
> They went, and they did not go.

### III. Seann Spioradan nan Ròdach

**What it holds.** The Old Spirits keep the reverence of the hearths as it was before the Hall: "reverence for ancestors, for the vein, for the weather that decides the mining season" ({{date:II-0005}}). The chronicle calls it the island's oldest continuous tradition. It has no order of worship and no council. Its god, as the map gives him, is Crom, the High Spirit, but the annals never name Crom. Every telling of the Old Spirits speaks of the ancestors and the weather, not of any one power over them. When the High Spirit was first called by that name, or by whom, is not known.

**Rites and sayings.** The living heads of families name their ancestors aloud. The names of the dead are cut on wooden posts, which are recut each generation as the old ones rot ({{date:III-0056}}). The faith keeps the walk to Allt an Àigh as its own, and after the Keepers declined the stream it took Muireach Liath's ruling for its saying:

> *Buinidh an t-allt do na sinnsearan agus don t-sìde.*
> The stream belongs to the ancestors and to the weather.

It blesses what comes from the vein and nothing else. When copper was found behind Àth fhiadhaich, the town's Seann Spioradan house held no blessing for it ({{date:V-0205}}).

**Holy places.** Dùn ìseal ({{place:burg:315}}) and its spirit-house; Seann Dunn ({{place:burg:18}}), where the east-coast houses built a common hall for naming the ancestors of forty families ({{date:V-0094}}); Allt an Àigh, with the walkers' hall at Achadh gharbh ({{date:V-0049}}); and Làrach an Teampaill, which at the houses' request was entered by the library as "measured and let be" ({{date:V-0121}}).

**History.** By the end of the Holy Age the Hall's list counted the old folk as "a company of its own" ({{date:II-0246}}). In the Age of Sundering they built a guest-house at the stream, and its book records pilgrims from every shire ({{date:III-0175}}). Under the humans this was the first faith to refuse the Company's ore on principle. The priests at Achadh naomh burned peat and taught "that ore burned for wages is ore burned without the ancestors' leave" ({{date:IV-0067}}). In the Long Drought the Administration's watchman at Allt an Àigh, posted to charge pilgrims for the water, was recalled after one season ({{date:IV-0222}}). In the New Age the Rite's custodians discouraged the walk and did not forbid it ({{date:V-0048}}).

**Where it is held now.** 126 burgs, all Ròdaich, spread through the east from the north-east coast to the south. The largest are Seann Bhral ({{place:burg:110}}), Achadh mhòr ({{place:burg:395}}), Baile Mòr mhòr ({{place:burg:365}}), Muileann bhàn ({{place:burg:502}}), Achadh naomh ({{place:burg:145}}) and Dùn dhearg ({{place:burg:422}}). The map marks temples in twenty-four of its towns, more than for any other faith. The chief are Dùn ìseal, Seann Dunn, Seann Tarr ({{place:burg:34}}), whose shrine draws the whole north-east at the great feasts, and Cathair naomh ({{place:burg:192}}), whose elders the shrine-keepers of the north answer to. The rest are the shrines of wells, springs, fords and holy woods, and the houses of the largest towns.

### IV. Creideamh nan Ròdach

The Faith of the Ròdaich is the Obsidian Rite in its orthodox form, "organized doctrine rather than diffuse folk reverence, built around the leyline and the unbroken line of the obsidian-touched" ({{date:V-0028}}). As an organised body it is the youngest of the Ròdaich faiths. As a rite it claims to be the oldest. Both are true. Its god on the map is Dòrsair, the Monster of the Gates, which stands at the door between the mountain and those who take from it. The annals do not record that name before the map. Its seat is at the southern Seann Skell ({{place:burg:431}}), where its largest house stands ({{date:V-0029}}). Its saying is the Law's own:

> *Làn an dùirn, agus gun tuilleadh.*
> The fill of the fist, and no more.

It holds nine burgs. The largest are Seann Skell, Tobar dhearg ({{place:burg:398}}), Muileann naomh ({{place:burg:326}}), Caol ruadh ({{place:burg:15}}) and Àth fhionn ({{place:burg:93}}). Its history is given in section XII.

### V. Creideamh an t-Seabhaig

**What it holds.** The Faith of the Hawk is the Rite's older form. It teaches that the vein is Mòd, the Hungry Hawk, "who must be fed the measured handful and no more, since a hawk overfed will not hunt and a hawk starved will turn on the hand" ({{date:III-0062}}). The map calls it dualist because of those two dangers: too much and too little both ruin, and the measure lies between them. The Hawk holds that the measure belongs to the hawk and not to any custodian.

**Rites and sayings.** Each household feeds its hawk before it eats, and the handful is burned at dawn with the hawk on the wrist. The fowlers who began the rite gave its first saying ({{date:II-0158}}):

> *Is acras an lasair.*
> The flame is a hunger.

Gormshuil's sayings were written down after her death as Leabhar an t-Seabhaig, the Book of the Hawk. The copyist marked every saying he had not heard from her own mouth, and those outnumbered the rest ({{date:III-0065}}). The saying most often cut over hawk-house doors is:

> *Cha shealg seabhag sàthach.*
> A sated hawk will not hunt.

**Holy places.** The oldest hawk carving on the island is over a door at Àth mhòr ({{date:I-0131}}), and it is older than any rite. The rite itself began at the Seann Skell of the northern coast ({{place:burg:132}}). The first house of the faith is at Cnoc bheag ({{place:burg:20}}).

**History.** The dawn-hawk spread along the north coast in the Holy Age, and as it spread the Keeper wrote that it was no concern of the Hall's ({{date:II-0159}}). Later it became the first rite to refuse the Hall's measure-check outright ({{date:II-0216}}). The faith was formalised, not founded, when Gormshuil nic Artair gathered the old rite at Cnoc bheag into the first house of Creideamh an t-Seabhaig ({{date:III-0062}}). A second house followed at Ros gheal ({{date:III-0063}}) and a southern one at Muileann naomh ({{date:III-0101}}). The schism came when a council at Cnoc bheag split over whether Mòd's measure could ever be raised ({{date:III-0140}}). Those who called it fixed kept the name of the Hungry Hawk. The Fed Hawk were turned out to the northern Seann Skell ({{date:III-0142}}), and their petition to raise the handful by a quarter was the first recorded request to alter the Small-Burning Law ({{date:III-0143}}). It was refused, and the record does not follow the Fed Hawk as a separate house after that.

Under the humans the Hawk resisted before any organised resistance existed. Murchadh Seabhag preached that Mòd starves those who eat faster than the land can feed them ({{date:IV-0066}}), and his sermon was the first Ròdaich text the Company's agents were told to confiscate. When hawk-priests were barred from the camps ({{date:IV-0095}}), the crews carried the sermons in themselves ({{date:IV-0162}}). Ìomhar Seabhag's shrines served as meeting houses for the Lamp-Watch ({{date:IV-0284}}), and when the war came he called it Mòd's own hunger turned back on those who fed it ({{date:IV-0348}}). In the New Age the Hawk houses refused the Rite's order of worship ({{date:V-0030}}) and named their own custodian at Inis ìseal ({{date:V-0131}}). They wrote out their order, which until then had been spoken and never written, and they keep the copy locked away ({{date:V-0132}}). They accepted a place under the high custodian only on condition that they kept that order ({{date:V-0166}}). The quarrel with the Rite has quietened, but it has not ended.

**Where it is held now.** Fourteen burgs, all Ròdaich, along the north-central coast and up the Abhainn ìseal. Cnoc bheag is the largest, and its Hawk house is the richest of any faith outside the capital ({{date:V-0161}}). After it come Inis bhàn ({{place:burg:371}}), Ros gheal ({{place:burg:24}}), the northern Seann Skell and Ros leathan ({{place:burg:161}}).

### VI. Feallsanachd an Fhèidh

**What it holds.** The Philosophy of the Deer is a school, not a church. It formed around one question, why the vein answers some hands and not others ({{date:II-0021}}): "the first attempt to reason about the obsidian rather than simply revere it." It does not deny that the vein is holy. It asks what the holiness does. None of the school's records in the library show the stag, Camaran, being worshipped. His name first appears "in texts much younger than these events" ({{date:II-0023}}). The map counts the Deer among the faiths, and the school has not objected.

**Rites and sayings.** Its one public observance is the yearly walking of the bounds of its sacred forest ({{date:III-0092}}). Everything else it does is argument. Its oldest question, as the masters put it to beginners, is:

> *Carson an làmh seo?*
> Why this hand?

A student who thinks he has solved it is given Aonghas mac Coinnich's answer ({{date:IV-0114}}):

> *Chan e sin a' cheist.*
> That is not the question.

**Holy places.** Coille Naomh Muileann chiar ({{place:marker:23}}), "marked sacred, long before any organized faith claims it formally" ({{date:I-0048}}), where the white stag was first seen ({{date:I-0050}}); Dùn thais ({{place:burg:77}}), with the first stone temple of the stag's people ({{date:II-0233}}); and Caol shean ({{place:burg:197}}), the school's house in the New Age.

**Where it is held now.** 133 burgs, all Ròdaich, in the north-east, along the east coast and in the eastern hills. The largest are Caol bheag ({{place:burg:475}}), Baile gharbh ({{place:burg:131}}), Dùn thais, Caol shean and Caol gharbh ({{place:burg:443}}). The map marks sixteen of its houses as temples, though the school calls them halls: among them Dùn thais, Caol shean, Caol bheag, Ros fhionn ({{place:burg:76}}) and the church villages whose chapels served the silver-miners and the old bridge. Muileann chaol ({{place:burg:246}}), the library town, is a Deer town. The school's history is given in section XIII.

### VII. Creideamh an Aon-adharcaich Dhuibh

**What it holds.** The Faith of the Dark Unicorn holds "that light and dark are two powers in balance and that the vein belongs to both" ({{date:III-0089}}). Its beast has two natures, one that keeps and one that devours, and so does the vein ({{date:II-0220}}). It is the faith of the capital and the court. Its saying is the wood-watchers':

> *Tha dà nàdar aig a' bhèist, agus aig an fhèith.*
> The beast has two natures, and so has the vein.

**Rites and holy places.** Its priests read omens for rulers and bury by their own rite. King Aonghas mac Uisdein was the first king buried that way ({{date:III-0096}}). Its floors are laid in black and white, and the floor of its great house at Cathair mhòr ({{place:burg:23}}) took eleven years ({{date:III-0198}}). Its sacred wood is Coille Naomh Cnoc bheag ({{place:marker:24}}), near the lesser Cnoc bheag south of the capital ({{place:burg:504}}). That town is not the Hawk river port of the same name.

**History.** The faith began as a watch. A small company settled at the edge of a wood no one had ever cleared and watched for a black horned beast that walked in it ({{date:II-0114}}). Its quarrel with the stag's people over both sacred woods became the holy-wood dispute, "settled by dividing custody rather than choosing a winner" ({{date:II-0117}}). Anna Ruadh took a wood-watcher into her household at Cathair dhearg to read the omens ({{date:II-0210}}). Cathair dhomhain refused one, and the rivalry between the two towns took a religious turn "it never loses" ({{date:II-0211}}). Cathair dhomhain is a Unicorn town now, with its own temple. The faith was formalised when King Aonghas took up the teaching of Uallach ({{date:III-0089}}). By royal grant its priests then took sole custody of the western wood, and the other claimants withdrew for reasons the record does not give ({{date:III-0091}}).

Under the humans it split over the Residency's coin. The priests at Cathair dhearg blessed it ({{date:IV-0175}}). Those who refused went to the sacred forest, where they received custodians who had lost their seats ({{date:IV-0179}}). Sixty years later the capital's temple sheltered the council that Strake had locked out ({{date:IV-0315}}). In the forest the dissenters sat with custodians and the Lamp-Watch when the council of the mining regions was formed ({{date:IV-0322}}). In the restored kingdom the house at Cathair dhearg got back the keeping of the regalia and refused the Rite a share of it ({{date:V-0033}}).

**Where it is held now.** 103 burgs, all Ròdaich, across the middle and south of the island. The largest are Ros dhomhain ({{place:burg:27}}), Cathair mhòr, Àth shean ({{place:burg:67}}), Seann Chwen ({{place:burg:36}}) and Baile ghlas ({{place:burg:45}}). The map marks seventeen of its temples. Five are the great houses of Cathair mhòr, Cathair dhomhain ({{place:burg:26}}), Cathair dhearg ({{place:burg:19}}), Ceann leathan ({{place:burg:22}}) and Cuan uaine ({{place:burg:104}}); the others are the priests' towns, such as Cathair naomh ({{place:burg:231}}) and Cnoc bhàn ({{place:burg:183}}), where acolytes are trained for the capital's temples, and the chapels along the roads to the capital. Cnoc naomh, whose people once kept the old reverence "with no hawk, wolf, sting or stag in it" ({{date:II-0246}}), is a Unicorn town on the map.

### VIII. Creideamh a' Mhadaidh-allaidh

**What it holds.** The Faith of the Wolf holds that one god watches the whole island as a guard watches a road ({{date:II-0217}}). That god is Clach, the Resplendent Direwolf, "who keeps the island as a wolf keeps its range" ({{date:III-0086}}). The god's name is the common word for *stone*. The first wolf was a white stone at the gate of Caol leathan, on which road-guards swore their watch, and the annals say that "later ages will read more into the stone than they did" ({{date:II-0100}}). The record does not show the Wolf claiming the vein. When the Rite was formalised, the Wolf houses sent no witnesses, only "a letter saying they wish it well" ({{date:V-0031}}).

**Rites and sayings.** Oaths are taken before the wolf-stone. The Wolf's houses nurse the sick. At Inis chrom its preachers died doing it, "and the town becomes the Wolf's for good" ({{date:III-0118}}). Its saying comes from the road-guards:

> *Mar a ghleidheas am freiceadan an rathad, gleidhidh Clach an t-eilean.*
> As the guard keeps the road, Clach keeps the island.

**Holy places.** Caol leathan ({{place:burg:1}}), whose Wolf house is the largest of the faith and rings a bell for the hours ({{date:V-0087}}); Inis thais ({{place:burg:166}}), where a second wolf-stone of white quartz was dragged from the mountain ({{date:II-0161}}) and the first temple to Clach by name was built ({{date:III-0087}}); and Baile gheal ({{place:burg:232}}).

**History.** The wolf-sworn were soldiers before they were a faith. The faith was formalised when Dòmhnall mac Ailein, a net-maker of Inis thais, gave their one god a name ({{date:III-0086}}). Its schism was over the dead. Those who would not pray to Clach for the dead kept the temple at Inis thais, and those who would built a house at Baile gheal ({{date:III-0191}}, {{date:III-0193}}). The record does not say the two houses have been reconciled. In the New Age the Wolf houses have acted as banks as well as churches. They issued chits on house paper when coin ran short ({{date:V-0027}}) and lent the money for Doire shean's harbour ({{date:V-0119}}). They also published a count of their own people that matched the kingdom's to within a hundred ({{date:V-0219}}).

**Where it is held now.** 21 burgs, all Ròdaich, in the south-west. The largest are Doire shean ({{place:burg:324}}), Inis thais, Inis chrom ({{place:burg:92}}), Baile ghorm ({{place:burg:170}}) and Seann Warr ({{place:burg:64}}). The map marks temples in eight of its towns: Inis thais, still the chief shrine of the faith, Caol leathan, Baile gheal, Doire shean, Inis chrom, Caol fhiadhaich ({{place:burg:191}}), Baile Mòr fhionn ({{place:burg:204}}) and Cnoc àrsaidh ({{place:burg:433}}), which holds the oldest shrines of the Wolf.

### IX. Creideamh na Scairpe Buidhe

**What it holds.** The Faith of the Yellow Scorpion holds that one power stings the liar wherever he is, and that the scorpion is only its sign. It was the first creed on the island to call itself the only true one ({{date:II-0218}}). Its god is Cnut, and its central teaching is about the sea: "Cnut closed the western sea behind the fleet ... it must not be tried again" ({{date:III-0120}}).

**Rites and sayings.** Oaths of trade are sworn on a scorpion's sting ({{date:II-0086}}). No boat of the faithful may go out of sight of the headlands, and on the west coast steering by the stars was lost entirely ({{date:III-0123}}). At the harbour mouth the priests pray ({{date:III-0212}}):

> *A Chnuit, cùm a' mhuir dùinte.*
> Cnut, keep the sea shut.

Since {{year:III-0223}} that prayer has been said on the day of the empty-harbour rite. So on one quay, on one day, some mourn the departed and some pray that no one follows them. Neither side is pleased.

**Holy places.** Seann Skell in the west ({{place:burg:489}}), where the creed was declared; Baile àrsaidh ({{place:burg:420}}), where the sting-oath began; Seann Vell ({{place:burg:127}}), which has the first scorpion carved in yellow stone for worship ({{date:II-0219}}); and Cnoc ghorm ({{place:burg:142}}), the largest house in the north.

**History.** The sting-oath went from Baile àrsaidh up the coast to Ros bheag ({{date:II-0160}}) and became a creed at Seann Skell. The faith was formalised when Mànas mac Ruairidh named its power Cnut ({{date:III-0120}}). A worked timber longer than any tree on the island, held with nails no smith on Rodos could place, came ashore at Ceann mhòr. It was carried to Seann Skell, shown to the Scorpion's priests, and burned ({{date:III-0236}}). That was twenty-five years before the humans came.

How the Tuathaich came to the Scorpion the record does not say ({{date:V-0044}}). By the early New Age most Tuathaich towns kept the faith. Cnoc ghorm's house taught in the humans' tongue and read the scripture in the Ròdais text used on the west coast ({{date:V-0045}}). The house at Seann Bhral turned away northern worshippers from its feast, saying "the god is the same and the house is not" ({{date:V-0103}}). They held the feast at Cnoc ghorm instead, and within a generation it was the larger of the two ({{date:V-0104}}). On the eve of the Long War the Scorpion house at Seann Skell refused to bless the enforcers' depot ({{date:V-0303}}).

**Where it is held now.** 86 burgs: 35 Ròdaich and 51 Tuathaich. Its seat, the western Seann Skell, is the largest town on Rodos. After it come Caol mhòr ({{place:burg:95}}), Cuan shean ({{place:burg:282}}), Ceann mhòr ({{place:burg:143}}) and Baile fhiadhaich ({{place:burg:134}}).

### X. Creideamh nan Tuathach

**What it holds.** The Tuathaich communities of the north keep their own folk tradition ({{date:V-0042}}), "including, quietly, the First Canoe fragment the Ròdaich priesthood still calls superstition." The map calls it shamanic and names its god Donn, the Eternal. Beyond that the record holds almost nothing, and what it has comes from outside the faith. Few Tuathaich would speak to the chronicle clerks ({{date:V-0080}}). The north's books stayed in private keeping ({{date:V-0227}}). The request to archive Tuathaich oral history at the library was tabled rather than rejected, and it is still tabled ({{date:V-0296}}). This appendix does not know the faith's rites, its sayings, its holy places, or whether Donn is the Tuathaich's own name for their god.

**The First Canoe.** The only one of the faith's tellings known outside the north is the fragment its tellers set in the Ancient Age: "a named steersman, a first crossing" ({{date:I-0038}}). The steersman's name is in no Ròdaich record. The record does not say how families whose forebears came over the water in {{year:IV-0001}} came to keep a telling set so long before the Sundering. This appendix leaves it where its tellers set it, notes that the north still keeps it in the present day ({{date:V-0394}}), and does not resolve it.

**Where it is held now.** Twelve burgs, all Tuathaich, along the north. The largest are Doire ghlas ({{place:burg:209}}), Muileann leathan ({{place:burg:234}}), Inis mhin ({{place:burg:9}}) and Baile ghlas in Siorrachd Àth àrsaidh ({{place:burg:235}}).

### XI. Rùn-dìomhair na h-Oidhche Glaise

**What it holds.** Of the Mystery of the Grey Night the record knows the name, the god, one custom and one town. The god is Sìne, the Peaceful Goose. The custom is "one night of silence in the year", and outsiders are not told which night it is ({{date:V-0043}}). No saying is known.

**History.** The cult first appears in a Mission report that human labourers near Baile chrom were meeting at night in the fields to pray to a grey goose ({{date:IV-0262}}). The Mission called it a superstition learned from the Ròdaich, and the Ròdaich disowned it. From the Ancient Age the record keeps a telling of the grey geese that winter on Eilean ghorm and are never hunted there ({{date:I-0055}}). The record does not connect the two, and this appendix does not either. After the Severance the vigil-keeper Hannah Crane held the rites openly ({{date:V-0043}}). A magistrate fined her, and the vigil moved indoors ({{date:V-0108}}). The keeper of Taigh-solais Baile chrom ({{place:marker:12}}) reported that the vigil was kept in the lighthouse's shadow ({{date:V-0123}}). In the Long War soldiers searched the town on a night when it was wholly dark and silent, and they found nothing ({{date:V-0345}}).

**Where it is held now.** One burg: Baile chrom in the far north-west ({{place:burg:305}}). It is not the Baile chrom in the hills ({{place:burg:187}}), and the record takes care to keep the two apart ({{date:IV-0155}}).

### XII. Of the Obsidian Rite

The Rite is older than any name for it. Its root is the slab over the crack and the handful in the cup. What the chronicle calls the Obsidian Rite, and the map calls Creideamh nan Ròdach, is the part of the old reverence that holds the vein itself to be sacred. It is not the sign of a god or the seat of a spirit. It is the holy thing.

**The vein as sacred.** The Rite holds that the vein bleeds because it is alive in some way the Ròdaich are not asked to understand. What is broken from it must be burned in measure or returned. The vein answers a line of people, and that answering is itself holy. The doctrine of 1935 is "built around the leyline and the unbroken line of the obsidian-touched" ({{date:V-0028}}): the vein under the ground and the blood of those it has answered. Fionnghal nic Artair's defence of the Small-Burning Law, the only one that survives, argued that "the Law was never about the ore but about the people who would become whatever the burning made them" ({{date:IV-0065}}). She wrote it two generations before Aisling was born, and it has been read as prophecy since. She did not claim to be prophesying.

**Under the Keepers.** In the Holy Age the Rite belonged to the Hall. The Keepers held the cup, took the census of hands ({{date:II-0142}}) and sentenced households that burned beyond the measure ({{date:II-0028}}). Their roll holds one hundred and forty names ({{date:II-0248}}).

**Under the vein-house.** Each ship of the Sundering carried one sealed measure of ore and no more ({{date:III-0016}}). The vein-house above Muileann dhearg kept the custody-book, and in {{year:III-0138}} the crown and the custody were joined. The Rite's worst hour in that age came in war, not heresy. Raghnall Ruadh, holding the vein-house, refused the handful to every hearth in the shires that stood against him, "and the act turns more people against him than any battle" ({{date:III-0109}}). The council refused the Fed Hawk's petition and refused Clann Choinnich's request to smelt silver with the ore ({{date:III-0208}}), "the first request to burn the ore for work rather than worship."

**Under the humans.** The Small-Burning Law lapsed without repeal "once it stops being profitable to enforce" ({{date:IV-0063}}). The Rite lived on in shrines at the pit mouths. At Cill dhìreach the crews left the first piece of ore from each new gallery, and the Company allowed it "because it costs a stone a gallery" ({{date:IV-0150}}). At Cill ghlas the custodians kept the rites at the mouth of the oldest cutting ({{date:IV-0151}}). They agreed the word *fuil-ghuail* there ({{date:IV-0251}}), and the Rending swallowed that shrine ({{date:IV-0271}}).

**Formalised.** The Rite was formalised on {{date:V-0028}}. Dòmhnall mac Thormoid was seated at the southern Seann Skell as Àrd-choimheadaiche, and his first charge was a written order of worship: "there had never been one" ({{date:V-0029}}). The Hawk refused the order and the Wolf stood aside. When the council kept the night shift, the custodians "enter a protest and then bless the shafts" ({{date:V-0052}}). The Rite has worked that way ever since. It protests how the kingdom uses the vein, and then it blesses it.

**The Depletion.** The high custodian ruled that dubhan was "not forbidden, since it is not ore, and not blessed, since it is not ore" ({{date:V-0246}}). When the beacons were converted ({{date:V-0251}}), the obsidian flame of Taigh-solais Tobar dhearg was carried to Seann Skell and left to burn out on the altar ({{date:V-0252}}). When the coinage seal lost its obsidian ink, the high custodian protested. The council replied that the kingdom had never said it held the vein sacred ({{date:V-0288}}).

**The Rite now.** One face of the Sloc Mòr is kept open under the Rite's custody ({{date:V-0381}}). Twelve hewers work it, the last of their trade ({{date:V-0382}}). On {{date:V-0392}} they cut the year's altar-ore. It filled four baskets, and the high custodian divided it among the houses by lot.

That is why the Rite holds only nine burgs. Most Ròdaich who hold the vein sacred keep it at the hearth, as the Old Spirits do, or at the local altar, as the Hawk does. The Rite is their church in name, and it is their whole faith in only nine towns. It was formalised after the vein had begun to fail. In less than a century it went from an order of worship for the whole island to four baskets of ore.

### XIII. Of the Deer's Question

> *Carson an làmh seo?*

The Philosophy of the Deer began with one question, asked at Muileann chiar in the Holy Age: why does the vein answer some hands and not others ({{date:II-0021}})? Some have made much of how close *fèidh*, deer, sounds to *fèith*, vein. The school's own texts do not.

**Forbidden and allowed.** The Hall shut the galleries to the stag's people "on the ground that a question asked of the vein is a demand made of it" ({{date:II-0024}}). They were later let back in on an oath "to ask nothing of the vein aloud" ({{date:II-0066}}). The Hall called that a reconciliation, and the school called it a truce. Long after the truce the stag's people made the first Ròdaich letters out of cutters' tally-notches ({{date:II-0088}}), and nearly every later hand on the island descends from them.

**Birth or effort.** Apprentices kept strike-books, written accounts of every blow ({{date:II-0125}}). Reading them, Donnchadh mac Iain found that the ore bled brighter for older cutters, whatever house they came from ({{date:II-0162}}). The chronicle records the split that followed: "a breakaway sect argues the vein answers effort, not birth — a quiet, centuries-early rehearsal of the argument the Long War will eventually be fought over" ({{date:II-0164}}). The breakaway school at Muileann chaol did not write a cutter's parentage in the book at all ({{date:II-0165}}). The Keeper Raonaid Dhubh heard both schools and gave no ruling, "saying the vein has not asked the Hall to decide" ({{date:II-0166}}).

**The years of disputation.** At Dùn thais the question was argued by rule ({{date:III-0074}}). Eilidh Dhubh's tracts for the effort-school were read aloud at harvest gatherings across the north-east ({{date:III-0094}}). Then Raghnall mac Chaluim showed that the heads of the custodian houses lived a few years longer than their tenants, and asked whether the vein answers the hand that holds it ({{date:III-0171}}). The house was later reopened only on condition that the question of longer lives was not raised ({{date:III-0230}}). His table is nearly seven hundred years older than the first of the immortal generation.

**Under the humans.** Samuel Wren asked the masters why the vein should be holy. They asked him why his fuel should be burned ({{date:IV-0056}}). Aonghas mac Coinnich asked why the humans cannot see the colour in broken ore, and said it was the wrong question ({{date:IV-0114}}). Tolley closed the hall at Dùn thais ({{date:IV-0186}}). The last public disputation asked whether a question may be forbidden, and the Administration's clerk recorded that it may ({{date:IV-0187}}). After that the masters taught under the trees ({{date:IV-0188}}), until the last of them died in {{year:IV-0247}}.

**The revival.** The school reopened in {{year:V-0162}}, when its question became "suddenly urgent again as the vein itself starts running short." Seonaid nic Ghill-Eathain lectured in the sacred wood ({{date:V-0163}}), and the school took a house at Caol shean, where students debated the census note on the obsidian-touched openly ({{date:V-0165}}). When the Mine Board searched for a second vein, she wrote that "nobody has asked why there is one vein" ({{date:V-0175}}). The Board filed her letter, and the search found nothing ({{date:V-0176}}). The school's own word for its revival is *dubhan-tùsail* ({{date:V-0273}}).

**What the question has come to.** In the Holy Age it asked whether the ore bled for birth or for practice. In the Age of Sundering it asked whether the vein lengthened lives. Under the humans it asked why the humans could not see the colour. Since {{year:IV-0250}}, when the custodians agreed a plain word for what the vein had begun to do to a few children, the answer has had a name but no reason. The masters do not claim that the question caused the Long War, and no one has accused them of it. They would say only that it was asked in the Holy Age and has not been answered since.
